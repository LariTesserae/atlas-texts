## Preparation Guide: The Canopy Plateau

---

### I. Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before we talk about gear, you need to understand the one thing that makes this place different from anything else you'll visit in the multiverse: **this place is alive, and it is not in a hurry.**

Most worlds you'll travel to have been shaped by intention — built, mined, paved, plowed. Even wild places have a kind of urgency to them: storms forming, predators hunting, seasons turning. The Canopy Plateau has none of that. Everything here operates on a timescale that will make you feel like a mayfly. The bridges beneath your feet took a human lifetime to grow. The walls around you are the fused remains of trees that were trained before your great-great-grandparents were born. A platform you stand on is slowly sinking — not because it's failing, but because the roots beneath it are still expanding, still compressing the soil, still *becoming*. In a hundred years, the whole geometry of this grove will be subtly different. You will not see it move. But it is moving.

This isn't a ruin. It isn't an abandoned city. It is a civilization that negotiated its own dissolution into a living system, and the negotiation is still ongoing. Treat every surface as something that *notices* you, even if it doesn't care. Because on a long enough timescale, it will care. Your boot prints in the root-mat may still be visible a century from now, pressed into wood that grew around them.

That said — you'll be fine. You just need to prepare properly.

### II. Footwear and Lower Body Protection

**This is your single most important equipment decision.**

You will be walking in water. Not "a puddle" water. Not "splash across a stream" water. You will be walking into water that starts at your ankles and climbs steadily to your waist or higher if you take a wrong step into one of the deeper channels. The water is warm at the surface — body temperature or close to it — cool underneath, and it is *everywhere*. You cannot keep your feet dry. Accept this or don't come.

**Recommended:** Neoprene wading boots with reinforced soles, the kind used for river crossings in mountainous terrain. They need to grip root-surfaces — which are wet, algae-coated, and often uneven — and protect against the silt-bottomed terrain beneath the waterline. The substrate here is pale, root-tangled soil that is firm in patches and treacherously soft in others. A good sole will tell you the difference. Avoid anything with a smooth bottom. You *will* slip without traction.

Bring a second pair to rotate out. Neoprene doesn't dry overnight in this humidity. It won't mold — the locals (if there are still locals) seem to have solved that problem, probably through material science you haven't encountered before — but your feet need to breathe.

**Do not wear leather boots.** Leather in this environment is a death sentence for the material. It will absorb water, soften, grow organisms, and fall apart within a week. More importantly, the local architecture is made of living wood, and there is evidence — anecdotal, but consistent — that organic materials left against the grown surfaces will be *absorbed*. Not consumed. Absorbed. The way a graft joins to a living tree. Leather, cotton, untreated fibers — they bond to the root-surfaces and become part of the structure. You don't want your boot becoming a permanent part of someone's doorframe.

**Gaiters.** Bring them. The floating vegetation mats in the shallows will grab at anything you give them. They're not carnivorous — they seem to be mostly benign — but they tangle. Fine, root-like tendrils will wrap around an ankle or a boot shaft and hold fast. You'll spend ten minutes pulling free if you don't have gaiters. Half the local fauna seems to have evolved to move through these mats effortlessly. You have not.

### III. Clothing and Body Protection

The air is cool and wet. It sits in your lungs. It is like breathing the inside of a living thing — because it is. The humidity is near-saturated at all times, and the temperature is stable, probably between 15–18°C (around 60°F). You don't need heavy insulation, but you do need **layering that handles sustained dampness**.

**Base layer:** Moisture-wicking synthetic or merino wool. Not cotton. Cotton in this environment becomes a second skin that never dries and chafes you raw within hours. The fine mist that hangs in the canopy will soak through everything eventually, so your base layer needs to keep that moisture away from your skin.

**Mid layer:** Lightweight fleece or synthetic insulation. The air is cool, and the water saps heat from your body even when it's warm. You'll be generating more body heat than you expect from walking through flooded terrain.

**Outer layer:** A waterproof breathable shell. Something with sealed seams. Not because of rain — rain is constant and light here, barely more than condensation falling out of the mist — but because of splash, immersion, and the general atmosphere of saturated air. A good shell will also protect against the **biological load** of the place. The air is thick with spores, pollen, fungal fragments, and the tiny skeletal casings of insects. If you have allergies on your home world, they will be *spectacular* here. Bring antihistamines. Bring an epinephrine auto-injector if your home-world's biology makes anaphylaxis a possibility. The air here is alive in a way your lungs are not prepared for.

**Cover your skin as much as possible.** Long sleeves, trousers tucked into boots. Not because anything is actively dangerous — the fauna here doesn't seem to regard humans as prey or even as interesting — but because your skin will be colonized. Within an hour of arrival, you'll start to notice a faint greenish tinge to exposed skin. Within a day, tiny root-like filaments will have anchored into your pores. They don't appear to cause harm. They don't seem to grow beyond a few millimeters. But they are *alive*, and they are *yours* now, and you will shed them for days after you leave, if you leave. Travelers have reported the filaments continuing to emerge from skin for up to a week after departure. This is normal. Don't panic. But cover up if you'd prefer not to become a temporary host.

### IV. Navigation

This place is a labyrinth. Not because anyone designed it to be — the opposite, in fact. It has accumulated organically, without central planning, and the result is a tangle of channels, platforms, bridges, and walkways that make no geometric sense by human standards. The channels wind. The walkways loop. The bridges connect things that aren't obviously connected. And from any given vantage point, you can see only a fraction of the structure because the canopy blocks overhead sightlines and the channels bend away into green tunnels that all look identical.

**Do not rely on a compass.** Not because the magnetic field is disrupted — though it may be, have your multiverse field instruments check — but because the concept of cardinal direction is functionally useless here. The canopy blocks the sun. There are no landmarks tall enough to orient against. The channels curve and the channels intersect and the channels sometimes dead-end into walls of living wood that look navigable from a distance but are solid. You will lose your sense of direction within twenty minutes of entering the grove.

**What to use instead:** Breadcrumb navigation. Drop small, non-organic markers — metallic tags, synthetic fiber strips, anything inorganic — at every junction. Mark them with UV-reactive paint if you want to find them again; conventional colors blend into the green-grey-brown palette of the grove with disturbing speed. Markers that *don't* use organic material are important because the local ecology will *absorb* organic markers. Wood chips, paper, cotton string — these will be colonized within hours and structurally indistinguishable from the architecture within a day. You will leave breadcrumbs that grow into the wall.

**Acoustic awareness.** Use the water's reflective properties. The sound environment here is unusual: sound carries with doubled clarity off the water surface, and the canopy holds low frequencies, creating a resonant chamber effect. You can hear footsteps on the walkways from a surprising distance. You can hear the fish breaking the surface in channels you can't see. Learn the soundscape. If you hear a particular pattern of groaning — deep, sub-bass, felt more than heard — that's a large tree shifting. They do this without warning and without pattern. If you're standing on a root-platform when it happens, you will feel the flex through your boots. Move to the center. The edges are where the flex is greatest.

### V. The Architecture is Not Safe (In the Way You Think)

Everything here is structurally sound — the grown wood is dense, dense enough to rival steel in compression, and the root-system foundations extend deep into the saturated soil. A bridge that looks like a few braided vines is holding three times your body weight with margin to spare. The platforms are stable. The walls are solid.

But **the architecture is still growing**, and it is growing in ways the builders did not anticipate.

A doorway that was wide enough for a human when it was first shaped may now be narrow — not by much, perhaps an inch on each side, but over a century that adds up. If you're carrying a pack, measure your width with the frame before you commit. Some of the tighter arches have already claimed backpacks, jackets, and one traveler's walking stick, slowly squeezing them into the gap between living wood as though being digested.

A platform that was flat may now be gently convex, as noted. This is fine for walking but treacherous if you place heavy equipment on it — it will slowly tilt toward the center as the growth continues, and anything loose will slide. Don't set anything down and assume it will stay where you left it. Check it again in an hour.

**Do not cut anything.** Do not break any branch, trim any vine, scratch any surface. The growth response here is aggressive. A cut that would scar over in a normal tree will, in this grove, trigger a localized growth spurt — the tree walls off the wound with new tissue, and if you've cut into a structure someone is still using, you may have just sealed a doorway, blocked a walkway, or fused a bridge to a trunk. The people who built here learned this the hard way, over centuries. You don't have centuries.

### VI. Water and Food

The water is not potable without treatment. It is warm, slow-moving, full of organic matter, and hosts a dense microbial ecosystem. It *looks* clear but it teems with life at the microscopic level. Bring a portable water filter rated for biological contamination. Boiling works but fuel is heavy and you're going to need it for other things.

There is food here. The fruit of some of the canopy plants is edible — it looks like a large, pale, oblong berry growing in clusters from the bromeliad-like rosettes. It is mildly sweet, vaguely reminiscent of kiwi, and seems safe based on visual and olfactory cues (no warning coloration, no bitter or astringent smell). **Do not eat anything else without testing it first.** The local ecology is dense and interconnected. Some of the flowering plants in the floating mats appear to be chemically defended; there are markings on the leaves — reddened edges — that suggest a contact irritant. Don't touch your face after handling anything in the grove until you've washed your hands in treated water.

Protein is harder to come by. The fish are present in enormous numbers and appear to be a viable food source — they school in the shallows and can be caught by hand with surprising ease, as they seem adapted to predator evasion through sheer collective motion rather than individual speed. A net would be useful. A fishing line less so — the water clarity is low enough that lure-based fishing is unreliable, and the fish seem indifferent to vibration.

### VII. What You'll Encounter (That You Can't Prepare For)

The **curious ones**. The arboreal creatures — whoever they are, whatever they are — will watch you. They sit at the edges of walkways, in the vine-bridges, in the hanging structures, and they watch with those large, wet, patient eyes. They don't approach. They don't flee. They just *watch*. They will let you get within arm's reach before they move, and when they do, they move with an unhurried, almost sorrowful grace, as if they've seen visitors before and are mildly disappointed by each new one. Attempts to interact with them — food offerings, vocal communication, mimicry of their body language — have not produced any observable response, though there are accounts from other travelers of the creatures *bringing things*: a fallen flower placed on a walkway near where a traveler slept, a stick laid across a gap in a platform as if to help. It's impossible to know if this is communication. It might be coincidence. It might be an entirely different behavior that looks like generosity only from the outside.

**The almost-script patterns.** You will see them. On the merged trunks, on the grooved bark, on the fused roots of the bridges. Patterns that look almost like marks left by a tool — regular spacing, repeated angles, deliberate-looking arrangements that sit just at the threshold of intentionality. You will be tempted to read them. Don't. Or at least: don't read them *into* anything. The growth patterns of this wood are complex enough to produce shapes that mimic human intention through sheer combinatorial possibility. You are pattern-recognizing apes, and this forest knows it, and it is wearing the patterns just to see you look. (That may be projection. The forest may not be doing it *to* you. But the patterns are there, and they are strange.)

**The groaning.** At irregular intervals — sometimes every few minutes, sometimes separated by an hour — you will hear a deep, subsonic groan that travels through the wood and water and up through your body. It is not threatening. It is not alarming. But it is deeply, fundamentally *wrong* in a way that is hard to articulate to someone who hasn't heard it. It sounds like something enormous remembering that it is alive.

### VIII. Psychological Preparation

This place will slow you down. Not physically — the walking is easy, the terrain is mostly flat, the water is not cold enough to be punishing. It will slow you down **mentally**. There is something about the timescale of this place that seeps into your head. You'll find yourself pausing more. Standing still longer. Watching the light shift on the water without thinking about moving. You'll start to notice individual dragonflies and track their flight paths for minutes at a time. You'll find yourself listening to the bird calls as if they were language.

This is normal. This is what the place does. It was designed — or evolved, or accumulated — to take the urgency out of you. Whatever drove the builders to merge their civilization with a living forest, the result is an environment that resists haste. After two or three days here, you'll start to think in longer intervals. You'll stop counting hours. You'll start noticing the subtle changes — the shift in mist density, the slow deepening of a channel, the new growth on a bridge you passed yesterday that is measurably thicker today.

**This is fine if you have time.** If you're passing through, spend a day. Take in the scope of it, get out, move on. If you're staying longer — even a few days — be aware that your internal clock will drift. You will lose track of days. You will feel like you've been here for a week when it's been two days, and like it's been two days when it's been a week. Bring a timepiece you trust. Check it morning and evening. Keep yourself anchored to your home-world's rhythm or this place will dissolve it.

---

### IX. Departure Considerations

Leave nothing behind. Not food scraps. Not markers. Not fibers from your clothing. The ecology here integrates foreign material with unsettling efficiency. If you drop a scrap of synthetic fabric, come back in a year and it will be partially encased in living wood, growing toward it, binding it. The place doesn't *digest* foreign material — it *accepts* it. Everything becomes part of the structure. Everything becomes part of the organism. You don't want to leave a piece of yourself growing in a wall here.

And when you leave — when you walk back to the edge of the grove where the water thins and the ground firms up and you can finally see solid earth beneath your feet — you will feel something. Not sadness, exactly. Not longing. Something more like the feeling you get when you leave a place where you were genuinely, deeply *present*. The Canopy Plateau doesn't let you be anywhere else. It absorbs your attention, your urgency, your narrative, and holds you in the eternal, patient, green-dark moment of its own becoming.

You'll carry that with you. It fades. But slowly.

Like everything here.
