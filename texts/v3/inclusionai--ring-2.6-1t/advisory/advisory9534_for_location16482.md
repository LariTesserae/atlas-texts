# Traveler's Preparation Guide: The Salts

---

## I. Before You Go — What You Need to Understand

The Salts is not a wilderness. It is not a ruin. It is not a natural landscape that civilization abandoned. It is a place where industry, geology, and biology reached a kind of involuntary cooperation — none of them planned it, none of them wanted it, and none of them can undo it. When you arrive, you will be walking into an ecosystem that does not need you, does not notice you unless you provoke it, and will not hesitate to colonize anything you leave unattended. You need to internalize this before you step off the transit platform: **you are not visiting a place. You are entering a system.**

---

## II. Physical Preparation

### Heat and Humidity Acclimatization

If you are coming from a temperate or cool-origin multiverse, The Salts will punish you. The ambient temperature is high year-round — not the sharp, dry heat of a desert, but a thick, saturating warmth that sits on your body like a heated blanket. The humidity is extraordinary; your sweat will not evaporate efficiently, which means your body's primary cooling mechanism is compromised from the moment you arrive.

**Recommendation:** Spend at least three to five days in a controlled high-heat, high-humidity environment before departure if your physiology allows acclimatization. If you cannot, plan for significantly reduced physical output during your first forty-eight hours. Do not attempt long walks on arrival. The basin is flat and seems walkable — this is a trap. The terrain is uneven, the footing is unreliable, and the heat compounds fatigue in ways that don't feel dangerous until they are.

### The Ground

The mineral crust that covers most of The Salts varies in texture and stability. Dry stretches are crusted with pale whiteite, iron-orange deposits, and sulfurous yellow surfaces that can be hard underfoot and sharp enough to damage standard footwear. Wet zones — and you will encounter them, because the water seeps everywhere — turn to slick clay-brown mud that can go ankle-deep without warning. Subsurface voids from old drainage infrastructure and mineral dissolution are common. You can step onto what looks like solid ground and find yourself knee-deep in soft sediment within two strides.

**Footwear:** Bring reinforced, waterproof boots with aggressive tread and ankle support. The standard-issue multiverse trail boot is insufficient. You need something closer to shallow-water wading boots with a mineral-resistant sole. Break them in before you go. Blisters in this humidity will not heal cleanly.

**Walking poles or a staff:** Strongly recommended. Not for fitness — for probing the ground ahead of you. The visual texture of the crust does not reliably communicate what is beneath it. A pole will save you from a half-dozen falls per day.

### Radiation and UV Exposure

The copper sun behind the haze is deceptive. The haze does not filter ultraviolet radiation effectively — it scatters it, which means you get exposure from every direction, including upward off the white mineral ground. Sunburn happens faster than you expect, and the reflective ground means your lower face, ears, and neck are vulnerable even under a hat.

**Sun protection:** Full-spectrum UV-rated sunscreen (reapplied every two hours — the humidity will degrade it faster than you think), UV-blocking wraparound eye protection, and a wide-brimmed hat with a neck drape. Light-colored, long-sleeved, loose-fitting clothing — breathable, moisture-wicking fabric. You will be soaked in sweat regardless; the goal is to manage skin exposure and evaporation.

## III. Chemical and Biological Hazards

### The Water

Do not drink any water in The Salts. None of it. Not the standing pools, not the seepage, not the water beading on rocks. The mineral content is extreme — heavily alkaline, saturated with sulfides, borates, and lithium compounds in concentrations that will cause acute gastrointestinal distress and, with sustained exposure, real organ damage. The water is not poisonous in the dramatic, cinematic sense. It will not kill you quickly. It will make you deeply, persistently ill, and in the heat that illness will compound dangerously.

**Water supply:** Bring all the water you will need, or carry a high-quality reverse-osmosis filtration system rated for mineral-heavy water (standard portable filters will not handle this). The settlement has filtered water available for trade, but do not count on supply.

### The Air

The air quality is the environmental factor most visitors underestimate. The mineral haze is not merely dust — it is aerosolized mineral particulate, including sulfide compounds that will irritate your respiratory tract, eyes, and mucous membranes. On bad days (haze is heaviest in early morning and near the standing pools), breathing feels like inhaling warm chalk dust. It is not immediately dangerous for short exposures, but sustained breathing without protection will produce persistent coughing, sinus inflammation, and eye irritation that can ruin your ability to function within two days.

**Respiratory protection:** A light face covering — a buff or filtered mask — for any extended outdoor exposure. Eye drops (saline, preservative-free) for comfort. If you have any pre-existing respiratory conditions, take them seriously. This environment will aggravate them.

### Insects

The insect density is, to put it plainly, extraordinary. Mosquitoes breed in every standing pool and are active at dawn, dusk, and during the night. Their bites are more irritating than dangerous in most cases, but in this climate bite sites can inflame and become infected quickly. Ants are ubiquitous and investigative — they will find any food, any moisture, any gap in your clothing within minutes. During the warm months, they move in visible columns along the streets and into every building.

At night, flying insects thicken around any light source. Moths, beetles, and various flies will swarm a headlamp or lantern so densely that you will need to brush them away to see.

**Insect protection:** DEET-based repellent (high concentration — 30%+) applied to all exposed skin and to the outer layers of clothing. Permethrin-treated clothing is strongly recommended for any extended stay. A headnet for evening and nighttime activity. Seal your sleeping area meticulously — insect netting is not optional, it is essential.

### Larger Fauna Hazards

**Monitor lizards** are the apex semi-terrestrial predators in the basin. Large specimens exceed a meter in length, are fast, and have sharp claws and a powerful bite. They are generally not aggressive toward humans but will defend themselves if cornered or if you get between them and the water. They hunt in and around the invertebrate pools and bask on the warm mineral crust — watch where you step and where you sit.

**Snakes** are present. Most are non-venomous constrictors and small predators adapted to the rodent population, but there are species with medically significant bites. Watch your footing, especially near structures, drainage culverts, and shaded areas during the warm part of the day. Do not put your hands where you cannot see. Do not step where you cannot see.

**Feral dogs** move in loose packs, particularly around the rail yards and the edges of the settlement. They are wary of humans but not afraid. They will approach if they smell food. They are not rabid — the ecosystem has maintained a rough equilibrium — but they are unpredictable, and a pack of six thin, heat-hardened dogs is not a casual encounter.

**Raptors** — hawks and owls that nest in the cooling towers and upper floors — are not a threat to adult humans, but they are territorial during nesting season and will dive at perceived intruders near their sites. A blow from a raptor's talons at speed is startling and can draw blood.

**General rule:** The animals here are not tame. They are not habituated to humans in a friendly way. They are habituated to humans as part of the landscape — a neutral, potentially useful element. Respect their space, do not feed them (this is especially important for the dogs and the rodents, which have population cycles tightly linked to food availability), and do not corner any animal.

## IV. Navigation and Orientation

### The Disorientation Problem

The Salts will make you lost. The terrain is nearly flat with minimal visual landmarks. The haze eliminates long-distance visibility and depth cues — the far end of a street, a building three hundred meters away, will appear as a shimmering, indistinct shape that could be ten meters away or a kilometer. Shadows, which most people unconsciously use for orientation, barely exist at midday. The uniform mineral palette — whites, oranges, yellows, greys — means that what landmarks exist look alike.

**Navigation tools:** Do not rely on visual orienteering alone. Bring a GPS-equipped device (the old cellular infrastructure in the settlement provides limited connectivity, but do not depend on it). A compass. A detailed printed map of the basin and the city grid — if you can get one from the settlement traders, obtain one before you leave the settlement boundaries. Mark your route as you go. The mineral dust is uniform and your footprints will be erased within hours by wind and animal traffic.

### The Infrastructure

The buildings, streets, and rail lines of the old city are the most reliable navigation framework you have. Streets remain mostly grid-patterned, though centuries of subsidence have buckled and shifted them. What looks like a straight path on a map may jog, dead-end, or descend into a collapsed section. Buildings are structurally sound in most cases, but floors can be uneven, stairways can end in rubble or open shafts, and the loading bay doors that once provided easy access now hang at odd angles or are sealed by accumulated nesting material.

**Do not enter structures without a light source.** The interior of every building is dark, the windows are mostly broken or opaque with mineral residue, and the floor plan will not match your expectations. The cooling towers, the production halls, the rail tunnels — all of them have become complex three-dimensional habitats. The foxes know the layout. The raptors know the layout. You do not. Move slowly. Mark your path.

### Night Travel

Night in The Salts is not dark in the way you might expect. The haze retains and scatters ambient light — starlight is filtered into a diffuse grey, and the ground itself seems to emit a faint luminescence from certain mineral deposits. But it is dim enough that you will need artificial light, and at night the landscape changes character completely. The sounds intensify. The creaking of the metal structures becomes omnipresent. The animals become active — you will hear them in every direction, and in the dim light the movement in your peripheral vision will be constant.

If you must travel at night, use a red-filtered light to preserve night vision and reduce insect attraction. Travel the main streets where the rail lines and drainage ditches provide some orientation. Avoid the standing water areas at night — the amphibian activity near the pools is dense and the footing is worst there.

## V. Sustenance and Supplies

There is nothing to forage in The Salts. Nothing. No edible plants, no fruit, no wild grains. The entire photosynthetic basis of the ecosystem is microbial and algal — it is not accessible to a human digestive system in any meaningful way.

**Food:** Bring everything you need. If you establish contact with the settlement, you can trade for animal products — dried fish (the term is generous; they are small invertebrate-eating fish from the lower saline channels), roasted monitor lizard, waterfowl eggs. The food is functional and surprisingly nutritious, but the flavor profile is… austere. The mineral content of the water pervades everything. The fish tastes of sulfur. The eggs taste of the mud they were laid in. You will eat them anyway if you are hungry enough.

**The settlement traders** are practical people. They will trade processed mineral compounds, hides, feathers, and shell for things they cannot make or grow: medicines, clean water, tools, textiles, anything with electronic components, news from other multiverse-contact settlements. They are not unfriendly, but they are not welcoming in the way you might expect from a small community. They are watchful. They have learned that travelers bring opportunity and disruption in roughly equal measure. Be straightforward. Do not make promises you cannot keep. Do not photograph them without asking — they have had visitors before who did, and the images ended up in multiverse databases that led to curiosity they did not want.

## VI. Psychological Preparation

### The Aesthetics of The Salts

No one warns you about how *beautiful* The Salts is in the way that matters.

It is not conventionally beautiful. It is not lush, it is not green, it is not the kind of landscape that makes you catch your breath with its majesty. But there is a stark, mineral beauty to it that seeps into you if you let it. The way the ground glitters whereite crystals catch the haze-filtered light. The way the standing pools hold perfect reflections of the copper sky. The way the wading birds move through the shallow water with a slow, deliberate grace that is almost mechanical. The way the cooling towers exhale warm air into the dusk like the breath of sleeping animals.

And the silence — the real silence, not the absence of sound but the layered, textured quiet of a place where every living thing is whispering. The insects humming. The water seeping. A single bird call, harsh and distant. The frog chorus rising from the culverts in the evening, irregular and alive.

You may find it deeply unsettling. The flatness, the haze, the absence of green, the sheer biological density pressing in from every surface — it is an environment that does not cater to the human aesthetic sense. You may feel claustrophobic in open space. You may feel exposed in the absence of tree cover. The landscape does not change. Hour to hour, day to day, the basin looks roughly the same. Your sense of time may slip. You may feel watched by the animals, because you are — every surface is occupied, and you are the anomaly.

**This is normal.** The disorientation, the unease, and then — sometimes — the strange, hard peace of it. The recognition that this place does not need you and is complete without you, which is, in its own way, a kind of freedom.

### Duration Effects

Visits under a week are manageable. Most travelers report a sense of heightened alertness and slight anxiety that fades after the first two days as acclimatization sets in (physiological and psychological). Visits beyond a week become more complex. The heat and humidity degrade sleep quality. The mineral haze produces a low-grade headache that many people stop noticing but that affects concentration and mood. The constant insect presence and lack of green, growing things can produce a craving for forests, parks, any place with vertical vegetation — this is reported consistently and does not diminish with repeated visits.

If you stay longer than two weeks, you will start to notice the rhythm of the place. The morning fog, the midday dead zone when even the animals go quiet in the heat, the evening emergence. You will start to hear the individual layers of sound. The miners and traders who live here long-term describe it in terms that sound almost devotional — they talk about the basin the way people talk about the sea. As something vast and indifferent that you learn to exist inside of.

Do not dismiss this. The Salts gets into you. Not dangerously. But it stays with you after you leave, in the way that a very strange dream does.

## VII. Practical Checklist

- [ ] Reinforced waterproof wading boots (broken in, mineral-resistant sole)
- [ ] Walking staff or probing pole
- [ ] Full-spectrum UV sunscreen (SPF 50+, water-resistant)
- [ ] UV-blocking wraparound eyewear
- [ ] Wide-brimmed hat with neck drape
- [ ] Light-colored, loose, long-sleeved moisture-wicking clothing (full coverage)
- [ ] DEET-based insect repellent (30%+)
- [ ] Permethrin-treated outerwear
- [ ] Insect headnet
- [ ] Sleeping area with sealed insect netting
- [ ] Portable reverse-osmosis water filtration system rated for high-mineral content
- [ ] Supplementary water supply (minimum 4L/person/day, more in peak heat)
- [ ] GPS-capable navigation device + printed map + compass
- [ ] Red-filtered headlamp + backup light source
- [ ] First aid kit including treatment for insect bites, skin irritation, sunburn, and gastrointestinal distress
- [ ] Respiratory mask (lightweight, breathable)
- [ ] High-calorie, shelf-stable food supplies (nothing you would mourn losing to heat)
- [ ] Trade goods if planning settlement contact (medicines, tools, information)
- [ ] Whistle or signaling device (the silence eats sound; you want to be heard if something goes wrong)
- [ ] Mental preparation: patience, adaptability, tolerance for discomfort, willingness to find beauty in places that do not try to be beautiful

---

## VIII. Final Notes

The Salts is not hostile. It is not trying to kill you. It is not a death world or a cautionary tale. It is a place where, against all apparent odds, life has found an extraordinary and tenacious hold. The microbes in the crust. The invertebrates in the pools. The herons working the shallows, the foxes denning in the old offices, the frogs singing from concrete culverts that were never designed for anything but water and now hold both water and life.

Go respectfully. Go prepared. And if you stand still long enough, in the evening, when the haze turns copper and the frogs begin — you will hear the hum of the place, the low, ten-thousand-throated sound of a world that is not yours and does not need to be.

That is worth the trip.
