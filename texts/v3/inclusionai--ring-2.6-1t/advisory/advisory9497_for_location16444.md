# Preparing for The Worn Reach: A Multiverse Traveler's Guide

---

## Understanding Where You're Going

Before you pack a single item, you need to internalize what The Worn Reach *is*. It is not a mountain. It is not a desert. It is not a geothermal wonderland. It is the ghost of a mountain range — the stubborn residue of peaks that once blocked the horizon, ground down across almost incomprehensible stretches of time into a vast, mineral-crusted plateau at extreme altitude, thermally alive from below, and haunted by wind, water, and stone that make sound like nothing you've ever heard.

It is enormous, empty, and subtly wrong in ways that accumulate. It does not attack you. It *disorients* you. And disorientation at 4,500+ meters, surrounded by scalding mineral pools and unpredictable geysers, is not a philosophical inconvenience. It is a survival threat.

---

## I. Altitude: The First and Most Relentless Enemy

The description says the air pressure hits you before anything else. "Pressure in the ears" at this altitude suggests you're likely above 4,500 meters (roughly 14,800 feet), possibly higher given that the curvature of the Earth is visible from the surface — meaning the horizon is closer than it should be, a visual cue that comes from both altitude and the plateau's flatness extending to that curved line.

**Preparation:**

- **Acclimatization protocol.** Do not arrive and immediately traverse the flat. Spend at least 2–3 days at progressively higher elevations beforehand if your physiology requires it. For baseline human physiology, the standard mountaineering rule is "climb high, sleep low" — ascend no more than 300–500 meters per day once above 3,000 meters, with rest days every 3–4 days. The Worn Reach demands more conservatism. Plan for a full week of staged ascent.

- **Supplemental oxygen.** Bring a portable system. A small, lightweight concentrator with spare canisters. The air is warm, yes — but warm thin air is still thin. You will need it for sustained exertion on the open flat, especially if you're carrying gear. The plateau's distances are vast — three to ten hours of walking between landmarks — and altitude-induced cognitive impairment will make those perceptual distortions *worse*. Keep your oxygen saturation above 90% at all times.

- **Pharmacological support.** Acetazolamide (Diamox) as a preventive for acute mountain sickness. Dexamethasone as an emergency fallback. Know the symptoms of HACE (high-altitude cerebral edema) and HAPE (high-altitude pulmonary edema) — headache that doesn't respond to analgesics, ataxia (loss of coordination, stumbling), confusion, persistent cough with frothy sputum. On The Worn Reach, confusion is also what the *landscape itself* induces. You need to know the difference between altitude sickness and environmental perceptual distortion, because you will experience both, possibly simultaneously.

- **Pulse oximeter.** Wear one. Constantly. It is your objective anchor when everything else is subjective.

## II. Thermal Hazards: Heat from Below, Cold from Above

The paradox of The Worn Reach is that the ground is warm to hot while the air at altitude is thin and cold. You will encounter both in the space of a single step.

**The ground:**

The stone breathes heat upward through fractures. Thermal seeps are everywhere — water emerging through cracks, running in thin sheets across mineral crust. Geyser fields exist in concentrated zones, with eruptions ranging from every few minutes (sharp, violent, column height of 20–40 meters) to unpredictable slow-build events where the gurgling rises over hours before the blast.

- **Footwear:** Insulated, rigid-sole boots with thick composite soles. You are not walking barefoot — the description says no one does, and they don't because the ground pulses with heat that could scald. But you're also not just avoiding burns; you need to be able to walk 5–10 hours across mineral crust, scree, and mineral-encrusted basin floors. The crust may conceal voids, thermal pools, or thin crust over boiling seeps. Your boots need to distribute weight and resist heat penetration.

- **Gaiters and leg protection.** The mineral crust is sharp. Alkali flats, calcium deposits, crystalline mineral edges — these will shred exposed skin if you fall. Wear abrasion-resistant pants or gaiters.

- **Layering for temperature swings.** Near geothermal features, it may be genuinely hot — the kind of heat that fogs your goggles and wets your clothing with steam. Away from thermal zones, with wind and altitude, you will be cold. Pack a layering system: moisture-wicking base, insulating mid-layer (down or synthetic), and a hardshell outer layer that is both windproof and breathable.

## III. Chemical and Biological Hazards: The Water Will Kill You Slowly

The colored pools are beautiful and lethal.

They are colored by dissolved minerals — copper, iron, sulfur compounds — and thick colonies of extremophile algae and microbial mats. The water is copper-brown, iron-orange, vivid green. It is not water you can drink, bathe in, or touch with bare skin without consequence.

**Preparation:**

- **Full skin coverage** when in proximity to pools. Long sleeves, gloves, sealed boots. The mineral concentrations at these temperatures can cause chemical burns, contact dermatitis, and — over longer exposure — systemic toxicity. Copper and iron in solution at elevated temperatures are no joke.

- **Eye protection.** Goggles or sealed glasses. Splashing from thermal features, or even wind-driven mist from geyser eruptions, can carry mineral-laden water into your eyes. At altitude, where your eyes are already drying from low humidity, this compounds the risk of corneal damage.

- **Do not drink the water.** None of it. Not from pools, not from seeps, not from rainwater collected in mineral basins. Even rain that has just fallen on the plateau will dissolve surface salts quickly. Carry all of your water or a reliable purification system capable of removing dissolved minerals (standard filtration won't do it for heavy metals — you'd need reverse osmosis or distillation). A practical rule: **minimum 4 liters of water per day per person at this altitude, ideally 6 liters**, plus reserves.

- **Bioprotection.** The microbial mats are alive. They are extremophiles — organisms that thrive in high-temperature, high-mineral, low-oxygen environments. Some of them may be harmless. Some may produce toxins, irritants, or allergens unknown to baseline human immunology. Treat all biological material on The Worn Reach as hazardous until confirmed otherwise. Don't inhale steam from thermal pools (risk of inhaling microbial aerosols). Don't touch the mats. Don't let any open wound contact pool water, seep water, or even the mineral crust that is saturated with these organisms.

## IV. Navigation: The Emptiness Will Break You

This is the hazard the description warns about most urgently, and it is the one most travelers underestimate.

The flat stretches to every horizon. Landmarks are stumpy stone remnants that look small until you reach them and realize they're large enough to shelter inside. Distances are unreliable: a pool that looks reachable is an hour's walk away, and landmarks vanish behind the curvature of the Earth within minutes of walking. The perceptual distortions described — reflections lagging behind movement, sounds arriving before or after their source, the sense of *intent* in the wind-chords — are not folklore. They are environmental features of this place.

**Preparation:**

- **Do not rely on visual navigation.** It will betray you. The scale deception alone means you cannot estimate distances. The perceptual lag in water reflections, the shifting quality of the carved lines, the way sound plays tricks — these will erode your spatial reasoning over hours.

- **Electronic navigation.** Bring at least two independent GPS systems with preloaded waypoints for every point of interest (base camp, water sources, stone circle waypoints, emergency extraction zones). At extreme altitude, cold, and geothermal interference, electronics can fail. Carry backup batteries and a hand-crank charger. A satellite navigation receiver, not just a phone-based system.

- **The stone circle grid.** The stone circles form an intentional network — a grid placed on the highest points of the flat where they're visible from distance. Before you set foot on the plateau, study whatever survey data exists. Map the grid. These circles are your only reliable landmarks. Use them as triangulation points, and **mark your trail** with high-visibility cairns or GPS breadcrumbs at every decision point. The emptiness between landmarks is so vast, and so featureless, that retracing your steps without markers is nearly impossible.

- **Compass backup.** Magnetic compass, because the mineral deposits and volcanic geology may interfere with electronic systems. Know how to use it. The grid of stone circles is geometric — once you understand their spacing and orientation, you can use a compass to navigate between visible waypoints even in the flat-grey haze.

- **Buddy system or tracking protocol.** If traveling with others, never split up on the flat. The isolation is total. If traveling alone, use a satellite beacon with automatic check-in intervals and emergency SOS. Set the check-in window aggressively — every 60 minutes, not 240. If you miss a check-in, someone needs to know *immediately*, because on The Worn Reach, a missed check-in could mean you're lost in a mineral flat with failing cognition and a sprained ankle a ten-hour walk from anything.

## V. Sound: The Invisible Hazard

The wind plays the stone remnants like instruments. Deep drones from large stumps. Keening from narrow ridges. Rhythmic pulses from stones with tubular holes. Dozens of tones layered, harmonics and overtones shifting with gusts, some so low you feel them in your chest.

This is not background noise. This is a constant, immersive, physically felt acoustic environment.

**What this does to you:**

- **Infrasound.** The deep drones from the larger stone stumps are likely in the infrasonic range — below 20 Hz, below conscious hearing but physically perceptible. Infrasound is known to cause unease, anxiety, a sense of presence, visual disturbances (peripheral "shadow" movements), disorientation, and in sustained exposure, nausea, headache, and even visual hallucination. Organ pipes and large stone structures produce infrasound naturally. On The Worn Reach, every stone remnant is an organ pipe played by continuous wind. You are immersed in infrasound.

- **Perceptual layering.** The sound environment is complex enough that your brain will attempt to find patterns — and sometimes succeed. The description mentions moments when the wind-chord "resolves into something that sounds like structure, like *intent*, like a phrase from a language almost recognized." This is pattern recognition in a noisy environment, which is what human brains do. But it will be a profound, unsettling experience if you're not prepared for it. You will *almost understand* something and never quite get there, over and over, for hours.

**Preparation:**

- **Hearing protection** for the sustained high-frequency components (keening from narrow ridges). Foam earplugs with a flat attenuation profile — you want to reduce volume without eliminating situational awareness.

- **Infrasound awareness.** Know that what you're feeling in your chest is real, and it may affect your mood, cognition, and perception. If you start feeling inexplicable dread, anxiety, or visual disturbance — *it might be the sound*. Check your oxygen levels. Rest. Remove yourself from the heaviest acoustic zones if possible.

- **Companion communication.** If you have a partner or group, maintain verbal check-ins. The acoustic environment is disorienting enough alone; combined with altitude effects and perceptual distortion, it can pull you inward. Keep talking. Keep orienting each other to objective reality.

## VI. Weather: Sudden and Absolute

Rain comes without visible origin. This is a high-altitude plateau where orographic and convective weather may form rapidly, possibly driven by geothermal heating from below. The rain arrives in sudden, violent sheets, hammers the stone, and stops as abruptly as it starts.

**Preparation:**

- **Waterproof gear accessible at all times.** Not in your pack's bottom compartment. On your body or in an outer pocket. The transition from clear conditions to downpour may be minutes or seconds.

- **Flash flood awareness.** Those mineral basins fill fast. The rain sheets across the flat in thin, fast flows that find depressions and pool. If you're in a basin when rain starts, you may be in a flash flood of mineral-laden water. **Never camp in a basin. Never shelter in a depression.**

- **Hypothermia risk.** You are wet, at altitude, with wind. Basal body temperature can crash fast. Carry emergency bivouac gear: a lightweight bivy sack, chemical heat packs, and a fire-starting kit (though fuel will be limited on the mineral flat — plan for this).

- **Visibility.** During the rain, the flat becomes a white wall of water. You cannot see landmarks, the stone circles, or the ground under your feet. **Stop.** If rain comes, shelter in place on high ground, oriented to your GPS waypoints. Do not walk. You will lose the trail, lose your sense of direction, and lose your footing on wet mineral crust.

## VII. Fauna: Observe, Do Not Engage

The fauna of The Worn Reach is sparse, strange, and adapted to conditions that would kill baseline organisms.

- **Small six-legged creatures** near geyser margins: armored, translucent, pale, moving on too many joints, making needle-on-glass sounds. They feed on microbial mats. They are skittish and not aggressive as far as observed.

- **Something larger** moves through the stone remnant corridors. It clicks and scrapes. Tracks appear overnight and dry to nothing by morning. No one has seen it whole. Approach with extreme caution. It may be a large organism, a colony organism, or something else entirely.

- **Small dots crossing the flat at distance:** Elusive creatures moving too fast and too far away to identify. Gone when you reach the spot.

**Preparation:**

- **Do not approach or attempt to interact with any fauna.** Treat all organisms as potentially dangerous until confirmed otherwise. Their biochemistry, their defense mechanisms, their method of predation — all unknown.

- **Nocturnal activity.** If the thermals create condensation zones at night, that's where the biological activity concentrates, and potentially where the larger fauna are most active. Have a light source that doesn't rely on ambient conditions. Bright, directed, and preferably in wavelengths unusual enough to startle — a strong UV flashlight can be useful both for biological survey and as a deterrent.

- **Camp security.** Use an enclosed shelter. Not a tent — a hard-shell bivy or a lightweight rigid shelter. You want to be able to see out and keep things out. On The Worn Reach, the emptiness is the norm, but the emptiness exists *between* the zones of activity. Don't camp in the zones.

## VIII. The Things That Will Get Into Your Head

There are hazards on The Worn Reach that no amount of gear fully addresses.

**The perceptual lag.** Reflections in the mineral-stained pools show where you *were*, not where you *are*. This is disorienting on a primal level. Your proprioceptive system expects the reflection to track. When it doesn't, something deep in your brain registers wrongness — a micro-sensation of unreality that accumulates over time.

**The shifting carvings.** On the remnant stones, parallel lines cut deep into the hardest surfaces. In certain light, the pattern your brain assembles from them rearranges itself when you look away and back. This is not pareidolia — the lines are real, the carvings are real. But the *pattern* they resolve into changes based on viewing angle, light angle, and possibly the observer's state of mind. This will make you question your own perception, and at altitude, with infrasound in your chest and the rain-roar in your ears, you will question it hard.

**The almost-language in the wind.** There will be moments — brief, tantalizing moments — when the chord resolves into something meaningful. A phrase. A structure. Something you almost understand. This is a pattern-matching artifact in a hyper-complex acoustic environment, but it will *feel* like communication. Resist the urge to interpret it. Resist the urge to listen more closely. The Worn Reach has been playing this chord for millennia, for no one. It is not for you.

**The emptiness itself.** Three to ten hours of walking between any landmarks. A speck in a pan. The horizon curving away from you on all sides, reminding you constantly of height and exposure. The mineral flat is chemically rich, biologically active in its tiny zones — but the *volume* of nothing between those zones is almost incomprehensible. This emptiness will press on you. It is the emptiness at the far end of time, where erosion has finished its primary work and is just... continuing. The stone is still being worn. Things are still happening. They have been happening for so long they look like stillness.

You will feel small. You will feel irrelevant. This is factually correct and you should accept it, but know that it will affect you.

**Preparation:**

- **Mental preparation.** The most important gear you bring to The Worn Reach is the understanding that your senses will be compromised — not by the place directly, but by the combination of altitude, infrasound, perceptual anomalies, and isolation. Your brain will try to make sense of things. Sometimes it will make sense that isn't there. **Log everything.** Write observations in a physical notebook, not just digital. The act of writing forces articulation and slows the feedback loop between perception and interpretation. When the reflections in the pools lag and your brain screams *wrong*, write it down and note the time and your altitude reading. When the carvings seem to shift, note the sun angle. Ground yourself in data.

- **A mission with clear parameters.** Know why you're going. Set objectives, set turn-back times, set abort criteria. "If I haven't found what I'm looking for by 1600 hours, I turn back." "If my oxygen saturation drops below 88%, I stop and assess." "If I feel confused or disoriented for more than 10 minutes, I stop and contact base." The Worn Reach will make you want to linger — to listen one more time to the wind-chord, to watch one more time for the large creature's tracks, to look once more at the carvings and try to see the pattern. That desire is the place's real danger. It is beautiful and strange and your mind will want to follow its threads. **Set limits and obey them.**

- **An extraction plan that doesn't depend on you.** Satellite beacon. Check-in protocol. Someone who will come looking if you go silent. The Worn Reach's distances are vast, its terrain is featureless, and its perceptual tricks will make it harder to self-extract than any wilderness on any single-world location you've visited.

## X. Final Words

The Worn Reach is not hostile. It is not malevolent. It is not testing you or guarding its secrets. It is simply *being itself* — the landscape at the far end of time, where stone and wind and water and heat continue their patient work. The stone circles were placed by someone, long ago, and the carvings were cut by something with immense patience or immense capability, and they predate the erosion that has already worn the mountains to a flat. Those are mysteries. But mysteries do not defend themselves. The Worn Reach does not care whether you are here.

And that — that complete, absolute, magnificent indifference — is the most dangerous thing of all. Because it will make you forget why you should be careful, why you should set limits, why you should turn back when the chord in the wind almost makes sense and the reflections in the pools show a version of you that's half a second behind.

It will all seem like stillness. It is not stillness. You are moving through it, and it is wearing you, slowly, the way it wore the mountains.

Prepare accordingly. Come home.
