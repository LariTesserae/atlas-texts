# The Basin of Knocking Teeth

---

You hear it before you see it — before you even stand inside it. The sound comes up from below, or from everywhere, a low roar that isn't quite mechanical and isn't quite natural, a density of noise so thick it has texture. It presses against your ribs. By the time you've descended the first few meters into the basin's lip, the world behind you is gone: the sky, the horizon, the idea of distance.

---

**The land itself is young.**

That's the first thing you understand beneath the noise. The rock underfoot — where the rock shows — is sharp. Not jagged in the dramatic way of mountain stone, but *new*. It looks almost surprised to be there. Pale andite and darker basalts with edges that haven't yet been softened into roundness. The erosion here hasn't had time to be polite. Canyons cut into the basin walls are still V-shaped, still steep, their interior faces streaked with iron-red stains that weep slowly whenever the warm air thickens enough to squeeze out moisture. Condensation gathers on anything cool — the shaded side of a boulder, the underside of a leaf the size of a door — and beads into water that threads downhill in thin, temporary creeks. These creeks appear and vanish within meters. They are born from nothing, run briefly over stone that is still warm from some heat beneath, and are swallowed by root-mats or absorbed into soil so dark and wet it looks like it's breathing.

The basin is broad — maybe fifteen kilometers across, irregular, ringed by young scarps and ridges that still remember the tectonic event, the collapse, the eruption, whatever violence shaped this bowl. From the rim, it would look green-black, a stain of life too dense to parse into individual things. The air over it shimmers faintly, not with heat — the temperature is warm but not punishing, somewhere between skin and bathwater, sustained by the geothermal under-breath of the stone and the sheer metabolic output of everything growing in it.

---

**The vegetation is ferocious.**

It does not grow in the way you expect. It *advances*. On the outer slopes, where the stone is newest, the first colonizers are not small. They are already large — fern-like things three meters tall, their fronds overlapping into continuous walls of green-black, each frond shedding spores in visible clouds when you brush past. These pioneer thickets give way, within what might be only a few meters of descent, to something older. Something stranger.

Trees. But not like any trees you have a name for. Their trunks are pale, almost white, smooth and faintly warm to the touch, and they rise straight and columnar from the raw soil without branching for fifteen or twenty meters. At their crowns they erupt into enormous parasol-shaped canopies, layered one above another, interlocking with the canopies of neighboring trunks, forming a ceiling so dense that the light — what little there is — never reaches the ground in anything but the thinnest, most spectral threads. The canopy is not green. It is dark: deep olive, burgundy, black-green, the color of living things that have never needed to be seen. The air beneath the canopy is warm, close, saturated with moisture that hangs there like something you could wring from the atmosphere with your hands. Breathing feels like drinking.

Between the trunks, the understory is a wall. Not a metaphor — an actual, physical wall of growth. Shrubs with broad waxy leaves press against vines that press against root-exposures that press against the trunks of smaller trees growing in the crotches of larger ones. There is no ground to speak of beneath the first layer of soil and leaf litter. Everything is growing on everything. Mosses cover the mosses. Fungi — pale, fleshy, some faintly bioluminescent in colors that shift between blue-green and a dim, sickly amber — thread through the detritus in networks so extensive they seem structural, like mycelial architecture holding the whole dark mass together. The mushrooms themselves stand at waist height, some as wide as a human face, and they pulse. Slowly. Almost imperceptibly. A rhythm that isn't the wind because there is no wind down here.

---

**The darkness is not empty.**

It has mass. Inside the basin's canopy the light level is near zero — only scattered photons arrive from above, filtered through five or six layers of leaf, frond, and spore-curtain. What illumination exists comes from below. The fungal networks glow. Not brightly. It is a light you feel more than see — a faint blue-green phosphorescence that traces the roots, the fallen trunks, the wet stone faces of the youngest ravines. It makes the understory look like the inside of something alive. And scattered through the lower growths, along the stems of certain enormous flowering plants that push up through the mat in sudden eruptions, there are organs — pale, bladder-like structures that open at irregular intervals and release their own light: a dull amber, brief, startling. They smell of copper and overripe fruit. They last for minutes, then wither. Others open in their place.

The effect is of a landscape lit by its own internal processes — its own digestion, its own signals. Nothing here evolved to be seen. Everything here evolved to be *felt*, to be detected by chemical trace, by vibration, by heat.

---

**The fauna is not something you observe. It is something that happens to you.**

It begins immediately. Before your foot fully crosses the basin's lip, the sounds arrive in layers so dense they become a kind of weather.

Insects — if that word is even adequate — produce the first layer. A high, constant, papery whirring that seems to come from inside your own skull. They are small, iridescent, their wings too fast to see, and they move in clouds around the luminescent fungi, around the flowering stalks, around anything that produces heat or scent. Their collective sound is a hiss, a crackle, almost electrical.

Above that: the amphibians. The basin is laced with seeps and temporary pools that form in the erosional channels and drain just as quickly, and these pools are full of creatures — wide-bodied, slick-skinned animals colored in patterns that shift as you watch, as though their pigmentation is responding to the sound around them. They call. Not in the way frogs call. Their vocalizations are low, resonant, multi-tonal, and they produce them in concert, hundreds at a time, so that the air over a wet patch vibrates with a chord that seems to come from the earth itself. You feel these calls in your sternum. Behind them, underneath them, there is a deeper sound — a slow, guttural pulse that might be very large animals moving through the undergrowth, or might be the sound of gas release from the warm soil, or might be something else entirely. You cannot locate it. It does not stop.

Larger fauna move through at the margins. You see them — brief, fragmentary. A shape, four-limbed, low to the ground, moving between the pale trunks with a speed that seems impossible given the density of the vegetation. Something with too many eyes, briefly visible on a trunk before it sinks back into the mat. A creature the size of a small cart, armored, clicking, dragging itself through the root-tangle and leaving a trail of crushed luminescent fungi that glows briefly brighter where it has passed, as though injured. The fauna here is not afraid of the dark. It *owns* the dark. Every sound is answered instantly. Every movement is tracked by dozens of sensory organs you cannot see.

The density is the key. There are too many organisms per cubic meter for anything to move without touching something else. The understory is a continuous substrate of bodies, living and dead, feeding and being fed upon. A dead tree, fallen only hours ago based on the freshness of the break, is already colonized — insects inside, a fungal mat spreading across the bark from the soil side, something nesting in the hollow where a root ripped free. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is still. The biomass here is older than the landscape itself, as though it arrived ready-made and has been pouring itself into this young terrain like liquid filling a mold.

---

**The weirdness.**

It is subtle. You may not notice it at first, because everything else is so loud and so alive. But there are things.

The stone underfoot, on closer inspection, is not entirely stone. It is warm — not from geothermal heat, which would be uniform, but in patches, in patterns, as though something beneath it breathes. Press your hand flat against the rock face in one of the young ravines and you will feel, faintly, a rhythm. Not mechanical. Organic. And the stone itself, when you look closely — when your eyes adjust to the fungal glow and you kneel close enough to press your face against it — is not inert. It has a texture that is almost like petrified wood, but the grain moves. Very slowly. As though the minerals are rearranging themselves on a timescale you can almost, but not quite, perceive.

The water that condenses and flows through the basin does not behave entirely like water. It is clear, it is wet, it runs downhill — but it also, in narrow channels between root-masses, runs *upward* for short distances. A few centimeters. A few seconds. As though pulled by something above, or repelled by something below. The larger creeks don't do this. Only the thinnest rivulets, the ones that form between root fibers and stone, the ones you must be almost on top of to notice.

And there is the knocking.

Underneath everything — underneath the insect whir and the amphibian choruses and the clicking and rustling and the deep subsonic pulse — there is a rhythmic knocking. Low. Regular. Coming from everywhere and nowhere. It sounds like knuckles on wood. It sounds like a beak against bark. It sounds like stone tapping stone underground. It does not sync with anything visible. No creature you can see produces it. No vibration you can trace explains it. It is simply there, part of the soundscape, as fundamental as the humidity, as persistent as the glow.

The old animals — the ones with too many eyes, the ones that watch you from the trunks with an attention that feels *directed* — they seem to orient to it. When the knocking changes pace, the fauna changes. Subtly. The insects shift their flight paths. The amphibians adjust their pitch. Something large and unseen moves more quickly through the understory. As though the knocking is a command. As though the basin itself has a heartbeat, and every living thing inside it is keeping time.

---

**The rules of this place.**

Nothing is built here. Nothing has been made. The basin is raw, unshaped by intention — and yet it feels organized, hierarchical, *managed* by the sheer biological authority of everything growing in it. The landscape is still forming. The ridges are still sharp. The erosion channels are still cutting down. And the life is already so thick, so saturated, so aggressive in its occupation, that the stone is already losing. The plants are breaking the rock with roots, with acid, with the slow patient pressure of biomass that is never not growing. In a hundred years this basin will be smooth. In two hundred, the ridges will be rounded into hills. The young violence of the terrain is being consumed, swallowed, absorbed into a living mat so dense and so layered that it is becoming its own geology.

You are not welcome here. You are not unwelcome. You are irrelevant to the processes at work. The fauna is aware of you — omnivorously, anxiously aware — and the vegetation is entirely indifferent. You are warm, you are damp, you are dark, and you are loud. The basin does not care if you stay. It will not notice if you leave. It has been doing this — growing, collapsing, feeding, knocking — since before the stone finished cooling, and it will do it long after the stone forgets it was ever sharp.
