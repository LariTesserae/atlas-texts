You stand on a plain that has no name because it has always been here, and the people who live on it name nothing after themselves because the land is too large for ownership and too patient for memory.

The elevation is the first thing your body understands before your mind does. The air is warm but thinned, pulled taut across a world whose entire surface sits kilometers above any datum that has meaning here. There is no sea. There has never been a sea. The planet — if you can call it that, this great slow body of mineral and heat — has no basins low enough to hold liquid. What passes for a horizon is the gentle curve of a world that feels more like the interior of a bowl than a sphere, the edges of your vision meeting the sky at a distance so far that the atmospheric haze turns the line into something uncertain, a suggestion rather than a boundary.

The light is normal in the way a word can be normal while describing something that has no analogue. It comes from a sun that is warmer in tone than the one you remember — not red, not orange, but the color of bread, of resin, of late-afternoon warmth caught in glass. It falls evenly across the plain. There are no shadows sharp enough to cut. In the mornings the light arrives slowly, as though the world is being gently reminded that it exists, and in the evenings it withdraws with the same deliberation, tinting the mineral flats a faint copper that fades to ash and then to a blue-black that is less darkness than a change in the kind of seeing.

The ground beneath you is stone. Not rock in the jagged sense — not rubble or scree or the debris of catastrophe. This stone has been wind-polished for so long that it has achieved a kind of smoothness that feels almost biological, like skin. It is mineral in every way:ite and basalt and veins of metallic ore that have oxidized into colors you've seen in closed eyes but never attributed to a physical surface. Rust reds. Oxide blacks. The pale sulfurous yellow of mineral deposits that have been sweating out of the stone for centuries, crusting in thin crystalline films that shatter underfoot with a sound like breaking glass, except quieter. Much quieter. Sound here is a rare currency. The wind moves across the plain and you hear it not as a roar but as a long, slow exhalation, the air passing over stone surfaces so smooth that it generates a low tone — not quite a hum, more a pressure behind the eardrums, a frequency felt in the chest.

The landforms are old. They have been here long enough to have settled into themselves, to have stopped fighting the forces acting on them and begun to collaborate. Ridges run in long parallel lines across the plateau, formed by millennia of wind abrasion that has carved the softer stone away and left the harder veins standing like the ribs of something enormous and long-buried. Between the ridges, shallow basins collect dust — fineite powder so fine it behaves almost like a liquid, rippling in the breeze in patterns that look deliberate, agricultural, though nothing has planted or tended them. In the center of the largest basins, the dust is so deep that it swallows sound. You can stand at the edge and shout and hear nothing. The powder absorbs it completely, as though the ground is digesting your voice.

There is no water. Not a molecule of it in the open air, or so few that they exist as a concept rather than a presence. The erosion that has shaped this landscape is thermal and aeolian — heat and wind, the slow patient dialogue between a sun that warms stone by day and a sky that chills it by night, expanding and contracting, cracking faces a millimeter at a time over centuries. You see the evidence everywhere: boulders split cleanly down their centers, as though hatcheted by an invisible hand. Faces of cliff that have flaked away in thin sheets, exposing new stone beneath that is lighter in color, almost white, startling against the dominant palette of rust and charcoal. These freshly exposed surfaces will darken over decades, slowly re-oxidizing, and the cycle will repeat. The landscape breathes. Just slowly.

And yet it is not dead.

This is the thing that unsettles you first, before the scale or the elevation or the strangeness of the light. It is the fauna. They are everywhere, and the fact of them should not be possible. There are no plants here. No root systems, no photosynthesis, no chlorophyll, no green. Nothing that converts light into biomass in any way you recognize. And yet the animals thrive. They thrive with an abundance that seems almost casual, the way you might find insects in a wet forest — not rare, not precious, simply *present*, distributed across the landscape with a density that suggests they belong here more than the stone does.

The largest creatures are grazing animals of a kind that has no proper analogue. They move across the plateaus slowly, herds of six or eight visible from your vantage point, their bodies the size of industrial vehicles — compact, heavy, built low to the ground as though gravity here is slightly more personal than elsewhere. Their hides are mineral-stained, dark withite deposits that have accumulated over their lifetimes, forming a kind of exoskeletal plating that grows thicker as they age. They feed on the stone itself. You watch one lower its broad, flat head to the ground and begin to chew, and the sound it makes is a wet grinding, a slow rhythmic rasp of specialized dentition against rock. They secrete an acid from glands along their jawline that softens the mineral surface, then they scrape it away and swallow the paste. Their metabolism is so slow that a single feeding session lasts hours. They do not hurry. Nothing here hurries.

Smaller fauna move through the landscape in ways that are harder to track. You see them at the margins — at the edges of the thermal vents, where geothermal heat pushes up through the stone and creates warm zones that attract congregations of creatures. Clawing things with too many limbs, moving with a skittering gait across the warm rock surfaces. Filter-feeders that anchor themselves in the wind channels between the ridges, their membranous bodies catching mineral dust from the air and extracting what they need. Burrowers. Climbers. You catch a glimpse of something with a translucent body, its internal structures faintly visible, moving across a rock face in the way that ice moves down a slope — slowly, continuously, reshaping itself to the surface.

Some of them have learned the industrial zones.

You can see the towns from your elevation — not far away, maybe five kilometers, which in this thin air and flat terrain puts them well within sight. They are clusters of heavy construction, built from the same mineral palette as the landscape itself, as though the settlements grew out of the ground rather than being placed on it. Metal and stone, fused together in ways that suggest the builders ran out of time or materials or the distinction between the two. The structures are layered — new construction built on and around and into older construction, the way sediment accumulates. You can read the history of a town in its vertical cross-section: the oldest foundations are the densest, the most carefully fitted, and the newer additions grow outward and upward with less precision, more urgency, more scaffolding.

The towns are industrial. You hear them before you fully see them: a low mechanical hum, the sound of engines and generators and the heavy rhythmic pounding of some process you can't identify from this distance. Smoke — or something like smoke, a pale mineral vapor — rises from clusters of stacks at irregular intervals. The factories here run on heat differentials. The ground is warm at depth, and the machinery taps into that warmth, converting thermal energy into mechanical work through systems of pistons and turbines that have an aesthetic somewhere between clockwork and geology. The sound of it carries far across the plain, arriving at your position as a murmur, a background texture.

The settlements are sparse in the way the parameter suggests — isolated presences separated by vast stretches of empty mineral plain. You can see the rail lines connecting them: heavy-gauge track laid directly on the stone, stretching to vanishing points in every direction, the rails themselves warm from the sun and faintly humming with electromagnetic current. Cargo cars sit on sidings outside the towns, loaded with material you can't identify from this distance — dark lumps, maybeite, maybe refined metal, catching the bread-colored light.

And then the weirdness settles in, as it does, not as an intrusion but as a recognition.

There is a quality to the air at this elevation that makes distances unreliable. You look at a ridge that should be a kilometer away and your eyes tell you it is close enough to touch, then moments later it has receded. The atmosphere here bends light in subtle ways — thermal gradients between the sun-warmed stone and the cooler upper air create a lensing effect that compresses and expands the visible world. You've heard that the early surveyors mapped this land and produced charts that showed distances fifty percent larger than they actually traveled, because the optical compression made everything seem farther away. They learned. They adjusted. But even now, with calibrated instruments, the plain resists being fully measured. It looks one size and is another.

The fauna do strange things. At what passes for dusk here — the copper fading to that blue-black — the larger grazers stop moving and stand perfectly still, facing the same direction, their bodies cooling in the night air. During this period their metabolic processes shift, and they emit a sound: a deep, subsonic pulse that you feel in your ribs rather than hear with your ears. It happens in unison. Every animal in the herd, every animal on the plain, the same pulse at the same interval. The ground vibrates faintly. The mineral dust on the surface rearranges itself into rippled patterns that are too regular to be random, too complex to be mechanical. No one has explained this. The people in the towns have theories. None of them agree.

There are formations in the deepest basins — structures ofite that rise from the dust in columns and spirals, some of them meters tall. They grow. Slowly. Over decades. The mineral deposits accrete from the dust itself, bonding together in patterns that look crystalline, purposeful, almost architectural. Geologists have studied them and found no consistent internal structure, no repeating lattice, just a kind of organized complexity that refuses to resolve into a process. The townspeople leave offerings at the base of the tallest ones. They don't pray there. They just stop, briefly, the way you might pause at the edge of something you don't understand but have decided is not hostile.

The quiet is itself a presence. Not silence — there is too much life, too much industry, too much wind for silence. But the sounds here are sparse and distinct. Each one arrives individually, uncluttered by a wash of ambient noise. A bird — if that is what it is, a small skimming creature with metallic feathers that catches the light and throws it — makes a short bright call, and you hear it with a clarity that borders on intimacy. A railcar shifts on its track half a kilometer away, and the sound reaches you as a clean metallic note. The industrial hum is constant but low, and in its constancy it fades from perception, becoming less a sound than a pressure, a way the air holds tension. In the gaps between these sounds, the world is so quiet that you begin to hear yourself — your breathing, your heartbeat, the faint tinnitus of your own nervous system. The quiet here does not empty you. It fills you with yourself.

The warmth is dry. It sits on your skin without clinging. Sweat evaporates almost instantly, leaving behind a faint mineral residue — the dust gets into everything, a thin film ofite on your arms and face, catching the light in a way that makes you, briefly, part of the landscape's palette. The air tastes of dust and heat and something faintly metallic, like licking a battery. It is not unpleasant.

Night comes on slowly, the blue-black deepening from the east like a stain in stone. The temperature drops, and the rocks begin their ticking — the thermal contraction, the faces pulling inward, the cracks closing. A sound like a field of clocks. The fauna shift and settle. The towns glow with reflected heat-light, warm amber windows in the cooling dark. The formations in the basins stand motionless in the starlight, and if you watch them long enough you might convince yourself they have rotated, or grown, or leaned slightly toward something you cannot see.

You do not see the stars well here. The atmosphere at this elevation is thin, but the mineral dust scatters the light into a faint haze that softens everything. The stars are there — more of them than you've ever seen, and closer, or so it seems — but they shimmer and shift in the thermal currents, and they look less like points and more like organisms, drifting slowly in a dark mineral sea.

This place has been like this for a very long time. The stone remembers nothing because stone does not remember, but the shape of the land carries the memory of every wind that has passed over it, every thermal cycle, every slow geological breath. The settlements are recent — decades, maybe a century — and already they are beginning to look as though they grew from the stone rather than being built upon it, the mineral dust accumulating at their foundations, the fauna nesting in their warm corners, the rail lines developing a patina that will, in another century, make them look as old as the ridges they cross.

Nothing ends here. Nothing begins. Things continue.
