# The Drowned Uplands

---

The water is everywhere, and has been for a long time.

Not the violent water of floods or storms — though there is weather here, and it makes itself known — but the water of *settlement*, the water that has arrived and decided this is where it lives now. It fills the high valley to perhaps thirty feet of depth, this dark cold body, and the valley walls rise beyond it on three sides, substantial slopes of stone that have been standing long enough to have stopped caring about their own existence. The rock is the color of iron-filing and old bruise, and where it meets the waterline it wears a band of different darkness, stained by years of contact, the tide-mark of something permanent.

The light — what light there is — comes from no obvious source. It is the light of deep overcast, of sky that has agreed to be a ceiling rather than an openness, grey transitioning to grey, the mountains above the waterline fading into cloud somewhere near their tops. You cannot see the sun. You are not sure there is one. The hour might be late afternoon or early morning or the middle of a day that simply chose not to brighten. The light reaches down through the water and becomes something else: not illumination but a diffuse and bluish *ambient*, a faint glow distributed through the column of liquid as if the water itself has a mild and reluctant luminescence. Enough to see by. Barely. The shapes of things below the surface resolve only gradually, and some of them do not resolve at all.

Underwater, there is vegetation.

This is where the lushness has gone — down into the cold. The floor of the drowned valley is not bare stone but a dense garden of acquatic growth, and *dense* is the wrong word because nothing here is exactly crowded, but the plants are *large*, and they occupy the space with the confidence of organisms that have been here for many generations. Long trailing fronds the color of dark jade hang suspended in the water column, barely moving. Broad flat leaves the size of sleeping pallets lie in slow rotation just above the lakebed, their surfaces furred with something finer still — a fur of filaments, each one individually invisible, collectively creating a softness, a texture you feel before you understand you're feeling it. Kelp-forms, but not kelp, reaching from anchors in the rock upward toward whatever light they can find, their stalks translucent near the base and darkening as they rise, as if the plants are processing light into color as they grow. 

The vegetation moves. This is important. There is no obvious current — the water is not flowing anywhere in particular — but the plants move anyway, a slow deliberate movement that follows no single direction and keeps no single rhythm. One frond drifts left while another, three feet away, drifts right. The broad leaves rotate and then rotate back. This is not entirely explained by water dynamics. The movement is the strangeness, or the first strangeness, or the strangeness that other strangenesses depend on.

Above the waterline, along the slopes, a different flora. Mosses, first — vast territories of moss, deep green tending toward the blue-green of deep-cold plants, coating every surface of available rock in a continuous soft layer. The moss is heavy with retained water, saturated, glistening, and where you step on it — if you step on it — it releases a sound like quiet exhale and holds the print of your foot for longer than seems right before recovering. Interspersed with the moss are low shrubs of an unfamiliar profile: branches not bifurcating in normal woody patterns but growing in long single arcs that curve upward and then over, like frozen waves, like something interrupted mid-gesture. They have leaves, small and dark, spade-shaped, growing in clusters at intervals. They do not look like they should be here. They look like they came from a lower and warmer place and have been slowly adapting for several hundred years, and have not yet finished.

The structures.

There are three of them visible from the waterline, at intervals along the southern slope. They are built of the same stone as the valley, which makes them easy to miss — which might have been intentional. Dry-stacked, roofless, their walls standing to a height of perhaps four feet and no more, as if they were never meant to be full enclosures but something else, something that required walls but not ceilings, that required a defined interior without needing to be a shelter exactly. The oldest of them has lost a section of wall to some past event — not collapse, exactly, the stones are still present and have not scattered far, it is more that they have slowly separated from each other, a loosening, a relaxation. Inside the intact ones, the floor is the same moss as everywhere, unbroken. No human intervention has occurred inside these walls for long enough that the moss has forgotten there were walls.

In the water near the tallest of these structures — the structure closest to the water's edge, its base perhaps six feet from the waterline — there is a wooden stake driven into the lakebed in the shallows. Simple worked wood, rough-hewn, the end driven to a point by cutting rather than by any formed tool, the cuts visible as long diagonal faces. The wood has been in the water long enough to have darkened and softened but not long enough to have fallen apart, which means the person who drove it there was perhaps two or three generations ago, perhaps less, perhaps more. A cord is attached to the stake at water level — plant-fiber, braided in a simple three-strand technique — and the cord extends downward into the darkness, descending at a slight angle as if there is a very slow current pulling it, and disappears. What it is attached to cannot be seen from the surface. It disappears slowly rather than quickly, which suggests it extends a significant distance.

The sound.

The loudness is surprising, given the absence of visible causes. Some of it is water: the valley receives rain periodically, or not rain exactly — a precipitation that is heavier than mist and lighter than rain, a sustained downward drift of water-mass that is atmospheric enough to blur the distinction between being in water and being under sky. When this falls on the lake surface it creates a sound of continuous hiss, a white sound, a sound that fills the valley wall to wall and makes distances uncertain. When it falls on the moss-covered slopes it makes almost no sound, absorbed immediately. The shrubs catch it on their curved branches and drip it, and the dripping is audible, a complex non-rhythm, variable and overlapping.

But there is another sound, lower, that does not change with the rain or cease when the rain pauses. It comes from the water. From below the water. A sustained bass note, not quite constant — it oscillates slowly, taking perhaps forty seconds to move from its lower frequency to its higher and back again — and it is loud enough to be felt as much as heard, a vibration in the chest cavity and in the stone underfoot and in the roots of the teeth. It could be geological. It could be tectonic stress expressed acoustically through the water column, which amplifies and transmits sound differently than air, which is well understood. That is one explanation. The other explanation involves the plants moving when no current moves them, and the cord extending into darkness, and the walls that required no ceilings.

There are no animals. No birds, though there are places for birds — ledges on the valley walls, cavities in the rock, the arching branches of the wave-shrubs at heights appropriate for nesting. No insects, though the vegetation is present and healthy and there is no visible reason insects could not thrive here. No fish in the water, though the water is cold and oxygenated and full of vegetation that would support them. No tracks in the moss softness, no scat, no secondary evidence of anything that moves of its own volition. The absence is total and has the quality of an absence that is *maintained* rather than accidental — as if the place has come to some arrangement with animal life that does not include animal life.

The cold is real. It settles in the joints and in the extremities, and it moves through wet clothing faster than through dry, and everything here is wet — the air is wet, the stone is wet, the moss is a delivery system for wet, your breath comes out visible in the grey light. The water of the lake is colder than the air above it, and the air above it is cold. At depth the water would be very cold indeed, cold enough that the plants' adaptations to it represent a significant biological achievement, cold enough that the movement of those plants in the absence of current is even more notable than it would otherwise be, because metabolic processes slow in cold and the energy required to move without current is metabolic energy.

The valley has been this way for a long time. The erosion is finished — or rather, the erosion is in balance, in the state where the forces that wear things away and the forces that rebuild them have reached an agreement about how the landscape should look. The waterline sits where it sits and does not appear to be rising or falling. The slopes are stable, the moss continuous, the rock faces above the moss-line smoothed by long exposure to the heavy air. Boulders that fell from those faces at some earlier and less stable time now sit on the slopes, colonized entirely by moss, indistinguishable in color from the slope itself except in shape. The place has a quality of having arrived at itself.

In the water, slowly, the broad leaves rotate.

The low tone continues.

The cord extends downward, taut, into the dark water, and whatever it is attached to does not move — or rather, whatever it is attached to does not move *faster* than the very slow current pulling the cord at its slight angle. It is possible that it moves at that rate exactly, that the tension in the cord is the tension of something resisting, matching, holding itself in one place by moving at precisely the speed that would produce that angle.

You cannot stay here long without feeling that the place is engaged in something. Not against you, not toward you — the place does not register you in any obvious way, and the absence of fauna means there is nothing to be startled, nothing to change its behavior in response to your presence. But *engaged*, the way a room in which a long and quiet conversation was held is still, hours later, in some relationship with what was said in it. The valley is attending to something, and has been attending to it for long enough that the attention has become structural, has become landscape, has become the particular way the moss grows thicker near the waterline than above it, has become the particular spacing of the three roofless structures along the southern slope, has become the low oscillating tone moving through the water and the stone and the air.

The precipitation drifts down. The light does not improve. In the shallows, a frond turns against no current, deliberate as a slow hand.
