# The High Green Dark

---

## Arriving

You become aware of it before you can see it. The sound arrives first — a layered, tectonic roaring that isn't wind and isn't water and isn't any single living thing, but is all of them at once, pressing against the eardrums with the pleasant insistence of a crowd heard through a wall. Then the cold. Then the smell: green so concentrated it has become something else, a ferrous tang, almost mineral, the smell of photosynthesis operating at industrial scale in darkness and thin air.

Then your eyes adjust, and you understand where you are.

---

## The Terrain

This is a plateau, but the word fails almost immediately. Plateau implies horizontality, implies something finished. This surface has not finished becoming itself. The rock underneath — granite or something harder, something younger than granite, stone that has not yet learned patience — pushed itself upward recently enough that its edges are still thinking about sharpness. Ridgelines cut the sky with the precision of freshly struck flint. The faces of exposed stone, where you can find them, which is rarely, bear no smoothness. They are all angle and intention, a geometry of emergence.

But the stone is almost entirely irrelevant. It is covered.

---

## The Vegetation

*Covered* is insufficient. *Occupied* is closer. *Replaced* is closer still.

The plants do not grow on this landscape. They *are* this landscape. The distinction between substrate and living thing dissolved here long ago — or perhaps never existed. What looks from a distance like terrain is, on approach, a surface of continuous, interlocking botanical life so densely organized that the concept of soil becomes theoretical. Something roots in something, which roots in something, which roots in something, and at no point do you reach bare ground. Perhaps bare ground is down there, many meters below, a geological rumor.

The primary stratum is moss, but calling it moss is like calling the ocean *water*. It moves in sweeps and continents across every available surface, including the vertical ones, including the overhanging ones, including the undersides of ledges where cold air pools. The moss here is not the bright chartreuse of lower-altitude mosses. It runs to deep colors: olive, forest, a green so saturated with darkness it becomes almost black at depth. In places it grows to knee height in unbroken drifts, and pressing your hand into it is pressing your hand into something with the consistency of a mattress, of accumulated time.

Above the moss: ferns. But ferns at altitude evolve strange priorities, and these have evolved strangeness beyond that. They do not reach toward light in the usual way. They are organized more like antennae — vast fronds splaying horizontally to catch whatever diffuse luminance percolates down through the permanent cloud cover, arranged in loose tiers that step upward in the darkness. The largest specimens are tall enough to disappear into the mist above your head. Walking among them is like moving through a hall of slowly rotating satellite dishes, each one tracking something invisible.

Above the ferns — and this takes some time to resolve — the canopy, if it can be called that. The high plateau is not treeless; the absence of visible trunks below does not mean an absence of trees. What has happened is that the trees — ancient, dwarfed by wind and cold, shaped by decades of storm into the horizontal — have grown into each other so completely that they form something like a low ceiling, a vaulted lattice, a nave without walls, crusted on every surface with yet more moss, more fern, more lichen. From below, looking up, it resembles the underside of the sea: all hanging growth, dim motion, organic architecture. The trees themselves are invisible within it. The structure persists without visibly being held up by anything.

And through all of this: moisture. Not rain, precisely, though rain comes. The plateau sits in cloud for long portions of each day, and cloud at this altitude is not cloud as seen from below. It is fog with ambitions. It moves through the fern-hall, deposits itself on every frond, runs down into the moss in a slow and constant trickle, hangs in droplets on the underside of every leaf. The water table here is plant-body. Moisture is stored in living tissue, transported through living tissue, released by living tissue. When the plateau breathes out in the early morning — and it does breathe out, you can watch it — the exhalation is visible.

## The Emptiness

Here is the contradiction that takes longest to understand: for all this abundance, for all this mass and interlocking complexity of living things, the space is *empty.*

The fern-hall is high. The understory is dark and vast. You can stand in one place and see, through the pillared dimness, for what feels like hundreds of meters in any direction — which at altitude means you are seeing across distances that would take hours to walk. There is almost nothing in that space. No shrubs filling the gaps. No dense undergrowth clogging the mid-level. The moss forms the floor; the fern-lattice forms the ceiling; and between them is a cold, dark, enormously empty volume of air.

Sound travels through it like sound through a cathedral.

This is why the noise is so strange. In a dense forest, sound is absorbed, eaten, muffled at short range. Here, with so much empty space between the living surfaces, every sound propagates. The drip of condensation off a fern twenty meters away arrives with startling clarity. The impact of an animal's feet on moss — which you will hear long before you see what makes it — carries as a low, organic percussion that bounces between the fern-arches and returns changed.

---

## The Weather

The plateau generates its own weather, which is a terrifying sentence to understand from inside it.

The scale here is continental — this is not a plateau in the sense of something you could cross in a day, or a week, or perhaps a season. It has its own pressure systems. It has its own winds, which have learned to move among the ferns and through the canopy-lattice and have developed preferences, habits, recurring patterns. When a weather event arrives on the plateau, it arrives the way weather arrives on a continent: with buildup, with drama, with an extended aftermath.

The cloud comes in from the west in waves you can watch from any exposed ridge — thick, grey, cold, moving fast at the plateau's edge and then slowing as it descends into the fern-hall, becoming fog, becoming drip, becoming part of the plant-water cycle, becoming eventually cloud again as the vegetation exhales it. This cycle takes roughly a day, though *roughly* is doing heavy work in that sentence.

When the cloud is between waves, there is a period of near-clarity: not sunlight, because the sun is low and the angle is steep and the air is cold and everything conspires against warmth, but a diffuse grey luminance that filters down through the canopy-lattice and turns the moss a dozen shades of grey-green, turns the hanging droplets into brief, sourceless sparkle, turns the fern-fronds silver along their edges. This is the closest the plateau comes to beautiful in the conventional sense. It lasts approximately forty minutes. Then the next wave comes in.

When a full storm arrives — and storms arrive here with the confidence of something that knows it is on home territory — the sound in the fern-hall becomes extraordinary. The canopy-lattice above flexes, and the sound of that flexing is the sound of something immense shifting its weight. The condensation stops being a trickle and starts being a rain falling from the interior of the canopy, which is not the sky but which performs the function of the sky. The moss, already saturated, begins to move: water runs across its surface, finds channels, follows the young and still-sharp geometry of the rock beneath, carves small temporary rivers.

The animals disappear during storms. They know things.

## The Fauna

The animals of this place have solved a specific problem: how to be abundant in emptiness.

The solution is vertical distribution and temporal staggering. Different species own different hours. The spaces between presences are vast — you can walk for an hour through the fern-hall and encounter almost nothing — but what you encounter, when you encounter it, is emphatic.

The grazers of the moss-floor are large and slow and built for cold. Something like elk but wrong: lower-slung, denser, with a coat that looks like moss and functions similarly, absorbing moisture, retaining it, becoming in certain lights nearly invisible against the plateau floor. They move in small groups, distantly spaced, and the sound of their movement — the sustained soft compression of moss underfoot, a sound like something very large breathing — carries for long distances in the fern-hall's acoustic space. You hear a herd before you see it, and what you hear sounds like the plateau itself adjusting its posture.

The canopy-lattice has its own residents: things that have abandoned the ground entirely and live in the interlocked branches above, traveling through the horizontal forest by feel and by memory, using calls that echo off the fern-fronds below and return information about distance and obstacle. These calls are part of the plateau's baseline sound, which is why the baseline sound is so complex. What seems at first like undifferentiated noise resolves, with patience, into layers: the low percussion of grazer-movement, the mid-range echo-calls of canopy creatures, the sharp tap-and-drip percussion of condensation at various scales, the structural groan of the canopy-lattice under wind, and underneath all of it, sometimes almost inaudible, sometimes briefly dominant: a sound with no obvious source, low and slow, that might be the rock itself cooling, the young granite still contracting as it rises, still settling into whatever it is becoming.

Predators exist. You know this because the grazers know it, and the grazers' awareness of this is a kind of information that radiates outward. The moss-floor herds have a quality of organized alertness that is its own form of sound — not any specific noise but an *absence of inattention*, a group-animal quality of readiness that you can feel at close range the same way you feel a room where everyone is listening for something.

## The Structures

Occasionally — every few hours of walking, or every few kilometers of ridgeline — you find something that was made.

The structures are simple and they are old, though not as old as the rock and not as old as the moss-system. Someone came here. Someone built. The evidence is modest: a low ring of the sharp local stone arranged with a deliberateness that cannot be natural, now filled to the brim with moss, now with a fern growing from its center. A post, wooden, driven into a gap in the rock, now entirely sheathed in lichen, its original surface unknown, its original purpose speculative. In one sheltered place, the curved remains of a wall, knee-high, long-collapsed into a coherent rubble, suggesting a shelter, suggesting someone who needed sheltering, which is the saddest and most practical form of archaeology.

The tool-marks on the stone, where you can find them, are hand-tool marks: something struck against stone, something pressed into soft ground. No metal. No cutting precision. What was made here was made with patience and simple instruments by people who understood that patience and simple instruments were sufficient.

The most recent structure is impossible to date. A kind of frame built from the horizontal canopy-wood — branches cut or more likely pulled free — lashed together with something fibrous and now mostly decayed, leaning against a rock face that gives it a fourth wall for free. The frame is not collapsed. The person who built it may have been here recently. The moss has already begun its work on the lashings.

Nobody is here now. The plateau seems entirely certain of this.

## The Rules of This World

Here are things that are true here and that took time to understand:

**Time moves differently at altitude and at scale.** This is not metaphor. The plateau's weather systems operate on timescales that make human presence feel incidental. A pressure system that takes three days to cross the plateau has not noticed the people who sheltered beneath a canopy-lattice arch while it passed. The moss-system has been accumulating for long enough that the distinction between this moss and the bedrock beneath it is a distinction the moss does not recognize. To walk here is to walk through something with a memory much longer than yours, which is communicated not by any mystical means but by simple physical evidence at every step.

**The darkness is not the absence of light; it is a condition.** The plateau is high enough that the sun's angle is steep and the atmosphere thin and still the light does not fully arrive. The cloud cover intercepts what might have arrived. The canopy-lattice intercepts what gets through the cloud. The moss floor receives a dim, scattered, sourceless luminance that is sufficient for an ecology that has adapted to it over very long time. Human vision at the moss-floor level is workable during the brief clear periods. During cloud-cover, and especially at the edges of day, the fern-hall is a space of shapes and gradients rather than details. You see *that* something is there before you see *what* it is. The animals operate on this principle also, and have learned its implications.

**Sound is the primary information channel.** This follows from the darkness. Every creature of any complexity that lives here has elaborate acoustic strategies. The calls of the canopy animals are information technology. The silence of a predator is itself a form of communication to those evolved to notice it. The weather is audible before it is visible. The grazers locate each other by the sound of their own movement in the fern-hall's acoustic space. If you are here and you are not listening, you are nearly blind. If you are here and you are listening, you can know most of what is happening within a considerable radius without seeing any of it.

**The vegetation is not passive.** This should be obvious and is not. The moss-system is actively managing the water cycle, the soil chemistry, the temperature of the surfaces it covers, the acoustic properties of the floor it forms. The fern-canopy is tracking available light with a precision that looks like intention from below. The canopy-lattice trees are engaged in resource negotiation with each other via their root systems, which in this thin-soil environment are extremely shallow and extremely extensive and deeply entangled. The plateau's vegetation is not a static backdrop. It is the operating system. Everything else — the weather cycles, the animal distribution, the structure of the sound environment, the retention of moisture in a cold and intermittently arid highland — is running on top of it.

---

## Being Here

What you feel here — if you stay long enough and stop trying to resolve the initial sensory overload into something manageable — is the specific experience of being at the periphery of a system's attention. The plateau is occupied with itself. Its processes are large and self-referential and have been running for long enough to have achieved a kind of closed completeness. You can walk through it for days without disturbing it in any way that will persist. The moss will recover the impression of your footstep in hours. The animals will resume their routes once you have passed. The weather will continue regardless.

There is a quality of indifference here that is not hostile. The plateau does not wish you ill. It does not wish you anything. It is busy. Its business is complex and ancient and conducted entirely without reference to your presence or absence.

The people who built the stone rings and the wooden frame understood this, and built anyway: small, sensible, temporary structures that asked nothing of the plateau but a windbreak and a dry hour. They knew the terms.

The cold, at night, with the cloud sitting at the fern-level and the condensation dripping from every surface and the distant percussion of something large moving through the moss-floor in the dark, and the canopy-lattice shifting overhead with the wind's new preference — the cold is total and the sound is constant and the darkness is thorough and the green smell is everywhere.

You are in it. That is all. For now, that is sufficient.
