# The Plateau of Settled Ash

---

Begin with the altitude. You are high — genuinely, pressingly high — but you would not know it from the horizon, because the horizon here is not a distant thing. It is close. The plateau extends outward in every direction to a radius of perhaps thirty kilometers before the edge arrives, and because the edge is not a cliff so much as an *ending*, the sky simply takes over where the ground stops. You are on the roof of something. You are on the roof of a world that has forgotten it has a roof.

The ground underfoot is not rock in any active sense. It was rock, once, in some epoch so removed from the present that the word "once" fails to carry the necessary distance. What remains is the memory of rock — a substance like compressed powder, like the finest mineral flour pressed under geological patience into something that holds its shape not through hardness but through sheer exhaustion of all remaining change. Walk on it and it does not crunch. It does not give. It accepts your footfall with a faint, toneless compression, a sound like pressing a thumb into very old felt, and then it is done with you. The color is the color of a fire seen through frosted glass — ochre bleeding into a reddish-brown that in certain angles of the dim light reads almost as purple, almost as nothing at all.

This surface has been here long enough that it has reached its final form. Erosion is not a present-tense verb here; it is a past-tense achievement. The wind worked this place for an age that preceded the current configuration of the stars, and it is finished. What remains is what would remain if you removed every force that moves material from one place to another and waited. There are no sharp edges anywhere on this plateau. Not one. Every angle has been rounded to a suggestion of itself. Boulders that were once boulders are now lobes, nodes, gentle protrusions from the surface that look less like geology and more like the slowly breathing forms of something sleeping face-down in the mineral dust. They have been this shape for longer than multicellular life existed elsewhere. They will be this shape long after.

---

The city is here.

Or rather: a city is here. It was placed here, or grew here, by processes that are not immediately legible. It does not announce itself. You do not see it from a great distance as a skyline or a glow, because the light is wrong — or rather, the light is almost nothing. There is light, but it is the lowest possible version of light that still permits sight: a dim, sourceless, brownish-orange luminescence that seems to emanate from the dust itself, or from very far below the dust, or from the underside of the flat sky, which is not a sky in the conventional sense but a ceiling of particulate haze at an altitude perhaps three hundred meters above the plateau's surface, through which nothing — no star, no moon, no sun — penetrates. The light is thermal in character, as if the warmth of the place — and it is warm, genuinely warm, the still air carrying a dry heat that settles on the skin without moving — as if the warmth itself were slightly luminous.

In this light, the city resolves slowly as you move toward it.

It is large. Not vast, not overwhelming, but large in the way that a town built over many generations is large — not because anyone planned its scale but because scale accumulated. The buildings are made of the same material as the plateau, because of course they are: this place provides only one material, and that material is itself. Structures rise from the ground in rounded towers, in wide-shouldered halls, in long low buildings that run parallel to one another at intervals that suggest streets but are not quite streets — they are more like the spaces that remain when buildings decide where they want to be. Nothing is sharp. The corners of buildings have the same rounded-down quality as the boulders in the open plateau, which either means the buildings are very old or means that whoever built them understood that this place does not permit sharp corners to persist, and built accordingly. Probably both.

The buildings are three, four, five stories. Some are taller — one structure near what might be the center of the settlement rises to perhaps twelve stories, a rounded monolith, dark-windowed, its surface cross-hatched with shallow channels that once might have been decorative but now read as simply the pattern of ten thousand years of thermal cycling expanding and contracting the same stone, over and over, until the surface cracked in its preferred directions and then stayed cracked, because everything here has reached its preferred state.

There are pipes. This is important. The industrial infrastructure is not hidden — it runs across the surface of buildings, between buildings, underground and then above-ground again through grated vents that exhale nothing visible but carry, faintly, the sense of pressure moving through them. The pipes are metal, or were metal, or are some substance that replaced metal when metal became unavailable or inconvenient. Their color is close to black, close to the darkest version of the ochre everything else is made of. They have not rusted in the conventional sense because there is essentially no water here — the air carries less than two percent humidity, a number low enough that exposed skin feels it within minutes, a slow tightening, a pulling of moisture back from the surface of things. What the pipes have done instead is oxidize in the dry heat into a stable ceramic crust, the outermost layer of the metal converting to a mineral sleeve that is now more mineral than metal, holding the shape of the original pipe, preserving its function.

Machinery operates here. But it operates quietly. This is the strangest thing about the industrial character of the place — there is evidence everywhere of engines, of mechanical systems, of infrastructure scaled beyond the individual — and yet the sound is almost nothing. The machinery has been running for so long that it has worn itself to its minimum-friction state. Bearings that once clattered have worn smooth. Vibrations that once transmitted through structures have damped out through ten thousand iterations of the same cycle. What remains is a very low hum, felt more in the soles of the feet and the jaw than heard through the ears, and occasional low exhalations from the pipe-vents, and the sound of metal settling — not contracting with cold, which does not exist here, but a slow, patient acoustic signature of metal that has been at operating temperature for so long that "settling" has come to mean something different. It means: the sound a system makes when it is already settled and is simply continuing to be settled.

---

What is the machinery doing?

This is not entirely clear.

The pipes lead to processing facilities — buildings whose purpose can be inferred from their scale and their lack of windows and their multiple entry-points for large pipe-runs — but the product of these facilities does not make itself known. There is no output visible. No stacks of finished goods, no waste product accumulating. Either the output is the plateau itself — the machinery maintaining some condition of the ground or the air or the depth below the surface — or the output has long since been stockpiled somewhere that is not here, or the machinery is producing something that takes longer to produce than the observation of it can resolve. The city feels less like a place where things are made and more like a place where something is *sustained*. A process is being sustained. What process is hard to say.

The streets — the spaces between buildings — are empty of traffic. Mostly. There is the rare figure, moving slowly, at a distance that makes them difficult to resolve in the brownish dim. They are not numerous enough to constitute a crowd or even a group. They move without particular urgency, without the quality of people going somewhere important. They move the way a mechanism moves when it is doing what it was designed to do and no more. There are perhaps six such figures visible across the entire settlement, from any given vantage. Perhaps fewer. They do not appear to notice one another. They do not appear to notice you.

The fauna of the plateau is, similarly, almost not there. Occasionally — not reliably, not predictably — something moves at the edge of the dim light. Not an animal in the familiar sense, or not obviously. It is the right scale for an animal, roughly, the scale of something between a large rat and a medium dog, but its movement is wrong: it moves without hesitation, without the exploratory quality of a creature navigating a space, as if the plateau presents no obstacles to it, as if it knows exactly where it is going. It crosses the visible space and disappears into the darker spaces between pipes and foundations. There is no sound associated with it. There is no evidence it was there once it has gone.

It does not repeat often enough to establish a pattern.

---

Sit in the city's approximate center — near the base of the twelve-story monolith, on the powder-soft ground, in the warm dark — and feel what is true about this place.

It is stable. This is its primary quality and it is not a small quality: stability, genuine stability, the stability of systems that have had all their instabilities removed by time rather than by design. The warm air does not move. The light does not change — there is no flicker, no suggestion that a source is responsible for it, no cycle that might produce a brighter moment or a dimmer one. The machinery hums at exactly the frequency it has hummed for as long as the machinery has existed. The dust settles, but it was already settled, so the settling is more of a conceptual settling, a settling-of-principle.

You could stay here for a long time before anything changed.

You could stay here indefinitely.

The warmth helps. It is the warmth of a room that has been heated for a very long time at a very stable temperature — it is not the warmth of sunlight, not the warmth of combustion felt nearby, but the warmth of an environment that decided on a temperature and then stopped deciding. It is comfortable in a way that makes comfort feel like the wrong word, because comfort implies that something has been adjusted to suit a preference. Nothing here has been adjusted for a very long time. The warmth simply is.

The dryness is the only challenge to the body. The air takes moisture from the eyes, from the lips, from the exposed forearms, at a rate that is noticeable but not aggressive. It is patient about it. The city must have some provision for this — water, however scarce the planet's supply, must be somewhere in the system — but the provision is not visible. Perhaps the pipes carry it. Perhaps the machinery below produces it as a byproduct of whatever sustained process it is engaged in. Perhaps the residents, such as they are, have adapted to the aridity over the long history of the settlement and need less than would be expected.

---

Look up.

The haze ceiling is uniform. It has always been uniform. There is no event in the sky to reference, no signal that time is passing overhead. The warmth from below meets the haze from above and neither wins.

Somewhere at the edge of the plateau, thirty kilometers in any direction, the ground stops and the air continues and below the edge — much further below, in an abyss that would be impressive if there were light enough to see it — is whatever the plateau is the roof of. Mountains. A different world. A long-cooled volcanic event. It does not matter from here. From here, the edge is simply where the plateau is done.

The plateau is not done with anything, exactly.

The plateau is just done.

And it has been done for so long that done has become its natural state, its operating condition, the mode in which it functions and hums and processes and waits without waiting, because waiting implies the expectation of change, and expectation of change implies the kind of hopefulness the plateau abandoned in an era before the concept of hopefulness could be said to have ever existed here.

It is warm. It is dark. It is very, very quiet. The buildings stand.
