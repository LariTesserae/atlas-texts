# The Singing Cliffs of the Becoming

---

Begin with the sound, because the sound is where you meet this place first — before your eyes adjust to the dim, before the cold settles into your collar and the backs of your hands. The sound arrives before everything.

It is not wind, though wind is part of it. It is not stone grinding against stone, though that is also part of it. It is the sound of a mountain *deciding to be a mountain*, and that sound is enormous. A sustained chord played by no instrument ever built — somewhere between the resonance of a struck bronze bowl the size of a cathedral and the low, pressurized moan of a continental shelf flexing in the dark. The frequency lives in your teeth and behind your eyes. You do not hear it so much as you are surrounded by it on all sides, inside and out, the sound moving through your skeleton like something remembering it always had permission to.

Stand with that for a moment. Let your ears accept it. They won't stop ringing for days after you leave, if you leave.

---

Now: look up.

The peaks are wrong. Not wrong in a threatening way — wrong in the way that an unfinished sentence is wrong, not malicious but genuinely incomplete. They are high — stupendously, vertigo-inducingly high, the kind of elevation where the sky overhead begins to deepen in color even at midday, where you can see the curvature of weather systems from above rather than living inside them. These cliffs and spires reach into a sky that is not quite the right shade. The stone faces are not weathered. There are no talus slopes, no alluvial fans, no marks of water running down because there has never been water running down. There has not been *time* for water to run down, and besides there is no water.

The rock faces are fresh-fractured and luminous in an unsettling way — like the inside of a geode perpetually exposed, never oxidized, never colonized. The minerals show. Veins of selenite catch what little light exists and hold it for too long before releasing it again. Feldspar planes reflect at the wrong angle. In places the stone is translucent and you can see depth inside it — not a trick of the light, actual depth, other layers of material becoming legible inside the rock as if the mountain is still sorting itself out, still deciding where to put things.

There are peaks that did not exist yesterday. This is not a figure of speech.

---

Watch the cliff face carefully for ten minutes. Fifteen. Be patient.

There: a crack, opening with geological slowness, maybe three millimeters in ten minutes — but opening *visibly*, which stone does not do. The crack spreads and from it comes a brief release of pressure, a breath of mineral dust, pale as chalk, pale as ash, which the near-windless air holds suspended far longer than it should before it drifts, and as it drifts it makes a shape — not a recognizable shape, not a face or a hand, but a shape with *intention*, something that moves like it knows where it is going.

Elsewhere, twenty meters up a black-veined face, a ledge is extruding itself from the cliff. The ledge was not there three hours ago. It is not pushing dramatically outward — it is *leaning* out, the way a sleeper slowly rolls in their sleep, and the stone where it extends is brighter than the surrounding rock, uncut by cold, uncrazed by temperature variance, because it has existed in cold for only a few hours and has not yet learned what cold does to it.

The plateau above — and there is a plateau, vast and open, extending for dozens of kilometers in the thin air — is a surface of this same raw material. The ground is not soil. Soil takes time and biology and water and death. The ground is exposed lithic surface, a plane of mineral that is also still arriving, still compacting, still exchanging pressures with the bedrock below. To walk on it feels different from walking on ordinary stone. Ordinary stone is inert. This stone is occupied with something.

---

But then: the plants.

Here is where the place refuses to be simple.

The vegetation should not be here. By any ordinary logic — no water, extreme altitude, dim light, temperatures that settle around five or six degrees even in the mild hours — there should be nothing. Or lichen, at most. Flat gray things hugging surfaces, taking centuries to cover a hand's width.

What is here instead is genuinely lush, which does not mean tropical or warm or wet. Lush here means *dense with presence*, means alive in a way that refuses understatement. It is sparse in distribution — scattered across the plateau and ledges in isolated colonies, much open space between each gathering — but each colony, where it occurs, is an extravagance.

They have solved the water problem with a different chemistry.

The dominant forms are broad and low, built like cushions but with rigidity — not soft to the touch but structured, their cellular architecture internal and tight. They are a color that reads as green but is actually a deep blue-green, saturated, almost metallic, the color of a peacock feather seen in low light. They do not photosynthesize in any ordinary sense. They take mineral vapor from the microcracking stone and process it. Where the ledges breathe their chalk-pale dust, these plants sit at the edge of the release and absorb it. They are eating the mountain's becoming.

Some of the larger colonies have developed vertical structure — not trees, nothing with wood in it, but upright forms, almost architectural, branching at intervals in symmetries that feel too regular, too aware. The branches end in structures that might be described as leaves but function more like receivers: broad and cup-shaped, their inner surfaces covered in a coating that collects static charge from the dry mountain air and transduces it into something metabolically useful. When two of these structures are near each other and the charge is right, a spark passes between them — small, blue-white, silent beneath the overwhelming sound of the forming stone — and the plants respond to this, their tissues brightening briefly, a change in turgidity moving through the colony like a slow wave.

The plants are communicating. It would be condescending to call it communication. It is something more fundamental than communication. They are, in the parlance of a later and more sophisticated science, *processing*.

The roots — if they are roots — go directly into the fresh stone and participate in its formation. This has been theorized but never observed anywhere else: a symbiosis between living tissue and unset geology, the plants guiding the crystallization of certain minerals, the rock forming around the root system and through the root system in ways that benefit both. The plant gains anchor and access; the stone gains something. What the stone gains has not been determined. The stone gains something and the something is not nothing.

---

The light.

The light here is dim but not dark. Dark would be easier to accept. Instead the light is dim in the way of deep overcast — you can see, you can see everything, there are no shadows because nothing is bright enough to cast them. The sky overhead is a pale gray-white at the horizon and deepens going up toward zenith, where it is something between slate and pewter, a sky that seems farther away than skies usually seem, as though the atmosphere here is thinner not only physically but perceptually, as though the usual bargain the sky makes — that it will stay close, will curve down and meet the ground at a comfortable distance — has been quietly renegotiated.

The light comes from everywhere and from nowhere. In the old colonial settlement — such as it is, such as it was — they kept fires burning through the day, not for warmth but for something to look at with specificity, some local source so that the eye had a point of reference other than the general gray.

---

The settlement.

Far across the plateau, at a distance that takes three hours to cross on foot over the uneven raw stone, there are structures. Four of them. Five if you count what remains of the fifth.

They were built by people who were solving problems with the tools immediately available to them, which means the structures are stone on stone, dry-stacked, the individual pieces selected from what the mountain offered up and arranged by hand into walls. The walls are thick — knee-width at least — because the people who built them had learned, or intuited, that thin walls here do something. Thin walls resonate. The sound moves through thin walls and does things to the people inside them. Thick walls, heavy walls, still conduct the deep chord of the forming mountain, but they muffle its extremes. You can sleep inside a thick-walled structure. You cannot sleep inside a thin-walled one. It was learned empirically.

The structures are low, partly subterranean — cut into the plateau surface — with roofs made of flat stone slabs laid across the walls. The technology is early but not unintelligent; the construction shows an understanding of load paths, of how weight distributes, of where a lintel needs support. There are no nails because there is no metal here. There is no metal here because the ore that exists in this stone has not finished becoming ore. It is still in process. You cannot smelt a mineral that is still deciding what it is.

Inside the structures — you can look through the doorways, which face not east or west but toward some middle angle that later analysis will reveal corresponds to the lowest harmonic resonance of the plateau — are objects. Tools of bone and antler, though there is no fauna here now, which means the people who made these tools brought them from somewhere else or came from somewhere else or the fauna has been gone for some time. Scrapers. Wedges. A long-handled implement whose purpose is unclear but which shows significant wear at the handle end, suggesting it was gripped repeatedly and turned, suggesting rotation, suggesting the people here were turning something against something else.

There are also, on the interior walls, marks.

The marks were not made with pigment. They were made with the point of a hard tool pressed into the raw stone, and they go *into* the stone in a way that has not healed or closed or been covered. The marks remain fresh. The stone has tried, in the years since they were made, to slowly fill the incisions — to compress and re-form across them — but the process is too slow to have made progress, and so the marks remain as they were left, a catalog of lines that repeat, that rotate, that achieve something through iteration that a single mark does not achieve.

Some researchers have called these maps. Others have called them hearing aids. Others have called them something in a language that has no translation.

---

Here is the rule of the place, which becomes apparent only after long exposure:

*The mountain is learning, and the plants are teaching it.*

This is not metaphor. This is not projection. The geological process that is forming this landscape — the uplift, the crystallization, the slow extrusion of new stone from the interior of the planet — is interacting with the biological process colonizing its surface in such a way that the two processes have begun to inform each other at a rate too slow for any individual human observer to perceive directly, but too fast for any geological model to explain without incorporating the biological factor.

The plants grow along the lines of minimum stress in the forming stone. The stone, forming, begins — over centuries — to follow the geometry of the plants. The relationship is a loop. Neither came first. Neither drives the other. The loop drives itself.

This is what the sound is. The sound is the loop, expressing itself acoustically because there is nowhere else for it to go. Because the mountain is still young and has not yet grown a geography thick enough to absorb it.

The people who built the stone structures understood this — imperfectly, mythologically, in the way that pre-theoretical intelligence understands deep patterns: as stories, as sacred geometries, as the correct angle to face a door. They were not wrong. They lacked the framework to be precisely right but they were not wrong.

They have been gone long enough that the stone is beginning to slowly fill the marks on their walls. Not long enough for it to finish.

---

Stand at the edge of the plateau as the dim light fails toward something even dimmer — not night exactly, the sky never goes fully dark here, it just deepens further, the pewter becoming gunmetal, the gray becoming something without a good name — and listen.

The sound does not stop at night. The sound does not stop. The sound is the one constant here, the one fully stable feature, and this is ironic because the sound is the sound of instability, of formation, of the not-yet-complete. But within that continuous instability there is a kind of permanence. The chord changes — over decades, over centuries, as new features emerge and old ones find their final forms — but the *fact* of the chord does not change.

Somewhere across the plateau, in the dim, one of the plant colonies flickers. A spark between two receiver-leaves. A slow wave through the tissue of the colony, a brightening. Some piece of information passed, some calculation completed.

And thirty meters to the east, a new crack opens in the stone.

Three millimeters wide. Emitting its pale mineral breath.

The plants at the crack's rim are already leaning toward it.
