# The Hanging Town

---

The first thing you notice is how quiet it is for a place so full of movement.

Something is always shifting here — a leaf turning, a wing adjusting, a tendril reorienting toward a source of moisture you cannot locate — and yet the aggregate of all this motion produces almost no sound. The rustling has been swallowed. The chirping has been swallowed. What reaches you instead is a kind of pressure against the eardrums, the acoustic equivalent of dim light: not silence exactly, but sound at its lowest working concentration. Occasionally a drop of condensation falls from one broad leaf to another several feet below, and this small percussion travels farther than it should, as though the air here holds sounds reluctantly and releases them only one at a time.

---

You are high up, though the summit is not visible and the bottom is not visible either.

This is not a peak. It is a shelf — a ledge of geology that thrust outward from the cliff face sometime geologically recently, which is to say it is young by stone standards, which is to say its edges are still sharp enough to catch cloth and score skin. The rock here hasn't had time to round. It broke away from the parent wall in slabs, and those slabs tilted and settled against each other like a stack of enormous books dropped from a great height, and over these tilted planes the settlement was built, incrementally, without plan.

The stone itself is pale grey with a greenish cast, the green coming partly from the mineral composition and partly from the fine moss that has colonized every surface not in use. The moss arrived quickly — moss always does on young, damp stone — and it has made the rock look older than it is. This is the great deception of the place. It looks ancient. It is not.

---

The settlement fits into roughly the area of a large cathedral or a small village square, which is to say it is not large, which is to say every part of it is legible from almost every other part, which is to say it is the kind of place where you can know where everyone is by inference rather than observation.

The structures are built into the angles the stone makes. Where two slabs lean against each other, someone has sealed the gap with wattle and packed it with clay, hung a door-skin across the opening, and called it a house. Where a broad flat slab overhangs another, the overhang becomes a roof and the walls grow to meet it. Nothing here was constructed independent of the stone's own architecture; the builders read the rock's intentions and completed them. The result is a settlement that does not sit on the landscape so much as continue it, the way a sentence continues a thought.

The materials are what could be carried up or grown here: the wattle, thin-split timber from the stunted trees that root somehow in the cliff's crevices below this level, clay harvested from a seam exposed in the break, twine made from the inner bark of something that grows in dense tangles at the ledge's outer margin. The tools that made these things are simple tools. Wedges. Pressure-flaked blades. Weighted hammers of stone hafted with wrapped sinew. Nothing here required heat to make, except the fired clay of the water vessels, which are the settlement's one luxury, fat-bellied and stoppered with carved wooden plugs, stacked in a cool corner where condensation keeps the air just damp enough to prevent cracking.

---

The settlement is sparse within its smallness.

This sounds contradictory but it isn't. There are perhaps twenty structures. Many are empty — whether temporarily or permanently is unclear, and no one seems inclined to clarify. The occupied ones are distinguishable by small signs: a bundle of vegetation hung to dry from a line strung between two points, a vessel left outside with a cloth over its mouth, a deliberate arrangement of stones that might be functional or might be meaningful or might be both. The unoccupied ones stand open-doored and patient. Their interiors are dark. Things live in them.

This is what the sparse density means here: not that things are absent, but that the living things are not the kind that fill space the way large animals fill space. The inhabitants are small, and there are very few of them, and they leave between them large volumes of air and stone. It is the fauna that is overwhelming. It is just not the fauna you would have expected.

---

The vegetation is the thing that makes no immediate sense.

At this elevation, in this cold (the air sits usually between ten and fifteen degrees, dropping at night toward the edge of frost without quite reaching it), with moisture that is intermittent — the morning condensation that sheets the stone and drips from every leaf-tip until midmorning, the occasional rain that comes in sideways from the open air off the cliff-face, the seep from a crack in the rock face at the back of the ledge that keeps one corner perpetually damp — with all of this, you would expect something scrubby, something adapted to difficulty.

What you find instead is extravagance.

The vegetation here grows with the eagerness of something that has discovered a secret. Broad-leafed climbers have covered the back wall almost entirely, their leaves overlapping like scales, each leaf large enough to hold in two hands. They are very dark green, nearly black in the dim light that reaches the ledge — the light here is the diffuse, filtered light of deep overcast, or of a sky partially blocked by the overhang of the cliff above, and the plants have responded by broadening every surface available to catch it. Beneath the climbers, mosses have built up into structures: not flat carpets but standing forms, pillowed and domed, some of them knee-high, the texture of something between fur and water.

The young stone offers the plants another unexpected advantage: fresh rock is mineral-rich, and the seep water dissolves those minerals and carries them across the ledge in thin films invisible to the eye, and the roots and rhizomes and mycelia reach for them constantly. You can see the results of this reaching in how the plants have oriented themselves — not always toward light, sometimes toward the stone itself, tendrils working into fresh cracks with a patience that will eventually deepen them.

At the outer margin of the ledge, where it drops away, there is a belt of something that is not quite tree and not quite shrub: dense, branching, the stems pale and slightly translucent at their tips, the leaves narrow and slightly waxy. They grip the edge with exposed roots that wrap the rock lip like fingers. This belt is the reason the ledge feels enclosed, feels like a room: it is the wall that completes the fourth side, and behind it is the open fall of air and the smudged suggestions of terrain far below, barely visible through cloud.

---

Now the fauna, which is the most important thing, which is the strange thing.

The insects arrived first, which is usually how it goes. But the insects here are not background. The insects here are the ecology.

They are small individually, most of them, but there are so many that they constitute a layer of the environment the way soil constitutes a layer, the way atmosphere constitutes a layer. They move through the vegetation in waves of microscopic purpose. They work the moss, the stone, the soil-in-formation between the rock slabs. They maintain channels and structures and relationships too small and complex to follow. Watching any one of them tells you almost nothing. Watching the aggregate tells you something, but not enough.

Some are not small. There are beetles here the size of a child's fist, their carapaces the color of wet slate, who move slowly across vertical stone faces at night when the condensation is heaviest. Their movement is methodical and produces no sound. They eat something — the moss, perhaps, or something that lives in the moss — and leave behind them a slightly disturbed surface that re-closes within a day. They are the largest thing on the ledge that is not a person.

There are also the jumpers: a category, not a species, because they appear to be several different animals that have converged on the same locomotion. They are small, mottled to match the pale stone, and they move in sudden relocation — still, still, still, then elsewhere, with no visible intervening motion. They hunt. What they hunt is also unclear; the food web here is baroque and recursive in ways that suggest it has been elaborating itself for longer than the stone has existed, which is impossible, which suggests these animals came from somewhere else and brought their complexity with them.

The birds are the strangest part.

There are no birds. This is the strange part. At this elevation, on a ledge, with this much biological activity — there should be birds. Instead there is an absence shaped like birds, a series of ecological roles left empty, and something has filled them that is not birds. It has wings. It lands on the vegetation and on the edges of vessels and on the peak of the outermost structure. It moves through the air in paths that are slightly wrong for birds — too angular, too considered, with pauses in positions that flight should not permit. In the dim light it is almost impossible to see clearly. The people of the settlement do not look at it directly.

---

The people of the settlement are not many, but they are active.

They move through the space with the efficient economy of people who know exactly where everything is and have known for a long time, which is interesting because the settlement is young and the people are not old. They carry things. They arrange things. They tend the water vessels, checking the stoppers, tilting them to listen for the sloshing confirmation that they remain full. They harvest from the vegetation — certain leaves, stems, the output of whatever the mossy structures are doing — and process the harvest immediately, at work surfaces built into the angles of the stone, using the simple blades, using their hands.

They are quiet in the way the place is quiet. Not silent, but producing sounds that stay local, sounds that don't travel far. This might be cultural — a learned or enforced practice of not projecting sound into the open air — or it might be something the place has done to them, some acoustic accommodation to an environment that swallows sound. The distinction may not matter. Both produce the same behavior.

They watch the fauna with an attention that borders on professional. They notice the jumpers. They notice the beetles' paths. They do not watch the winged things directly, but they orient themselves, slightly, in awareness of where those things are, the way you orient yourself in a room where someone you are afraid of is standing.

---

There are several active processes running simultaneously at all times, and part of what makes the place difficult to hold in mind is that none of them are synchronized.

The condensation cycle runs on the temperature differential between night and day. The seep at the back wall runs on whatever pressure the water table exerts from within the cliff, which varies, which means the corner goes from damp to slightly wet to damp again on a schedule no one has predicted successfully. The plants grow — perceptibly, if you watch a tip closely for long enough, which the inhabitants do, which is part of their work. The insects conduct their incomprehensible commerce. The beetles make their nightly circuits. The things that are not birds appear and disappear according to rules that are either very complex or very simple and look identical from the outside.

The rock itself is active. This is the young-stone fact: the ledge is still settling. Not dramatically, not seismically, but at the level of slow creak and minute shift, the slabs continuing the conversation they began when they broke. The inhabitants have learned to read this — a certain resistance in a doorframe that wasn't there yesterday, a new hairline in a sealed wall, the tilt of a vessel that sat level last week. They respond with small repairs, with small adjustments, with the patient re-leveling of what has shifted. They are, among other things, maintenance workers for a building that is also a geological event.

---

The light at midday is still dim, filtered through the overcast and through the vegetative ceiling that the climbers and the outer belt together create. At the back of the ledge, in the perpetually damp corner, the light barely reaches. Things grow there that are adapted to almost no light — translucent, soft, their shapes unfamiliar, their edges hard to determine. The inhabitants rarely go to that corner. When they do, they go briefly and return to the relative brightness of the rest of the ledge with something like relief.

At night the light goes entirely. The moss glows, very faintly: this is not a dramatic bioluminescence but a genuine dimness, blue-grey, the color of thinking about a color rather than seeing it. It is just enough to prevent complete disorientation. It is not enough to see by. The beetles move. The winged things move more, or perhaps become simply more present, more detectable by some sense other than sight. The inhabitants sleep inside their stone-slab structures, the door-skins closed.

The cold at night is real. The elevation makes it real. Inside the structures, body heat and packed moss for insulation bring the temperature to something tolerable, and in the morning the inhabitants emerge into air that has dropped several degrees overnight and carries the sharp scent of damp stone and the green-mineral smell of the moss, which is different from the daytime smell — cleaner, less alive-smelling, more elemental. The condensation has sheeted everything. The leaves drip. The stone drips. The water vessels, left outside, have filmed with condensation that the inhabitants collect with a cloth and ring into a bowl. Nothing is wasted. There is not enough water to waste any.

---

The overall impression of the place, taken as a whole, is of something that is doing a great deal of careful work to remain exactly what it is.

The rock works to settle into stability. The plants work to colonize every available surface. The fauna works at its vast, intricate, incomprehensible maintenance. The inhabitants work at the tasks that living at elevation on a young ledge in intermittent moisture requires. The winged things work at whatever they work at, which may be watching, which may be something that watching is only a part of.

Nothing here is finished. The ledge is not the shape it will eventually be. The settlement is not the settlement it will eventually be. The ecology has not yet reached whatever equilibrium it is moving toward. All of it is in the middle of becoming something, and the dim light and the quiet and the cool and the smallness of the space create the impression that this becoming is very focused here, that you have found the site of a becoming and come to stand inside it.

This is what the quiet is for, possibly. To not interrupt.
