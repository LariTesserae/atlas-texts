# The Noise Garden

---

Begin with the silence that isn't silence. You notice it wrong first — you hear it before you see anything, hear it before you understand what hearing means here, before your body has decided what this place requires of it.

The sound is total. Not loud in the way that damages: loud in the way that *occupies*. Every frequency owned. Every gap between frequencies negotiated and claimed. It arrives from all directions at once including from inside the sternum, from inside the back teeth, from the small bones of the inner ear vibrating at their own preferred resonances, separately, simultaneously. You do not go deaf. You go *full*.

---

The place is not large.

A basin, roughly — though *basin* suggests a depression, a hollow made by subtraction, and this feels more like a room that grew, a chamber that the mountain decided to have. Perhaps forty meters across at the widest reading, perhaps sixty if you measure the extensions that finger outward under overhangs. The walls are not walls exactly. They are the mountain becoming itself: schist in long diagonal planes, grey-green with age, faces polished and re-polished by millennia of precisely nothing — no water, no ice, no rain. Whatever smoothed this stone did so without wetness. The polish is dry. Intimate. The surfaces have the quality of things that have been touched ten thousand times by the same careful hand, the hand of elevation and thin air and time so settled that it has stopped caring about its own duration.

Above, a narrow aperture of sky. The light comes through it cleanly: normal light, honest light, mid-afternoon somewhere in a season that leans toward cool without committing to cold. The shadows inside the basin are long and specific. They know where they belong.

This is a high place. You felt the ascent in your thighs before you arrived.

---

Now: the moss.

This is where *lush* reconciles with *no water*, and the reconciliation is the strangest thing about the place before the stranger things become apparent.

The moss is everywhere. Not growing — existing, being, *insisting*. It covers the schist faces in thick continuous pelts, deep as a thumb is long, in colors that range from the grey-green of jade to something approaching amber at the edges of certain shadows. It climbs. It cascades. It finds every horizontal surface and makes it irrelevant by refusing to let horizontal be a constraint on where moss goes. There are cushions of it bulging from vertical faces like held breath. There are curtains of it depending from the overhangs, moving slightly — moving, yes, though no wind is currently blowing, though no wind seems able to enter this enclosed basin.

The moss is moving on its own.

Not all of it. Not all at once. But continuously: somewhere in the visible field, some portion of the moss is doing something — extending a frontier by a millimeter, or rapidly (rapidly for moss, which is still slow by other standards, but faster than moss should be able to go) retracting from a section of stone and re-forming slightly to the left. The aggregate movement over the course of a minute is small. The aggregate movement over the course of an hour would be measurable. Something in the biology of this moss is wrong, is accelerated, is running on a different clock, and the result is that the walls of the basin are slowly, constantly, turbulently *shuffling*. Breathing, almost. If breathing were a democratic process undertaken by ten million individual units with differing opinions about direction.

Between the moss: ferns. The ferns have no business being here, at this elevation, in this dryness, but they are here and they are extravagant. Not tall — low, dense, the fronds spreading horizontal and broad to catch the shaft of light from the aperture above. Some fronds are wound in tight coils still. Some coils are unwinding as you watch, visibly, measurably, at a speed that is simply too fast. A fern frond that should take a day to unfurl is unfurling over the course of twenty minutes. The interior of the coil is pale and secret-looking, chartreuse, freshly made. Several fronds have already unfurled, spread, and are now — curling back. Starting over. Or not starting over: continuing a process that has no start or end, only phases.

Below the ferns, at the stone floor: tiny plants you don't have names for. Succulent-adjacent. The color of verdigris. Packed close in communities of dozens, each community maintaining its boundary against the next with a precision that looks territorial, looks *decided*. 

---

The fauna is where the sound mostly comes from.

Insects. The word is insufficient. Insects if insects had been redesigned at the nano-engineering level for acoustic output, for resonance, for the conversion of metabolic energy into sound with an efficiency that violates what should be thermodynamically possible. The basin is full of them in the way that a good forest is full of light — they are the medium, not the object. Beetles with elytra that function as tuned plates. Something fly-adjacent that beats its wings at a frequency your ears experience as a tone, a real specific sustained tone, and the thousand of them produce a chord that changes as they move and cluster and disperse. Moths or moth-like things that are mostly still on the moss, wings flat, but even stillness here is loud: their wing-scales are structures, actual engineered structures, and they are resonating passively in the general acoustic field, adding to it, filtering it, re-emitting portions of it shifted in phase.

There are larger things. Not large: finger-length, a few of them. Moving fast along the fern-stems in ways that are too smooth for walking, too direct, almost like they're running tracks invisible to you. They pause, exactly, and from the pause a sound emerges — not from a mouth, from the whole body, from some internal structure that causes the chitinous casing to become a speaker. The sound is a burst of something that resembles language in its structure, in its having-of-edges, though it communicates nothing to you. It is received. Something elsewhere in the moss answers. The exchange is a millisecond long and contains more information density than you have any way of measuring.

Birds: a few. Perched at the aperture above, not entering, watching. Their presence is felt more than seen. They sing in the human-audible range, and under other circumstances their singing would be remarkable, would be the main event of the acoustic space, but here it is a thread in a fabric and you find it almost restful for its simplicity.

The sound does not stop. The sound does not diminish. It accumulates. The basin's stone walls have learned the resonant frequencies of everything that lives here and they cooperate, amplifying certain registers, softening others, but the net result is always: more.

---

The structures.

Two of them, visible. Possibly more concealed by the moss.

The first: a column, or what remains of a column, or what is becoming a column — the tense is difficult. It stands perhaps a meter and a half high, its base wide and footed solidly on bedrock, narrowing toward the top where it terminates not in a break but in what looks like an intended terminus, a finished surface, though nothing was ever placed there. The material is not stone. It is not metal. It has the visual quality of graphite but the surface behavior of something else entirely: tiny structures cover it, regular as crystal but not crystal, regular as machined but not machined, and the structures are themselves covered in smaller structures, and this recursion continues to some limit you cannot observe with bare eyes but feel certain does not bottom out at any scale you could reach with instruments you have. The column is doing something. Not visibly. But the air near it has a quality, and the moss for a ten-centimeter radius around its base has stopped its turbulent motion and gone still and is a slightly different green: deeper, as though more satisfied.

The second structure is more distributed. It is, perhaps, a wall — a former wall — that has been grown into so thoroughly by the moss that the structure and the biology are no longer separable. You can see, in cross-section where erosion has cut through, that the two are woven. The wall grew structure into the moss as the moss grew structure into the wall. This is not colonization. This is collaboration that has been proceeding long enough to produce something that is neither and both. The remaining sections of this wall-thing are irregular, chest-height or lower, running perhaps eight meters before disappearing into the basin's edge. They have a finished-ness that the column also has: not abandonment but completion, or completion of a phase, or arrival at an equilibrium that the builder and the moss negotiated together.

Who built these. The question is available and the answer is not.

---

The cool.

It sits inside the sound like a different sense. The temperature is not cold — your breath is not visible, your hands are not stiff — but there is a persistent freshness that works against the season suggested by the aperture's light. It comes, perhaps, from the moss, which is always undergoing processes, which is always releasing something into the thin high air. There is a smell that belongs to the cool: green-mineral, old, the smell of chemistry that does not use water, that uses something else, some solvent that exists in thin films inside plant tissue at high elevation when the plant tissue has been redesigned or selected or slowly brought over long time into a new way of processing the world.

Breathe. The air is thin — this is a high place — but breathing does not feel thin. It feels like the available oxygen has been organized. Presented. As though something in the basin's ecology is concerned with visitor experience in a way you cannot entirely attribute to coincidence and cannot entirely attribute to intention.

---

The turbulence.

You have been describing it around the edges and here it is directly: this place is not resting. This place has never rested. The distinction between the turbulence here and the turbulence of a storm or a bazaar is that storms and bazaars have breaks, have lulls, have the implication of eventual cessation. This has no such implication. The moss moves because the moss always moves. The insects produce sound because the insects cannot stop producing sound — it is not behavior, it is being. The ferns cycle through their unfurling because unfurling is the permanent state of the ferns, the cycle their metabolism runs, the way their particular clock keeps time.

The column's invisible work continues at whatever pace it has always continued.

The wall negotiates with the moss.

The tiny territorial plants at your feet have, in the time you've been standing here, shifted their collective boundary by perhaps two centimeters in a direction that seems to involve you: involving your warmth, your exhalation, the barometric presence of a large animal in a space calibrated for smaller ones.

Change accumulates without tipping into destruction. This is the nature of *established turbulence*: not equilibrium, which is static and eventual, but a dynamic balance that is always *solving*, always in the middle of the calculation, never at the answer because arriving at the answer would mean the system had stopped. Everything here is in process. Everything here is doing its work. The doing is continuous. The work is serious.

You are standing inside a machine that is also a garden that is also a mind of some distributed kind, thinking slowly at every scale, loudly, in the thin cold light of an afternoon that will end but whose end is of no particular concern to any of this.

---

Stay long enough and the sound will stop being sound. It will become the baseline from which silence would be the aberration. You will start to hear inside it: tones, rhythms, something that is almost pattern. Whether the pattern is there or whether you are making it, as minds make patterns, as minds cannot stop making patterns — this question will become interesting to you, and then the distinction will become interesting to you, and then you will wonder whether the distinction exists, whether a pattern made by a mind is different from a pattern made by chemistry and evolution and the resonance of stone, and the wondering will feel like a contribution to the place rather than an interruption of it.

The moss will notice you in whatever way moss notices.

It will file you somewhere.
