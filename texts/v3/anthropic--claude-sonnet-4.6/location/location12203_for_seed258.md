# The Plateau of Grown Walls

---

Begin with the floor, because the floor is where everything starts here.

It is flat. Not the flatness of erosion, not the flatness worn down by rivers and patience and geological time — this flatness is *original*, a first flatness, the kind that has never been anything else. The rock underneath — and there is rock, somewhere down there — has not yet learned to be a hill or a valley. It simply is. It came into being flat and it remains flat and it will, by every sign available, remain flat for a very long time. This is not a plateau shaped by ice or uplift. It is a plateau that emerged as a plateau, the way bread rises level, the way a thought arrives already finished.

You are standing on something that the world is still deciding about.

---

But what you are standing *on*, specifically, what the sole of your foot meets, is not rock at all. It is root. A dense matt of root and rootlet, pale and cream-colored where it catches light, deepening to ochre in the shadows between strands. The whole surface of the plateau is this — a woven substrate of plant material, a living floor, and it gives slightly underfoot in the way that a very old mattress gives, or the way a bog surface gives but without the instability, without the sense of drowning. This surface holds you. It is confident. It has been doing this — making itself into a floor — with total commitment and without interruption, and it is not done but it is not in a hurry.

Out of this floor the things that you would call walls grow.

They are not trees, quite, though they are made of trees. They are columns and arches and panels of living matter in which the individual plant has become somewhat beside the point. Figs have sent down aerial roots that thickened into pillars. Banyans have reached sideways and merged. Something vine-like but with the girth of a forearm has grown in flat planes, creating curtains of green that catch the wind — and there is wind, a warm persistent wind from the south, the kind of wind that carries the memory of somewhere hotter — creating curtains that move all at once, not leaf by leaf but as whole panels, as if the plant has understood the concept of a wall and made itself into one. Between these panels: gaps. Gaps are important here. This is a sparse construction.

Not a jungle. A *building*.

An open building, a building without a roof, a building being built by the plants themselves according to an architectural logic that does not map onto anything human-made, but which is clearly a logic and clearly being followed. There are corridors. There are chambers. There are what you would have to call — standing in one — *rooms*, defined not by walls on all four sides but by three walls and the strong suggestion of a fourth, by the way sound behaves, by the way light collects. The rooms are large. Town-square large. You could put a hundred people in the smallest of them and they would not feel crowded.

There are no people.

---

Water is everywhere in the way that water sometimes is everywhere without being pooled anywhere visible. The air is saturated — not uncomfortably so, not tropical, but beyond merely humid. Surfaces drip. The undersides of the great flat leaves drip in rhythms that do not correspond to any rain, because there is no rain happening, because the sky is clear, pale blue going white at the edges. The dripping is the leaves pulling moisture from the air itself, condensing it, releasing it. In the gaps between the living walls, threads of vapor catch the light and vanish. The floor-mat of roots drinks constantly. You can hear it.

That is one of the sounds.

The others:

The sound of dripping, which is loud enough in aggregate to be something like white noise — not a trickle, not a stream, but a broadband hiss of ten thousand small percussions on the root-mat and on the broad lower leaves of the understory plants, which are wide as dinner plates and cup-shaped, designed by selection to catch and hold before releasing. When you walk through a corridor of this place your presence disturbs the dripping and the sound shifts around you like a wake.

The sound of the panels of vine-wall moving in the warm wind. This is a sound between rustling and a lower thing, a sound you feel in the sternum if you stand near it. The panels are not leaves exactly — they are laminate structures, layer on layer of growth over years, and when the wind moves them they flex and speak in harmonics you cannot quite resolve into discrete tones. It is possible to mistake this sound, for a moment, for a crowd. For many voices in another part of the building.

Occasionally, infrequently, the sound of a bird. Just one kind. You never see it. It calls from high up in the canopy-columns, a single note, then three descending, and then nothing for a long time. It does not seem to be communicating with anything. It does not seem to need to. You hear it four times in an hour and feel grateful each time.

---

The light falls into the rooms in the way that light falls into old cathedrals, in certain slanted quantities, tracked across the floor as the sun moves. But the sun, today, does not seem to be moving much. Or perhaps it is, and the roof-edge of living matter above is compensating — growing as you watch, but no, the dynamic here is almost nil, nothing is changing on any timescale available to a person standing in the middle of it. The light is where it is. The shadows are where they are. The vapor-threads catch the light and the dripping makes its sound and the panels flex and speak in the warm wind and none of it changes into anything else.

This is a place in equilibrium with itself. A finished argument. A system that has found its answer.

And yet.

---

The strange thing — and you begin to understand it as a strange thing only gradually, only as you move deeper into the plateau and the rooms give way to other rooms and the corridors bend in ways that should not geometrically work given the routes you have taken — is that this is all *made*. You do not have the tools to say this with certainty, there are no right angles, no poured concrete, no human signature of any kind, but you come to feel it with increasing conviction: this was arranged. The plants were placed. The corridors do not arrive at their junctions by accident. The rooms do not have their proportions by chance.

Something — not human, not using any device you know how to name, not doing anything that falls into the category of technology as you understand it — something organized this. Cultivated it. Returned again and again over a very long time to make choices: this growth, not that one, this pillar, not that one, this corridor leading here, this room opening into this other room.

The plateau, then, is a building. A building made entirely of living things, built by a thing or things unknown, at a scale large enough to be walked through for days. Wet. Warm. Loud with its own internal weather. Unchanging now that it is done — or now that the builders have stopped, which might be the same thing, or might not. The rooms continue for as far as you can see from any high point, which is not very far, because the plant-walls interrupt every sightline. But the plateau is wide. It extends. There is more of it past the next curtain of moving vine-panels, and more past that, and more.

---

What fauna exists is present mainly as implication. Something has left marks on the root-floor — not footprints, the surface is too springy to hold impressions long, but *wear*, the subtle compression of paths used many times by something of medium weight. The paths do not lead to any visible destination within the rooms. They lead through walls, apparently — through panels that are slightly more open than they appear, or that open for those who know how. You push against one and it holds. You go around.

The bird calls from somewhere above, once, and then three descending, and then nothing.

A millipede, enormous, red-brown, eight inches long, crosses the root-floor ahead of you with the purposeful unhurriedness of a thing that has never had a predator. It goes into the shadow of a fig-pillar and does not come out the other side.

You watch the space where it was.

The dripping continues. The panels move. The light holds its angle with unreasonable patience.

---

The warmth is the warmth of a greenhouse, though there is no glass, no ceiling, no containment — just the open sky above the top edges of the living walls, sky going white at the horizon in every direction. The warmth is the warmth of a system that generates its own heat, that has enough living mass to hold and recirculate the day's warmth into the night, not that you have seen the night, but you feel it in the surfaces — the root-floor is warm through your soles, the pillars are warm to the touch, everything in here gives back what it has received and then a little more.

It is not uncomfortable. It will not become uncomfortable. Nothing here is becoming anything.

You sit in the center of one of the larger rooms and the sound of dripping is very loud and even and the sound from the walls rises and falls with the wind and you understand, sitting here, that you are inside something's idea of a place. Not a trap. Not a temple. Not a garden in any sense the word normally carries. Something prior to those categories. A spatial thought, expressed in the only medium available, which was time and root and photosynthesis and slow growth guided by something patient enough to wait out centuries.

The plateau extends. The rooms continue. The light holds.

You stay a while. Nothing changes, and this turns out to be a kind of comfort.
