# The Sediment Sea

---

Below the thermocline, below where light makes any claim on the world, the water is not dark so much as it has forgotten that darkness is a condition requiring contrast. Dark here is a substance. It has been here long enough to have settled into every available space, the way sediment settles — not falling so much as becoming permanent.

This is the bottom of something very old.

The seafloor — if you want to call it that, though the word *floor* implies a place where standing might occur — is not a floor at all but a surface so extensively worked by time that it has stopped being a surface and become more of a philosophy. Millions of years of pressure have pressed the substrate flat. Billions of years of small depositions have layered the mud so many times over that the layers have compressed into each other and lost their individual identity, the way stacked papers eventually become one object rather than many. What is beneath your hands, if you could reach down into it, would not crumble or yield the way sediment does when it is young. It would resist softly, like memory resists — you push and find that it was already including you, that your touch has arrived in a place that was prepared for touch ten million years before you thought of touching.

The rock outcroppings that interrupt the mud at intervals are not jagged. They are not even particularly rocky. They are round-shouldered, lobed, and so smoothed by their long submersion that they read less as geological features than as presences — shapes that arrived at their current form through patience rather than violence. A once-sharp fault-scarp runs northeast to southwest across several kilometers of basin, but it is now simply a long, low rise, a suggestion of a ridge, an old argument between tectonic plates that has been so thoroughly mediated by time that only the faintest scar of it remains. You would walk over it without noticing, if you could walk, which you cannot.

---

The water column above the floor is — and this is the fact that matters most — absolutely packed.

Not with fish. Not primarily with anything that has a name.

Packed with *matter*. With presence. With particle.

Suspended in the full water column from the floor to some impossible distance above — the kind of distance you can only measure in time rather than space, since light takes so long to arrive here from the surface that it ceases to be the same light by the time it might theoretically reach the floor, though it does not reach the floor — suspended in all that water is an almost continuous material: diatom shells, foraminifera casings, the silicified remains of radiolarians in forms of such geometric complexity that they look like they were designed, like someone was making a point. Marine snow, but here not so much falling as occupying. The particles do not fall through empty water; they inhabit water so full of other particles that each descent is a negotiation, a slow threading through neighbors.

The density of this suspended matter is not visible, because nothing is visible. But you would feel it. Moving through this water, if you could move, would feel like moving through something that was not quite committing to being solid but was no longer quite fluid either. Not viscous — the temperature is too close to four degrees Celsius for viscosity in the ordinary sense — but *populated*. Every cubic centimeter a neighborhood.

This is what the ocean floor looks like at scale: not emptiness, not the dramatic and alien terrain of popular imagination, but a continuous, extremely patient conversation between particles, an ongoing settling, an ancient accumulation that has never stopped but has long since stabilized into a kind of energetic permanence. Things are still arriving. Things are still leaving. The state of the place does not change.

---

The turbulence is not where you would expect it.

There are no currents here in the way that currents exist in shallower water — no tidal sweeping, no thermal convection of any speed a person would register. But there is movement. Constant, chaotic, small-scale movement that the instruments would show if instruments were here.

The near-bottom boundary layer is turbulent in the technical sense: flow over rough substrate generates eddies at the millimeter scale, and these eddies compete and collapse and regenerate in patterns that are deterministic in principle and unpredictable in practice. Thermodynamically, this place is far from equilibrium. The sediment exhales methane in slow columns — not violent, not fast, but constant and rising — bacterial mats at the sediment interface are running oxidation-reduction chemistry at the full pace of their metabolism, which is not fast by human standards but is ceaseless, and the gradient they maintain between the sulfidic sediment below and the oxygenated water column above represents a kind of slow riot, a standing wave of chemical disequilibrium that has been rioting in exactly this way for geological time.

Beneath the seafloor — three, four, fifteen meters down — the real turbulence is happening in geological slow motion: pore water is migrating under pressure, sediment is compacting and dewatering, methane hydrates are shifting their stability fields in response to temperature gradients measured in fractions of a degree per meter. This is the turbulence of deep time — not chaotic in the way a storm is chaotic, but chaotic in the way a market is chaotic, with a thousand simultaneous negotiations all proceeding independently, all influencing each other, none of them individually legible but the whole of them constituting something that has a character, that makes choices in aggregate, that will eventually arrive somewhere no single participant planned.

---

The plants — what few — are not in the water column.

They cannot be. No light.

Near the sediment surface, near methane seeps where chemistry substitutes for sunlight, there are mats of bacteria that do not quite qualify as plants but fill the niche that plants might otherwise fill: primary producers, converters of chemical energy into biological structure. These mats are pale — off-white, yellow-white, sometimes a sulfurous orange — and they have a texture like wet felt. They cover areas of several square meters around each seep, sometimes overlapping adjacent seeps into longer irregular fields. They do not have edges so much as gradients: dense at the center, increasingly sparse toward the periphery, finally becoming isolated threads and patches that trail away into the mud and disappear.

There is a kind of tube worm — sparse, widely spaced, a single specimen every thirty meters or so in the areas near active seeps — that rises from the sediment in a pale red plume, extending itself into the chemical gradient, conducting its business. The worms do not communicate. They do not respond to anything except chemistry. Each one is alone in a functional sense, occupying its small cylinder of water, maintaining the exchange across its gill surface that allows it to live in a place where almost nothing can live.

Between the seep fields there is nothing green. The mud is brown-grey, the color of a thing that was once many colors and has averaged them over sufficient time. Occasional tracks cross the surface where something moved — something small, something that processes sediment, something that has been here long enough to have behavior rather than just reflexes.

---

The sounds.

There are almost none.

The deep ocean absorbs sound. This is known and strange — you might expect the opposite, that water would conduct sound efficiently, and it does conduct it efficiently over horizontal distances, the sound channel at mid-depth carrying whale song for thousands of kilometers. But down here at the floor, within the sediment itself, sound arrives and stops. The particulate-saturated water damps vibration. The mud below damps it further. What remains, acoustically, is a texture rather than a sound — a very faint, very distributed, very low-frequency presence that may be the sum of all distant shipping noise, all distant seismic activity, all the ocean's own bulk movement averaged into something below the threshold of hearing but not below the threshold of influence.

At close range: the soft irregular sound of methane bubbles leaving sediment. Not a constant stream but a pulse — blurp, silence, blurp, silence. Some seeps maintain a thin continuous trickle. Some go minutes without a sound and then release a cluster of bubbles so quickly that they sound almost like a word.

Nothing else.

No fish pass. No bioluminescent creature flashes. What fauna exist here do so on their own terms, which are terms of great patience and great economy — terms, in other words, that do not generate sound.

---

If a person could be here — which they cannot without equipment of such mass and artificiality that the equipment would be the event rather than the place — what they would experience is not the dramatic alienness of popular imagination. No glowing creatures, no dramatic vents, no abyss of individual sublimity.

What they would experience is *scale* without reference points, and density without visibility, and a kind of ancient stability that functions like stillness but is not stillness — is, in fact, the opposite of stillness, is continuous process that has been continuous for so long that it has become indistinguishable from rest.

The place does not know that it is the bottom.

It does not experience itself as below anything. It is simply the place where the conditions are these conditions, where pressure is this pressure, where time is this thick, where the business of chemistry proceeds as it has proceeded, where the sediment is still arriving, one particle at a time, to join the body of a record that no one is reading.
