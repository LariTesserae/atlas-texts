# The Plateau City

---

The plateau goes on long enough that the far edge disappears not into horizon but into weather. On a clear morning you can sometimes see the far rim — a faint darkening, a suggestion of escarpment — but most mornings are not clear, and so the plateau seems to simply continue, to be the world's floor, to have no argument with infinity. Clouds form over it. Not passing clouds but forming ones, rising from the stone itself where moisture has been pressed upward through limestone capillaries for longer than the word *ancient* comfortably describes.

---

## The Stone

The rock here is a sedimentary limestone of particular character: pale grey-white where it is dry, which it almost never is, and darkening through slate toward near-black where water has pooled in its pores, which is nearly everywhere. Stand anywhere on the plateau and press your palm to an exposed face and your hand will come away cold and lightly damp. Not dramatically wet — not dripping, not streaming — but covertly wet, persistently wet, wet in the way that certain silences are persistent. The rock is full of water the way old bread is full of salt. It is not a surface thing.

The limestone has had long enough to settle into every configuration it intends to occupy. Columns that looked for ten thousand years like they were about to topple have decided not to. Pavements of flat rock have cracked along lines that have become drainage channels that have become small carved valleys that have become stable things with their own ecology. Nothing here looks like it is in the process of becoming something else. The erosion is *finished*, or finished enough. The landscape has arrived at itself. There is a quality of self-satisfaction to the stone — not smugness, but completion, the way a river delta looks when it has sorted all its sediment into exactly the right fans.

The surface is textured in overlapping scales. At the largest scale, there are low domes and broad shallow basins — the plateau breathing in slow geology, its chest rising and falling over epochs. At middle scale, the pavements and their grikes: joints in the limestone that weather has opened into vertical slots, irregular, following the rock's own grain, some a hand's-width, some wide enough to fall into, all of them filled with a darkness that hides small wet things. At the smallest scale, the rock's surface is pitted and channeled in rills smaller than your finger, each rill a miniature river valley in its own right, and where two meet there is a tiny confluence, and where many meet there is a shallow basin the size of a palm, and in that basin, always, water.

---

## The Water

Not a river. Not quite. Water moves here but refuses to commit to linear form. It appears in the basins and seeps downward through the grikes. It surfaces fifty meters away in a different basin, lower by a handsbreadth. It weeps from the undercut faces of low limestone scarps where horizontal strata meet and the bedding plane becomes a slow faucet. In places the whole surface is wet enough that each footstep on bare rock leaves a dry print for three seconds before the moisture reclaims it.

There are pools — dozens, perhaps hundreds, scattered across the vast pale floor, each one of different depth and character. Some are no deeper than a book. Some are pools in the proper sense, dropping two meters into cold black clarity where the bottom is visible as a pale ghost. The water is cold. Not the cold of ice or the cold of wind but the cold of the deep earth, the temperature of caves, the temperature of permanence. It takes the body a moment to accept this water touching it.

The clouds that form above the plateau shed their load back onto it in fine mist rather than rain, more often than not. The mist does not fall so much as condense out of the air at whatever surface it encounters — the rock, the small plants, the occasional tuft of pale grass, the stone walls. Visibility changes. An hour of stillness and clarity, then twenty minutes of soft grey. The mist is not cold exactly; it is the same temperature as the air, which is itself the same temperature as the stone, which is to say: cool enough that a person standing still in it begins to think about warmth.

---

## The Built Things

They are old. This is the first and most important thing about them: they are old enough that the limestone walls have become part of the limestone plateau, drinking the same water, hosting the same slow biological process on their faces. There is moss in the joints where there was once mortar. There are patches of the pale green-black crust, the crust that takes a century to form a centimeter, that speak to duration beyond what any one builder intended.

The settlement — it is too vast to call a town, too horizontal to call a city, too organic to call either without caveat — covers the plateau. *Covers* may be the wrong word; it is woven into the plateau, has grown from it, returns to it at the margins. Buildings of pale limestone, cut from the same beds they stand upon, so that wall and floor share the same geology. The stone was quarried from grikes widened by tool, from scarps undercut by hand, from depths opened and sealed again; the plateau gave the city its body and the city gave the plateau its form, and the two are now inseparable enough that a geologist arriving from elsewhere might study the walls before realizing they were walls.

The buildings are low. Two stories are common, three are unusual, four mark something that wanted to be important. The rooflines are not uniform but they do not vary dramatically — they form a low continuous texture at the plateau's surface, the way sediment layers form a texture in section: complex up close, readable as a single thing from distance. The streets — and they are streets, with the logic of streets, of intention, of routes that went somewhere and still do — are narrow by the standard of places that planned themselves for vehicles. They planned themselves for people on foot in light rain, which is what they got, and so the streets are shoulder-width in the oldest parts and twice that in the newer parts, which are only relatively newer, which is still old.

Water moves through the built city the way it moves through the rock: not in straight declared channels but in systems that found their own level. Stone-lined gutters follow the street's edge, but the streets follow the rock's contour, and so the gutters curve and drop and sometimes cut diagonally across a corner where two levels didn't agree. Cisterns are cut into the plateau itself, roofed with flat slabs, fed by channels that collect the mist and the surface seep. They are full. They are always full.

The buildings have no glass. There are openings in the walls — arched, mostly, small — and these openings are dark in daylight, suggesting interior depth. When the light is flat and grey there is no difference between the light inside and outside, no threshold. When the sun comes through the clouds briefly, the interiors are dark and the exteriors bright, and the arched openings look like eyes, like mouths, like voids. The walls are dense with this — openings on openings, stories of openings, the city a system of solid and void in a ratio that doesn't favor either.

There is no wood that wasn't salvaged from somewhere else. There is no metal visible. There is rope, plant-fiber, plant-based. There are things that are clearly hinges, clearly latches, but made of carved stone, of bone, of what might be ceramic fired from the plateau's clay deposits. The city built itself from what the plateau offered, and the plateau offered stone, clay, bone, plant, water, and weather, and so that is what the city is made of.

---

## The Plants

Sparse is not nothing. Sparse on the plateau is: pale grass in long tufts rooted in grikes, three blades or twenty with no in-between, the roots running a meter down to where the moisture is reliable. Hart's-tongue fern where a scarp overhangs enough to call itself a sheltered face, the fern's thick waxy leaves the most intensely green thing visible, an almost aggressive green against all the grey. Liverwort on the north-facing wet patches, making a seamless layer of olive, no individual plants, just a presence. In the grikes themselves, small things: the odd flowering plant with the evolutionary patience to have found a crack it could use, blooming in a color that seems improbable at this altitude, pink or yellow or white, a single stem, a single flower, visible from two meters away as an interruption.

The built city has plants on its walls. Not gardens, not cultivation — colonization. The same liverwort. The same fern in gutters where silt has accumulated. Pale lichen in species that require particular mineral chemistry and a century of stability before they begin. The walls, read closely, are a record of which stones were laid first and which were replaced and when, written in lichen chronology.

There is no canopy. There is no shade from vegetation. The sky is always visible.

---

## The Life

Birds are present but rarely visible. A call from somewhere in the grey air — a pipit, a chough, something working the thermal boundary where the plateau's warmth-differential creates brief columns. The calls arrive already attenuated by the wet air, already absorbed, already half-returned to silence. You hear one and then you are not certain you heard one.

In the pools: small things. Invertebrates, pale and adapted to cold dark water, visible as movement when you look directly into the pool, invisible when you try to focus on them. The ecology here is quiet in all the ways that matter — not absent but unhurried, not barren but spare, an ecosystem running on careful margins, spending nothing it doesn't have.

The people, if there are people, leave no evidence right now that distinguishes their presence from the building's long occupation. A door that is open was open before you arrived. A gutter drains. A cistern is full. The city functions the way a body functions while asleep — maintaining, cycling, without apparent agency.

---

## The Air and Sound

The air is quiet enough that you can hear the mist. This requires still weather and a certain quality of attention but it is possible: a faint percussion, a sibilance, the sound of water finding surfaces to condense upon. The distant sound of water leaving a high basin and joining a lower one, the transfer brief and low, finished before you've decided it began. Once in a while, a long interval apart, the call from above. Otherwise: the sound of a vast system engaged in low-frequency process, the hum of weather and water and time, below the threshold of what most people would call sound.

The city does not add to this. The city is stone, and stone does not make sound without reason. There is no creaking, no hum of machinery, no underlayer of industry. If a stone settles in a wall it makes a sound that is over in a moment and leaves no echo because the wet air takes echo early.

---

## What This Place Is

The plateau is vast enough to have its own weather and old enough to have arrived at its own character, and the city is old enough to have become part of both. The relationship between wet stone and built stone, between the plateau's own hydraulics and the city's engineered version of them, between the sparse biology of altitude and the long occupation — this relationship is not a conflict and not a resolution. It is a third thing. A co-existence so long that the parties have begun to share grammar.

This is a place where processes are active but not urgent. Water moves. Weather shifts. The cloud comes and goes. The biology proceeds at the pace biology chooses at this altitude in this temperature. The city's systems — cisterns filling, gutters draining, the thermal variation between day and night causing the stone to exhale moisture in one direction and absorb it in another — cycle without hurry.

To stand here is to stand at the edge of a conclusion that has been almost-reached for a very long time and shows no interest in finally arriving. The plateau is in the process of being the plateau. It has been in this process for longer than memory and will continue. The city is in the process of being the city in the same way. The mist is forming over the far rim, the far rim that may or may not exist, and by the time it reaches you it will be indistinguishable from the air itself, and the stone under your damp hand will be exactly as cool as it was before, and the pool at your feet will have no opinion about any of this, and will hold its small cold depth of pale water regardless.
