# The Plateau Mills

---

The plateau exists at a height where the atmosphere begins to forget itself. Not quite the dead zone above treeline — something else, something that shouldn't be possible at this altitude — but the air here is thin enough that every breath carries a faint metallic taste, like licking a coin left in snow. The cold is not the cold of winter arriving. It is the cold of a thing that has never been otherwise. It sits in the rock itself, in the permanent frost that keeps the ground locked several meters down regardless of season, and the cold radiates upward through the soles of your boots with a patience that is not indifferent — it feels almost intentional, almost curious about your warmth.

---

The plateau is immense. This needs to be understood before anything else can be understood.

From any given point, the edges are not visible. The horizon here is not where the land drops away but simply where the atmosphere hazes to a uniform grey-green, the color of altitude, the color of distance measured in days rather than hours. To walk from one side of this plateau to the other would take weeks, and during those weeks the landscape would not substantially change — the same dark basalt underfoot, fractured in polygonal sheets by freeze-thaw cycles that are younger than they look, the same shallow bogs sitting in every low depression, the same improbable stands of vegetation clinging to every windbreak and south-facing crack. The plateau is large enough to generate its own weather. Clouds form over its center and move toward its edges. Storms begin here without reference to anything below. The plateau is a country, except that countries have borders and this place has only the slow diminishment of itself.

The rock is young. This is obvious once you see it — the basalt sheets have not yet rounded, not yet surrendered their edges to the long patience of erosion. Fracture faces catch the grey light at sharp angles. Where water has pooled in the cracks and frozen and thawed, the breaks are so fresh they look deliberate, like scored stone, like something that cracked open this morning. In places the rock has split to reveal interiors still dark with moisture, still smelling faintly of the deep interior — a sulfurous, mineral smell that rises and disperses in the thin air. The plateau is still becoming itself. It has not yet learned patience.

---

Water is everywhere and nowhere visible until you look carefully.

There are no rivers, or almost none. What there is instead: the constant seep. Water moves through the surface of this place laterally, horizontally, at a depth of centimeters, always moving but never accumulating into anything directional enough to call a stream. In the low places between fractured basalt sheets, marsh has formed — cold marsh, nearly colorless at first glance, but look again. The sphagnum here is deep green going to rust at the tips, colors so saturated they seem unreal against the grey stone. Sedges have colonized every crack where soil has begun to gather. Their blades are knife-narrow and perfectly upright in the still air. Cushion plants fill the spaces between, moss campion and some others unnamed, building their low dense hemispheres over years, over decades, a millimeter at a time, and from a distance they read as a texture rather than as individual organisms — the plateau's skin, slowly regrowing over exposed stone.

In the mornings, and in any dip or sheltered depression throughout the day, the atmosphere condenses and the vegetation weeps. Water beads along every blade and needle and runs in small clear paths down the faces of the youngest rocks. The rocks themselves, where lichen has not yet colonized, appear to sweat. This is not metaphor. The temperature differential between rock surface and air is sufficient that condensation forms on stone that faces the slight warmth of the overcast sky, and those droplets merge and run and drip into the moss below with a sound — a very small, very regular sound — that you notice only when you stop and hold still, and once you notice it you cannot unhear it.

The bogs do not smell of rot. They are too cold for rot. They smell instead of cold water and iron and something faintly green, almost medicinal, the smell of plant chemistry operating at the edge of viability.

---

And then there are the mills.

They appear on the plateau with the logic of a dream — not clustered, not in a recognizable city-form, but distributed across the vast surface at irregular intervals that suggest planning without revealing its principle. You walk for an hour across bare stone and bog and then one appears on your left, enormous and specific, and you walk for another two hours and another appears ahead of you, slightly different in configuration but unmistakably of the same family, and you begin to understand that the entire plateau, the whole continental shelf of it, is threaded through with these structures.

They are industrial. The bones of the architecture are iron and poured concrete — concrete that has taken on the grey-green of altitude, that has developed its own lichen colonies, that fits into the landscape with the unsettling ease of a thing that has been here long enough to be colonized but not long enough to crumble. The structures are not ruins. This is crucial and strange. The mills are operating.

Each mill is some variation on the same theme: a primary structure of four to eight stories, its walls punctuated with industrial windows in steel frames, many of them broken and admitting the cold freely, which seems not to matter; attached to this a series of lower outbuildings in corrugated iron, rust-red and orange, connected by enclosed walkways elevated on iron stanchions above the bog; and rising from the primary structure, always, the stacks. The stacks are the first things you see from a distance — vertical columns of dark iron or brick, rising fifty, seventy, ninety meters into the thin air, and from their tops issuing a continuous column of white steam that rises vertically (the air here has very little lateral movement) for hundreds of meters before dispersing into the general overcast.

The steam is the source of much of the moisture. The plateau's atmosphere is, in part, a product of the mills.

---

Inside — and the doors stand open, or are absent entirely, and there is nothing to stop entry — the noise is extraordinary.

The sound inside a mill is the sound of industry at full capacity, which is not one sound but a layered phenomenon. There is the bass foundation: some massive reciprocating mechanism deep in the building, felt as much as heard, a rhythmic compression of the air that recurs every few seconds and seems to be the building's heartbeat. Above this, the constant roar of combustion in chambers you cannot see, a sound like a river but more aggressive, more insistent. Above this, the higher frequencies of metal on metal, of pipes under pressure, of valves releasing — a complex texture of industrial voices that never resolves into rhythm, never settles into pattern, but maintains a continuous loud complexity. Above all of this, occasional: the sharp clang of something falling, the groan of a structure adjusting under thermal expansion, sounds that rise above the general roar and then are swallowed by it.

The machines run. The mechanisms turn. Belts travel between massive iron wheels. Steam moves through pipes that sweat with heat differential, the warm pipes running through the cold air of the opened mill floors. Gauges read pressures. Valves open and close on their set schedules. Everything functions.

There are no workers.

There have been no workers for — it is impossible to say. The stools beside the monitoring stations are empty. The logbooks are open on their lecterns, the last entries in handwriting that has not faded because the cold and the dryness have preserved it, the ink still dark, but the date of the last entry is unclear — the notation system is unfamiliar. The mugs on the shelves have been washed and placed upside down to dry. The drying is complete. No one has returned to turn them right side up.

The machines do not need them. The machines continue.

---

Outside, between mills, the plateau returns to itself.

The silence between mills is not silence — there is always the distant machinery, a low presence at the threshold of hearing that never entirely disappears, that becomes the baseline against which all other sounds are measured. But it is quiet enough to hear the dripping condensation, the soft movement of sedge blades in the occasional breath of wind, and something else: a very low, very intermittent sound that seems to come from beneath the stone, which may be the deep freeze-thaw of the permafrost, which may be the young rock continuing to adjust to stresses formed when this plateau emerged from whatever process generated it, which may be the plateau speaking to itself in a language too slow for comprehension.

The vegetation between mills has, in places, reached up to colonize the lower portions of the structures. Moss on north-facing concrete walls. Sedge clumps at the base of stanchions. Lichen beginning its slow patient work on the corrugated iron, red-orange on rust-orange, almost invisible until the light finds a particular angle. The plateau is not reclaiming the mills — this gives the wrong impression, implies conflict, implies the mills are abandoned in the way abandoned means when something has stopped. The mills are not stopped. The vegetation is simply doing what vegetation does, which is to take hold of any stable surface and begin. The mills are stable. The mills are very stable.

---

The light here is the light of high altitude under a permanent thin overcast — bright without being warm, shadowless, coming from no single direction but from the whole sky at once. Colors are true but muted. The green of the moss is green, but quietly. The rust of the corrugated iron is rust, but considered. Only the white steam from the stacks has any quality of brightness, any sense of movement against the static grey above.

At the edges of the plateau — distant, theoretical — the clouds that form over the center have tracked outward and now hang below the plateau's rim, visible as a white plain far below when you approach what you believe might be an edge and look over. The world below is obscured. Only this plateau is real. Only this plateau is present.

There are no birds. There are no insects. There is nothing that moves of its own will except the machinery and the water.

---

The rules of this place, as they become apparent:

The mills run. They have always run. They will continue to run. Whatever they are processing — and there is evidence of input and output, of material moving through the system, though the raw material is not visible arriving and the product is not visible departing — they process it without pause, without failure, without need. The maintenance that keeps them running is performed by the machines on themselves, or by some mechanism not yet observed, or does not need to be performed because the machines do not degrade. One of these explanations is true.

The plateau does not change. Not in the way landscapes change — the stone does not erode at any rate that observation reveals, the bogs do not advance or retreat, the mills do not rust further. It is as if the plateau reached its current state and then stopped, not froze in the sense of being stuck, but arrived at equilibrium and declined to move further in any direction. The condensation drips. The steam rises. The machines turn. The lichen grows at its imperceptible rate, which may be the one exception, the one process that has not reached its endpoint, the one sign that something here still has somewhere to go.

You are not supposed to be here, or: there is no category for whether you are supposed to be here. The plateau does not acknowledge visitors. The mills do not acknowledge entry. The logbooks do not update. The mugs stay upside down. The machinery makes its noise and its noise fills every interior and the cold makes every exterior simultaneously vast and specific, and the steam from ten thousand stacks rises into the overcast and the overcast reflects it back as moisture and the moisture settles on the stone and the vegetation and drips, and there is no reason any of this should stop.
