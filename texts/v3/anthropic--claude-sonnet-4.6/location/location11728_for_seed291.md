# The Verdant Works

---

The valley is low and cold and impossibly full.

Begin with the cold — it is the first thing, the organizing principle, the reason everything else has arranged itself the way it has. Not the cold of altitude. Not the clean cold of open snowfields or the theatrical cold of mountain passes. This is the cold of a basin, a cupped-palm cold, the kind that settles and stays. The air here has weight and residence. It came in at some point — off a sea, through a gap in the hills to the north, along the surface of some slow river — and found that it could not easily leave. It sits at roughly four degrees and does not apologize for itself. Breath comes out visible. Metal is too cold to touch without attention. But the cold is also wet. Not wet the way rain is wet, not drenching or dramatic, just — persistently humid. Condensation on every surface by morning. Moss on the undersides of rails. A general dampness in everything: the fabric of coats, the inside of buildings, the soil, the wood, the lungs. It is the kind of cold that seeps rather than strikes.

Start again, wider. Look from somewhere elevated — a ridge to the south, perhaps, or the window of a slow train on an embankment — and take in the whole.

The valley extends for perhaps twenty kilometers, broader at its center, narrowing at the far ends where the hills press in. It is not dramatically beautiful. The hills are low, rounded, covered in dark conifers and something paler between them — birch, maybe, or some other cold-climate tree that holds its silver branches out in a way that looks both empty and crowded. The floor of the valley is entirely occupied. That word: *occupied*. Not settled. Not developed. *Occupied*, the way a room is occupied when someone has lived in it for many decades and has never thrown anything away, never reorganized, never pulled back. There is no visible ground. Rooftops and canopies and towers and smokestacks and crane jibs and greenhouse glass and packed vegetation — these form a continuous upper layer, a dense skin over everything below, and the valley breathes through this skin in thin columns of vapor that rise and immediately lose coherence in the cold air.

---

The city — if it is a city — does not have the grammar of a city.

There are industrial works here. That is clear. You can see the scale of them from any vantage: hulking shed-forms, the pale brick of older factories, the corrugated iron of newer ones, gantries and hoppers and cooling towers emitting their soft plumes. A canal runs through the middle of the valley in a line too straight to be natural, though its banks are now so overgrown, so colonized by reed and willow and some broad-leaved aquatic plant that forces its flat pads up through the cold water, that the straight line is obscured for long stretches. The canal is not the river. There is also a river, smaller, older, crooked, that runs alongside the canal for part of its length and then diverges, disappearing under the built fabric at one point and reemerging somewhere else. Both bodies of water are low — this is not a wet climate, not really, just a persistently damp one — but both are moving.

And between the industrial buildings and running through them and growing over them and somehow coexisting with them is the vegetation.

This is the strange thing, and it takes time to understand.

The vegetation is not decorative. It is not the planned greenery of parks or the scrubby persistence of weeds in cracks. It is dense, organized in the way that a forest is organized — vertical layers, competition for light, species sorted by their tolerances — except that it is growing through and in and around the infrastructure rather than separately from it. A factory wall supports a climbing plant with waxy dark leaves that has reached the roofline and begun to spread across the roof itself, not killing the building but thickening it, making it into something with more dimensions than it had. Between two large sheds, a corridor of space that might have been intended as a loading area has become a dense thicket of something — birch again, or alder, hung with lichen, the ground below impenetrably dark. The loading area may still function. Equipment may move through that thicket. It is hard to tell. The canal banks are almost entirely obscured by vegetation; the reed beds extend further than seems possible given the low water level. Greenhouse structures — and there are many, long linked spans of them, some enormous — are full of green, glass fogged with interior warmth and humidity, though their outside is cold and condensation-streaked.

The impression is not of nature reclaiming industry, which would have a particular drama to it, a particular narrative of before and after. It is something more simultaneous. Industry and vegetation co-present, co-occurring, neither overcoming the other. As if both arrived at the same time and simply began — together, packed together, too busy with their own processes to arrange themselves neatly.

---

There is something else, something harder to name, that belongs to the weirdness parameter on whatever instrument is being used to take readings of this place.

The geology here is *young*. That is the word available for it, though young is not quite right either. The valley floor is — *soft*. Soft in a way that has structural implications. When you stand at the edge of a canal path and look at the exposed bank, the layers of material visible are unusual: dark peat, certainly, and clay, but also layers of something pale and fibrous — vegetable matter in the process of becoming geological, not yet coal, not yet compressed, still recognizably *once-living* — and below that, glimpsed in deeper exposures, something that looks almost crystalline, pale and granular, as if the valley floor is still figuring out what kind of rock it intends to become. The ground shifts slowly. Buildings built fifty years ago have settled, are still settling; foundations move incrementally; a warehouse at the valley's center has a floor that tilts slightly, noticeably, the tilt having increased over the decades the building has been in use. This is not cause for alarm. It is cause for shimming, for recalibrating, for accommodating. The buildings accommodate. The infrastructure accommodates. The entire built fabric of the place has learned to move with the ground, and the ground, in turn, seems to be in some slow dialogue with the weight placed on it.

The vegetation roots go very deep here. Deeper than they should. The roots of the alder in the loading corridor, where someone once dug for a utility line, were found to extend eight meters down into that pale crystalline layer. What they were doing there, what they were taking from that depth, is not clear. The trees look healthy. More than healthy. They look preoccupied.

---

Sound.

The valley is mostly quiet, which is the strangest thing about it given how full it is.

The industrial works operate. Machinery turns, compresses, conveys, processes. Somewhere, something hisses with controlled steam. Somewhere, a motor runs at a constant low frequency that you feel more than hear, a vibration in the soles of boots on any hard surface. But the sound does not propagate. The density of the place — the packed vegetation, the irregular buildings, the bodies of water, the cold damp air — absorbs and scatters it. Sounds arrive muffled, directionally confused, stripped of attack. A factory that is visibly working, visibly in motion — conveyors moving, light flickering through high windows — produces, at fifty meters' distance, almost no sound. A crow calling somewhere in the alder thicket sounds very close and also very far. The canal makes a small, persistent sound where water moves over a sill, and this sound is disproportionately audible, seems to be one of the governing sounds of the place.

People work here. They must. There is evidence of people everywhere: vehicles, tools left out, a coat on a hook inside a greenhouse visible through the glass. But they are rarely seen, and when they are seen, they are quiet. This is not the enforced quiet of a library or the reverent quiet of a church. It is the working quiet of people who are concentrating — heads down, attending to something, absorbed in process. A man moving a barrow of something green and heavy along a canal path looks up briefly, nods, continues. He does not seem to find the valley strange.

---

But the turbulence is real. The turbulence is the other governing condition.

From the observation point on the ridge, over a period of an hour, the valley *changes*. Not catastrophically. Not visibly at any single moment. But if you look at the same quadrant of the valley twenty minutes apart and then look again, there is a sense that things have shifted, that the density of the place has redistributed itself. A crane that was still is now in slow motion. Steam or vapor that was rising from one location has moved to another. The canal's surface, where visible through the reed beds, shows different patterns of movement. Somewhere in the western section — the older factories, the ones where the walls are thickest with climbing plants — something appears to be being demolished and simultaneously rebuilt. Scaffolding is present; so is falling masonry; so is new brick. The process is simultaneous. Demolition and construction are not sequential here. The place is metabolizing.

This is the turbulence: not storm-state, not chaos, but a continuous slow process of modification, replacement, adjustment. The valley is always in the process of becoming what it will become. The young geology moves. The vegetation extends and restructures. The buildings settle and are modified. The industrial works change their processes, route their outputs differently, repurpose structures. It is an anthill in the sense that an anthill is never finished, never static, always in the middle of a project that has no completion date because completion is not the point. 

The cold holds it together, somehow. The cold is the constant. The cold keeps things from moving too fast. Without the cold, this metabolism might become something unmanageable. With it, the process runs at the pace of a careful hand.

---

Late in the day — the light here is low and horizontal even at noon, winter-light, the sun staying close to the hills — the valley takes on a different quality. The vapor columns from the works catch the low light and go gold, and the greenhouse glass goes orange, and the canal, where visible, reflects a darkening sky between the reed stems. The cold intensifies slightly. The sounds that were present — motor-hum, crow, water over sill — continue unchanged. A smell comes in with the evening: something vegetable and mineral together, the smell of damp earth and stone and growing things and machine oil, a complicated smell that takes some time to sort through.

The lights come on in the sheds. High windows go yellow. The greenhouse interiors, already foggy with condensation, glow green from inside, as if they contain their own weather. The crane in the western section is still moving, visible as a silhouette against the last light in the sky, its long arm sweeping slowly back and forth in an arc. Something is being moved. Something is being placed. The valley is still in the middle of becoming what it will become, and the night does not interrupt this. The night is just another condition to accommodate, and the valley accommodates.

The vegetation does not sleep. The roots continue their descent into the pale crystalline material. The ground continues its slow conversation with what is built on it. The canal moves. The river moves, underground and above it, carrying cold water that carries fine sediment, fine and pale and granular, the valley slowly and constantly processing its own material, making and remaking the substance of itself.

It is very full here. It is very cold. It is, in some way that is difficult to articulate to someone who has not stood in it, *alive* — not metaphorically, not loosely, but in some more specific and slightly unsettling sense: engaged in the processes of a living thing, metabolizing, growing, adjusting, working through something that has no name and no end.
