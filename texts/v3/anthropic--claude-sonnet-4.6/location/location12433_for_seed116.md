# The Tank

It is not large. That is the first thing to understand. Everything else follows from that.

The space is roughly the dimensions of a cathedral nave — perhaps forty meters end to end, perhaps twenty wide — but that comparison already misleads, because cathedrals open upward toward aspiration and this place opens downward toward weight. The ceiling is close. The ceiling is always close. It is a vaulted thing of riveted iron plate, sweating constantly, dark with age and mineral seep, and it sits only four meters above the waterline. The waterline is everywhere.

You are inside a tank.

---

Not a tank as in military vehicle. A tank as in *holding vessel.* As in the great sealed cisterns that were built — when? The construction is industrial in its bones: I-beams, bolted flanges, welds that have been re-welded over re-welds until the seams have become geological formations in their own right, ridges and valleys of steel-over-steel accumulated across what must be decades of maintenance performed by workers who never asked why the tank was here or what it was holding. The walls are corrugated iron, but so ancient, so many times repainted and sealed and re-sealed, that the corrugation has softened into something that reads more as *texture* than as *structure.* The paint — there are layers visible where a section of wall has delaminated near the southeast corner — goes gray, gray, red, gray, black, institutional-green, gray again. An archaeology of utility.

The floor is submerged. This is simply the condition. Water sits at roughly sixty centimeters depth across the entire flat bottom, with a little variance — perhaps five centimeters of tidal action from the recirculation pumps, which run on their own cycle, slow and tidal, moving the water in a wide counterclockwise pattern. This takes hours, one full revolution. You can tell by watching a piece of floating duckweed complete its circuit.

The water is warm. Not uncomfortably so — perhaps thirty degrees, the temperature of a shallow tropical bay left in afternoon sun. This warmth is maintained by pipe-fed heat from somewhere below, from systems that hum at a frequency too low to hear and too pervasive to ignore. Your sinuses know the frequency. Your back teeth know it.

---

The extreme elevation is not, at first, obvious. You are enclosed, after all. There is no horizon. There is no sky. And yet.

The tank is situated — perched — on something impossibly high. You understand this not by sight but by the *behavior of the structure.* There is a particular quality of vibration in the walls that speaks of exposure, of wind-load, of lateral forces that a ground-level structure would never feel. When the external wind is strong — and it must be strong, whatever is out there, whatever altitude this tank occupies — the whole space shudders, not with violence, but with the slow tectonic patience of something that has been shuddering for a very long time and has learned to shudder efficiently.

The access hatch on the north wall — a heavy circular port, dogged down with eight manual levers — is sealed. It is always sealed. Where the gasket has failed slightly at its base, not water but *cloud* sometimes pushes through. A thin curl of moisture, not quite vapor, not quite fog, something in between, that drifts inward on certain pressure cycles and dissipates within a few meters. The cloud comes from outside. Outside is not accessible. Outside is up there, where the elevation lives, where whatever world supports this structure exists at the top of something that has stood long enough to become permanent.

The erosion is out there too. Whatever landform or construct originally supported this tank is very old now. Ancient in the sense that implies *finality.* Not decay exactly, but resolution — the way a mountain range becomes, eventually, just a slightly raised plain, all drama gone, all contingency resolved. The tank has been here since the interesting geological period ended. It has watched the erosion complete itself through its sealed walls and did not comment.

---

Now: the vegetation.

This is the strangeness, and you had to understand the container before you could understand what fills it.

The plants grow on platforms. There are perhaps fifteen of them — platforms of varying sizes, the largest maybe three meters by two, the smallest barely a stepping-stone — floated on the water's surface or mounted on legs of rusted rebar driven into whatever lies beneath. Each platform is a different substrate: some are old wooden pallets gone black with moisture and age, some are wire mesh over which soil has been packed and sealed against the water, some are purely hydroponic, trailing roots into the warm dark below.

And on these platforms: extraordinary growth.

Given the constraints — the enclosed space, the artificial light banks (four of them, industrial fluorescent arrays, two currently active and cycling through dawn-and-dusk sequences on what seems to be a twenty-six-hour timer, slightly wrong, slightly drifting), the warm humid air, the recirculated water — the plants have interpreted their situation as *tropical.* Not metaphorically. Literally. The duckweed on the water surface has achieved a thickness that is almost architectural. Around the platform edges, things that might have been ordinary houseplants once — pothos, philodendrons, something broad-leafed and nameless — have grown to the proportions of undergrowth, their leaves the size of dinner plates, darker green than green should be, because the light is slightly wrong and the plants have widened every chloroplast in response.

Near the north wall, in the quadrant below the access hatch where the cloud-seep provides slightly different moisture conditions, there is a growth that defies easy identification. It has the structure of a fern but has grown vertical to nearly two meters, its fronds curving back toward the light in a way that looks intentional. It sways without cause. It sways continuously. Perhaps the pump-current produces air movement at this scale; perhaps there is something in the temperature differential. But the swaying gives it an animal quality, a slow breathing, and you find yourself checking whether it responds to your presence, and you are not entirely sure it doesn't.

Between the platforms, the open water. Sparse. There is a phrase: *balanced with emptiness.* The space between the platforms is genuinely empty — just the warm dark water reflecting the industrial lights, multiplying them, making the ceiling glow from below as if the light comes from everywhere at once. The platforms are islands in a genuine sense. You could wade between them — sixty centimeters of depth, warm water, soft sediment that has built up on the tank floor over years of dead plant matter and deposition — but you would feel the crossing as a passage.

---

The fauna is in the water and in the air.

In the water: something has been here long enough to establish a food chain. At the bottom: organisms too small to see but evident in the slightly turbid quality of the water, the faint green haze of suspended life. Mid-column: small fish, perhaps eight centimeters long, pale and slightly translucent in the way of things that have lived many generations without full-spectrum light. They school in groups of twenty or thirty, drifting through the current in loose formation, and when the lights cycle to their dawn phase — brightening over forty minutes from nearly nothing — the fish rise to within a few centimeters of the surface, oriented upward, waiting. For what? For the old world, maybe. For open water. They seem patient about it.

At the surface: a community of insects that should not exist at this altitude, this isolation. Water striders work the margins of the platforms. Something dragonfly-adjacent, with slightly too-long legs and wings that beat just slightly too slowly for their apparent size, patrols the central open water. It has been many generations since anything like it was outside. Its navigational instincts have reorganized around the tank's geometry. It knows every corner. It has memorized the recirculation pattern.

In the air, above the platforms, in the warm thermal layer that collects near the ceiling before being redistributed by the circulation fans: small birds.

Two of them. The same species, or closely related. Dark, finch-sized, with beaks that have adapted — or seem adapted, though this is probably imagination applied to chance — toward the specific insects available in this specific space. They roost in the fronds of the large fern. They have been here long enough that they seem completely unbothered by the industrial sounds, by the lights cycling, by the faint shuddering when the wind hits the walls at altitude. They are *native.* This is their land.

---

The sounds:

The pumps, deep and slow. A pressure-cycle in the pipes that serves as the space's heartbeat — once every four seconds, a soft hydraulic knock, not alarming, just present. The fluorescent lights hum. The metal breathes: expansion and contraction as the thermal systems cycle, producing random sequences of ticks and pops from the walls and ceiling that sound, when you are tired, like footsteps. The birds call to each other: brief, complex exchanges, three or four notes, and then silence, and then an answer. The fish are silent but the dragonfly-adjacent thing produces a sound — not quite a buzz, more of a low-frequency *presence* in the air, felt in the chest rather than heard. Water against the platform edges: lapping, patient, rhythmic, slightly out of phase with itself as the recirculation wave reflects and interferes.

It is loud here in the sense that there are *many* sounds, all the time, overlapping without resolution. But none of them are alarming. The loudness is the loudness of *occupied space,* of a place with too much happening to be quiet. It is the kind of loud that produces, after time, a feeling of being held.

---

The rules of this world:

You cannot leave through the hatch.
The light is slightly wrong, always.
The warmth is maintained by systems you cannot inspect.
The plants have exceeded their instructions.
The fish remember an ocean they have never seen.
Nothing here is natural but everything here is *adapted,* which is a different thing from natural and may in some circumstances be better.
The erosion is complete outside. Whatever drama occurred out there, it is finished. The tank holds the remainder.
Nothing grows old inside at quite the normal rate.
Time is marked by the pump-beat, the light-cycle, the slow revolution of the water. These do not agree with each other. You can feel the disagreement as a slight vertigo if you pay attention to it. Most residents have learned not to pay attention to it.
The birds have a song for the cloud that comes through the hatch seal. They only sing it when the cloud comes in, and they stop before it dissipates. They know something about the outside that they cannot tell you in a language you speak.

---

There is a platform near the center, larger than the others, with an old metal chair bolted to it. The chair faces nowhere in particular — or everywhere, which is the same thing in a circular space. The chair's legs are orange with rust and its seat is a wooden plank, swollen with moisture, soft as cork. Someone sat here, once. Someone who installed the fluorescent lights and stocked the fish and brought the first plant cuttings in a bag from somewhere else and thought: *this can work.* Someone with an engineer's optimism, which is a specific kind of optimism that believes function creates conditions for life, that a system maintained well enough becomes a place.

They were right. They were right in a way they probably didn't expect.

The chair is empty. You can sit in it. The water moves around your platform in its slow counterclockwise drift, taking the duckweed with it, the fish following the current's edge, the birds watching from the fern with the incurious attention of things that have no predators and no pressing concerns. The lights are at mid-cycle. The pipes knock their four-second heartbeat. Something outside makes the wall shudder, just slightly, just once — a gust against the iron at the top of the old eroded world — and then it's quiet again, quiet in the way this place defines quiet, which is to say: full.
