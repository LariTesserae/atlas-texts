# The Cistern District

---

You are below the city, but the city has not stopped.

This is the first thing to understand. The infrastructure continues downward — not as foundation, not as basement or undercroft — but as intention. The city planned for this depth. The city *wanted* this depth, and so here the city is, going about its business in the dimness below the street-level, busy with processes that have no particular interest in being observed.

---

The chamber you have entered is not large by the standards of what's above. Perhaps forty meters across, perhaps a little less — the far walls are difficult to judge in the low amber light that seeps from conduit-channels cut into the ceiling. Those channels carry something luminous and slow, not quite liquid, not quite light alone, moving with the deliberate pace of sap through a cold tree. It's enough to see by. It's not enough to feel comfortable. The ceiling arches overhead at maybe twelve meters at the apex — sufficient height to make the space feel open, insufficient height to forget that you are underneath something. The rock of the arch is new. Sharp where it was carved. The cut marks are still crisp, the exposed stone faces pale as split bone, not yet darkened by time or moisture. This is a recently-opened place. The city has only just decided to be here.

The walls weep.

That's the word that surfaces, the right word: weep. They don't leak. They don't drip in the way of a forgotten pipe. They *weep*, a continuous fine expression of moisture across the pale cut stone, forming no pools because the floor is designed to accept it — a lattice of shallow channels barely a centimeter wide, machined into the composite substrate with impossible precision, routing the gathered water toward collectors set into the walls at regular intervals. The collectors are translucent cylinders, and within them something processes the water in real time. What enters is groundwater, faintly mineral, carrying the chemistry of the deep substrate. What leaves the collector outlets — thin tubes that disappear into the wall before you can follow them — is unclear. Probably not water anymore. Probably something the city has a use for.

The floor between the channels is dry. Exactly dry. Suspiciously dry.

---

The sound is the loudest thing here.

Not the sound of machines — though machines are running, somewhere in the walls, behind the composite, behind the weeping stone. You can feel that more than hear it. A low-frequency hum that lives in your sternum rather than your ears, a periodicity suggesting rotation or pumping or something that has no analogue you can name. No — what you hear, what rises above that substrate frequency and fills the chamber, is the sound of the water itself. Amplified by the arch, caught and redirected by the geometry of the dome, every drop that forms and falls from the ceiling (and they fall constantly, a slow rain that never quite reaches you because the lattice channels begin at the wall-bases, because the dome's curve is engineered to direct the drips away from the center) — every drop speaks. A continuous, layered, overlapping conversation in the language of impact. The stone, being new and sharp-edged, doesn't absorb this sound the way old stone would. It returns it. The chamber is loud with water that hasn't gone anywhere.

If you stop moving and simply listen, you begin to hear differentiations. Drop into shallow channel: a short, bright note. Drop into collector cylinder: a resonant, longer tone, the cylinder singing back. Water moving through the floor-lattice: a collective hiss, directionless. Behind everything: that sternum-hum, that evidence of machinery that has not bothered to identify itself.

---

The vegetation found the moisture before the city was quite finished.

This is unusual. This is the unusual thing about this place, the quality that makes it more itself than a simple utility chamber. Along the base of the walls, where the weeping stone meets the floor channels, something has grown. Not planted. Not designed. The city's engineers — if this city still has engineers in the traditional sense, which is unclear — may not have anticipated this, or may have anticipated it precisely and said nothing. Either is plausible.

It is a low vegetation. A creeping, dense, almost mat-like plant life. Dark green, tending toward blue-green in the amber-tinted dimness. Mosses, certainly, of several kinds, forming a continuous strip along the wall-bases perhaps a handspan wide and as thick as a thumb. But also: something else. Broader-leafed than moss, lower than fern, spreading outward from the moisture gradient as far as the light will allow — which is not far, given the dimness. The plants nearest the collectors are more vigorous. The plants farthest from the wall, where the light is slightly better and the moisture slightly worse, look tentative, exploratory. Like a slow, weeks-long argument about whether this territory is claimable.

It is warm enough for them. The chamber holds warmth generated by its own processes — the machinery in the walls, the slow luminous fluid in the ceiling channels, perhaps something geological — and so the plants have not had to struggle against cold. They've had to struggle against dim. They are adapted to dim, or they are adapting, in real time, at whatever pace leaves conduct that negotiation.

There is no animal life. Not even insects, not even the thin annelid life that creeps through wet soil. Either this place is too new for them to have arrived, or something about the water-processing chemistry persuades them otherwise, or they simply cannot get here — the city's transitions between levels are not the kind of transitions that small creatures navigate easily. Whatever the reason, the plants grow in silence that is theirs alone. No pollination happening. No seed being carried. The ecosystem, if it becomes one, will have to invent itself.

---

The center of the chamber is empty.

This is significant. In a space this small, with this much infrastructure built into every surface, the center is deliberately empty. A circle of floor perhaps eight meters in diameter where the channels don't run, where the composite substrate is uninterrupted, where nothing has been placed or grown or routed. The city thought about this space and decided to leave it alone. Or the city's systems thought about it and declined to occupy it for reasons that were not explained. It sits there in the dimness like a cleared throat, like a pause, like a question asked very quietly beneath all the loud water-sound.

If you stand at the center of that empty circle, the dome sings back your footsteps from all directions at once, the water-sounds arrive at you from all points of the compass equidistantly, and the amber light from the ceiling channels makes shadows that point inward rather than outward. It is a small effect. It is not nothing.

---

The city above is doing something to the water table. This chamber is evidence of that. The weeping is not natural — or not purely natural — because the geology here is young, the stone not old enough to have its own water history. The moisture is coming from pressures introduced by the city's infrastructure: the weight of layered construction above, the thermal activities of systems that don't declare themselves, the managed hydrological cycles that the city runs as a matter of routine the way older cities ran sewage. The chamber sweats because the city has breathed on it.

The plant life knows this, somehow. The greenest, most assertive moss has positioned itself at the exact drainage points that receive the highest-pressure groundwater inflow. It found those points in the dark, in the weeks between the chamber's completion and now. How it navigated, how it measured, the city does not explain and the moss does not say.

---

There is one door. It is at the far end of the chamber, set into the curved wall, sized for a person. It is open. Beyond it: a passage, unlit, that bends out of sight, and from which the sternum-hum arrives slightly louder than it does through the walls. Whatever the machinery is, the passage leads toward it.

The door was not locked when this place was finished. 

Whether that means visitors are expected or simply that the city has stopped bothering to make distinctions of that kind is not immediately clear.

The water speaks. The amber light moves slowly through its channels. The moss grows at its secret pace. The city continues its processes below its own streets, in this small, loud, dim, wet, inhabited emptiness, getting on with things.
