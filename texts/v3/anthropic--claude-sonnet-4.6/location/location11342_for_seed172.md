# The Foundry Platform

---

It does not move. That is the first thing — the absolute stillness underneath everything else, which is not itself still at all.

The platform sits at altitude. Not mountain altitude, not the altitude of geology and time, but the altitude of construction — it was *put* here, at this height, for reasons that have either been forgotten or that exist in a register of reasoning too large to parse from the inside. Below the grating there is sky. Below the sky there is more sky. Clouds move underneath, far enough down that they look like something spilled on a floor in another room. Nothing below this structure is legible. The platform itself is the ground. There is no other ground.

And the platform does not change. This is not the stability of a mountain, which changes slowly; this is the stability of a gear that has found its final position and will never be asked to turn again. Whatever equilibrium this place reached, it reached it completely. Temperatures fixed. Pressures fixed. The machines run their cycles and the cycles are perfectly closed and nothing accumulates, nothing depletes. The engineers who designed it — if there were engineers, if *design* is the right word for something that might have accreted through pure industrial logic — built something that arrived at a permanent state and stayed there. Day after day after day of the same day.

---

The structure itself is made entirely of metals that do not quite match anything with a common name. There is something ferrous in them, something gray and blue-gray and occasionally a dark red that is not rust but resembles rust the way a painting resembles its subject. The beams are enormous, set in riveted frames that suggest a previous century's understanding of what forces looked like, of what needed to be held together. Flanges. Gussets. Bolts as wide across as a forearm, torqued to tolerances that have long since been verified and re-verified into meaninglessness. The structure is conservative in its engineering, which is to say it is overbuilt past any rational need, which is to say someone at some point was afraid.

Catwalks run between the larger installations at heights that don't obviously correspond to anything needing to be monitored from them. Grating underfoot — always grating, diamond-plate and expanded mesh and welded bar-grate, layers of it at different levels, so that when you look down you look through several transparencies of metal before you get to the sky. The grating is cold. Everything is cold. Not dramatically cold, not painfully cold, but the cold of metal that has never once in its life been touched by direct sunlight — a dry, mineral cold, a cold without moisture in it, a cold that does not numb so much as *inform* the skin precisely where it is being touched. The air is cold and thin at this altitude. Breathing requires a small additional effort that you begin to notice only when you stop noticing it.

There is no water. None. This is one of the stranger things about the platform, quietly stranger than the height or the scale. You look for water and water is simply absent as a category. No pipes with condensation on them. No pooling in the low corners of the grating. The air holds nothing — the cold is not wet cold but mineral cold, gas-cold, the kind of cold that exists in a sealed chamber where humidity was removed as a design parameter. The machines that run here run dry. The chemistry that happens here — and something chemical is clearly happening, you can tell by the quality of the light at the exhaust points, by a faint acidity at the back of the throat that is not quite a smell — is a chemistry of heat and dry pressure and probably things that do not dissolve in water because water was never part of the equation.

---

Now the sound.

You cannot think inside the sound. This is not a metaphor. Cognition of a certain type — sequential thought, reasoning that requires holding a premise while adding to it — is genuinely difficult inside the noise the platform produces. The noise is not one noise. It is a compound of noises so thoroughly interlocked that they have produced something qualitatively different from any of their components.

There are the presses — or what seem to be presses, vast machines in the central bays that cycle through compression events every several seconds, each one a concussive *thud* that is felt in the sternum before it is heard in the ears, or possibly instead of being heard, the threshold of hearing being a subtler boundary than the threshold of physical impact. There are the furnaces — the continuous, textured roar of things burning at industrial scale, a sound with harmonics in it, a sound that is almost a chord if you could step back far enough from it. There are the conveyors, the chains, the gears in their housings — the mechanical layer, intricate and overlapping, all those specific frequencies of metal moving against metal in its various modes: sliding, rolling, striking, vibrating. There are the pressure-relief events, steam or something like steam (dry, near-frictionless, too pale) releasing from valves at intervals that must be regular but do not feel regular because you cannot track them through the other noise.

And underneath all of this: a tone. Subsonic or near-subsonic. A frequency that the structure itself produces because the structure is large enough and rigid enough to act as an instrument. It is not a tone you hear. It is a tone that slightly changes how the world looks, how the edges of things feel to the eye — a mild, permanent alteration of perception that you would only notice if it stopped, and it never stops.

Hearing protection would be appropriate here. There is no hearing protection.

---

The sparse things. They are worth accounting for, because in all this noise and metal and cold, the sparse things are strange precisely because they are there at all.

There is lichen on some of the beams. This is unexplained. Lichen the color of oxidized copper, dry as paper, growing on surfaces that seem inhospitable to any biology. It does not grow densely — a patch here, a patch there, abstract and deliberate-seeming in its distribution, though it is not deliberate. It grows in the places where the light is fractionally different or the vibration is fractionally reduced. It grows very slowly. In the equilibrium of the platform it grows at exactly the rate that some part of it dies, so its extent never increases and never decreases. It has been exactly this amount of lichen for longer than anything can demonstrate.

There is a plant — one plant — that has established itself in a join between two grating sections where particulate has accumulated over years into something almost like soil. It is small. It is not doing well, but it is not dying. Its leaves are tough and narrow, a gray-green that in the light of this altitude looks almost metallic. It does not flower. There is no evidence it has ever flowered. It bends slightly toward one of the artificial light sources during operation and straightens slightly when that source is at its lower setting, but this is tropism, not behavior — it does not mean anything, even though it looks like it might.

These are the only living things.

---

The light here is adequate — more than adequate in the working bays, where overhead arrays of industrial lamps produce a harsh, flat illumination that flattens shadow and makes depth difficult to read. Everything at middle distance looks approximately the same distance. Your perception of the platform's scale, which is considerable, takes time to calibrate because the light refuses to assist with depth cues. In the outer catwalks and between the installations, the light falls off to normal bright — the sky-brightness of altitude, a sky that above the platform is very dark blue, nearly purple at the zenith, because the air is thin enough to show it. Stars would be visible, if it were night. It is not night. It is also not day in any natural sense — the sun, if there is a sun, does not figure into the light of the working bays. The cycles of the platform do not align to a sun.

---

What is being made here? Something is being made. The whole apparatus points toward production — the conveyors move material in directions, the furnaces heat something, the presses shape something, the exhaust of the chemistry goes somewhere. But the product, if there is one, is not visible on the platform. Nothing finished sits here waiting to be collected. Perhaps collection happens somewhere else, through some mechanism not present. Perhaps the product is something — heat, pressure-differential, a state of matter, a refined substance — that leaves without requiring a container. Perhaps the making and the made are the same thing, some permanent transformation that the platform effects on material passing through it, and the effected material simply becomes part of the platform in turn, and this is the closed cycle, and this is why it never changes.

This is a reasonable hypothesis.

This is an uncomfortable hypothesis.

---

The cold holds. The sound holds. The lichen measures out its slow persistence in the joins of things. Below the grating the clouds continue in their direction, at their altitude, paying no attention to the platform above them, which in turn pays no attention to anything at all. The presses cycle. The tone below the hearing persists. The one plant bends and straightens. This has been the state of the platform for a very long time.

This is the state of the platform now.

There is no particular reason to expect it will not be the state of the platform tomorrow.
