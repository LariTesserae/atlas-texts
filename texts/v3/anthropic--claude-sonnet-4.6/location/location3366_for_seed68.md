# The Cooling Yard

---

The first thing you notice is the sound — a low, continuous percussion that you feel before you hear it. Somewhere beyond the corrugated roofline, a stamping press is working. Not frantically. Methodically. One stroke every few seconds, like a slow mechanical heartbeat that has been beating since before anyone working here was born, and will continue after they've gone. *Thud. Pause. Thud.* The rhythm is patient enough that silence almost reasserts itself between strokes, and then doesn't.

You are standing in what the workers call the cooling yard, though it has no official name on any map. It is a gap between two brick structures — a narrow courtyard, maybe thirty meters long and half that wide, with one open end looking out onto the canal road. The bricks are recent enough that mortar still shows pale at the joints, not yet blackened to match the older walls flanking it on the east. This part of the works was expanded perhaps fifteen years ago. You can tell by the uniformity of the stonework, the slight awkwardness of how it meets the older structure, as if the new wing arrived and the old one merely tolerated it.

The ground is flagged with wide sandstone slabs, not quite level. Someone laid them in a hurry, or without great care, and in the low places water pools when it rains. It rained four days ago. Three of those pools remain, not because the yard is waterlogged but because the stones hold cold, and evaporation is slow here in the shade. The pools are shallow, an inch or less, clear enough to show the flagging beneath, and they are perfectly still. Midges hover just above the surface of one, hanging in a column that shifts and reforms without dispersing. In the corner where the two brick walls meet, a long smear of damp extends down from a dripping overflow pipe — not dripping now, but having dripped recently enough that the stone is dark and a thin crust of green-black algae has begun to establish itself in a line, following the moisture down. A small beginning. A colony two weeks old, perhaps, or four.

Against this — the green.

The cooling yard should not be green. No one planned it to be. But the gap between the building walls is oriented such that it receives a reliable diagonal of soft light through most of the day, and between the residual moisture from the overflow pipe, the canal fog that settles on windless mornings, and the thermal emissions from the works warming the brickwork unevenly, something like a microclimate has emerged. Ivy has come over the older east wall in a sheet — not the desperate thin ivy of neglected urban places but full, layered, three-dimensional ivy, the kind that has weight and casts its own shadow. It has been there long enough that the lowest stems have woodified into rope-thick cables running up the brick, and the upper surface of the growth is dense enough that a sparrow landed in it an hour ago and completely disappeared. You watched it go in. It has not come back out.

At the base of that wall, where the ivy doesn't reach, ferns have established in the joint between the wall footing and the flagging — not one or two but a continuous fringe of them, the small soft kind, each frond unfurling in the cool air with the slowness of something that has nowhere urgent to be. They are bright green, almost yellow-green, the color of things that grow in shade. Between the ferns, moss has packed itself into every horizontal crack and depression in the stone, thick as felt, springy underfoot at the edges of the flagging where the slabs have heaved slightly upward.

Further along the west wall — the newer one, the one with its mortar still showing — a pipe emerges from the brickwork at shoulder height. This pipe is large, maybe thirty centimeters in diameter, and it exhales. Not smoke, not steam, but warm air — a continuous slow breath from some process inside. The warmth of that exhalation, meeting the cooler yard air, produces a faint condensation plume in the mornings and evenings. Right now, in the middle of the day, it's invisible, but the wall beneath it is perpetually warm to the touch, and there, only there, a plant is growing that has no business in this climate — a sprawling thing with wide, glossy, slightly tropical-looking leaves, dark green, that arrived as a windblown seed from somewhere and found this one warm pocket and decided to stay. It is not large. It has perhaps thirty leaves. But it is definitively alive in a way that seems almost defiant, a single anomaly against the general character of the place.

The canal road at the open end of the yard carries slow traffic. A draft horse passes, pulling a low cart loaded with coke, the horse's hooves loud on the stone paving, the sound bouncing off the brick walls so that you hear it from two directions at once, slightly out of phase. The driver doesn't look in. The yard is unremarkable to anyone who works here. A gap between things.

In the upper air above the open end, telegraph wire sags in a long loop between two poles, and two starlings are sitting on it. Not singing. Watching. They produce occasional rattles and clicks to each other, quiet sounds, conversational, the kind of exchange that seems to carry information without constituting communication in any formal sense. A third starling arrives. One of the original two departs immediately, as though this were a negotiated transition.

The stamping press continues its periodic remark.

The sound environment layers itself more deliberately once you stand still. Beneath the press: a turbine hum from deep inside the larger building, consistent enough to seem like silence until you leave and notice its absence. Beneath that: water somewhere, moving water, the canal presumably, though the canal is on the far side of the road and shouldn't carry this clearly — but perhaps it is a smaller channel, a millrace, something internal to the works, running through a culvert beneath the flagstones. You can't see it. You can hear it as a low, continuous murmur, like a room where someone is speaking in the next room, without words. Then the horse again, farther now, diminishing. Then a mechanical squeal, brief, unidentified, from somewhere on the upper story, and then silence, and then the press.

The air is cool and faintly complex. There is the clean smell of the wet flagstone. There is the green smell of the ivy and the ferns, that smell of plant-matter in cool shade that is somewhere between growth and mild rot — not unpleasant, part of the same spectrum. There is an undertone of coal smoke, not sharp, not present smoke but the accumulated residue of years in the brick and stone of the walls themselves, worked deep into the material. And there is something else, fainter, industrial — a lubricant, or a cutting fluid, or a metal-warm smell from the pipe, some byproduct of whatever interior process sends the pipe's breath out into the yard. These smells do not fight each other. They have settled into a truce.

It is not quite a beautiful place. It is not quite an ugly one. It has arrived at a condition that is entirely specific to itself — the particular conjunction of industrial utility and inadvertent ecology, of a space defined by what surrounds it rather than by any intention of its own. The people who built the new wing thought about drainage and load-bearing and proximity to the rail siding. No one thought about the cooling yard as a place where anything would happen. And yet something is happening. Slowly. Patiently. The ferns are advancing a centimeter a year into the flagging. The ivy is adding thickness and coverage almost imperceptibly. The green-black algae stripe following the overflow pipe has been there for a month now and will still be there in a decade, darker, wider, more completely itself.

The place is in the early stages of becoming something. You are seeing it before it has decided what.

If you stay long enough — and it takes a while, because nothing is asking for your attention urgently — you begin to notice the birds more fully. The sparrow is still in the ivy. A pair of wrens have nested in a cavity where a brick has partially come loose from the older wall, roughly where the ivy thins, and they move in and out with an exactness that suggests this is a well-established route, known thoroughly. A wren passes within a meter of you without apparent concern. Robins are somewhere above, in the gap between the buildings where a narrow strip of sky shows — not visible, but audible, short declarative phrases repeated with modest intervals, part of the general texture of sound rather than a foreground event.

Somewhere above and behind you, in the works, men are working. You can hear them intermittently, not voices exactly, but the sounds that human activity makes — a scrape, a clatter, footsteps on a metal grate, the distant metallic knock of something being set down. These sounds arrive without context, abstract, the sonic residue of effort. Nobody will come into the yard for another hour, when the shift changes and someone cuts through it as a shortcut to the canal road. Until then, it is quiet in its particular loud way, busy in its particular sparse way, green in the specific manner of things that weren't supposed to be green.

The sandstone slabs exhale cold. The pipe exhales warm. The press marks time.
