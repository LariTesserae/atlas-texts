# The Verdigris Plateau

---

## Approach

You know you're getting close before you see anything. The rail line begins to slope upward and the engine behind you changes its note — a lower, more effortful groan, the kind of sound that carries meaning about gradient and altitude and what it costs to rise. Outside the window the ground is already giving way. Not collapsing. *Giving way* — the way a very old thing steps back to let something else take precedence. The scrub at the lowland margins thins, the soil vanishes under a creeping mat of something pale and dense, and then you're in the moss-country proper and the train is a loud intruder in a place that has not needed loud things for a very long time.

The plateau is not announced. It arrives around you gradually, the way a mood does.

---

## The Ground

There is no ground, not as such. There is *substrate* — a living accumulation of moss and lichen and sedge and something that has no common name, a low fibrous organism that predates most of the continent's other plants by an order of magnitude geologists find uncomfortable to state plainly. It is pale green shading to blue-grey at the edges of wet patches, almost silver in the diffuse midday light. Underfoot — if you are foolish enough to be on foot here — it compresses like memory foam, like wet bread, like something that would prefer you to reconsider. Four inches of sponge. Eight inches. In the old depressions it goes down further than anyone has seriously tried to measure, because measuring implies a bottom and the substrate has decided not to have one in the ordinary sense.

The plateau's surface reads as flat from a distance. From within, it reveals itself as rippled, corrugated by erosion so ancient it has softened back toward smoothness again. Billions of years of freeze and thaw and wind and rain have worked the underlying rock down to its preferred configuration: broad, low rolls like frozen swells, like the memory of waves, each crest maybe three meters above each trough, stretched over hundreds of meters of horizontal distance. The living mat follows all of it perfectly. There is nowhere the substrate is absent. There is nowhere you could put your hand down and touch mineral material. The rock has not been exposed here since before there were creatures complex enough to see it.

Water moves through all of this rather than over it. Rain falls — briefly, coldly, more like a decision than a storm — and the plateau absorbs it. It reappears in the troughs as seeps, as shallow channels dark with dissolved organic material, as small pools that hold the sky like discarded mirrors. In the dry intervals, the moss holds the memory of water, its cells compressed with stored moisture, slightly warmer than the air. You can feel it radiating against the back of your hand if you crouch. The plateau breathes moisture without releasing it. It keeps what it catches.

---

## The City

But the city.

The city is the reason the rail line exists at all, the reason anyone has mapped this altitude, the reason the plateau is interrupted by the one thing that interrupts it.

It does not look like it was built so much as *declared* — a dense arrangement of stone structures rising from the living substrate in a roughly radial pattern, maybe four kilometers across at its widest, with the industrial works at its eastern margin and the residential districts stepping westward in layers of increasing age and decreasing height. The buildings are made from the plateau's own rock, quarried from below the substrate at enormous effort, and they have the same quality as everything else up here: the quality of having been here long enough to have opinions about it.

The stone is a blue-grey basalt, cut and stacked in the industrial era's preferred style — thick walls, narrow windows, arches that take their weight seriously. No ornamentation that isn't also structural. The buildings are dark and dense and spaced widely relative to most cities their age, because the plateau's cold and the difficulty of resupply discouraged the usual tendencies toward crowding. There is, as a result, much space between structures. Between the buildings: the substrate, uninterrupted, the pale living mat pushing right up to foundations and in some places quietly beginning to digest them.

The walls are colonized. This is the right word. Every vertical surface below three meters has been taken by moss, lichen, a creeping grey-green fern that colonizes mortar joints and works its way upward toward window ledges. Above three meters, the stone is still dark and bare. Below three meters, the city is being slowly, patiently incorporated into the plateau's project. The transition line — the horizontal boundary between bare stone and living colonization — is absolutely consistent across every structure, a demarcation so precise it suggests a rule, an agreement, an understanding that the city has not been consulted on.

Rooftops have gone entirely. The city's upper surfaces have become the plateau's upper surface. Roof gardens unintended and undesired, deep with sedge and moss and the low sprawling plants that have no common names, all of it spilling over gutters in long green curtains. The buildings wear it. Some of the older structures in the western quarter have rooflines that have softened, compressed, lost their angularity to the weight and moisture of accumulated growth. From above — from wherever above would be — you could not distinguish the city's footprint from the plateau's surface. The plateau has absorbed the city's top. It is working on the bottom.

## The Industrial Works

The eastern works are loud.

This is the primary fact about them. A stamping mill, two smelters, a reduction plant for the minerals extracted from below the substrate via shafts that descend through the living mat, through the accumulated millennia of growth, into the ancient rock beneath. The extraction happens in the dark. The processing happens in the noise.

The stamping mill operates on a cycle of roughly two seconds — a deep, felt impact, a pause, another impact — and this rhythm propagates across the entire plateau in the cold air, arriving at distances far enough away that the mill itself is invisible, that you could mistake it for a geological event, for the plateau settling, for the heartbeat of something vast. Combined with the smelters' continuous roar, with the hissing of steam pipes and the clatter of ore carts on iron rails, the eastern quarter generates a sound environment that is not unpleasant so much as *assertive*. It insists on itself. It makes claims about industry and effort and the human scale of things.

But it does not carry past a certain distance. The plateau absorbs it — the moss, the sponge, the accumulated biomass, all of it acting as a medium that accepts vibration and disperses it, so the sound of the works diminishes faster than physics would normally suggest. By the time you reach the western residential district, you hear the stamping as something felt more than heard, a pulse in the substrate, in the moss, in the compressed water-heavy organism beneath your feet. The plateau conducts it as sensation rather than sound.

The chimneys of the works are the tallest structures on the plateau, and they release their smoke into the cold air with an enthusiasm that strikes visitors as slightly desperate, as if the works understand they are fighting for dominance in a place that does not share their values. The smoke disperses quickly. The wind up here is not dramatic — this is not an alpine gale — but it is consistent, patient, a westerly of moderate conviction that removes the smoke efficiently and reminds you that the air has its own agenda.

## Light and Weather

The sky over the plateau is almost always in motion. This is a feature of high-altitude systems and the particular humidity of the substrate beneath: clouds form, move, thin, consolidate, make decisions. But the light they produce is rarely dramatic. It is instead *comprehensive*, a diffuse grey-white illumination that reaches into shadows without eliminating them, that makes the green of the substrate glow without direct sun, that renders colors with extreme fidelity and no flattery. The moss is exactly the color it is. The stone is exactly the color it is. Nothing is enhanced.

Rain happens, as noted, as a decision. Clouds converge. The temperature drops a further two degrees. A cold, fine precipitation falls for between twelve and forty minutes, soaks immediately into the moss, and then stops. The clouds do not dramatically part. The light remains what it was. The only evidence afterward is that the substrate is slightly more luminous, slightly more itself, and that the pools in the troughs are fuller, darker, holding more sky than before.

Frost is frequent in the shoulder seasons. It renders every surface in white and makes the plateau into something almost too beautiful to look at directly — every strand of moss individually highlighted, every window on every building traced in white crystal, the stamping mill's vibration sending fractures across the rime on the ore cart rails in patterns that suggest language without offering meaning.

## Fauna and Other Life

Rare but present, as a condition that seems chosen rather than accidental. A single large bird — call it what you will, something in the corvid family, black and intelligent, operating alone — passes over the works in the morning, regularly, as if checking inventory. In the western district, where the buildings are oldest and the substrate has made the most progress on the walls, something small and grey-brown moves in the mortar gaps, quick enough to be always just recently present. Nothing else announces itself. There may be more. The plateau does not encourage announcement.

The substrate is ecology enough for what it is. The pale organism beneath the moss hosts its own complexity — microbial communities, fungal networks, organisms so small and so numerous that the plateau's living weight is distributed mostly in the parts you cannot see. What you see is the surface expression of something that goes very deep and has been going very deep for a very long time. The plants here do not compete so much as collaborate in the maintenance of a single enormous project: continuation. The project has been running since before the rock was this shape and will continue after the city finishes being incorporated.

## The Strange

Which brings us, eventually, to the thing that is difficult to describe in the terms normally available for describing things.

The substrate *knows* things.

This is not mysticism. Or rather, it is the kind of mysticism that becomes available when something is old enough and interconnected enough that the words for it outpace the words against it. The plateau's moss-and-lichen-and-unnamed-organisms network spans four hundred square kilometers of living tissue with shared vascular connections. Information moves through it — slowly, by chemical gradient, by pressure differential, by the physical connectivity of root-adjacent structures no taxonomist has gotten around to naming properly — but it moves. The network has states. It has something that is probably not memory but functions like memory. It responds.

It responds to the stamping mill by thickening at the eastern edge. Not immediately. Over decades. The substrate is denser within a kilometer of the works than anywhere else on the plateau, as if it is bracing, as if it has assessed the vibration and decided to meet it with more of itself.

It responds to the buildings by the colonization already described. The consistency of the three-meter line is too perfect for coincidence. Something in the substrate has a preference about how high it rises, and it rises that high everywhere with the consistency of a single decision made at altitude across the entire city's perimeter simultaneously. The buildings did not agree. The buildings were not consulted.

There are places in the western quarter where the oldest buildings have begun to lean, infinitesimally, toward the center of the city. Surveyors have documented this. The movement is measurable only across decades and is attributed, officially, to substrate compression and frost heave. But the lean is consistent in direction, and it is toward the city's center, and it is in all the oldest buildings, and the compression explanation does not quite hold up when you look at the numbers.

The substrate is doing something to the city's foundations that is not erosion and is not quite growth and has no other name yet because no one has stayed long enough to watch the full arc of whatever it is.

People who live in the western quarter, in the oldest buildings, sleep differently than people anywhere else. They report dreams of slow process. Of being incorporated. Of finding this agreeable. Workers who spend time in the substrate-adjacent spaces — the greener lanes between widely-spaced buildings, the rooftop gardens, the edges of the plateau beyond the city's perimeter — come back quieter than they left, and not in the way that fresh air quiets a person. In a deeper way. As if they have been in the presence of something whose patience is so vast that it recalibrates yours just by proximity.

The stamping mill, every morning, continues its assertion. Two seconds. A pause. Two seconds. A pause.

The plateau absorbs each impact and continues its project.

## To Stand There

You stand at the western edge of the city, where the last building — a three-story residence with windows boarded against a winter that ended fifteen years ago — meets the open plateau beyond. The city's noise arrives at you from the east, felt more than heard. The substrate continues past the building's foundation in an unbroken sweep toward the plateau's far margin, three kilometers of pale green-grey living material, low rolls of ancient topography, the occasional dark pool. The sky is in motion. The temperature is what it is: enough to keep you aware that you have a body and the body has preferences.

Behind you, the city carries on its loud, dark, slowly-colonized life.

Before you, the plateau extends to the horizon in the direction of something that has no word for urgency.

You are, here, very precisely between two systems — one that arrived recently and is making claims, one that arrived first and is making progress. The boundary is your feet, which stand on the substrate, which is already colder than you expected, which gives a little, which holds.

The moss is three meters away from your nearest foundation wall.

For now.
