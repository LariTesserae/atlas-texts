# The Canopy Steppe

---

Begin with what you notice first: the sound.

It arrives before anything visual resolves — a wall of biological noise so complete it functions less like sound and more like a medium, the way water functions as a medium. You are inside it. Millions of insects producing millions of tones in overlapping registers that cancel and reinforce one another until the result is something like a sustained chord struck on an instrument with no name, the fundamental note somewhere in the chest, the harmonics reaching up past hearing. Layered through this: birds — not individual birds, not a flock, but bird-sound as atmospheric phenomenon, as weather system, thousands of individuals whose calls have been reverberating off these same surfaces for so long that the landscape itself has begun to reflect and redirect them, the sound bouncing between enormous trunks and returning changed. And then the low, periodic thump of something large moving through undergrowth somewhere below. And below that again: the long, slow creak of wood expanding in heat.

The heat is the next thing. Not the wet suffocating heat of a lowland jungle — this is something drier at the edges, a heat with some severity in it, a heat that evaporates sweat quickly enough that you feel the coolness of evaporation even as your skin tightens. You are at altitude. The air is thinner here, just perceptibly so, enough that your breathing has some work in it, enough that the light is different — not brighter exactly, but less filtered, the ultraviolet arriving more directly, giving edges a certain crispness even in the diffuse shade beneath the canopy.

---

Look up. Look up before anything else.

The canopy is not a ceiling. It is a sky. The trees — and calling them trees feels reductive, feels like the wrong word for things that have been growing for long enough that they have passed out of the category of individual organisms and into something more like landforms — these structures rise so high that their uppermost branches disappear into their own weather. Not metaphorically. There is cloud up there, condensate forming in the cool shadow that the canopy itself casts on its own upper surface. Seen from below, the canopy moves in a slow, breathing rhythm, enormous masses of vegetation shifting in high winds you cannot feel from where you stand, a delayed, monumental sway like something between tidal and geological. Light passes through it in shafts and dapples and complex patterns that shift over minutes as the canopy breathes, pooling in one place, withdrawing from another, the forest floor alive with slow-moving illumination.

These are not trees in any familiar relationship to one another. The trunks — where they still function as trunks, where they haven't merged or interlocked or sent down so many aerial roots that the distinction between trunk and root and branch has become philosophical — the trunks are spaced widely. This is the surprising thing. Given the overwhelming mass of vegetation above, you expect claustrophobia at ground level, expect to push through wall-to-wall green. But the scale defeats that. The trunks are far apart. You could walk between them in long, clear strides. A cathedral built at continental scale, the columns spaced for giants. Between them: space. Real space. The sparse density of the ground floor is not absence of life but a consequence of geometry — the canopy is so complete, so high, so total in its light-capture, that almost nothing smaller can establish itself at the base. Almost nothing.

---

The ground itself is plant.

Not covered in plants — is plant. Generations of root systems have compressed and fossilized and been grown over and grown through until the soil layer, to whatever depth the soil layer extends, is more root than mineral, more cellulose than silica. When you press your heel down, the ground yields fractionally, a slight biological give, the way a firm mushroom gives. The surface is a mat of low-growing things: creeping groundcovers with leaves the size of thumbnails, succulent-adjacent plants that store what water they can in small, dense leaves, mosses in the shadowed pockets near the trunks where moisture accumulates. But these are sparse, scattered across the root-ground like organisms in a desert — and in a sense this is a desert, the light-desert cast by the canopy above, where rain reaches the ground only in reduced, filtered amounts and only where the canopy happens to part.

Because water is rare down here. The canopy intercepts nearly everything. Rain — when it comes, and it comes, intermittently, in storms that last an afternoon and then withdraw — falls onto the uppermost surface hundreds of meters above and is collected and redistributed by the forest's own plumbing: down channels in bark, through hollow stems, pooling in the cups of epiphytes, running slow along branches until it finds a path downward. By the time water reaches the ground, it arrives not as rain but as drip, as seep, as the slow exhalation of a system that has learned to hoard. There are wet zones and dry zones at ground level, not related to sky geography but to the hidden architecture of the canopy above, and they are remarkably fixed — the same patch of ground has been dry for centuries, the same hollow has accumulated water for centuries, both patterns established so long ago they have shaped the organisms that live within them.

The trunks weep, at certain hours, in certain conditions. Temperature drops at night, and by early morning, condensation runs down the bark in thin silver sheets, pooling at root buttresses in amounts large enough to be collected, to be drunk from.

---

The fauna.

Everything is alive with fauna and yet you rarely see it clearly.

The density of animal life in this system has no precedent in smaller ecosystems — but it lives in vertical layers, and most of those layers are inaccessible to ground-level observation. In the canopy: a complete civilization of organisms, whole ecological webs operating at height. The sound is their sound, almost entirely their sound, arriving from above and from all directions simultaneously. You glimpse things: a shape dropping between levels, a silhouette crossing a gap in the canopy, what might be a very large primate or might be something else descending a trunk headfirst, moving with unsettling fluency.

At ground level: different. At ground level the animals are enormous and sparse, spaced like the trees, each requiring a territory measured in kilometers. What passes in the middle distance, what made that sound, what left those marks on the root-ground — these are large animals and they move deliberately, unhurriedly, with the confidence of things that have few concerns. Their scale is appropriate to the scale of the forest. Some of them are browsers on the lower epiphytes, reaching up into the buttress roots for moisture-stored vegetation. Some of them are predators, and they are also large.

Insects are the exception to sparseness, but they are canopy insects mostly, their drone arriving from above like a signal from a distant country.

And there are things — this must be named — there are things in this forest that do not fit categories. Not dramatically, not in a way that announces itself. But: a vine that withdraws, slowly, from observation. Mosses that grow in geometric shapes too regular to be random, tessellating across a patch of root-ground in patterns that suggest either remarkable mathematics or something else. The way certain trunk-spaces hold a quality of presence that isn't fauna, exactly, isn't vegetable exactly, isn't interpretable. Old forests develop their own logic, their own strange densities of historical process, and this forest is old past the point where old means anything — its age has become a feature of its physics, the way a massive object develops its own gravity. Something about long-term biological persistence, scaled to continental proportion, seems to produce properties that are not predicted by the sum of smaller-scale processes.

The feeling — walking here, if you were walking here — is not of being watched. It is of being *included in a process you cannot observe*. Of mattering to something very large in a way you do not have the instruments to measure.

---

Marks of habitation.

Here and there, rarely, something made.

A platform built into a buttress root, the wood gray and weathered to the texture of stone but still sound, still bearing the marks of tools — simple tools, cutting tools, hand-fitted joinery, lashing with cord that has gone brittle but not yet failed. The platform is empty. It has been empty a long time but not so long that it has been reclaimed, because in the light-poor ground layer, reclamation is slow.

Higher: visible from below if you know to look, which you eventually learn — shelters built at the points where major branches fork, where a person could wedge themselves securely. Structures of woven material, some kind of basket-architecture, the weaving complex and accomplished. The forests were lived in, or are lived in, by people who understood that the ground floor was not the optimal plane of habitation, who built upward into the system rather than clearing a place within it.

No metal. Everywhere, evidence of working in wood and fiber and cord. A small hearth-ring of stones at the base of a trunk — the stones brought from somewhere, because the ground here produces no stones, is all root — the stone ring ancient, the ash within it gone, replaced by a very fine, very dry dust. The fire here was occasional, minimal, kept small, kept low.

Whether the people are still here is not immediately answerable. The forest generates enough ambiguity about categories of presence and absence that this question does not resolve quickly. Something moves, far off, at altitude. A shape on a platform you didn't notice until it moved. The silence is not an answer.

---

Weather cycles.

Midday: the full heat, the canopy shifting in the high winds, the light-pattern on the ground-mat at its most complex and bright. The sound reaches a peak — temperature seems to increase the output of the canopy fauna, a billion small metabolisms running faster, louder.

Afternoon: the light shifts through orange, and sometimes there is a storm — perceptible first as a change in the canopy's movement, the trees beginning to respond to a wind event still an hour away, their crowns registering it before it arrives at ground level. Then: rain, but you don't feel the rain, you hear it, an escalation of the canopy's sound as water strikes ten thousand acres of leaf simultaneously, a sound like static, like interference, like the forest receiving a transmission. What reaches the ground is delayed, individuated into drips and seeps, a different relationship with rain than you've known.

Night: temperature falls twenty degrees, which is remarkable and fast. The condensation begins. The day-sound gives way to a different night-sound, lower frequencies predominating, the insect register shifting to something more resonant and less frantic. Things move that didn't move in daylight. The darkness under the canopy is complete — not a single gradient of sky visible from ground level, no moon, no stars. The forest produces its own faint luminescence in a few places: a decaying log emitting ghost-light, an epiphyte's nightbloom reflecting nothing (there is nothing to reflect) yet visible.

You orient by sound in the dark. Sound has direction and distance and source. You learn to read it, or you don't go anywhere.

---

The overall impression, assembled from its parts:

A place that has been practicing being itself for a long time. A place at scale where the usual ecological rules still apply but have been run for so long and at such amplitude that emergent properties have appeared — strangeness not as accident but as the natural result of biological process given enough time and space to develop its own consequences. Hot and sparse and loud and breathing, high enough that the light is clear and the air is effortful, old enough that you are not a subject here but an element, briefly, in a process too large and ongoing to have a perspective on you.

The trees hold this. The trees have always held this.
