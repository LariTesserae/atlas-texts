# The Roaring Plateau

---

Start with the sound, because the sound is everything here.

It arrives before anything else does. You hear it long before you crest the final ridge — a continuous, foundational roar that you initially mistake for wind, or for something wrong with your ears, some pressure effect of altitude. But it doesn't fluctuate the way wind does. It doesn't gust or ease. It is simply *present*, the way gravity is present, and when you finally pull yourself over the lip of stone and stand upright on the plateau, you understand: this is the permanent voice of the place. The sound is made by birds.

---

The plateau sits at extreme elevation — high enough that the horizon curves very slightly, or seems to, though perhaps that is the thin air working on your perception. The sky is a deep, pressurized blue, a shade darker than what you're used to, the kind of blue that makes you feel the atmosphere is a finite thing with an edge somewhere nearby. The sun, when it comes, is *sharp* — not warm so much as bright and precise, a surgical light that throws shadows with hard borders and gives every rock face a dual nature: blazing white on one side, deep cold purple on the other.

The plateau is not flat. That word — plateau — implies a floor, a table. This is more like the remnants of something once massive, a highland that has been worked on for so long by ice and slow fracture and the differential expansion of heating and cooling that it has settled into its own complex grammar. There are ridges and basins, cols and broad shoulders of stone, all at roughly the same extreme altitude but arranged without particular order, the way a pile of enormous gray bread loaves might be arranged if the bread had been pressed and dried and compressed over millions of years until it became granite and quartzite. Nothing here is jagged in the way of young mountains. The edges are rounded. The cliff faces — and there are many — are sheer not from freshness but from long stripping, from the removal of everything that once wasn't perfectly hard. This rock has been here long enough to forget what it was like to be anything other than this.

The weathering is complete. That is the right word — *complete* — as though the landscape finished a project and now simply maintains it. Boulders sit in positions that suggest they rolled or calved off some face centuries ago and have not moved since. Frost-shattered debris collects in the angles between larger stones in piles of consistent size and character, suggesting a long-established rate of production. The lichen — gray-green, rust-orange, almost black in sheltered crevices — has colonized every surface with the patience of something that measures time in centuries. It doesn't look like it's growing. It looks like it has always been exactly this.

---

The vegetation accepts this. It does not argue with the altitude.

What grows here grows low, and grows slowly, and grows with complete commitment to its own survival. Cushion plants — tight, rounded, dense as upholstery — press themselves into the depressions between rocks where they can collect the slight advantage of a few extra degrees of shelter. Seen from above they look like clusters of dark green moss, but when you crouch and look closely you find them to be extraordinarily intricate: hundreds of tiny stems and leaves compressed into something almost architectural, a dome-form that resists the wind by presenting no profile to it. The cushion plants live for a very long time. Some of the larger ones, perhaps thirty centimeters across, are old enough that the center has died and dried to a pale gray while new growth continues outward at the rim — a slow, vegetable ring, advancing at a few millimeters per year, having been advancing for as long as anything here can remember.

There are sedges in the wet depressions, and a few prostrate willows — not small trees, but flat mats of willow, trunks as thick as a wrist pressed horizontal against the rock and sending up tiny flexible branches no higher than your ankle. They survive by refusing to be tall. Their leaves are the same as the leaves of their lowland relatives, just miniaturized, as though scaled down by some exact proportion dictated by pressure and cold. In a very sheltered spot between two large boulders — a spot that probably collects a little extra warmth from the south-facing stone — there is a small grass. Nothing else. Just this grass, growing in a crack where soil has accumulated.

The soil itself is shallow and intermittent. Mostly the ground is rock, clean and bare, with soil only in pockets: behind boulders, in the floors of basins, in the old channels of whatever meltwater once flowed here. The meltwater doesn't flow now, or doesn't flow much. The climate is cool and dry enough at this altitude that moisture arrives and departs quickly — a brief rain that runs off the hard rock surfaces, condensation that beads on stones in the morning and evaporates by midday, the occasional small snowfall that lingers in north-facing shadows for days before sublimating directly into the thin air without ever properly melting. In a few of the basins there are small shallow pools, dark and still, reflecting the severe sky. Their edges are fringed with the sedge and a delicate plant with tiny yellow flowers that appear and disappear quickly when conditions are briefly right.

The moisture in this landscape is a rumor. You can sense it — the air has a slight cold cleanness that is different from simple dryness — but you rarely see it directly.

---

Now: the birds.

They are why the sound is what it is.

This plateau is — for reasons that may involve updrafts, or the particular combination of height and exposure and the distribution of cliff faces and protected basins — one of those places that an enormous number of birds use simultaneously. Not one species. Many species. The cliff faces host nesting colonies: thick-billed birds, crow-sized, that sit in tight rows on the narrow ledges and produce a sound that is somewhere between a bark and a scream and is repeated thousands of times a minute across dozens of ledges simultaneously. From a distance this sounds like a single roar; up close it breaks apart into individual voices but there are so many of them that the effect is still overwhelming.

The broad open shoulders of the plateau host different birds — ground-nesters, compact and well-insulated, whose calls are lower and more variable, a layered conversation that runs beneath the cliff-colony roar. There are raptors riding the thermals, turning slow circles far up in the pressurized blue — large ones, with long fingered wings, that occasionally drop at astonishing speed toward the plateau surface and then pull up and return to height, sometimes with something small in their talons. There are small flocks of finch-like birds that move across the plateau in dense, murmuring clouds, flowing around boulders and settling briefly on cushion plants before lifting again as a single thing and moving on. Their collective flight-call is a constant dry chittering that adds a high frequency layer to the overall sound.

The sound has layers. That is the thing you keep returning to, because each time you think you have parsed it — cliff colony, ground birds, raptors' wing-whistle, the finch flocks, the wind that is barely present but still audible as a low fundamental hum across the stone surfaces — you notice another layer underneath or between the ones you've identified. There is something that might be a distant waterfall, or the resonance of wind in a cave somewhere below the plateau lip. There is the grinding percussion of loose stones settling, barely audible beneath everything else, the sound of the place maintaining its own equilibrium.

It is deafening not as an emergency is deafening but as a cathedral is deafening: an overwhelming fullness that is the product of many voices combining into something that transcends any of them individually.

---

There is no one here.

There are no structures. No walls, no cairns, no paths worn into the rock. The birds nest without any relationship to human decision. The cushion plants grow where conditions allow. Whatever rule organizes this place is entirely prior to human presence and entirely indifferent to it — not hostile, not welcoming, simply operating according to its own long-established logic.

The sense that someone *might* have been here, once, in some simpler time, is present but faint. Not an absence of humanity so much as a pre-humanity, a place where the scale and intensity of the non-human world is sufficient that human presence — if it ever came — would be incidental, a brief visit noted by no one and leaving no trace.

If there were people, they would be people with hands. People who knew cold and knew stone and knew that the birds here could be caught if you were patient and understood their logic. People who would have no interest in changing the place, only in passing through it, reading it. They would carry only what their arms could hold. They would sleep in the sheltered angles between boulders where the cushion plants grow deepest. They would know the names of all the birds in a language that had very precise words for the different kinds of wind and no word for silence.

---

The light, when it arrives at full midday, is almost hallucinatory.

At this altitude there is less atmosphere above you to soften or filter the sun, and the result is a brightness that somehow feels *prior* to color — a light that hits the pale rock faces and bounces back at you with equal energy, so that you find yourself illuminated from above and below simultaneously, your shadow growing faint and strange. The rocks, which in morning light appeared mostly gray, reveal at midday that they are not gray at all: they are pink and gold and rust and pale cream and spotted with the orange lichen that in this light glows almost neon against the cold stone.

The shadows in the fissures are not dark at this hour. They are a cold luminous blue.

Clouds, when they come, move fast — faster than you expect, at this altitude, though they rarely fully cover the sun for long. Their shadows cross the plateau in a minute or two, turning the colors off and then on again. But this is not a place of changing light: even the clouds are part of an established pattern, visitors who behave the same way every time they come. The light here in the middle of the day is always this bright. The shadows in the fissures are always this blue. This is not a place in the process of becoming something; it is a place that has arrived.

---

The temperature sits in a narrow cold band.

Not freezing — not today, not usually at midday in the present season — but cool in the way that makes you keep your hands moving, cool in the way that makes the inside of your nose feel clean and slightly dry, cool in the way that makes the distant stone surfaces appear harder and more final than they would in warmth. The cold here is not an event. It is a permanent characteristic, like the rock, like the birds. In the basins where the small dark pools sit, the water is very close to freezing without quite reaching it. The pools don't freeze and don't warm. They stay at the exact temperature that this place has determined is correct for pools.

At night, you suspect, it gets properly cold — genuinely cold, the kind that finds the gaps in whatever you're wearing — but you can't verify this because there's no night right now, no darkness, just the sun moving across the hard blue sky, burning.

---

What is unusual about this place is not dramatic. It doesn't announce itself. It accretes.

It's the finch flocks, which are slightly too coordinated — which turn as a single organism not just when a raptor passes over but also for no reason, in the absence of any apparent threat, as though responding to a stimulus that isn't visible. It's the cushion plants, which are present in configurations that seem deliberately spaced, each one exactly far enough from the others to be independent, as though some sorting process has been operating for a very long time. It's the sound, which is too constant — which varies in a narrow band of intensity and never falls below a certain threshold, even in the early morning when the cliff birds should be quieter and the finch flocks should be sleeping.

It's the sense that the equilibrium here is not just physical but also, in some way that is difficult to name, *decided*. As though the plateau has been here long enough to have preferences. As though the arrangement of pools and cushion plants and bird colonies and lichen-covered boulders is not merely the result of accumulated contingency but is the expression of something — not a will exactly, and not a design exactly, but a long, patient tendency toward this particular configuration and not others.

Nothing changes here. Not because nothing could, but because everything that was going to change has already changed, and what remains is what the place wanted to keep.

The birds roar on.
