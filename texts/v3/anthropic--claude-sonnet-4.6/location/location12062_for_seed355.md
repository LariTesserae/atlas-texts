# The Drowned City at the Edge of Waking

---

## What You First Perceive

Darkness, but not the darkness of closed eyes or sealed rooms. This is a darkness that has *settled*, the way silt settles — slowly, over long duration, finding its level. There is enough light to see by, just enough, and the source of that light is unclear. The light seems to originate inside the wet surfaces rather than reflecting from any external point. Stones glow the color of old teeth. The standing water carries a dim luminescence just beneath its surface, as though the liquid itself is the source, or as though something below the liquid is the source and has been there so long it has become the liquid.

You are in a city. You understand this immediately, the way you understand the grammar of a dream — not by reading signs or counting streets, but by the accumulated pressure of organized space. The organization is enormous in scale. You cannot see the edges of it. The darkness and the low-lying mist conspire to give you only the middle distance: perhaps two hundred meters in any direction before the city folds into indistinction. Beyond that edge, the city continues. You feel this with certainty. The city is enormous and has been here for a very long time.

But the city is mostly empty.

---

## The Built World

The construction is not any single tradition, and yet it is coherent. Low structures, mostly — almost nothing rises more than three stories, and most of what stands is one or two, as though the city has always known it belongs to low elevations, has always deferred to the sky in its own way. The materials are dark, dense, and porous. Something like fired clay but older, more uncertain in its composition. Something like basalt. In places, the walls are made of a material that looks like compressed ash pressed into brick, and the bricks have absorbed so much water over so long a time that they are now indistinguishable from wet earth — structurally sound, somehow, but soft to the eye, their edges gone. The corners of buildings have rounded. The angles have become suggestions.

The streets are wide and low, depressed slightly below the surrounding structures, and they hold water. Not flooded — these are not drowned streets, not ruined ones — the streets are designed to hold water. There are channels cut into the centers of the major thoroughfares, clean-edged channels even now, and the water moves through them with purpose. Slowly, but with purpose. Somewhere in the city there is a system. There are cisterns, reservoirs, collection surfaces. The city was built to be wet. It was built in full knowledge of the heat, the humidity, the way water would push up from the ground in the summer, the way condensation would bead on every wall and window in the hot pre-dawn hours and then run, rivulets finding rivulets, gathering toward the low points.

The buildings have no windows, or rather: they have openings where windows were, but the concept of glass has not touched this place. The openings are simply openings. Some are covered with frameworks of organic material — woven something, neither reed nor bone, gone pale and stiff with age and heat — that allow air to move through while slowing the entry of insects, but there are no insects now. The frameworks still serve, or still exist, which is not the same thing.

Many of the buildings are open at the top. Flat-roofed, and the rooftops are shallow bowls. Collection surfaces. Rooftop cisterns. In the heat, in the darkness, these bowl-roofs are full. The water in them does not reflect the sky because there is no sky visible — the mist has become something thicker above, not quite cloud, not quite ceiling, a suspension — and so the water in the rooftop bowls reflects only the luminescence that comes from itself or from nowhere, a flat silver-dark that makes each roof look like a mirror placed face-down against the belly of heaven.

Infrastructure is everywhere, and it is entirely biological in its materials. Pipes are sections of something hollow — enormous reeds, perhaps, or bones of animals no longer present, or the calcified tubes of organisms that secreted their own casings. They run along the walls, supported by fixtures of the same dark brick, and some of them still carry water. You can hear the water inside them if you press your ear to the smooth-worn exterior, though pressing your ear to things is not something the place encourages. The sound of water inside the pipes is a low, slow percussion, irregular. Not flowing. Moving. There is a difference.

Some walls have collapsed and some have not. The collapsed ones did not fall dramatically. They subsided. They leaned and leaned and came down over years or decades into themselves, and the rubble is so old that it has joined the ground, has become indistinct from the street, and tufts of something grow from it — pale and succulent-looking, low, watery-stemmed, the only vegetation present in any abundance. A plant that finds its purchase in rubble that has forgotten it was a wall.

---

## The Heat

The heat is constant and has no source. This is important. In a desert, heat comes from the sun, and the sun is the logic of the heat — you understand why it is hot, you can track the heat to its origin, you can imagine relief at dusk. Here there is no sun. The sky — the non-sky, the suspension above — has been dark for what feels like a long time, and yet the heat persists. It is not the heat of radiation. It is the heat of *storage*. The city has been absorbing heat for so long that it now generates it from within. The bricks exhale warmth. The water in the channels is warm. The condensation that forms on the walls and drips, endlessly drips, is warm when it touches your skin.

The humidity is total. You are damp before you have moved ten steps. Not from sweat, exactly, but from a kind of negotiation with the atmosphere — the air and your skin reaching equilibrium, the air winning. Everything here is in the process of reaching equilibrium. The city and the water table. The buildings and the heat. The mist and the air it floats in. The whole landscape is balancing, constantly, against itself, and the balancing has been going on so long that it has become the nature of the place rather than a process happening to the place.

Sweat and atmospheric moisture are indistinguishable. Your clothing adheres. The seams of things become the seams of your skin. And yet it is not unbearable, not quite, because the darkness is doing something for you — the darkness is creating in your nervous system a persistent expectation of coolness that delays the full reckoning. You are always almost about to feel cooler. You do not.

---

## What Lives Here

There is no animal life. This is the most significant emptiness. A city requires — or suggests — inhabitants, and the city is clearly built for inhabitants, built at an inhabited scale, with doors and corridors and channels designed for something mobile and purposeful. But nothing moves except the water.

The absence of fauna does not feel like death. It does not feel like extinction, like aftermath, like a place cleared by catastrophe. It feels like a place where the concept of fauna has not yet arrived, or has been quietly suspended. Like a world where the order of operations is different: first the water, then the stone, then the city, then... something else, in a sequence that has not completed. The city is not waiting for life to return. The city is not in mourning. It simply does not contain animals right now, the way a breath does not contain a breath — not absence, just a state.

The vegetation that exists is sparse and deeply strange. Pale. Fleshy-stemmed. It grows from the cracks in walls and the old rubble and along the edges of water channels, and it is the color of things that have never experienced daylight — ivory, cream, a yellowish white, the color of carved soap. It grows low and dense in whatever small patch it inhabits, and between patches there is bare stone, bare wet earth, bare warm water, nothing. The vegetation does not visibly grow in the time you are present, but you sense that it is growing, that its rate is simply different from the one your perception is calibrated to, and that over the course of a week in this place you would see it transform entirely, fill a street corner, subside, relocate.

Some of the plants weep. A clear fluid collects at the tip of each leaf — a slow drip, rhythmic enough to be mistaken for sound design. It falls into the channels. Whether this fluid is water the plant has drawn from the ground and released, or whether it is something the plant produces, is unclear. The distinction may not hold here.

---

## The Water, More Carefully

The water in the channels is moving toward something. You follow one channel for a long time — long enough that the scale of the city becomes clear, that you walk for what might be forty minutes and the channel is still the same channel, the city is still the city, there is no edge approaching — and the channel does not widen or narrow. It carries the same slow dark current, warm, luminescent, carrying nothing in suspension. Perfectly clear. You can see the cut stone of the channel bed, and you notice that the stone has been worn smooth by water over an enormous period of time, worn into gentle concavities, and there are no loose stones, no sediment, nothing at the bottom but the warm clear water and the faint light it carries.

The water emerges from the walls. In many places, pipes or simple openings in the masonry let water through, a constant trickle, and this has been happening long enough that the stone around the opening is stained with mineral deposits — white calcification, occasional rust-orange, a greenish-black in the wetter spots — building up and building up into formations that have become architectural in their own right. Growths from the walls that look like they were planned. Like someone designed stone drips, stone tears, to ornament the city and then left before explaining the joke.

In low depressions where the channels widen or streets meet, there are pools. The pools are still except for the channels feeding into them and the slow apparent outflow through grates of the same organic tube-material. Staring into the pools for a long time — which this place seems to encourage, seems in fact to gently require — you begin to see depth that should not be there. A pool half a meter wide and apparently shallow reveals, with patience, a depth that recedes past where light should reach. The luminescence in the water is coming from below, from below where the pool should have a bottom, from a source that is either very bright and very distant or very dim and very close, and there is no way to determine which.

Things seem to pass through the deep water of the pools. Not swim. Not float. *Pass through*, as through a medium, at angles that do not correspond to the horizontal surface of the water. A sense of transit. Of the pools being not a terminus but a passage, something moving from elsewhere to elsewhere using the water as a hallway.

---

## Sound

Quiet. The quiet of a library, of an abandoned hospital, of a landscape that has had sound removed rather than never had it. You become aware of sounds only when you stop moving, and when you stop, the layers appear.

First: your own internal sounds. The breathing. The blood. The heat in your ears. These sounds are too loud, briefly.

Then: water. The drip from the weeping plants. The slow movement in the pipes, that irregular percussion — not a rhythm, but almost, never quite resolving into a pattern you can predict or nod to. The channel water, which makes almost no sound at its current pace, but *almost* no sound and no sound are different, and in this quiet you hear the almost.

Then: the city itself, breathing. Expansion and contraction of the warm stone. A sound below sound, felt more than heard, a deep slow pulse that has no frequency you can name. Not heartbeat. Not seismic. Something between. The city is doing something constant and slow that produces this near-sound, and you become convinced — not frightened, exactly, but changed — that the city is not merely a collection of built things but a thing in its own right, a thing with process, a thing that is currently engaged in being, in the hot dark and the slow water.

There is no wind. No insect. No bird. No footstep but yours. The absence of these sounds is the loudest thing here, a silence that presses in its specific shape — the shape of an evacuated city, a form defined by what left it.

---

## What Is Breaking

The weirdness does not announce itself. It seeps. It arrives the way the water arrives — from below, through porous material, driven by pressure that has been building for a very long time.

Start with simple things: the shadows in this city fall in the wrong directions. The diffuse glow has no clear source, so shadows should be soft, non-directional, should radiate faintly from the base of each object. Instead, shadows are crisp and fall convincingly, as though from a strong source, but they disagree with each other — the shadow of a building falls eastward, and the shadow of a wall nearby falls southward, and the shadow of the weeping plant falls upward, rising from the plant's crown toward the invisible suspension above. The shadows are correct in themselves. They simply do not share a world.

Then: distance. Walking in this city does not reliably close distance. You see an intersection ahead, a place where four channels meet in a wide plaza with a central pool, and you walk toward it, and you arrive at it, but you are not certain you arrived in the expected number of steps. You are not certain the intersection was the one you saw. The plaza looks right. But there is a quality to its rightness — a slight over-correctness, a setting that is too precisely what an intersection should be — that suggests it may have been generated in response to your expectation rather than encountered as a pre-existing feature.

Streets double back without turning. You have walked in what you believe to be a straight line and arrived at a wall you are fairly certain you stood beside twenty minutes ago, but from the other side, and the channel is now flowing in the opposite direction, and this is either impossible or evidence that you have been wrong about something more fundamental than direction.

The buildings are changing. Slowly — the rate is beneath perception, and when you look directly at a structure it appears entirely static — but when you look away and return, the proportions are different. Not dramatically. A doorway that appeared chest-height is now slightly too tall. A set of steps has gained a step that was not there, or lost one. The rubble in a particular corner has redistributed. Nothing violent. The city is adjusting. The city is in the process of becoming, and the process is ongoing, and your presence is a variable it is accounting for, and it is incorporating you into its calculations in ways that you do not have language to resist or ratify.

Time is operating strangely. This is not a metaphor. You have been here — feel that you have been here — both for a few minutes and for several years. Both simultaneously. You are genuinely uncertain whether you have been in this city since before memory, have grown up in these dark warm streets, know these channels the way a childhood home is known, or whether you arrived eleven minutes ago from a world that had sun in it. Both feel equally supported by the evidence. Both feel accurate. The uncertainty is not distressing. This is perhaps the most distressing thing about it.

## The Rules of This World

*The city administers itself.* There are no inhabitants, but there is maintenance. The channels run. The water moves. The pipes, impossibly, have not clogged. Someone or something has continued to keep the city in its functional state, or the city continues to keep itself functional, which is a different kind of someone or something.

*Water here is not passive.* Elsewhere, water does what physics requires — it seeks the lowest point, it follows the gradient, it neither adds nor withholds itself. Here, water is more like an organ. More like a system with intent. The channels do not simply follow the lowest path; sometimes they rise, and the water rises with them, and this is not explained. The pools have depth that is not geometric. The water moves through solids, very slowly, and emerges from surfaces that have no internal space. Water here is doing something for the city, performing some function, and the city is doing something for the water in return.

*Darkness here is not the absence of light.* It is a substance. The darkness has texture, has depth, has the quality of something old and self-sustaining. In any ordinary darkness, your eyes adjust and find some photons to work with. Here your eyes adjust and find the darkness is equally dark wherever you turn, equally complete, equally present, but you can see. You can see because the darkness is showing you things rather than because light is reaching you. What the darkness chooses to show and what it elects to withhold follows rules you do not have access to.

*The city does not intend harm.* This is important. The wrongness here is not malevolent. The city that adjusts itself, the water with its impossible depths, the shadows with their divergent angles — these are not traps, not expressions of hostility, not attempts to consume or deceive. They are simply a city that has been operating long enough, in the dark and the wet heat, that it has developed its own physics, its own logic, its own way of organizing space and time that is internally consistent but foreign to the logic you arrived with. You are the anomaly here. The city is being patient with you. The city is making accommodations.

*There is no direction out.* This is not a threat. It is a description. The city extends in all directions and has no edge that can be approached by walking. If there is an edge, it is arrived at by some other means — by understanding something, perhaps, or by the city deciding to show it to you, or by a transformation in the quality of your own attention. Walking will not accomplish it. Walking in this city is for experiencing the city, not for leaving it.

---

## A Final Observation

There is a place — you find it, or it finds you, or the distinction is among those that do not hold here — where three channels converge into a single broad pool, and the pool is warmer than the surrounding air, and the luminescence in it is stronger, strong enough to illuminate the space above it faintly, to show the low ceiling of mist the city makes of its own breath.

At the pool's center, the water moves. Not flows — rotates, very slowly, a rotation you detect only after watching for some minutes. A slow turn. And the depth at the center of the turn is greatest, and the light from below is brightest there, and there is the sense — not the sight, not the sound, but the sense — of enormous space below the pool, of the pool being a skylight viewed from above, of something vast and dark below looking up through this warm bright water at the city that floats on its surface like a raft, like a dream, like a record of something that wanted very much to be organized and permanent in a world that runs on other principles.

The city is a margin note in the wet dark. A notation. Someone or something made order here, made streets and channels and collection-roofs and organic pipes, made a system for living in heat and water, and the system endures, and the system runs, and the system is the only evidence of what made it, and even the system is slowly becoming the medium it was built to manage — becoming water, becoming dark, becoming the wet warm place itself.

You are reading the notation. You do not know the text it annotates. You are not sure the text still exists. You are not sure it matters.

The pool turns.

The city breathes.

Somewhere in a pipe, water moves one increment closer to wherever it has been traveling for a very long time.
