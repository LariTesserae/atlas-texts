# The Canopy Above the World

---

Begin with the elevation. You are higher than mountains are supposed to permit, higher than the word *summit* was meant to reach. The air here — if you can call it that — is mostly water. Not mist, not cloud, not merely humidity: the atmosphere at this altitude has passed through some threshold and become something closer to a liquid medium. You breathe it the way a fish breathes, with effort, with a kind of constant negotiation. Rain is not weather here. Rain is the condition. It falls upward along the cliffs in curtains pushed by vertical winds, then falls again, and again — the water has no particular preference for direction at this elevation, only for being present, only for filling every possible space between things. The air is a body of water through which light no longer travels.

And there is almost no light.

What exists is not darkness the way a closed room is dark, not the punctuated dark of a night with stars. This is the dark of depth — oceanic dark, the dark that accumulates in layers until it has weight and texture. You are, in some sense, at the bottom of something, even though you are at the highest point in the world. The two facts coexist without contradiction. When enough water fills the sky, when the clouds stack themselves into ranges and the rain has rained for long enough that the centuries have forgotten a time before it, the summit of the world becomes its own abyss.

---

Now: the structure you are standing on, or in, or *of*.

There is no rock here. Or rather, there has not been rock here for so long that the absence of rock has become the permanent truth of the place. Erosion did its work — did it with the patience of something that has no concept of hurrying — and what remains after the grinding-down, the dissolution, the slow hydraulic argument between stone and water over millions of years of rain exactly like this rain, is not rubble, not sediment, not soil in any recognizable sense. What remains is what stayed when everything else left.

The plant life stayed.

It is not accurate to say the vegetation grows on a substrate. The vegetation *is* the substrate. This is the distinction that matters most, the one that takes time to settle into understanding: there is no surface beneath the plants, no hidden ground they are rooted into, no foundation they borrowed from geology. Over the immense span of this place's life, the plants solved the problem of erosion by becoming the answer to it. Root systems interpenetrated and calcified and fused and themselves became colonized by new growth until the entire mass — cliff face, floor, ceiling where there are overhangs, the bodies of dead trees converted immediately into new growth before they could fall, the whole architecture of the elevated world — is continuous living tissue. You do not stand on earth. You stand on the accumulated cellular memory of every generation of growth that ever happened here, compressed beneath you into something dense enough to hold your weight, though barely, though with a give to it that reminds you constantly that you are a guest on a living body.

But sparse. This must be said: it is sparse.

This surprises visitors, when visitors come — which they do not. But if they did, it would surprise them. Given the water, given the unbroken living fabric of the place, you would expect density, tangle, a green wall impenetrable to the eye. What you find instead is the arrangement of very old things that have finished competing. The great trunk-pillars of the canopy trees rise at intervals the way columns rise in a cathedral — deliberate-seeming distances, each one claiming its radius of space, the space between them genuinely open, genuinely airy in the way that only very tall empty volumes can be airy. These are not young forests jostling for light. Light is irrelevant here. The trees long ago gave up the evolutionary argument about sunlight and solved their energy differently, through the water, through the deep chemical relationship between root-network and water-column, through processes that predate the strategies of ordinary forests. They are very tall and very far apart and very old, and they have not changed their arrangement in longer than the word *age* accommodates.

The understory — if the space between the great pillar-trunks counts as understory — is similarly open. Broad flat plates of growth extend horizontally from the trunk bases at intervals, each plate a different species or a different expression of the same species, though the distinction may not be meaningful. The plates are meters across and a few centimeters thick, perfectly level, offering surfaces. Between the plates: open dark water-air. The vertical dimension of this place is astonishing and mostly empty.

---

The sound.

Loud is not a sufficient word, but it is the accurate word. The rain on living tissue — on the plate-structures, on the arched buttresses of the pillar-trunks, on the upper canopy that you cannot see but can hear as a separate register of impact far above — produces a continuous sound that occupies every frequency the ear can find. It is not white noise, though it has the property of white noise in that it masks everything beneath it. It has texture. The different surfaces — thin plates, thick trunk-bark, the hollow spaces inside the largest trunks where rain enters through cracks and resonates before draining out — produce layered rhythms that are not quite rhythms, patterns that almost organize themselves into repetition and then don't. Listening carefully, you begin to hear the fauna.

The fauna are not quiet. They have evolved into this sound environment or have always belonged to it, and they communicate in volumes and frequencies that carry through the roar of permanent rain. There are things — amphibian in character, though the category may not apply — that call from the plate surfaces in voices that cut through the rain the way a whistle cuts through wind: narrow, focused, shockingly bright in the acoustic dark. There are things moving through the open spaces between pillar-trunks, large enough that their movement through water-air displaces it, large enough that you feel their passage before you hear it and hear it before you see them, which you do not, because there is no light. They are not predatory toward you — nothing in this place evolved to recognize the category you represent — but they are large, and their footfalls on the living substrate send vibrations you receive through the soles of your feet as a kind of second hearing. The place is teeming by the counting of individuals. It feels empty because of the scale, because of the dark, because the great pillar-trunks have made a space that requires many presences to fill.

---

Temperature: cool enough to require attention. The water is not cold in a way that injures, but it is cool in the way that accumulates — the way a cool thing held long enough in the hand eventually takes something from you. The ambient temperature of the water-air sits just above the threshold that produces discomfort, which means it produces a slow, mild, continuous discomfort that one stops noticing only by never having arrived in a warmer place to compare. The amphibian things are untroubled. The plants are, in some important sense, the temperature, or define what temperature means here, the way very old things define the terms of the places they inhabit.

---

Nothing changes.

This is perhaps the hardest thing to receive about this place. The rain has always been this rain, the trunks have always stood at these intervals, the fauna have always made these sounds — not because the place is frozen or dead but because it has finished. It arrived at its final form and stopped. Every feedback loop closed, every successional process ran to completion, every evolutionary pressure achieved its answer, and the answer held. The species here are not evolving. The canopy is not growing taller. The plate-structures extend to their natural radius and extend no further. The rain carves nothing because everything left to carve has been carved and the living substrate resists further carving by growing into each new wound faster than the wound can deepen.

You could return in ten thousand years and find it identical, if you could survive the interval.

The old philosophy of the transient — that a place is always becoming something else — has no purchase here. This place is not becoming. It simply is, with the authority of a thing that has been for long enough that the verb *is* carries the full weight of all the time it survived to arrive at the present tense.

---

Stand still. The water-air moves through you. The calls of the amphibian things do not respond to your presence. Something large passes thirty meters away and you feel it in your feet. Above, in the inaccessible dark of the upper canopy, something is also loud, also alive, also indifferent.

The living floor gives, gently, under your weight.

It has been here since before the mountains were this height. It will be here when you are a briefly-held story in whatever keeps stories in this place — the water, probably, passing through the roots, carrying dissolved memory through the network.

It is not waiting for anything.

It arrived.
