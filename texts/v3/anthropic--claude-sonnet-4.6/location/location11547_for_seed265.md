# The Mudflats at the Edge of the Generating Station

---

You do not see it. That is the first thing.

The darkness here is not the darkness of a room with curtains drawn, not the darkness behind closed eyes — it is the darkness of a place that has decided not to be seen, that has organized itself around the absence of witnessing. Your pupils open as wide as they will go and receive nothing back except a faint warmth, a pressure, an intimation that something extends outward from where you stand for a very long distance in every direction. You know this is true. You know the scale of the place. But you cannot confirm it.

What you can confirm: the mud under your boots. It gives when you step — not suddenly, not treacherously, but with a slow, patient yielding, the way an old mattress yields, the way an argument yields when you stop pressing it. The water table is just below the surface here, which is to say the surface is barely a surface at all. It is more of a negotiated boundary, a temporary arrangement between wet clay and the air above it. When you lift your foot, the mud does not release immediately. It holds, considers, lets go. The sound is a low, soft pop — less sound than non-sound, a small cessation.

You stand somewhere within what may have once been a saltmarsh, or is becoming one, or has never quite committed to being one. The geology is young. Not young in the way people sometimes say it loosely — young in the technical sense, meaning: recently arrived at itself. The features are still discovering their own edges. The channels cut into the flat ground are crisp, almost architectural in their newness, their walls barely slumped, their banks holding the shape of the water that made them. If you could see them, they would look like incisions. They hold dark water that cannot be distinguished from the mud around them except by touch and by a subtle pressure on the skin of your forearm when you pass your hand above them — the breath of open water, slightly cooler, slightly more itself.

---

The warmth is substantial. Not unpleasant but insistent. It comes from below as much as from anywhere else — the water-saturated ground releasing the day's absorbed heat upward through the mud, through the thin grass, through your boots. The air itself sits warm and heavy. It is not humid in the way of a jungle — there is no lushness to account for — but the air has mass. It presses. When you breathe in, you get the smell of sulfur compounds from the anaerobic mud, and organic decay that is not unpleasant, and something metallic, something ozone-adjacent and electrical that does not belong to any marsh ecology. That last smell drifts. It strengthens and weakens in a cycle too slow to consciously track. At the edge of perception, you sometimes smell hot metal. Then lubricating oil. Then nothing again. Then the mud.

The heat from below has its own explanation. You know this even if you have not approached it: the Generating Station lies somewhere to the north-north-east, or to the south-west, or — and this feels possible — in multiple directions simultaneously, depending on when you locate it rather than where. It is a large-scale industrial installation of some age, its purpose uncertain and possibly evolving. What it generates is not a settled question. Power is one answer. But the marshland around it has been changed by its presence in ways that suggest the thing running through its cables is not only power in the conventional sense. The ground remembers where the buried lines run. Not by warmth exactly — more by a quality of attention, as though the soil itself is slightly more awake along those corridors.

---

The sparse vegetation does not reassemble a living mat across the ground. It occurs in clumps, in stands, in single stalks that you brush against and find surprising — surprising because you did not see them coming, because in this darkness you have given up anticipating the solid world and each contact feels like a small revelation. The grass blades are thick and waxy, the kind that can drink through their leaves, which in this condensing atmosphere amounts to a meaningful strategy. There are occasional shrubs — low, not confident in height, spreading instead along the horizontal. They have thorns you discover not by sight. Between the clumps: open mud, open water, open dark.

Some of the plants feel wrong in a way your hand reports but cannot specify. Not dangerous-wrong. More like: the geometry of the branching is unusual. The spacing of the leaves along the stem follows a logic you nearly grasp and then release. Some stems seem to occupy positions that are, on reflection, inconsistent with the positions they occupied a moment ago. This may be the darkness. This may be something else. The place does not explain itself.

---

Fauna here is dense. You know this through everything but sight.

The sound layer is quiet — *mostly* quiet — but what quiet means in this context is: no single loud thing, no calls, no percussion. The quiet is not empty. It is filled with small continuous sound that the brain sorts and eventually stops registering: the movement of many small bodies through mud and water, the gentle displacement of sediment, the occasional click or rasp that is not repeated but is replaced by a different click, a different rasp. A wet drag. A momentary increase in the breathing sound of the place — something exhaling, something larger momentarily present and then absorbed back into the dense dark.

Things move around your feet. Not threateningly. Not with any apparent interest in you, specifically. You are an obstacle, a warm feature, a curiosity that does not quite rise to being a priority. They navigate around you in the unhurried way of creatures that have been here long enough to know that patience is the fundamental skill. Fish, possibly, in the channels — you hear them sometimes, the gentlest disturbance of surface water, a tick. Invertebrates in the mud; you feel them through your boot soles if you stand still, small rhythmic pressures, lives being conducted at a different tempo than yours.

Occasionally, above: a displacement of air. Something moving horizontally through the dark, navigating by means other than vision. The passage is nearly silent. The warmth of a body, briefly. Gone.

What is harder to categorize: the sense that some of the fauna in this place occupies more than one position, or occupies position only provisionally. You become aware of this slowly, the way you become aware of a smell that has been present since you arrived. A sound — something between a creak and a drip, a meditative repetitive sound — that seems to come from the east, and also clearly from behind you, and these are not different instances of the sound but the same instance, the same occurrence, heard simultaneously from directions that cannot both be right. The creature or process making it is not located in the conventional sense. It is here, and here, in the way that a memory is here without being anywhere.

The mud is alive. This is not metaphor.

---

The built structures come to you gradually, because you walk into them.

First: a post, treated wood or possibly composite, chest height, sunk into the mud. Old enough to have acquired a soft skin of algae on its southern face (you know this only because you touch both faces and one is slick). Wires run from it — you trace them with your hand — into the dark. The wire is in good condition. This is recent enough. Whatever the post was marking, it has not been abandoned long, or possibly it is not abandoned at all.

Later: a corrugated surface, curving. A roof section, or a wall section that has achieved horizontal — it is half-submerged, one edge in the mud, the other elevated slightly by whatever it has come to rest against. It is warm. Warmer than ambient, and this is temperature not of decay but of something conducted through metal from a source some distance away.

Then: a concrete foundation, just the lip of it emerging from the surface at ankle height, a rectangle you cannot see the far end of. It extends into the dark for — far. When you walk along it, the distance surprises you. It should have ended. The vegetation changes along its edge; the grass here is thicker, more confident, taking advantage of the structural drainage the concrete provides. Insects, definitely insects, in this strip. You hear them before you reach it, a small dry sound that is the only dry sound in the whole landscape.

No complete buildings. But the parts of buildings, committed to the marsh at various angles, at various stages of a slow return. Slow. The dynamic of this place is the slowest dynamic — geological in its patience, its willingness to proceed without acknowledgment. The concrete was poured somewhere between twenty and a hundred years ago. The difference does not show. The mud is patient about this.

---

The Generating Station itself, when you become more certain of it, is sensed rather than arrived at.

You do not walk into it. Instead the air changes. The metallic smell becomes constant rather than intermittent. The ground carries a low frequency — not sound, not quite vibration, something between them, something the inner ear and the gut collaborate in detecting. A hum that may be real. A warmth that is now clearly emanating from a source rather than from diffuse ground-heat. The dark ahead has a texture to it, a slight difference in the quality of the nothing — the way the nothing is arranged over a very large structure is different from the way the nothing is arranged over open marsh. You understand that the building is enormous. You understand that it continues above you. You understand that it is operating.

What it is doing: uncertain. Turbines, possibly, though no single turbine could account for the scale of the felt hum. The electrical smell now carries something additional — not ozone, not exactly, but the smell of a quantity of charge so large that it has begun to interact with the materials around it in ways that charge does not normally interact. The wires in the ground here must be thick. Many of them. The ground accepts them.

Through a gap in something — you find this gap by feel, a doorway or a broken panel — warmer air exhales toward you. Not hot. Warm, and carrying the concentration of every smell the outside air had in diluted form. Metal warm from long running. Condensed water on interior surfaces. And: a smell of something biological, of growth or occupation, that is incongruous but not surprising. The station has had time to acquire its own ecology.

You do not enter. You stand at the threshold and listen.

Inside: the quiet deepens into something more architectural. The quiet of a large interior space — the silence of a cathedral, which is a different silence than outdoor silence because it is bounded and yet enormous, because it makes your small breathing audible to yourself. And under that: the machinery. Not loud. Old machinery well maintained runs quietly, efficiently, without the protest of something strained. This machinery has been running a long time. It has settled into itself. The hum is low and steady and does not change in frequency even when you tilt your head.

Something moves inside. Not machinery. Something else, larger than the creatures in the marsh, unhurried in the architectural darkness. You hear it in the quality of the interior silence when it passes between you and whatever is producing that machine-hum. A momentary deepening of the sound. A displacement.

It does not approach.

---

The reality of the place has been thinning since you arrived. You have not noticed this the way you notice most things — you have noticed it the way you notice yourself growing tired, which is to say you notice it all at once after a long time of not noticing. The mud under your boots is still mud. The warmth is still warmth. The smell of the marsh is still the smell of the marsh. But there are redundancies now, small loops in the world's logic. The post you encountered at the beginning of your walk — you encounter it again, and your hand reports the same slick of algae on the same face, and the wire running from it is the same wire in the same condition, and the mud under it has the same consistency of remembered mud. Either you have walked in a circle so gradual you could not perceive it, or the post has recurred. Both possibilities are held open by the darkness.

The sounds have begun to occur in the wrong order for causes and their effects. A small splash, then the silence before a splash, then the thing that makes splashes finding its position at the surface. The machinery in the station produces its warmth before it produces its hum before it produces the mechanical action that would account for either. Time here is not reversed — nothing so dramatic as that. But it is *patient* with itself in a way that the objects and events within it are not. The world is running a little behind its contents. This is what happens in places that have been changed by the passage of large-scale processes that do not recognize the usual hierarchies of cause.

The fauna has adapted to this. That is why their navigation makes sense even without sight. They have learned to locate themselves not in space but in the sequence of their own actions, which are reliable even when space is not. When a creature moves through water that occupies two positions, it moves through water. It is fine. It has been fine. You are the only one for whom this is disorienting.

---

You stay still for a while and let the place do what it does.

The mud settles further under your weight, incrementally, almost imperceptibly. If you stood here long enough, days, weeks, you would sink — not into quicksand dramatics but into the slow acceptance of the ground, the way fence posts eventually lean, the way old buildings develop a relationship with their foundations that was not in the original design. The marsh would take you on as a feature. Birds — if there are birds here, and the wing-sounds suggest there are — would navigate around you. The grasses would grow toward you slightly, to the warmth, to the new surface of your legs as they emerged from the mud. You would become something the rest of the landscape referenced.

The generating station would continue to hum.

The darkness would continue to hold everything it holds.

And somewhere in the mud, a channel would trace a new path around the new obstacle you had become — not this year, not next year, but in the geological patience of the place, in its absolute indifference to hurry — and the channel's banks would be crisp and young and freshly cut, the way all things are, here, arriving at themselves for the first time, continually.
