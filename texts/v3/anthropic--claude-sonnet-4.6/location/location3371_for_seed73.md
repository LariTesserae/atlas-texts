# The Thermal Gardens of the Canopy Spine

---

Begin with the heat, because it begins before anything else does.

It arrives before you clear the last ridge. A pressure, first — a quality of air that has been worked, breathed by too many things, passed through chlorophyll and bark and the wet interiors of orchid throats a thousand times before it reaches your lungs. The temperature is not violent. It doesn't strike. It settles over you the way a hand settles over a candle flame — not extinguishing, just enclosing, making you aware of your own warmth by surrounding it with something warmer. Thirty-eight degrees, perhaps. Forty. The numbers stop mattering quickly. What matters is the *persistence* of it, the fact that it has been this way for a very long time and intends to continue.

You are high. You are very high.

The elevation is not what you'd expect elevation to be. There is no cold, no thinning, no alpine bareness. The mountains here — and they are old mountains, established mountains, mountains that have been mountains so long they have almost forgotten the word — rise into a band of perpetual warmth where the atmosphere thickens rather than thins, where clouds don't pass over but through, where moisture wrings itself from the air against cliff faces and weeps down in sheets so thin they are more like solid fog than falling water. The peaks are not sharp. They were sharp once, geologically speaking, in whatever epoch counts as *once* for ranges this ancient. Now they are shouldered, humped, rounded at the tops like sleeping animals, their ridgelines so long and gradual that you walk their spines for hours before realizing you have been at the summit the whole time.

The rock is dark. Basalt where the cliffs still show their faces, covered almost everywhere else by a skin of living things so complete and so old that the underlying geology is a rumor, something you infer rather than see. Here and there a boulder breaks through, and its surface is not bare — nothing here is bare — but covered in a mosaic of mosses so dense they look painted, green-black and yellow-green and the particular blue-green that has no good name, each species holding its border against its neighbors with the patience of things that have been in exactly this configuration for two hundred years and expect to be in it for two hundred more.

---

The water is everywhere without being obvious about it.

This is not a place of rivers. There are rivers — you can hear one or two of them in the middle distance, not loud exactly, but present as a low continuous articulation beneath everything else — but the *water* of the place is more pervasive than rivers account for. The air is thick with it. Surfaces bead with it. You lean against a cliff and your palm comes away damp, not from rain (though it rained two hours ago, and will rain again in two hours, and the interval between is not dry but merely less wet) but from the rock itself, which is perpetually cool enough relative to the saturated air that it condenses moisture like the outside of a cold glass. The rock weeps. It has been weeping for as long as it has been rock. The moss drinks this and the ferns drink this and the small red-stemmed plants whose name you don't know drink this, and what they don't drink runs down in runnels so thin they're invisible until they catch light, accumulating in crevices and lip-edges and the natural bowls that form wherever two slopes meet at an angle the rain finds agreeable.

Underfoot: sponge. The ground, where there is ground not occupied by root or rock, is a layered accumulation — humus below, moss above, and between them strata of rotted wood and compressed leaf matter that compresses under your weight and slowly, with the patience characteristic of everything here, rises back. Walking is a negotiation. The earth participates.

The canopy is dense. You are not in the canopy — you are at altitude above the normal treeline — but altitude here doesn't mean what it means elsewhere, and so there is a canopy, or something that functions like one. Not the canopy of lowland forest, not the uniform ceiling of interlocked crowns, but something stranger: trees that grow in the crevices and shoulders of the high ridge, wind-trained from birth into horizontal shapes, their trunks no more than two meters tall before they fold sideways and spread parallel to the slope, accumulating width rather than height. They have been doing this for so long that some of them are architectural in their complexity, their lateral branches crossing and re-crossing those of their neighbors, the whole thing becoming a mat — a roof, almost — at a height of about two and a half meters, through which the sky shows in moving pieces when the wind shifts the leaves. The leaves are large. They need to be large because the canopy traps humidity and large leaves are efficient transpirers in high humidity and this is an old equation that these trees have solved. They are dark green. They are slightly glossy. They hold water on their surfaces longer than you'd expect, the individual drops persisting in the waxy microstructure of the leaf epidermis, catching whatever light makes it through.

---

Below the horizontal tree-mat: a second world.

The understorey is dense in the way that dreams of density are dense — not the density of overcrowding, not the crammed-together quality of things competing for the same space, but something more integrated, a density achieved through cooperation over long time, each layer finding the frequency of light the layer above it passes and making use of that, each organism occupying a niche so well-defined and so long-established that it fits its position the way a river fits its bed. Ferns occupy the middle height, the classic stage-set ferns, fronds large enough to use as shelter, growing in clusters from root masses that have been in place since before any human thing came here. Below the ferns: smaller ferns, and mosses in hummocks and sheets, and the occasional pale flower of something that has learned to bloom without much light and to do its pollination by means you'd rather not look at too closely — some of the fauna here is unusual.

The fauna is abundant. This is an understatement. This is a landscape that is *doing something with life* the way other landscapes do something with rock or water. Every surface is occupied. The bark of every horizontal tree-trunk hosts its own miniature succession — lichen at the oldest exposed sections, then moss, then in the moss small ferns no taller than your finger, and in the ferns the eggs of something, and on the eggs a mite too small to see without magnification, and on the mite something smaller still, all the way down to scales the technology can detect but the naked eye cannot reach. The insects are loud. Not individually, each one — any individual insect makes a modest sound — but collectively they produce a layered hum that sits under everything else, a frequency you stop noticing after an hour the way you stop noticing a refrigerator, and then notice again when it briefly stops, and realize it had never stopped before.

Birds exist here in varieties that seem implausible for the altitude. Brightly colored ones, the colors of warning or seduction or both. They call in sequences that feel syntactic, too structured for pure instinct, and you find yourself waiting for the next phrase the way you'd wait for the next measure of a melody you half-know. Something larger moves in the upper branches with a sound like a sail filling, a heavy organic sound, and you don't see what makes it.

The amphibians are everywhere near water, which means they are everywhere. Small frogs in colors that seem calculated — precise, deliberate reds and yellows, as if each one has been optimized to communicate one specific thing to one specific predator. They are loud in aggregate. In the hours around dusk, which stretches here because the ridges catch the last light and hold it, the frog-noise rises to something that stops being background and becomes foreground, becomes the dominant sensory fact of the environment, a wall of sound with the quality of something that has been doing this every evening for ten thousand years.

---

There are structures. A few.

They would be easy to miss, and are perhaps meant to be, or perhaps the question of intention is the wrong frame. They are not ruins — this is the first thing to understand. They don't have the quality of ruins, which is a quality of *having once been more*, of reduction, of incompleteness as a statement about time's passage. These structures have a different quality. They are exactly what they are. Small. Deliberate. Present.

The most obvious is on the shoulder of the second ridge, where the horizontal trees thin slightly and a flat area of exposed basalt allows you to see. Something has been placed there — a structure perhaps four meters in its longest dimension, irregular in outline, made of a material that is neither stone nor metal nor anything with a clear referent. It does not weather. The moss grows up to its edges and stops, not dramatically, not as if repelled, but as if the surface does not offer what moss requires, as if it is simply outside the system. Its surface is slightly warm to the touch even in shade. It hums at a frequency you feel in your back teeth rather than hear with your ears.

What it is *doing* is not immediately apparent, but doing something seems likely. It has the quality of instrumentation. The quality of a thing placed with purpose and still serving that purpose, though the purpose may be a long purpose, a purpose measured in decades of continuous operation rather than anything a human project timeline would recognize. There are three or four more of these, scattered across the valley when you look down from the ridge. They do not communicate with each other in any way you can detect. They may communicate with each other in ways you can't.

The technology that made these — and something made them, or something in the universe that is at least partially causal produced them by means that include something like intention — does not appear anywhere else in visible form. But it may appear in invisible forms. The extraordinary density and health of the vegetation in certain areas, the unusual distribution of particular species, the fact that the canopy-mat trees seem to have arrived at a form that is architecturally too efficient for pure evolutionary drift — these things suggest the possibility that the landscape itself has been assisted. Grown, perhaps. Not designed, exactly, because design implies blueprint and this is too organic, too willing to be surprised. Participated in, maybe. Co-authored.

The biology here is almost entirely conventional in its biochemistry and deeply strange in its outcomes.

---

Looking out from the high spine, which you can do from the clearings where the canopy-mat trees have not yet colonized, the valley below resolves as a green bowl holding heat and moisture like a cradled thing. The scale is valley-scale, the kind of space that takes a day to cross and three days to properly understand. The ridges that enclose it are not walls — they are gradual, they lean toward each other, they suggest enclosure without enforcing it. On a clear moment (the cloud comes and goes; *comes* is perhaps too dynamic a word, it would be better to say the cloud and its absence alternate on a rhythm measured in hours, and the transition is always slow, always gradual, the cloud arriving like a consideration rather than a weather event) — on a clear moment, you can see four distinct vegetation bands in the valley, color variations so subtle they require distance to read: the darker green of permanent shade, the brighter green of mid-canopy, the yellow-green of grass and herb margins near whatever water runs at the valley floor, the particular pale dusty green of the tree-mat on the far ridge.

The light is ordinary light. This matters. All of this — the heat and the density and the height and the strangeness of the small humming structures and the layered biological abundance — all of it occurs in ordinary light. Not dramatic light. Not the golden-hour light that makes landscapes perform for photographs. The light here is the light of middle afternoon in a slightly overcast sky, diffuse, directionless, filling the air as much as it illuminates from one direction. This makes everything look exactly like itself. There is no shadow to create mystery, no oblique light to dramatize form. The fern is just the fern. The basalt is just the basalt. The strangeness of the place is therefore structural rather than atmospheric — it inheres in what the place *is* rather than how it's currently lit.

This is not a landscape that performs for observers. It has been what it is for a very long time. It will be what it is after you leave. Whatever the small warm structures are doing, they will continue doing it through seasons and decades, their purpose entirely independent of your understanding of it. The ferns will continue being abundant. The ridge will continue sweating. The horizontal trees will continue their slow lateral decision. The frogs will continue their dusk announcement.

You are in it briefly. It is in itself, continuously, at a pace you can barely perceive as dynamic but which is, if you have the patience and the time frame, full of change — the slow change of things that have already worked out the large questions and are now occupied entirely with long accumulation, with the very gradual movement from one good equilibrium toward another.

The heat does not let up. The green does not thin. The sound continues, layered, the insect stratum and the bird stratum and the water stratum and under all of it, barely, the tooth-felt hum.
