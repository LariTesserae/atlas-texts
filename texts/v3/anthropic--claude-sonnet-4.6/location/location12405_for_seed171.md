# The Green Noise

---

Start with the sound, because the sound arrives before anything else does.

It is not loud in the way a waterfall is loud, or a crowd, or an engine. It is loud in the way pressure is loud — the way your ears report something your body already knows. It comes from everywhere beneath the threshold of direction. You cannot turn toward it because you are inside it. The sound is the place's breathing, and the place is breathing at continental scale, and you are a small thing caught inside a breath that has been exhaling for longer than you have existed.

What you are hearing — if *hearing* is the word — is growth.

---

Begin at what might be a surface, though *surface* is already the wrong concept. There is no ground here in any sense you would recognize. What your feet might rest on, if you had feet, if resting were possible in this density, is a compressed mat of rhizomes thick as bridge cable, interlocked so completely that you could not slide a sheet of paper between them. Below that mat, more rhizomes. Below those, the structures from which they grew — older roots, ten years old or ten thousand, it is impossible to say — because this landscape is young in its *form* but ancient in its *matter*, a distinction that will become important. The rock beneath is barely rock yet. It is proto-geology, mineral slurry in the last stages of solidifying, still warm from its own becoming. The roots have found it. The roots are threading it. The roots are, in some measurable sense, *deciding* what it will be.

This is a plateau — elevated, broad as a continent, broader — and the plateau is not a thing that was here before the vegetation. The vegetation is making the plateau. Has been making it. Will continue making it at scales of time that require a different kind of attention to comprehend.

---

The light arrives as something green has eaten something gold and then thought better of it.

There is a sky. You can intuit this because the light comes from generally upward, filtered through so many successive canopy layers that *up* is more of a philosophical position than a direction. The uppermost layer — call it the first canopy, though it is also the last, depending on which way you are counting — consists of something that is not quite tree and not quite coral reef and not quite anything with a common name. Trunks, if they are trunks, rise and immediately become something else — lateral structures, joining other laterals, forming a lattice so dense that the architecture of the thing is indistinguishable from the biology of the thing. Vascular systems run horizontally for kilometers before turning vertical again. What you would call a tree in any other landscape is here just a *feature* of a larger connected body, the way a finger is a feature of a hand is a feature of a person is a feature of a crowd that shares a circulatory system.

Below the first canopy: a second. Below that, a third. The counting becomes suspect somewhere around the sixth or seventh layer, because the categories begin to interpenetrate — epiphytes growing on epiphytes, each successive layer dimmer, cooler, slower, and wetter, until you arrive at the rhizome mat, which you are thinking of as the ground, and discover that the light there is a memory of light, a stain, something photons said about themselves a very long time ago before the canopy absorbed the rest.

Nothing grows up here by growing up. Everything grows by negotiating with everything else. The negotiation is biochemical. It is constant. It is, at scale, what you are hearing.

---

The water.

Water moves through this place the way blood moves through tissue, which is to say: through pressure differentials you cannot see, through channels you would not recognize as channels, at rates that seem wrong for liquid and correct for something else. 

It drips from canopy to canopy. Each layer has developed surfaces specifically for this, leaf-geometries that catch falling water and route it inward toward channels that distribute it laterally before releasing it downward to the next layer. Nothing is wasted. Nothing runs off. Rain that falls on the uppermost canopy may take three weeks to reach the rhizome mat, redirected a thousand times, chemically altered at each stop, arriving changed.

Between layers, in spaces that are approximately spaces — gaps in the mass, irregularities in the density — mist gathers and moves in slow horizontal sheets. The temperature is cool enough up here that the mist is genuine mist rather than evaporation, and the mist is so persistent that certain surfaces have evolved specifically to drink it: pale velvet structures, lateral and enormous, that absorb atmospheric moisture directly. They look, in the dim light, like open hands. There are millions of them. The sound they make when the mist moves through is part of the larger sound, the breathing, the planetary green noise.

Stones weep where they exist, which is rarely, which is only where the root-lattice has not yet fully colonized the mineral substrate beneath. At these rare bare patches, water seeps up from below — the water table in this embryonic geology is almost at the surface everywhere, the rock being still too unconsolidated to hold much pressure — and the stone surface glistens with a slick that is part mineral, part microbial, part something that has not been formally categorized, being new.

---

And here is where the *tech* parameter enters, though it enters slowly, and enters strangely, because there are no machines here, no artifacts, nothing built.

What is here instead is this: the biochemical negotiation between organisms has reached a complexity at which the word *negotiation* begins to fail. The signal-passing — chemical, electrical, pressure-based, and involving at least two other mechanisms that current biology does not have clean names for — happens at densities and speeds that produce emergent behavior at the macro-scale. The canopy is not thinking. This needs to be said clearly. The canopy is not thinking the way you think or I think or anything thinks in a way we would recognize.

But the canopy is *computing*.

Not metaphorically. Not in the loose way we sometimes say a river *decides* which way to flow downhill. The distributed biochemical network that constitutes this place processes information about moisture gradients, light angles, structural stress, chemical intrusion, temperature variation, and a hundred other variables, and it produces *coordinated responses* across distances of thousands of kilometers within timescales that should not be physically possible given the apparent bandwidth of the signaling mechanisms we can identify. There is bandwidth we cannot identify. The mechanism is real but not fully describable in current terms — something about phase-change signaling in the vascular fluid, something about a class of pressure wave that the root matrix conducts differently than any known medium.

The result is this: when the mist thickens on the eastern edge of the plateau, something adjusts in the canopy chemistry on the western edge. When a section of rhizome mat begins to fail — rare, but it happens, the geology beneath still shifting — the surrounding sections thicken and reinforce before the failure is complete. The system knows itself. Not consciously. Not the way you know your own name. But the way your immune system knows your blood type. Deeply, without language, correctly.

This is what makes the scale make sense. The vastness is not incidental. The vastness is necessary. You need a system this large to do computation this complex, and the computation is why the system is this large. Size and intelligence — if we allow ourselves that word, at arm's length — are each other's cause.

---

No animals.

This is not because animals were excluded. This is because the density of the plant-mass offers no ecological niche that an animal body could occupy — no gaps in resource space, no available calories that aren't already being cycled back into the system at the point where they'd need to be intercepted. A seed-eater would need seeds that fall free, and no seeds fall free here; they are caught, redirected, replanted, reclaimed. The system has a closure about it. Not hostile. Not deliberately excluding. Simply complete in a way that leaves no opening.

The only things moving through this space are the water, the mist, the slow coordinated adjustments of the canopy, the growth of root-tips through crystallizing rock — and the sound.

There are no birds. This means the sound has a particular quality: it is entirely non-biological in the sense of *animal* biology, and yet entirely biological in origin. The absence of calls, of wingbeats, of anything with intention inside it, gives the noise a strange purity. It is the sound of process without purpose. It is deafening and it is clean.

---

The plateau's edges, somewhere far away in the dim green middle distance — or what you imagine to be middle distance, because there is no horizon here, no clear line, just a gradual thickening of the density until the concept of *far away* becomes purely nominal — the plateau's edges are dropping. Slowly. Over thousands of years. The weight of the living mass is compressing the proto-geology below, pushing it into shapes the rock was not yet ready to take, and the shapes are becoming real in real time.

Mountains are forming. Not in any human timeframe. But faster than they should form from rock alone.

The vegetation is making them. Has decided — without deciding — that the shape it needs to be stable at this scale requires certain structural supports, and the supports require a certain substrate topography, and the substrate topography is being produced by the weight and chemistry of the living mass pressing down into the lithic clay below.

This is the embryonic erosion — not the wearing-away kind, but the *initial-forming* kind. The landscape is practicing itself. It has not settled on what it will look like. The margins are still in negotiation. The only stable thing is the center, and the center is stable only because the center has been here longest, has had the most time to reach its own solutions.

The sound comes from the center. The sound comes from whatever the center has become, which is: something that breathes. Something large enough to have weather of its own, and weather it does have — the dim canopy air producing its own circulation, its own moisture cycles, independent of the sky above — something vast enough that you could walk for years in any direction and not reach its edge.

Something that is, in all the ways that matter, *alive*, and in all the ways that matter, *singular*, and in all the ways that matter, not yet finished becoming what it will be.

---

Stand in it.

Listen to the green noise. Let your eyes adjust to the dim, which will take longer than you expect, and will never be fully satisfying — you will always feel like you are about to see something clearly that refuses to come clear.

Feel the moisture on your skin. Feel the cool of the altitude, the cool that is distinct from cold, that has an edge to it rather than a depth. Feel the pressure of the sound, which is really the pressure of ten thousand kilometers of coordinated process happening all at once, in every direction, including down.

The rock beneath your feet is deciding what it is. The roots are helping it decide. The mist is a little bit of the decision. You are, for the moment, inside a question that this place is asking itself.

The place will arrive at an answer. It always does. It is just that the answer takes geological time, and in the meantime: the noise, the green, the wet, the dim, the slow impossible work of a world practicing becoming a world.
