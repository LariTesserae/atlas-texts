# The Drowned Plateau

---

Begin with the water.

It is everywhere and it is not a body of water in any sense that word *body* preserves. It is not contained. There is no vessel, no shore, no bed. The water here is the medium through which the world occurs — the atmosphere, the gravity-weighted air, the climate itself. You are high, impossibly high, on a plateau so ancient it has forgotten it was ever anything other than flat, and the water moves through the air at every altitude simultaneously: as mist that is nearly indistinguishable from cloud, as cloud that is nearly indistinguishable from the plateau's own exhalations, as slow upwelling curtains of warm vapor that rise from hidden fissures in the stone and immediately become part of the sky. Rain is not an event here. Rain is a condition. It falls with almost no sound — just the softest continuous consonant, the world's own breath turned audible, a *hhhhh* so low and so everywhere that you stop hearing it as rain and begin hearing it as silence.

The plateau is old. This requires sitting with.

Not old in the way mountains are old, which is to say mountains still know what they are, still carry the memory of uplift in the angles of their faces, still look like something that happened. This plateau has moved past that. It has been planed by time beyond any erosion that might be called dramatic. The process here was not violent; violence implies energy, and energy implies change, and this place has spent so long settling into its own final grammar that even change has quieted. What remains is stone that is nearly horizontal to a degree that feels deliberate, as though the land itself made an aesthetic choice. You could walk without ever noticing you were on terrain. The relief — the variation in height across what might be a thousand kilometers — is perhaps forty meters. Perhaps sixty. It is less than the trees.

And the trees.

The trees are the great fact of the place. They have had all the time erosion spent on the stone, but they spent it differently. They grew. Not upward particularly — upward is a young tree's ambition — but *outward*, and *downward*, and *through*, and *into*, and eventually *as*. The canopy is not a layer above you. The canopy is the sky. From inside — and you are inside, you are always inside here — you cannot find the edge of it. It extends in every horizontal direction until the water-air and the dim light make it continuous with itself. Root systems have, over geological spans, restructured the plateau's surface drainage. Where there were once channels, now there are root-topographies, vast slow sinuses of living wood that have redirected millennia of rainfall through themselves and deposited it back into the stone as something between water and digestion. The trees do not compete with the landscape. They *are* the landscape. The stone underneath is almost incidental — a substrate long since colonized into irrelevance.

The density of living matter here does something to the physics of space. Not metaphorically. When you look in any direction, there is no line of sight longer than thirty meters before it is absorbed by some surface: a trunk, a curtain of aerial roots, a shelf of moss thick enough to be called a hillside, a colony of epiphytes so large and so elaborately self-supporting that they qualify as an ecosystem rather than a plant. The horizon does not exist. The horizon has been replaced by more of this place. This is not claustrophobia — there is warmth here, and moisture, and something that functions like comfort — but it is an abolition of distance as a concept. Distance does not operate. Everything is adjacent to everything else.

The temperature is warm in the way a living body is warm. Not the warm of sunlight, which requires sky, which requires the canopy to end somewhere, which it does not. The warmth is endogenous. It comes from below, faintly geothermal, conducted up through the deep stone and released by the root systems into the humid air. It comes from the biological heat of decomposition, which is continuous and omnipresent — there is so much dead matter at every stage of dissolution that the process generates its own climate. The moisture in the air is body-temperature. You breathe it and it is not refreshing; it is intimate. It is the temperature of the inside of a mouth.

Under the canopy, the light is dim to the point of being a different substance from light as you usually know it. Not darkness — you can see, everything is visible — but the light has been so multiply filtered through layer after layer of living green that it has become something else, something almost nutritive-looking, a diffuse bioluminescent suggestion rather than illumination from a source. There is no shadow. Without a definite light-source, nothing casts. The entire world is in that strange mid-register that eyes use for dreaming, or for the time just before sleep, when perception still functions but the hard edges that separate one thing from another have already begun to negotiate.

This is where the strangeness begins to accumulate.

The plateau is vast — genuinely vast, continent-scale, a place with its own internal weather systems that have never needed to consult any ocean or any outside geography. Pressure systems form in the interior and resolve against themselves. Fronts develop. There are storms, slow and warm, that move across the canopy for what might be weeks, but from below they are indistinguishable from ordinary rain, from the ordinary condition, from the baseline. You would not know a storm was happening without instruments you do not have.

But the vastness does something to time. Across a distance large enough for weather to forget its own origin, across terrain so uniformly ancient that the stone cannot remember any other configuration, across a canopy that has been continuous for longer than most things have been anything — across all of that, causality becomes approximate. Not broken. Approximate. The spatial scale exceeds the rate at which events can inform each other, and so there are zones of this plateau — call them regions, though they are not marked — where something has already happened that hasn't happened yet at the center. Where the light is slightly different in a way that doesn't correspond to cloud cover. Where the rain falls at an angle no wind accounts for. Where the texture of the air carries information about the stone below in a way that violates the insulation of the root systems above it.

Rules of the world, then. Revised.

Cause and effect are not abolished. They are softened. Sharpened causality is a young world's property, like angles on stone, like ambition in trees. Here the world is old enough to have noticed that the strict sequencing of events is a convention, possibly a temporary one, and has begun to relax it. Not dramatically — there is no visible impossibility, nothing that would register as a violation rather than a wonder. But:

A tree falls, here, slowly, over what might be three hundred years, and the sound of its falling has been present in the air, as a sub-audible vibration, since well before the fall begins. By the time it is finished falling, the other trees have already adjusted their root geometry to manage the new light that will come — though the light never comes, because the canopy closes from above before the trunk reaches the ground, and the trunk itself, before it arrives at the forest floor, has already been colonized so thoroughly that it qualifies as standing rather than fallen. The event was, in some sense, cancelled. But the vibration remains in the stone, the record of a falling that didn't finish, and the root geometry remains in its adjusted configuration, and so something happened and nothing happened and the plateau holds both versions with equal composure.

The fauna is rare and this, too, fits. An ecosystem this dense and this active is supported overwhelmingly by its plants, its fungi, its microbial life — the vast underground exchanges of chemistry and information that have been building their vocabulary since before there were animals to name things. What animals remain have made themselves appropriate to the place: they are quiet, slow, adapted to a world where sight is limited and distance is theoretical. There are things that move at the periphery. Things that are briefly visible before the density of the vegetation — which does not thin; which is the same, densely, in every direction — relocates them, or was always concealing them. A shape at thirty meters that is not quite a tree and not quite anything else. The sense of being processed by something's attention.

They are rare but they are watching.

The built world barely exists, which is the right ratio for a place this old. There are walls — or rather: the idea of walls, walls in their final form, which is to say stone that was once arranged by intent and has since been so thoroughly consumed by root and moss and slow water-work that only a geologist could distinguish them from natural outcropping. Someone was here. Someone understood that this was a place worth marking. They marked it with stones and the stones are now the plateau's own again. If you look carefully — and carefully here means something different, means tuning your perception to the dim-light-no-shadow register and waiting for the geometry to resolve — you can find angles in the root structures that do not occur naturally. You can find a depression in the moss that is too regular to be accident. You can find, once, a single carved surface, horizontal, now serving as the floor of a hollow where several large roots have formed a chamber, the carving facing up and readable if you are willing to put your face close enough to the stone to feel its breath. What it says — what the marks indicate — is unclear, and not because the language is lost but because the marks themselves have been held in moisture so long that they have softened at the edges, become suggestions of marks, and what they suggest is so simple and so large that the mind slides off it the way water slides off moss.

Sit for a moment in the sound.

Which is nearly nothing. The rain is too fine and too everywhere to be heard as rain. The roots do not creak. The trees do not drip — they absorb. No wind penetrates below the first thirty meters of canopy. Occasionally something shifts, somewhere, at the limit of earshot — a sound that is more the memory of a sound than a sound itself, the way the stone remembers a fallen tree. The silence here is not empty. It is saturated. Every possible sound has been absorbed into it, every frequency at every time, until silence is the name for the total, for the sum that is too full to distinguish any of its parts.

The dynamic of the place is enormous and invisible. The root systems are exchanging resources across hundreds of kilometers of mycorrhizal network. Weather is developing and resolving. Trees are falling in slow motion over centuries. Chemistry is happening in the stone. The water is moving through the air, through the roots, through the stone, through the air again in a cycle that has been running, continuously, since before there were eyes to observe it. All of this is active, vigorous, metabolically urgent, and all of it is below the threshold of perception. The plateau is furiously alive and silent. It is doing everything at once and none of it registers as event.

You have been here before. You have not been here before. The plateau makes no distinction between these.

The rain continues.
