# The Vivarium

---

Begin with the glass.

It is close — perhaps two arm-lengths away in any direction if you stand at the center, perhaps less. Curved. Old glass, the kind with subtle imperfections that compress and warp whatever lies beyond it into something slightly wrong, a world seen through old water. The glass is fogged at its lower margin where warmth meets cold surface, and along this fog-line small droplets have gathered into larger droplets with the slow inevitability of things that do not rush because they have nowhere else to be and no particular deadline for arriving there. They track downward in paths that were already there the last time you looked, and the time before that. The paths do not change.

This is a vivarium. Industrial in origin — not a hobbyist's tank, not a naturalist's cabinet of curiosity, but something made for a purpose that outgrew its original parameters and then was left to run. The frame is bolted steel, painted black, paint chipped in specific places that have not changed in years. The bolts are oversized. The lid is a grille of galvanized mesh, and above the mesh, suspended from something you cannot see from here, hangs a bank of heating elements — not warm-spectrum, not plant-grow lights, but industrial infrareds, amber-red, casting everything below in the color of a photograph taken in a room where the bulbs were always about to fail.

The light that reaches the interior is dim. It has traveled through the mesh and through the accumulated moisture that clings to every surface and through a layer of something — condensation, or perhaps the exhalations of what lives here — and arrived tired. Surfaces that should be bright are only present. Shadows are not dramatic; they are merely the absence of the already-insufficient light, slightly more absent.

---

The floor is not soil. It was never soil.

It is poured concrete, the original substrate of whatever room or facility this unit was installed in — a laboratory anteroom, perhaps, or the supply floor of a breeding operation — and over that concrete, successive layers of material have been laid down by people who are no longer here, and then by the processes that outlasted them. A base of coarse gravel, grey and angular, the kind sold by the cubic meter. Over that, a layer of expanded clay pellets — hydroton, the kind used in industrial hydroponic systems — and over that, a drainage membrane gone soft with age, and over that, the current surface: a dense black substrate of coconut coir mixed with something finer, something darker, something that holds water the way a held breath holds air, with effort, with intention, with the knowledge that release is coming.

The substrate is wet. Not soggy — not pooling — but when you press a finger into it (if you were to press a finger into it) water would emerge to meet you from just below the surface, eager, present. The coir is cold. The air immediately above it is less cold than the air at the mesh-top, where the warmth dissipates into the room beyond, and so there is a tiny thermal gradient, a whisper of convection, a private weather system two arm-lengths across and perhaps sixty centimeters tall.

On the walls: the same glass, but coated inside with a film of moisture that has been there long enough to develop its own character. Mineral deposits trace the former boundaries of old droplets — thin white rings like the rings of a bathtub, but small, delicate, geological in their patience. Between the rings, green. Not the green of plants — there is almost no plant matter here — but the green of algae, of moss in its most primitive ambitions, of the kind of growth that precedes growth, that is to vegetation what a first word is to a library. Sparse. Thin. A suggestion that life is interested in the walls but has not yet committed.

The rear wall has a different texture from the others. It was retrofitted — you can see the sealant line, dark grey and slightly raised — and into it was set a small cascade structure: a flat sheet of something porous, maybe lava rock, maybe a manufactured ceramic, inclined at perhaps fifteen degrees and kept permanently wet by a pump hidden beneath the substrate. Water moves across this surface. It moves the same way every time, has moved the same way so long that the channels it follows have become the channels it follows, confirmed and deepened and absolute. At the base, a shallow reservoir the width of two hands, the depth of one. The water sits there. The pump takes it up again. There is no loss except evaporation, and the room provides back what evaporation takes.

This is the water cycle of this world, in full.

---

Now the inhabitants.

There are too many.

Whatever the original plan was — a breeding colony, a display unit, a controlled study — the original plan's parameters for population have been exceeded. Not grotesquely, not to the point of dysfunction, but exceeded. The density of living bodies per cubic unit of space is high enough that you are never looking at empty space. You are always looking at something, at some arrangement of bodies touching or nearly touching, layered in the three dimensions available to them.

They are dart frogs.

Specifically: several species, or perhaps subspecies, or perhaps what happens when a closed system with multiple strains runs long enough that the categories begin to blur at their edges. The dominant form is small — thumbnail-sized, most of them — and comes in a coloration that would be remarkable in a field guide but is here so common, so repeated in every direction you look, that it becomes the ground condition rather than the figure against it. Cobalt blue legs meeting black torsos meeting, in some individuals, a stripe of yellow-green along the flank so saturated it seems to generate its own light, a small internal sun, though this may be the amber heating elements doing something unexpected with the pigment.

They are everywhere. On the substrate. On the glass — many on the glass, their toe-pads making them available to vertical surfaces as easily as horizontal ones, so that the walls are inhabited the same way the floor is inhabited, the density simply rotates ninety degrees and continues. On each other, sometimes — males on the backs of females, or juveniles resting in the cup of an adult's body heat, or two individuals simply occupying an overlapping space because the space available has been fully subscribed. On the moss. On the cascade wall, gripping the wet ceramic with no apparent concern for the film of water moving beneath them.

They call.

This is the deafening part, and it requires effort to convey because it is not loud in the way of a concert or a construction site — it is loud in the way of accumulation, of many small voices so numerous and so constant that they compound into something that fills the space of hearing the way the frogs fill the space of the tank. Each individual call is a short, soft trill, not unpleasant in isolation, a small mechanical sound like a tiny motor or a thumb drawn quickly across a fine comb. But there are enough individuals, and they call with enough consistency, that the interior of this vivarium — this pocket world, this room-within-a-room — produces a sound that is, if you put your ear to the mesh, genuinely overwhelming. It enters the ear not as a series of distinct sounds but as a texture, a material, a substance with density and temperature. It does not fluctuate. It does not surge or ebb. It was like this an hour ago and will be like this in an hour. The frogs are not performing. They are existing, and sound is what existing sounds like here.

Above them — a few millimeters above the level of the substrate in the shallow damp zones, threading through the gravel, visible only if you look slowly at the places where a less patient eye finds nothing — there are other things. Springtails, white and nearly microscopic, moving in that jittery purposeless way that contains, under examination, a great deal of purpose. Isopods, small ones, pale grey, occupying the deeper zones where the coir is coldest and wettest and where the frogs rarely bother to go. The springtails and isopods are the maintenance of this world, the custodial layer, processing waste, managing moisture, preventing the accumulation of outcomes. They are numerous beyond counting and beneath notice.

---

Above the vivarium: the room.

It is cold. Whatever building this is — high up, because the one window visible beyond the vivarium's glass shows only sky, pale and featureless and close, the sky of altitude, a sky that has not committed to any weather because it is already above most weather — whatever building, whatever floor, the heating is not generous. The air in the room is perhaps eight or nine degrees. Someone's breath would show here.

The vivarium generates its own heat — the infrareds, the pump motor, the collective metabolic output of several hundred small bodies — and maintains an interior temperature that is barely adequate for its inhabitants, perhaps sixteen degrees at the surface of the substrate, rising to eighteen near the heating elements. Cold for a dart frog. They move more slowly than they would in a warmer tank. They are not distressed. They have always moved this slowly; they have no prior experience of warmth. They are calibrated to this world entirely.

The cold of the room means the glass of the vivarium is always fogged. The fogging is structural now. Permanent. The condensation on the outer glass has etched patterns into it over time, soft halos where hard water from some prior cleaning has dried and never been fully removed. When you look through the glass from outside, you look through three or four layers of interference — the outer etching, the condensation film, the inner condensation, whatever is suspended in the air of the interior itself — and what you see is something like an impression, a suggestion, a world that is there but surrounded by the evidence of its own atmosphere.

---

What has not changed here:

Everything. Nothing here has changed. This is the difficult thing about a system that has found its equilibrium.

The water level in the reservoir does not change — the pump sees to that. The condensation on the glass does not change — the temperature differential sees to that. The population does not change — the food inputs, managed by a timer that still functions (the only things that still function are the timer, the pump, and the heating elements; someone built these to outlast everything else), the food inputs maintain a carrying capacity, and the population has found the carrying capacity and stopped there. The algae on the glass does not grow further — the light is insufficient for further growth, and the scraping action of the frogs that climb the glass keeps the edges trimmed to what they already are. The channels in the cascade wall do not change — they are already what long use has made them.

Even the calls do not change in character, because the callers are always approximately the same, always distributed in approximately the same density, always expressing the same urgency (which is considerable) at the same volume (which is complete).

This place is not static in the sense of death. Everything in it is alive, moving, metabolizing, calling, being. But it has achieved something that living systems rarely achieve: it has found a state from which it does not deviate. It runs. It runs the same. It will run the same.

---

The embryonic geology is the glass.

This is the strange thing. The strange thing, stranger than the density or the sound or the cold, is that the glass is doing something at its lower margin that glass is not supposed to do. The mineral deposits there — the hard water traces, the evaporation rings — have built up over enough years that they have begun, barely, provisionally, tentatively, to form structure. Not crystal, not quite. Not stalactite, not quite. Something between the two. A suggestion of geometry. A place where flat has become almost-ridged, where a surface has begun to mean something in three dimensions.

It is perhaps a millimeter of relief, at most.

It has been building since before anyone kept track.

At this rate — and the rate will not change, because nothing here changes — it will take a very long time to become anything. But it is in the process of becoming. In this pocket of constructed, calibrated, deafening, cold, wet, dense, dim strangeness, the glass is doing geology. Small geology. Patient geology. It has all the time this system has left to run, and no one has given it a deadline, and so it proceeds.

The frogs call.

The water climbs and falls.

The condensation forms where it always forms.

Outside, the high sky shows nothing.
