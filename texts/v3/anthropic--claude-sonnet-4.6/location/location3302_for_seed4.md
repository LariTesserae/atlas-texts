# The Hollow City

---

Begin small. Begin with what you can actually see from where you are standing, which is not much.

The light here is close to nothing — not the honest dark of a room with the curtains drawn, but something more negotiated, more arrived-at. A little illumination filters down through layer after living layer above you, and by the time it reaches this level it has been translated so many times — through membrane, through water-film, through chlorophyll and cellulose, through the breathing wet mass of vegetation stacked above — that it arrives as something close to a stain rather than a light. A faint green-grey, the color of old pewter left in a garden. Enough to show you the shape of things. Not enough to show you their surfaces.

So. Shapes, then.

---

You are inside something. This is the first fact you come to understand, and it takes a moment because the inside is not enclosed in any way you have a ready name for. There are no walls of stone, no ceiling of timber. And yet you are unmistakably *within*. The sensation is of a room — perhaps four meters across at its widest, perhaps five — defined entirely by living matter. Overhead, the canopy of vegetation presses down so thickly that it functions as a ceiling, though it moves, constantly, in slow vegetable increments, in responses to pressures you cannot identify. Small repositionings. A tendril finding a new purchase. A broad succulent leaf releasing a bead of collected water that falls straight down and strikes the ground near your foot with a sound like a fingernail on a table.

Around you at roughly chest height, the walls are mosses and liverworts of uncommon density, attached to structures you cannot quite resolve in this light — they seem to be posts or pillars of some kind, old timber perhaps, or something harder, but so thoroughly colonized that the original material is entirely hypothetical. You could press your hand into the wall of this space and sink your fingers to the second knuckle before meeting anything that wasn't alive. The green would close around your hand like a cool compress. You know this without testing it.

The floor is different. Here the vegetation is more negotiated with, more contested. The ground is low and close to the water table — you can tell because the soil, where soil is briefly visible between dense mats of creeping growth, has the gleam of saturation. Water is right there, just below, just under the thin mineral skin of the earth, pressing up. In the small divots where the ground has been disturbed — a footprint, a root's heave — water stands in perfectly level mirrors, catching nothing, reflecting a darkness identical to the darkness above them.

It is cold. Not severely, not dramatically, but the cold has a quality of persistence here, of residency. It has been here longer than anything else. It lives in the moisture, in the condensation that forms on any surface that holds still long enough, in the water that has seeped through layers of vegetation and arrived here at the bottom having shed what little warmth it had absorbed during whatever brief transit it had through something like daylight. Your exhaled breath doesn't quite make a cloud — it's not that cold — but you can feel it leaving you warmly and returning not at all.

---

Now the animals.

This is where the strangeness sharpens.

There are so many of them that sound should be deafening, but it isn't, and this contradiction is the first genuinely unsettling thing about this place. What you hear is a quiet that is not empty — a quiet that is full, that is occupied and breathing. The animals are not still. But they are, almost without exception, silent.

On the vegetation-walls, the movement is continuous. Beetles of a size and variety that suggests this micro-environment has been selecting for something, for a long time, travel across the moss surface in routes that are not random — there is intention in it, or if not intention then a repetition so deep it has worn itself into the shape of intention. They are dark, most of them, and they are wet, because everything here is wet, because the moss releases water constantly in small expressions of saturation, and the beetles carry this water on their shells and it catches even this dim light and makes them briefly flash as they move. Flash and go dark. Flash and go dark. There are hundreds of them. Thousands, perhaps, across all the surfaces you can't quite see.

Lower on the walls, near the waterlogged floor, something else moves that you need a moment to identify. The scale is wrong before you understand it — you think you are seeing the wall itself shift, which would be frightening, but what you are actually seeing is an aggregation of small frogs, each one roughly the size of your thumbnail, each one a different shade of the same color range: brown-green, grey-green, the green of deep water, the brown of wet earth. They are packed, on the lower moss, with the looseness of a crowd that knows it has room but isn't using it. Sparse, but a sparseness of individuals who belong to an enormous population. Some of them breathe. You can see their translucent throats pulse. None of them call. They watch you, possibly. It's difficult to tell where their eyes are in this light, but you have the sensation of being registered.

Above, in the ceiling-canopy, there is movement of a different kind — larger, heavier, causing the vegetation to depress slightly at intervals, weight transferring along woven root-routes and interlocked stems. Something moves up there. Several somethings. Their scale is ambiguous from below; they could be rats, they could be larger than rats, they could be birds that have decided not to fly. Whatever they are, they are careful. Their movement is slow enough to seem considered. They have been here long enough to know how to move here.

Insects occupy the air in numbers that are visible even in near-darkness because of their motion — small clouds of midges that are not quite clouds, because they are too sparse, but that have a cloud's relationship to itself, each individual maintaining its position relative to the others in a structure that breathes in and out, expands and contracts, without any apparent stimulus. They do not make noise you can hear. They are simply present in the air the way the air is present.

In the water-mirror puddles, things move. Very small things. The physics of the surface — surface tension operating, a microorganism community thick enough to have changed the water's properties — means that the movement in the puddle is not quite the movement of something swimming through water. It is slower. More resistant. More deliberate.

---

And the city.

The settlement — call it that — is here, which is the second thing to understand and the second place where understanding requires revision.

It is primitive. It is also, in its way, elaborate. And it is very small.

The posts you thought might be original material in the walls — they are not original material. They were placed. They are young enough that they haven't fully rotted, haven't fully been incorporated; you can, in places where the moss has not yet fully colonized, see the marks of tools on them. Simple tools. Blades that left facets rather than smooth cuts. They were driven into this soft ground and then abandoned to the vegetation, which has taken them thoroughly, and now they are infrastructure of a strange kind — neither purely built nor purely grown, the building and the growing in process of becoming indistinguishable.

There are, you begin to notice as your eyes adjust to their own maximum adjustment, other structures. Small walls — perhaps knee-high at their tallest — of stacked stones that have not been shaped but have been selected: flat stones, stones that will lie stable, placed with care in configurations that suggest purpose without revealing it clearly. The stones are young, geologically — recently arrived from wherever water or pressure brought them up, their edges not yet rounded, their surfaces still holding the slight roughness of freshly-broken mineral. They are covered on their upper faces with the same wet moss that covers everything, but this moss is thinner on the stones, shallower-rooted, still negotiating.

Between these walls, the ground has been compressed in a way that is subtly different from the surrounding ground — a path, perhaps, or the memory of one, or the intention of one. A path that leads nowhere obvious. A path that exists at a scale for moving through this pocket of space without disturbing the frogs.

Hanging from the vegetation ceiling at one point — and this is the strangest thing, the thing that lingers — a small basket woven from plant material, hanging by a twist of vine, containing something you cannot identify in this darkness. It is too small to be a cage. It is too purposeful to be accidental growth. It is the size of a cupped pair of hands and it is motionless except for the very slow rotation that means the vine from which it hangs is slowly untwisting. It will finish untwisting and then twist back the other way and the basket will reverse its rotation and it will do this until the vine gives way or until someone comes back and rehangs it and the process begins again.

No one is here. Or perhaps: no one is visible. The distinction feels important.

---

The sound.

The sound is the quiet underneath which everything is happening.

Water falls in single drops at irregular intervals — from the ceiling-canopy, from the wet walls, finding paths down through layers of vegetation and arriving at the floor with a sound that is too small to be called a drip, that is more like the word *drip* said very softly by someone who is mostly thinking about something else. This happens continuously, which means at any given moment one of these sounds is occurring somewhere in the space, but they do not stack into a sound of rain. They remain individual. They remain separate arrivals.

The vegetation moves. The vegetation moving has no sound you can locate but has a sound you can feel — a sub-audible friction, the breathing press and release of living mass adjusting to itself.

Something in the ceiling moves and there is a brief, low creak — not a branch-creak, something more careful than that, something that stops immediately and is not repeated.

The frogs say nothing.

The beetles' feet on moss make no sound you can hear.

The midges are silent.

The water below the floor is silent.

---

The processes.

Despite the quiet — *inside* the quiet — this space is not still. The ecology here is in motion at every scale. You can understand this without seeing it directly: there is a decomposition happening in the floor-mat, complex and active, the wet cellulose being broken down by organisms that are doing it right now, converting the dead portions of the living mat into something that feeds the living portions. The beetles are part of this. The frogs are part of this, eating what the decomposition attracts, being watched in turn by whatever moves in the ceiling. The moisture cycle is active: the vegetation absorbs, holds, releases, the released water traveling down, collecting, pressing up through the soil, rising into the root systems of the vegetation, being absorbed again. A circuit with no break. A wheel turning slowly in the dark.

The new stone walls are being incorporated. The moss is advancing across them at a rate invisible in any individual moment but inevitable in the pattern. The timber posts are being consumed in a process so slow it feels like permanence but is not permanence. The small basket rotates. The midges redistribute. A frog moves two centimeters to the left for reasons of its own.

Something in the ceiling has decided to move again. More carefully this time.

---

This place was found, or made, or found and then made. The distinction is not quite clear and may not be important to whoever made it. It exists in a fold of the larger world — tucked under the weight of vegetation, at the bottom of something, at the cold wet floor of the living mass above. It is embedded in a settlement of some kind, because the built things are here and they are too deliberate to have arrived without intention, but the settlement above or around this pocket is not visible from within it, and this pocket may not be easily visible from the settlement.

There is the feeling — strong, persistent, not quite explicable — that this is not a place for humans to be primary. That it was made for purposes the maker understood in relation to the other lives here. That the basket was not made for a human to open. That the paths on the ground were not made for feet the size of yours.

The frogs watch. Or do not watch. The beetles flash and go dark.

The cold sits in the water just below the floor and does not move.

Overhead, the weight of the living world presses down.

The space holds.
