# The Hanging City of Muttered Stone

---

Begin with the sound, because everything else will be harder to find without it.

The sound is deafening in the way that a held organ note in a cathedral is deafening — not percussive, not violent, but *total*, filling every cavity of the body until you forget that silence is a thing that ever existed. It is the sound of water. All water. All the water that the mountain is holding and has always been holding, moving through stone channels so old that the stone has stopped resisting and begun *assisting*, shaped itself into organ pipes, into flutes, into sympathetic strings. The water does not fall — or it falls so constantly, from so many directions, at so many scales, that falling and not-falling have ceased to be different states. The mountain hisses. The mountain roars. The mountain speaks in a continuous chord that has no beginning and will have no end, and it has been speaking this chord since before anyone was here to call it speech.

Step back from the sound, or try to, and begin to understand what you are looking at.

---

## The Structure of the Place

The elevation is extreme. You know this not because you feel lightheaded — the air here is dense with moisture, thick, almost subtropical in its weight despite the altitude — but because of what you can see, which is too much. Ridges extend in every direction like the knuckles of a buried hand, and between them, deep green falls away into haze that is not distance-haze but vapor-haze, the accumulated exhalation of a landscape that sweats continuously. The sky above is not sky so much as light — pale, diffuse, sourceless, arriving from everywhere at once, flattening shadow without eliminating it, turning every surface into something that glows faintly from within. There is no visible sun. There has probably not been a visible sun here for longer than the city has existed.

The peaks around you — and there are peaks, multiple, running in a long corrugated spine — are old. Their age is not a guess; it is a quality of their surfaces, the way old wood has a quality of density and darkness that new wood doesn't. These peaks have been eroded to their settled shapes, their final shapes. They are not sharp. They are not dramatic in the way of young mountains. They are massive in the way of old things that have had time to become completely themselves: rounded at their extreme summits, yes, but not gentle — the roundness is the roundness of a shoulder muscle, a hard curve under skin, and the rock faces that drop from those summits drop steeply into the green before leveling into terraces and saddles. Every cliff face weeps. This is not metaphor. The rock is dark with permanent moisture and the moisture collects and falls from every ledge and overhang in curtains, in threads, in a continuous silver fray that is part of the roar.

The vegetation covers everything that is not vertical stone, and some things that are. It is jungle in the way that high jungle is different from low jungle — denser in some registers, more tightly arranged, the trees shorter and more muscled, their canopies not the great spreading parasols of lowland forest but close-packed, interlocking, dripping. Mosses three inches thick cover the trunks so completely that the bark beneath is hypothetical. Ferns emerge from the mosses. Bromeliads emerge from the ferns. Everything is layered on everything else; the floor is not ground but accumulated life, springy underfoot, giving, warm. The heat is enormous and wet and it presses against every exposed surface. There is no wind that you could call wind, only the slow constant exhalation of the vegetation and the spray-drift from the falling water. To breathe here is to drink. Your clothes are wet. They have always been wet. You have always been wet.

Between and among the ridges and terraces, the city grows.

---

## The City

It has no name in any language that records names. To name a place you must be able to stand outside it, but this place cannot be stood outside; it fills the available mountain as water fills a vessel, and there is no vantage from which it resolves into a shape that would hold still long enough to be called anything.

But city is the right word. Look at how the stone is cut. Look at how the terraces are not nature's terraces but increments of a larger intention: broad flat planes, level, precise, running in long arcs along the contour lines of each ridge like the rings of a tree sliced horizontal. The walls that hold the terraces are fitted stone, dark with age and moss, seamless, each block shaped so carefully that the moss grows *over* the joints without interrupting them. No mortar. The weight of the stone holds the stone. The city's walls are as settled as the mountain's walls; they have been here together so long that the distinction between constructed and geological is a question of geology.

There is no metal here. No glass, no fired clay, no tool-marks of the kind that tools leave. Whatever was used to shape the stone shaped it cleanly and then left no trace of itself. The buildings — and they are buildings, there is no doubt — are stone throughout. Their roofs are stone, corbeled into false arches or simply cantilevered, and the jungle has colonized those roofs so thoroughly that from above the city might read as pure forest. From within, walking the narrow paths between walls, you see the buildings as dense presences: rooms opening onto rooms, passages turning without warning, stairways cut directly into the terrace walls ascending to more terraces and more buildings and more stairways. The scale of the whole is regional. A valley's worth, a valley-and-a-half. Thousands of structures if you could count them, which you cannot, because counting requires that the things being counted hold still.

And here is the first difficulty.

---

## What the City Does

The city does not change. Understand this carefully, because it is where the ground shifts.

In a normal landscape, stillness is a relative term. A rock is still but the light moves across it. A tree is still but its leaves tremble. A building is still but its shadow rotates. Here: nothing moves that is not water. The water moves with absolute dedication and has always moved and will always move and this is not change; the water's movement is part of the city's stasis, as the spinning of electrons is part of the stillness of a table. Set aside the water, which is not separate anyway, and you find that the city does not change. The shadow does not move. The moss does not grow. The blossoms on the climbing plants that cover the upper walls — and they blossom, extravagantly, in colors that are too saturated to be named with confidence — those blossoms do not open or close. They bloom in permanent mid-bloom. The buds are always just-opening. The fallen petals are always mid-fall, caught in the humid air at heights that petals should not remain, distributed through the atmosphere of the city in a slow dense snow that does not land and does not rise, that has been falling since the city was new and will be falling when the mountain is finally sand.

The light does not shift. There is no morning here that is different from noon that is different from evening. The pale sourceless glow maintains itself, maintains itself, maintains itself.

The animals — and you will not find animals — left or never arrived or are implied by the city's structures (the narrow high passages that might be for birds, the pools at the base of each major terrace that might be water features or might be feeding stations) but are simply absent. No birds. No insects. No distant call or rustle in the canopy. Only the plants, which are everywhere and in which nothing moves. And the water, which is everywhere and in which everything moves, without pause, without alteration.

You begin to understand, slowly, that *you* are what is moving here. Not the city.

---

## The Deeper Strangeness

Walk deeper into the city, which takes a long time because the city is large and the paths follow the walls and the walls follow the terraces and the terraces were not laid out for speed. The buildings press closer. The canopy — the real canopy of the jungle growing from every surface including the roofs — closes above you until the diffuse light is twice filtered, emerald, thick, warm as the water running down every wall beside you. The sound is not louder but it is closer, as though the city concentrates it, channels it through the stone corridors until it has direction as well as volume, arrives from ahead of you, from behind, from below through the path itself.

The buildings have interiors. You can see through the doorways, which have no doors, only the rectangular dark that doorways make. Inside: more stone, more moss, chambers of different sizes, some with the low pools that might be ritual or might be practical, some with stone platforms at the right height to be beds or altars or tables — the function is absent and the form alone remains, and form without function becomes something else. You do not enter any of them. Not yet, and perhaps not ever, because of the following:

The interior of the city does not quite maintain the same rules as the exterior.

Outside: nothing moves but water. Inside — through the doorways, seen at the boundary, not entered — things are different in a way that is difficult to articulate because it involves your peripheral vision and peripheral vision is unreliable, everyone knows this. But what peripheral vision reports, consistently, every doorway, is: that the interior of the building you are passing is larger than the exterior. Considerably larger. The rooms visible through the door extend further than the building's walls permit. The ceiling is higher than the roof allows. This does not change when you look directly at it; it persists under direct observation, which is when you know it is not peripheral vision at all. The stone buildings are bigger inside than outside and the amount by which they are bigger is not consistent — this building by a little, that building by an enormous amount, that one apparently containing a space the size of a plaza, dark, enormous, moss-floored, with the sound coming from somewhere within it as if the sound originates there, as if all the water's voice is centered in that one interior room, which according to the exterior of the building should be the size of a closet.

The fallen petals are still falling at your eye level, permanently suspended, permanently falling. They are very close to your face and they are, in their colors, impossible: the red is redder than red, the white is white in a way that suggests white has been under-performed everywhere else you have ever been. They do not touch you. You have been walking among them for some time and they do not touch you.

---

## The Logic of the Place

The city is in equilibrium. This is the important thing; this is the frame within which the strangeness becomes not random but *necessary*. The city has reached a state. It was built — over a long time, with enormous effort, with a precision that implies tools but leaves no tool-marks, by people or by something that organized like people — and then it arrived at the state it was trying to arrive at, and it has been in that state ever since.

The state is this: the city and the mountain and the water and the plants have all become continuous with each other. The walls grow from the rock and the moss grows from the walls and the buildings grow from the moss and the water moves through all of it, and none of these is separate. The interior spaces are bigger than the exterior because the interior spaces extend into whatever is adjacent to space — the walls, the mountain's interior, the dimension that space has when it has been accumulating long enough that it spills. You can be inside and find you are in a room that also contains the mountain. This is what the builders were building toward.

They are not here to have arrived at it. They finished and then — and this is the sense the place gives, not fact but sense — stepped into one of the rooms that was bigger on the inside, and kept going, and did not come back out the way they went in, or came out somewhere else, or went far enough in that *out* stopped having a meaning. No bones. No artifacts, because everything is an artifact and nothing is identified as specifically theirs rather than the city's. No evidence of ending. Only evidence of completion.

---

## To Stand There

You are wet. You are always wet. The heat does not feel like heat after a while; it feels like being held, firmly, by a presence that is not unkind. The sound is so complete that you have stopped hearing it in the way you hear things and started feeling it in the way you feel your own heartbeat: always there, part of the condition of being in your body in this place.

The petals fall around you without touching you. The city extends in every direction further than you can verify. The buildings offer you their impossible interiors, patient, unhurried, neither beckoning nor forbidding. The mountain weeps from every pore. The plants do not move. Nothing moves but water, and you.

And you are beginning to wonder if the place distinguishes between those two things — the water and you — as carefully as you do. The water passes through. You are passing through. Perhaps this is enough to be, here, in a place that has already passed through everything it needed to and come out the other side into permanence, into the still point of the thing that finished arriving.

The sound holds you.

The light does not change.

The petals do not land.
