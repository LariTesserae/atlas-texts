# The Newborn Peaks

---

The mountains are still deciding what shape to be.

That is the first and overriding fact of this place — not that it is high, though it is extraordinarily high, but that it is *unfinished*. The rock does not have the dignity of age. It has not been scoured or smoothed or taught patience by ten thousand winters. It came up from below in living memory, geologically speaking, and it is still arriving. The ridgelines crack and shift. Faces of raw basalt split open along fractures that did not exist yesterday and will be wider tomorrow. You can hear it, sometimes — a low, reluctant groan like a door that has never been opened being forced — and then silence again, and then the silence proves to have been the brief pause between one rupture and the next.

You are very high. The air is thin enough that the darkness feels different from the darkness lower down — thinner too, somehow, a darkness that has less body, that presses less. Far above, stars are occasionally visible through what passes for sky, but they don't stay visible long. Not because of clouds exactly, though there is something like clouds: plumes of particulate matter, mineral dust and gas and the exhalations of fresh rock cooling at speed, drifting in slow lateral columns that obscure and then reveal. The light from those stars, when it reaches the ground, is brief and sourceless — it illuminates nothing in particular, falls on the wrong things, does not explain the terrain so much as suggest it. You see a wall of something. You see, perhaps, the pale underbelly of a ledge. Then you don't.

It is warm.

This is the wrongness you feel before you can name it: warmth, at this altitude, in this darkness. Your body expected cold. The cold never arrives. The heat comes from below — from fractures in the new rock where magmatic pressure still communicates upward, slow and stubborn, not dramatic enough to produce visible lava here (the venting is happening elsewhere, somewhere below in the dark, somewhere you cannot see but can, in certain wind conditions, smell: a sulfurous mineral breath that is more presence than odor) — and it radiates from the surfaces you touch if you touch them, and it keeps the air from being what it should be. You sweat. You feel faintly confused, the way bodies feel when their predictions fail.

---

The terrain is difficult to describe because it is not yet committed to any one form.

Imagine a field of raw geological intention — not rock so much as the *idea* of rock being rapidly prototyped. Spires emerge from plateaus, but the spires are not the graceful needles that millions of years of wind might produce. They are blunt, enthusiastic, a little naïve. They go straight up because up is what they know. Some have already broken along internal stress lines they didn't know they had, and the breaks are fresh — the exposed faces white-grey and almost luminous in what little light there is, sharper-edged than glass, dangerous in the way that only new things are dangerous, without the rounding-off that signals accommodation. The broken pieces lie close to their origins. Nothing has moved them far. There has not been time.

Between the spires are flat expanses of cooling surface rock that looks, in the near-dark, something like hammered metal — dark grey going to charcoal, with a faint iridescence where mineral compounds are doing chemistry in the heat. These surfaces are not stable. You would not cross them at a run. But they are large, spanning perhaps the area of several fields, and walking them slowly, feeling each step, you would find them mostly solid, mostly trustworthy, occasionally not — a thin-skinned bubble, a pocket of still-soft material beneath a cooled crust that gives slightly, just slightly, underfoot.

The scale of it is regional. This is not a single mountain. It is a range being born — a complex of peaks and subsidiary ridges and lateral formations that extends, as far as you can see in the dark, in all directions. The peaks are too many to count and still multiplying, or so it seems — you hear new cracking, new arrival, from directions you cannot identify. It is difficult to establish a center. The edges, wherever they are, must be far.

Scattered across this immensity, sparse as stars in a rich but not crowded sky: structures.

---

The structures demand attention, though they are few.

None of them are finished either — and here is where you have to ask yourself whether that is coincidence. Three that you can identify with any confidence are roofless. One is only a foundation, a rough rectangle of worked stone (worked stone, not raw extrusion — someone cut this) that sits slightly askew on the still-shifting ground, already being pulled out of true. Another is a series of walls, two or three courses high, the mortar between the blocks (mortar: so there was craft, so there was mixture, so there was intention and planning) already cracking where the ground has moved beneath it since construction. The walls enclose nothing now. They barely enclose space. They suggest a room that never became a room.

The worked stone of these structures is a different color from the raw geological material surrounding it — lighter, having come from somewhere else, quarried and carried, though from where is not apparent. The carrying would have been extraordinary. These heights are not casual. Whatever or whoever brought worked stone up to this altitude either had purpose so strong it overcame geography, or came from somewhere higher still (though you can see no higher, the peaks above going eventually into the particulate murk), or — and this thought surfaces slowly, like something heavy rising — the structures were already at altitude when the geology began. Were built here before *here* existed. Were sitting on stable lowland when the upthrust began and were simply carried up, floors becoming walls becoming rubble as the ground beneath decided to become a mountain.

That would make the structures older than the mountains.

You sit with this.

The mountains are new. The structures, if this theory is right, are much older. This is a place where the ancient has been elevated involuntarily, carried upward by forces indifferent to it, like objects on a rising tide.

---

There is almost no life here.

Almost — because there is something. Not animal, not plant, not exactly anything in the familiar catalog. At the margins of the cooling rock surfaces, where heat and mineral gases create microenvironments that violate the expected chemistry of altitude, there are formations that may or may not be biological. Crusts. Thin films. A pale branching trace along a crack face that might be a primitive chemosynthetic organism finding purchase in a world one step ahead of it, claiming territory before the territory has finished forming. If these things are alive, they are alive in the way that the earliest life was alive: barely committed to the idea, still working out the terms.

They make no sound.

Nothing here makes much sound. The great geological movement — all that turbulence, all that crack and shift and arriving rock — is mostly infrasonic or transmitted through the ground rather than the air. The air carries only: the occasional distant deep groan of major fracture. The movement of the mineral-laden wind through the new and irregular channels the peaks create, a sound almost but not quite like breathing. Sometimes a high, faint tone that you cannot place — too regular for geology, too intermittent for wind, too present to be imagination, though it might be imagination. You stop. You listen. It stops. You wait long enough to feel foolish. It resumes, briefly, in a slightly different direction.

The silence between these sounds is the defining feature. It is a silence with weight and texture — the silence of a place too new for acoustic history, where no sound has bounced off any surface long enough to create familiarity, where every sound is a first occurrence.

---

Something about this place insists on future tense.

Other landscapes carry their history — erosion is autobiography, a worn stone is a stone that has told you something about itself. Here there is almost no erosion. No autobiography. The rock surfaces have not decided yet what will happen to them. They do not have grooves or channels or the patient sculpting of weather. They have only the raw evidence of emergence: the fresh face, the sharp edge, the unmodified mineral truth of a thing that has just come into being and has not yet been argued with by time.

Somewhere below, out of sight and far down, water presumably exists. But not here. The chemistry of this place is dry and mineral and new — calcium silicates, various oxides, compounds that water would eventually have opinions about, but water has not arrived yet and the temperature ensures that even if moisture were somehow present it would stay as vapor, rise, join the slow plumes. The heat from below keeps this place dry in the same way an oven is dry: not absent of potential moisture but converting it, continuously, to something else.

You are standing on the edge of a newly formed ridge. The drop below you is not visible — darkness and particulate haze have swallowed it — but you know it is there, and that it is immense, and that the walls below are new and raw and beautiful in the way that only unprecedented things are beautiful: not yet catalogued, not yet comprehended, already astonishing.

The ridge creaks beneath you. Not loudly. Not alarmingly, exactly. But with intention. It is making more of itself, even now. You are standing on something that has not finished arriving.

You are very warm. The stars are briefly visible, then gone. Somewhere in the dark, new rock is pressing upward through old rock's place, and the old rock is only now learning that it has been displaced.

There is the high, faint tone again.

You listen.
