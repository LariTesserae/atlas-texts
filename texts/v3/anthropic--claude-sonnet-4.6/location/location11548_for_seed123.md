# The Pipe Hollow

---

You find it by accident, always. There is no path to it, only a gap between two corrugated walls of a decommissioned water treatment outbuilding, a gap barely wide enough to turn sideways through, and then you are inside something that has no business existing inside anything.

The space is roughly the size of a generous living room. Maybe twelve meters at its longest, seven or eight across, low-ceilinged by a web of old iron pipe that runs at head height in every direction — horizontal, branching, occasionally looping back into itself for no visible reason. The pipes are original to some industrial water-management system that was either abandoned or forgotten, or both in sequence. They are packed so tightly overhead that direct sunlight enters only in thin shifting needles, and the floor below them is a kind of perpetual almost-noon, warm and diffuse, the light bouncing off pale oxidized metal until it arrives at your eyes having touched everything twice.

The floor is concrete, but the concrete has been here long enough that it has become something else. Long enough that cracks have formed and widened and become jurisdictions. Long enough that what grows in those cracks has divided the surface into territories, and the territories have further subdivided, and now the whole floor is a dense packed map of competing small lives rendered in green and rust and near-black and a particular shade of amber that has no good name.

---

Start with the pipes, because they are what makes the place.

There are dozens of them — hundreds, maybe, if you count every branch. Iron pipes from some earlier decade of industrial confidence, when the factory or plant or pumping station (you will find no clear indication which) was in operation and someone believed in the permanence of things. Most are four to eight centimeters in diameter. A few are much wider, forearm-thick, running as mains along the longer axis of the hollow. They branch and rejoin in patterns that suggest they were added to incrementally, each new junction solving a problem that the previous junction had created, the whole system accumulating logic the way old buildings accumulate rooms — not designed, grown.

The pipes are not dead. This is the first strange thing.

They produce condensation. In the warmth of the hollow — and it is warm in here, warmer than outside, as if the enclosed space and the pale-reflected light have been saving heat like a pocket saves change — the metal surfaces are just cool enough against the ambient air to sweat. Not everywhere. Not constantly. But in patches and periods, the underside of a pipe will bead with moisture and the beads will travel the curve of the pipe and gather at low points and drip. The drip schedule is not synchronized. Various pipes drip at various rates, and the combined sound is a soft irregular percussion: *tik*, silence, *tik tik*, longer silence, *tik*, a small *pat* where a drop falls on a broad leaf rather than bare concrete.

It is otherwise quiet.

The pipes are covered. This is what you see when you look up and let your eyes adjust: not bare iron but a dense upholstered thickness of living material. Mosses colonize the upper surfaces where condensation pools the longest. Lichens — grey and sea-green and one mustard-yellow variety with a lacquered look — occupy the drier sections, cementing themselves in thin concentric rings outward from their centers. On the wider pipes, where there is more horizontal surface, communities of small organisms have established themselves in layers: lichen below, moss above, and threading through both, pale root-structures from who knows what plant whose leaves are somewhere in the spaces between pipes, seeking whatever light trickles in.

The effect, looking up, is of a ceiling that is entirely occupied. Not decorated — occupied. Every centimeter of iron is doing something, hosting something, has been claimed by something with a prior and ongoing use for that centimeter. The pipes have become a substrate. The original industrial purpose is now infrastructure for a different system entirely.

---

What grows on the floor is stranger.

The concrete has cracked along stress lines into irregular polygons, and in the cracks: a dark peaty accumulation of years of dripped condensation and fallen biological matter and windblown particles that found their way through the gap in the corrugated wall and settled. In this crack-soil, things grow. Sparse is the right word — sparse up top, meaning not many plants rise more than a few centimeters above the concrete surface. There are small ferns with fronds so fine they look like exhalations. There is a low creeping succulent, wrong-looking for the moisture level, as if it arrived as a seed from somewhere drier and compromised. There are mosses that extend from the cracks onto the concrete surface itself, wherever a pipe's drip has made the surface receptive.

But below the surface, if you could see it, the picture would be different. The roots and fungal networks in those crack-channels are dense. You can infer this from how the plants behave — the competition is clearly happening below-ground, invisible, as the above-ground portions of different species often stop exactly at the edge of the next species' territory with an abruptness that suggests something definite and contested is happening in the dark.

The fungal presence makes itself known in ways that don't require roots. Along the base of the exterior wall, where two surfaces meet and moisture accumulates by capillary action and the light is dimmest, bracket formations push out in overlapping shelves, pale cream shading to tan at the margins, each one perfectly semicircular, each one a few centimeters above the previous. They fruit rapidly — you can see it if you watch, or rather you can see evidence of it: last week's brackets have already dried to papery rigidity while this week's are still soft, and they are close enough that the fresh ones partially overlap the old.

---

The fauna does not announce itself and then it is everywhere.

The pipes are highways. Once you see this, you cannot un-see it. The horizontal runs are the main roads. The branches are exits. The whole network overhead is a city for things that do not need much vertical clearance and benefit from the warmth and moisture and concealment: beetles of several kinds, their shells reading as dark or iridescent depending on the needle of light that catches them; spiders that build webs not in the open spaces between pipes but *within* the moss covering, so that the webs are interior structures, accessible only through small gaps in the moss, trapping the even smaller things that live inside the moss itself; a species of small dark cricket (or cricket-equivalent; you cannot be certain) that moves in brief decisive bursts and is still so often and so completely that the movement, when it happens, seems like an error in perception.

On the floor, between the fern-fronds, there are isopods. Many of them. The cracked peat of the joints is exactly what they require, and they have taken to it comprehensively, rolling into their defensive spheres when disturbed and then unrolling with a kind of stubborn deliberateness, continuing their business. Their business is eating what is already eaten. They are part of the system that processes the fallen material and returns it to the crack-soil, completing the loop that the condensation and the plants and the pipe-organisms have set in motion.

There are two small lizards. Perhaps more, but you see two. They are the warmth-seekers; in this enclosed space the temperature is reliable, which for a lizard is nearly everything. One is currently on a wide pipe at about chest height, motionless in a band of diffuse light, its throat pulsing. The other is in a crack in the floor, visible only as a tail disappearing into shadow. They eat crickets and beetles. They are the apex of this particular world, and they hold that position without drama, simply by being present and efficient and taking what is offered.

---

Built into one wall — the longer interior wall, which may have once been the exterior of the original building — there is a meter-wide section where the concrete has been patched with something different, a later material, applied by someone with no particular skill. In the patch, a single pipe penetrates: larger than the others, maybe fifteen centimeters across, capped now with a rusted iron plug. The plug is not entirely flush. A narrow gap around its circumference is sealed imperfectly, and from this gap, on a schedule you cannot determine, a faint mechanical sound comes — a low shuddering vibration, more felt than heard, like something very large and very far away continuing to operate. It lasts two or three seconds. Then nothing. Then, later, again.

You cannot establish what it is connected to. The larger structure outside is derelict. There is no electrical line you can trace. Whatever system this pipe was part of, the system has not entirely stopped; it has become ambiguous. Something downstream or upstream of this place is still, occasionally, cycling.

The lizard on the wide pipe does not react when the vibration comes. It has heard it before.

---

The light in the hollow shifts not because the sun moves significantly in the time you spend here but because the pipes conduct thermal air currents that disturb the hanging moss-filaments between them, and when the filaments move, the light-needles move, and the whole interior of the space performs a slow continuous mild transformation, nothing settling, everything repositioning in increments too small to track but too persistent to ignore. The concrete alternately appears wet and dry as the light slides across it. The lizard's scales alternate between matte and faintly metallic. The bracket fungi's pale cream surfaces take on different temperatures of shadow.

It is active, this place. Nothing in it is resting from anything permanent; everything is in process, ticking through its particular cycle, occupying its particular pipe-length or crack-territory or shadow-pocket. The condensation accumulates and drips and the drip feeds the moss and the moss feeds the beetle and the beetle is eaten by the lizard and the lizard basks and the isopods process what falls and what is excreted and the fungus fruits and the cricket moves in its startling bursts and stops.

You are the only thing in here that does not have a clear function in the cycle. This is noticeable. The lizard on the pipe has turned its head to include you in its field of vision. It does not flee. You are warm and you block some light and that is most of what you are, in here — you are a temporary obstruction, a warm object of uncertain function, and the place is waiting, with the patience of places that have been tending themselves for a long time, for you to leave so that it can continue.
