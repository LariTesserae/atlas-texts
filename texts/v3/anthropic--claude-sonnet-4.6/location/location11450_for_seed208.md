# The Mud Flats of the Last Continent

---

The world here has been working on itself for longer than there is language to describe. Whatever sharp things once existed — ridges, scarps, the bones of mountains — have been rubbed away down to this: a vast low plain at or barely above the level of standing water, extending in every direction until the atmosphere absorbs it. The horizon is not a line. It is a gradation. Land becomes mist becomes sky by stages too gentle to locate precisely.

The ground is ancient clay, compressed to something near-ceramic in its deeper layers, but the surface is never quite dry. Water is always near. Sometimes it stands in thin sheets across the flats, half a finger deep and perfectly still except where something moves beneath. Sometimes it films the clay's surface like a second skin, so that walking across it — if anything walks — leaves prints that fill and mirror the sky before they close. The clay is gray-brown, the color of old pewter or wet ash, patterned by a billion drying cycles into the finest imaginable craze of cracks, each one a record, all of them together forming a script too vast and too ancient to be read.

Beneath the surface skin, three or four inches down, the clay goes soft again. Pockets of water. Lenses of accumulated moisture following seams laid down in some forgotten geology. Step wrong and you feel the ground flex under you, a slow reluctant give that takes a few seconds to register, like pressing on a bruise. The cracks overhead on the dry skin tell you nothing about what's below.

There are no plants.

Let that settle.

This is not a landscape between growing seasons, not a landscape waiting. Nothing is waiting. The ground is not between states. This is its state. What grows here is not growing in any sense the word usually carries: there are thick bacterial mats in the shallower water-surfaces, orange and black and a dark red that could be mistaken for rust, lying flat as paint across the clay. Colonies of something simpler than moss, something that processes sulfur and iron and does not need the sun in any particular way. They are not soft. Under the finger they feel like dense felt, or like the dry cap of an old mushroom. They do not smell biological. They smell mineral — like the inside of a struck flint, like wet iron left standing.

The water-filled depressions between the higher clay pans host more of this life, if life is the word. Filamentous orange and yellow structures just below the surface of the standing water, not quite attached to the bottom, drifting in whatever small current exists. They are neither beautiful nor ugly in themselves. They are ancient-looking. They appear to belong here the way the clay belongs here: as things that have simply always been, that outlasted more complicated arrangements.

---

The scale of the place is deeply felt rather than easily demonstrated.

You understand it gradually. You look across the flat and there is something there, a kilometer away, maybe two — a low darkening, a change in how the light sits. You walk toward it and it does not resolve quickly. What it turns out to be is another region of bacterial mat, larger, covering several hundred square meters of the clay in dark red-black. It is not remarkable when you reach it. But the walk told you something: this is a large place. There is a lot of it. The same low color, the same low geometry, in every direction, all the way to where it disappears.

Elevation changes exist but they are minute. An inch. Three inches. A ridge of upstanding clay four inches high and forty meters long that once — ten thousand years ago, a hundred thousand — was something else. The erosion here is the erosion of finished things. Nothing is being eroded anymore. There is no energy left in the land itself to work with. What remains is what the water and the slow chemistry have been unable to remove, and that remainder is vanishingly small. The oldest stable state. A world that has found its floor and is resting on it.

---

There are structures.

They are not impressive. They are not meant to be found — or they were not placed with finding in mind, or the people who placed them have long since ceased to care about that. A few of them are still recognizable as constructions: flat stones stacked three and four high, the stones themselves sourced from somewhere far from here because this flat produces nothing stackable. The stacks are maybe knee height. The stones are not dressed, not shaped beyond the selection of flat ones. They stand at intervals across the flat that suggest no immediate pattern, but that might — over a long enough view — suggest a former shoreline, or a former path, or a former boundary of something.

Some stones have been placed on their own, single and flat, flush with the clay surface, laid to cover something or to mark something with a gesture no one now can interpret. A few of these are cracked across. One has been pushed partially up out of level by some slow ground-movement and stands at an angle, as though it tried to become a standing stone and stopped halfway.

There is, at one location in the wide middle distance, a low structure of woven material — reeds or stripped stems of something similar, gathered from elsewhere. It is partially collapsed. The weavework is still visible: rough but coherent, the work of hands that knew how to make a frame, how to lash material into form. What it held, sheltered, stored — unknown. It is now a low brown shape against the gray clay, about the size of a large dog, pressed down but not dissolved. The damp has preserved it and softened it equally. It has the look of something that gave up trying to be upright many years ago and accepted its new configuration.

There may be others like it further across the flat. In the dim light and mist, things at distance are shapes only. Shapes might be structures. Might be geology. The eye cannot arbitrate.

---

The light.

There is light, but it is doing the minimum. The sky is a uniform gray that is not stormy, not dramatic — just sealed. Clouds indistinguishable from haze, haze indistinguishable from low mist, low mist indistinguishable from the air itself. The light that falls from this sky is diffuse and directionless and gives no shadows. Nothing casts a shadow here. This has the effect of making everything equally legible and equally dull: you can see the distant structures, the mats, the cracks in the clay, all with the same flat clarity. Nothing is lit. Nothing is hidden. Everything sits in the same gray-white ambient, and the absence of shadow makes the whole plain look slightly unreal, like a very large and very careful painting that forgot to include shading.

The cool air and the wet ground make the light visible in a second way: small wisps and tendrils of mist rise from the warmest of the bacterial mats, from the margins of the standing water, from any slight depression that has held the night's warmth better than the rest. These do not accumulate into fog. They rise a foot, two feet, and are absorbed. They are the only vertical motion in the landscape. They make the flat plain seem to breathe.

---

The sound.

Here is the thing. Here is the central thing.

The sound is enormous.

This must be understood before anything else about this place can be properly understood, because the sound is not incidental. It is not background. It is the governing condition. It is louder than you expect at every distance and it does not stop. It comes from the fauna, and the fauna are everywhere — not visible, mostly, but everywhere.

They are in the water. They are in the mud. They are along whatever passes for margins. And what they are doing, all of them, constantly, in overlapping waves that build rather than cancel, is this: they are calling.

Frogs, or frog-like animals — something at that level of organization, nothing stranger, but in numbers that defy casual understanding. They are in every depression, every sheet of water, every pocket of moisture in the clay. Their calls vary — some low and resonant, a sound you feel in the sternum more than hear with the ear; some higher and rhythmic, a fast repeated pulse; some a long drawn-out trill that sweeps upward in pitch before falling off. They do not call in sequence. They do not take turns. They call across each other, all at once, continuous, the calls so dense that individual voices cease to be individually meaningful and become texture, become weather, become the defining physical property of the air.

The sound carries and carries and carries across the flat plain because there is nothing to interrupt it. No trees. No hills. No walls. It refracts slightly off the standing water. It seems to rise from the ground itself. You feel, standing here in the dim wet air, that the sound is not being made at a particular place — it is ambient, it is the property of the atmosphere, it came with the landscape.

There are other sounds inside the main sound. Insects, if they are insects — a high layered shimmer of wing-noise that sits above the frog-chorus and fills the upper registers. Something from the water, intermittently: a plop, a splash, a heavy movement that sends rings across a standing pool before the surface re-seals to its mirror calm. Occasionally the ceramic-snap of clay thermal-cycling, making a quiet sharp sound that is immediately swallowed.

And wind. There is wind across this flat: low and steady, not dramatic, not enough to be uncomfortable, but persistent. It makes sound at nothing because there is nothing for it to sound at — no leaves, no reeds of height, no edges to whistle around. It moves across the bacterial mats and makes a sound like a long slow exhale and crosses the standing water and makes no sound at all and continues, becomes the back of the sound rather than part of it.

To stand here and try to think is to understand the limits of thought. The noise fills every available space, including internal space. There is no silence to retreat into. There has probably not been silence here in a very long time.

---

The animals themselves.

They are largely hidden. The frogs or near-frogs are in the water and partly in the mud at the water's edge, visible when you approach as a sudden perturbation — the water erupting in small panicked geometries, bodies launching and vanishing. They are not large. They are the yellow-brown of the clay, the brown-green of the bacterial mats. They have been selected, over however many generations, to look exactly like this place, and they do. You could be standing among thousands and see almost none.

But movement is everywhere, seen at the periphery, resolved to nothing when you look directly. The plain is alive in the way that dense simple ecosystems are alive: not with large visible presences but with a constant furtive motion just below the threshold of clear observation, as if the ground itself is restless.

Things move beneath the shallow water. Longer things. They leave wakes.

Whether these are predators to the smaller animals, or simply larger of the same kind, is unclear. The balance here feels held but dynamic: a system in motion, a system where something is always being caught and something is always catching and the net result is the continuance of the whole, the same rough numbers in the water and the mud generation after generation, world without end.

---

What it feels like to be here.

Cool. The air is cool and wet and sits against the skin without hostility. The moisture is not unpleasant — it is simply present, like a fact. Boots or whatever foot-covering exists will be wet within a short time. The clay smell is strong: an ancient, mineral, not-quite-alive smell, the smell of deep time made ambient. The bacterial mats add to it a faint iron note.

The noise does not feel threatening. It is too omnipresent to be threatening. It becomes, after a time, something like the opposite of threatening — a kind of completeness, a fullness, an assertion that this place is occupied and working and has been occupied and working since before the structures fell and will be occupied and working after they are dissolved. The humans who put stones here and wove the small shelter were visitors to this sound, not its makers. They came and went and the sound continued.

This is a place that has solved itself. The long erosion has taken away everything that the long erosion could take. What remains is stable, minimal, and full. The bacterial mat and the calling animals and the standing water and the ancient clay — these are not remnants of something. These are the thing. This is what was always under the more complicated arrangements, waiting with perfect patience to be all that was left.

The far edge of the flat, where land becomes mist becomes sky, receives the sound as well. It is probably indistinguishable from here, at that distance. The same dim air. The same wet clay. The same enormous noise rising from the ground and not stopping.
