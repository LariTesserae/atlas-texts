# The Cistern Plateau

---

The first thing you notice is the weight of the sky pressing water into everything.

Not rain — it hasn't rained here in the sense that rain falls and stops. The moisture simply *is*, a permanent condition of the air at this elevation, a cold white brightness in all directions, diffuse and total, as though the sun has been ground to powder and mixed into the atmosphere evenly. There are no shadows with edges. The light comes from everywhere the eye can reach and a little beyond, from above and from the pale stone surfaces themselves, which have been cut and fitted so precisely that their joints become visible only when you crouch and look along them — thin dark seams, slightly damp to the touch, running in the long straight lines of deliberate construction.

This is a plateau, but not a natural one. Or rather: whatever natural plateau once existed here has been so completely worked, surfaced, built-over, and built-upon that the original rock is no longer findable. You could dig and you would find older stone — but it would be fitted stone, earlier courses of the same anonymous effort, blocks beneath blocks, the construction going down as far as it goes up.

---

The place is vast in the horizontal sense — you can see for what might be two kilometers before the white haze softens distant forms into suggestions — but it does not feel open in the way of meadows or deserts. It feels open the way a full vessel feels open at the top: every surface occupied, every space between surfaces filled with something, but the sky above still unclaimed and indifferent.

The construction is not architectural in the sense of buildings. There are no buildings precisely, no rooms, no enclosed volumes. What there are are forms: low walls that don't enclose anything but divide the plateau into sections of unknown purpose; channels, shallow and precise, running at gentle gradients toward collection points; raised platforms of carefully laid stone at intervals that suggest a pattern too large to perceive from within it; stone basins, wide as rooms, some empty, some holding a few centimeters of standing water so still and flat they look solid. The water in the basins holds the sky perfectly. Looking into one is like looking up, except quieter.

Every surface is stone. Every stone has been touched by hands. The touching is old, clearly old — the edges of the blocks are beginning to show weathering, the early rounding that comes before true erosion — but the forms themselves are young in a geological sense. Sharp. Intentional. The joints still close, the channels still functional, the gradient still running where it was built to run. This is not a ruin. It has not started to become a ruin. It is, by all evidence, still being maintained, or being allowed to maintain itself through its own precision, which amounts to the same thing across long enough timescales.

---

There is almost no sound.

The wind at this elevation should be louder, and sometimes, at intervals you cannot predict, it briefly is: a low note across a channel opening, a soft percussion where a loose flake of stone sits in a fitting that is no longer perfectly tight, chattering once or twice and then stopping. But these sounds last seconds and then the silence reassumes itself, not as absence but as a presence — a quality of the plateau's design. The sound goes somewhere. It is absorbed by the packed arrangement of surfaces, by the angles, by the filled-in nature of a landscape with no hollow spaces to amplify.

Water makes sound here when it moves, but water moves slowly. There is one channel running at the moment, carrying water from what must be a collection surface further up a gentle rise, carrying it toward a basin below. The sound of it is a thin cold thread of sound, consistent, forgettable, present the way a heartbeat is present — noticeable only when you listen for it or when it stops.

The condensation is everywhere. Run a hand along any wall and it comes away damp. The blocks near the channel edges are darker along their lower courses, water-stained but not eroded, the dark rising slightly like a slow tide. In the morning — and this has the feel of a morning place, though the light gives no real time of day — the surfaces that face slightly downward will be running with beaded water that formed in the night. By whatever passes for afternoon, those surfaces will be dry enough to look pale again, and by the next cold cycle, the condensation will return.

The whole plateau breathes moisture in and out. Slowly. Daily.

---

There is no vegetation. This is the fact of the place that takes longest to settle into.

You keep expecting it. A tuft at a joint, some biological opportunist finding the damp and the mineral surface irresistible. But nothing. The stones are clean in the way of high cold places where organic chemistry hasn't gotten a foothold, or the way of constructed surfaces that are not hospitable to the biology that would colonize them — too bare, too smooth, too worked. Or perhaps the place was surfaced too recently; perhaps the spores haven't found it yet. Perhaps they will, in a hundred years, and thin films of something gray-green will begin to appear along the north-facing joints, and then the place will have entered a new phase, and will become something slightly different.

But for now: nothing alive. No animal life, not even insects, not even the slight movement at the corner of vision that turns out to be a small thing finding its way. The basins hold clear water. Nothing moves in the clear water. The construction proceeds in all directions without biological interruption.

What you might mistake for biology, briefly: there are deposits in some of the basin corners that are soft-looking and slightly irregular, pale ochre, building up slowly from mineral precipitation. They have the texture of something organic. They are not. They are what water leaves behind when it evaporates from the same spot for long enough — patient and mindless accumulation, the sediment of slow time.

---

The tools that made this place were simple.

You can see their marks. The faces of the blocks, where they are exposed, show the evidence: straight cuts that were not perfectly straight, corrected by patient dressing; the marks of wedges where stone was split along grain; the subtle undulations of a surface that was worked by hand with something hard and repetitive. There is no finish here that requires a machine. There is nothing a machine would have done differently in the results, but the process is visible as a human process, or something like a human process — something with hands, with tools made of stone or early metal, with patience distributed across many individuals across long time.

The scale of the construction is the thing that doesn't fit the tools. The plateau is huge. The work is everywhere. The implication is either enormous numbers of workers, or enormous duration, or both — a civilization that did this the way rivers make canyons, not through force but through the relentless continuation of a single action.

What were they making? The question surfaces and cannot easily be answered from within the thing itself. The channels move water. The basins collect it. The platforms are raised above the channel level. Whether this is for agriculture that no longer happens, for ritual purposes, for water storage in a place where water is precious (though it seems abundant), for some other regulation of a resource or environment — none of these answers feel complete.

The plateau does not explain itself.

---

When the wind dies and you stand still for long enough, the place offers this:

Cool bright mineral silence. The faint thread of moving water. Damp stone smell, clean and without the sweetness of anything biological, just mineral and cold. The sky, white with diffuse light, pressing gently down. The long seams of construction running to the limit of sight. The basins holding their mirrors of water, each one steady, each one holding the same pale sky, each one incrementally filling or incrementally evaporating according to rules set down long before this moment, rules that will continue operating long after it.

Patience, as landscape.

The world, built by hand, wet with sky, waiting — not for anything in particular, not urgently, but in the way that durable things wait: by simply continuing to be exactly what they are.
