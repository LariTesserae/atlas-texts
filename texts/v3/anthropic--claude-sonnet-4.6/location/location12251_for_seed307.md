# The Fumarole Pocket

---

Start with the fact of heat. Not warmth — heat as presence, heat as texture, heat as the first thing that reaches you before anything else does. It comes from below. It comes from the walls. It radiates off the minerals themselves as though stone has learned to want something.

You are inside.

Not inside a building — there are no buildings, there have never been buildings, the idea of building belongs to a world with patience and time. You are inside the mountain. You are inside a pocket the mountain has just now finished making, a blister in the rock that opened recently enough that the fracture lines along the walls still look — the only word is *surprised*. The edges of things here are sharp in the way that new breaks are sharp: not weathered, not softened, not yet old enough to round. If you touched the obsidian lip of the entrance, it would cut you. The basalt faces where the walls cracked apart have a freshness to them, a mineral brightness, like split fruit.

The space is small. Pocket-small. Perhaps four meters at the widest. Perhaps three. The ceiling is irregular — lumped with formations that have formed in *weeks*, not centuries. Sulfur deposits in citrus yellows and deep amber. Iron oxides bleeding rust-orange down wet rock faces, slow and continuous, a painting still being painted. The floor slopes. The floor is barely a floor. It is a series of shelves and lips and crystalline crusts over hollow spaces, and beneath some of those crusts: nothing, or worse than nothing, or heat moving in currents through dark.

High, somehow. That's the paradox of the place, the first thing that strains sense.

The pocket is small — contained, intimate almost — but you are also impossibly high. The mountain outside is a summit situation, the kind where the word *elevation* stops being a measurement and becomes a physical argument. The air outside would be thin, cold, hostile. The air inside is the opposite of that. Inside, the fumarole has its own atmosphere: thick and wet and close, heavy with sulfur dioxide and water vapor, a greenhouse sealed inside stone. You breathe it and feel it. The moisture condenses on every surface because the rock walls, for all their heat, are cooler than the gases rising through them, and so everything *weeps*. The walls weep. The ceiling drips. The obsidian flakes that jut from fracture lines have small tears running down their faces. You are wet. You became wet the moment you entered. The water isn't rain — there is no sky here — it is the mountain exhaling.

Outside, at this elevation, there are no plants. Below the pocket, on the slopes, perhaps some extremophile crust, some lichen thin as breath. In here: nothing botanical. Nothing that needs light in the way plants need light. The surface is mineral. Mineral and —

---

The fauna is the reality breakdown.

It begins subtly. You notice movement before you see creatures. Something shifts in your peripheral vision, along the base of the far wall, and when you look directly: nothing. Then you look at the nothing and the nothing is *textured*. The nothing has edges that move. The condensation on the basalt is not uniform. The wet patches are *organized*, in a way that feels almost deliberate, the way a dream feels deliberate when you're still in it.

They are there in quantities that exceed the space.

You start to see them and then you cannot stop seeing them. Small organisms — if organism is even the right word, if biology applies, if this is biology — clustered on every wet surface, on every sulfur formation, on the dripping ceiling in arrangements that look architectural from one angle and then look like pure accident from another. They are translucent at the edges. They pulse in cycles that don't match anything: not heartbeat, not breath, not any rhythm with a name. They are layered three and four deep on the richest mineral flows. They are touching. They are near-touching. The density of their presence in this small space is wrong in a way that makes your eyes water, or maybe that's the sulfur dioxide.

Their relationship to the rock is unclear. They might be *on* it. They might be *part of it* — a living seam in new stone, a component of the fumarole process itself, something the mountain is doing rather than something that arrived. Where the sulfur deposits are thickest, there they are. Where the iron oxide bleeds most freely, the ones that are rusty at their edges pulse the fastest. When the fumarole vents — in short rhythmic bursts, hissing and then silent, then hissing — they respond. Not with fear. With *appetite*.

There are thousands of them in three square meters of wall.

Counting doesn't work. Every time you count, you reach a different number. Not because they move fast — they move slowly, glacially, tidal — but because the category of *one* keeps failing to apply. Are those two or is that one thing that has a division in it? Is that a group or a single distributed body? The question feels important and then feels like the wrong question and then feels important again.

There is one that is larger. Probably. It occupies the highest shelf, where the heat is most intense, where the steam vents. It does not move the way the others move. It holds still in a way that has weight to it — a stillness that is a behavior, not an absence. It is watching, or it is the part of the system that watches. You are not certain it has a direction to watch *with*, but you feel watched in a specific way, from a specific location.

---

The sound is almost nothing.

That's the strangest thing. For a space so full — so saturated, so dense with process and creature and geological activity — it is nearly silent. The fumarole hisses, but softly. The mineral water that weeps from the walls hits the floor in irregular drips, each drop landing in a thin film of collected water, making sounds more felt than heard. The organisms make no sound that you can consciously detect, and yet — you have the persistent impression that at the edge of your hearing, something is happening. Not a sound. An almost-sound. The way a very high frequency becomes pressure rather than noise. You keep turning your head. You keep finding nothing but the dripping and the hiss and the silence folded inside the hiss.

Outside the pocket, at this altitude, there would be wind. You can feel its absence. The pocket is sealed enough that the mountain's exterior is theoretical — another world at the end of a crack.

---

The light is ordinary, which is the most disturbing thing about it.

Where does light come from, in a sealed fumarole pocket in the interior of a mountain at elevation? The question doesn't resolve. The space is lit. Not brightly — in the way a cloudy afternoon is lit, diffuse and directionless, casting no hard shadows. The sulfur formations glow with their own yellows, which might account for some of it. The iron oxides have a warmth to them that isn't quite luminescence but isn't quite not. You can see everything clearly, in detail, and you have no explanation. You stop trying to find one. The light exists. The pocket is visible. The thousands of organisms on the wet basalt faces are visible, in their slow pulsing, in their architectural density.

Normal light. Falling from nowhere.

---

What is the tech here? The question arrives and immediately seems wrong, seems like it belongs to a different frame. And yet: marks.

There are marks on the far wall that are almost certainly geological. Fracture patterns in the new basalt. Stress lines. And they are almost certainly *not* deliberate. They are almost certainly not: a record, a map, an accounting of something. The largest organism (probably) occupies its position above a concentration of these lines. They radiate from that area. If you imagine them as *made*, they make a kind of sense — the logic of early marking, of scratch and point, of here-is-a-significant-location. If you remember they are geological, they make geological sense. Both readings are available simultaneously and neither one cancels the other.

You have no tools. There is nothing made. Undeveloped. Untouched by construction.

The marks are there.

---

This is what is real: a pocket in young stone, high and hot and wet, sealed from sky, lit by sourceless light, alive past its capacity, weeping iron and sulfur continuously, hissing in slow cycles. This is a small place. It fits in the space between other things. Outside it, the mountain is enormous, cold-faced, wind-stripped. Inside it, the mountain is doing something in private.

The organisms — if that's the word — have lived here since the pocket formed. Or they are how the pocket forms. Or they are what the mountain thinks when it thinks, which is infrequently but seriously.

You are here because a fracture opened. You are not the first thing to notice that. The one that is probably largest and probably watching moves, finally — an almost imperceptible shift, a reorientation of its whole body toward you, or toward the fracture you came through, which might be the same gesture.

The dripping continues. The hiss and its silence. The water moving down basalt faces in organized, purposeful-seeming paths.

The heat is constant and increasing and makes no demands except the demand to be noticed.

You are in the middle of something's interior life.
