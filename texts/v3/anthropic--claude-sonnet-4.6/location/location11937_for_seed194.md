# The Interior of the Green City

---

You are inside something. That is the first understanding, and it doesn't come through the eyes — the eyes are useless here, there is no light at all — but through the quality of the air pressing against your face, the sense of mass arranged around you, the particular way sound moves when it has walls that breathe.

The darkness is not empty. You know this before you can explain it.

---

## What the place is

Somewhere far from any sun, or far beneath one, or simply in a place the sun has agreed to forget about, there is a city that was built inside a living thing — or that became a living thing, it is impossible to say now, the order of events has blurred past recovering. The distinction no longer matters to the place itself. It stopped mattering so long ago that the word *before* barely applies.

The city is small. A pocket of organized intention dropped inside a larger unintelligible fact. The streets, if they are streets, run through chambers roughly the volume of a decent house, or a modest hall, or a long room that turns gently at both ends and closes back on itself. The chambers connect through passages, narrow enough that two people might brush shoulders passing, though there are no people. There have been no people — no animals of any kind — for a duration the place does not track. The place does not have a function that requires tracking duration.

This is not a ruin in any dramatic sense. There was no collapse, no fire, no sudden departure. The city simply continued without its makers, and the plant life, which was always the dominant structural fact, simply continued too, and the two continuings proceeded so slowly, over so many quiet centuries, that what has arrived at now is not a ruin but a *resolution*. A final form. The thing the place was always working toward.

---

## The substrate

Everything solid in here is root.

The floor is root compressed into something so dense it might as well be stone — and in places it has mineralized, truly, dried calcium locked into wood-cellulose lattice, gray-brown and cool to the touch, almost cold, slightly rough. The walls are root woven with older root. The ceiling is where the root goes fine and fibrous and where the secondary growths anchor — thick ropes of something like moss but slower than moss, something that has had centuries to thicken one cell layer at a time into pads of near-felt, dark green-black in whatever light would show them, which there is none of.

The original construction — the primitive construction, hand-laid stone and rammed earth and timber framing — exists still, but only as geometry, as absence-of-plant, as the ghost of straight lines that the roots have not quite erased. You can feel the corner of what was a wall by the way the root changes: here it grows horizontal, laid in courses the way the original stones were laid, because the root found those stones and followed their logic, and even long after the stones dissolved into mineral trace, the root remembered their arrangement and repeated it.

Pillars exist. They are root-pillars, each one the product of perhaps forty or fifty individual root systems fusing over centuries until the seams between them are invisible — single objects now, continuous and vast, two meters around at the base, tapering slowly upward to where they meet the ceiling and branch into the mat above. Tap a pillar and feel your knuckle report: solid as oak, cool, faintly yielding, alive in the way that slow things are alive.

The streets between chambers are drier than the chambers. Not that anything here is wet — the water parameter is nearly zero, has been zero for an extraordinarily long time, some desiccation so old it predates the civilization that built here, some regional chemistry that decided against moisture at the founding of this place's geology. The plants have adapted entirely. None of them require water in any form you would recognize. They metabolize something else: a slow mineral vapor that rises through the rock below, sulfate ions perhaps, or something stranger, a chemistry that terrestrial botany would call impossible and that this place calls ordinary.

The plant cells here do not swell with water. They are packed tight with a denser fluid, almost resinous, faintly sticky at the surface of a cut if you could cut one, which you are not doing. The leaves — and there are leaves, in the chambers where growth gets access to some thin geothermal warmth rising from below — are waxy, small, thick as coins, and arranged in patterns that look almost deliberate: spirals on the pillars, arcs along the upper wall surface, tessellating grids across the ceiling-moss. This is not intelligence. This is what happens when a particular growth pattern is selected for across a timescale long enough that randomness smooths itself into regularity.

## The sound

The loudness is the most disorienting thing.

You expected silence, underground, in the dark, in a place with no animals and no wind. You expected the particular loudness of total quiet. Instead the place roars.

Not with anything sharp. Nothing that would make you flinch or turn. The sound is low and continuous and fills the chambers so completely that it stops being heard as sound and starts being heard as *pressure*, as a property of the air itself, as the texture of the atmosphere rather than an event within it. If you stood here long enough your nervous system would reclassify it as background and stop registering it as sound at all. You are not here long enough for that.

What you are hearing is the root systems growing.

That sounds impossible — growth is silent, growth is definitionally inaudible — but the growth here is not silent because the growth here is not individual cells dividing quietly in soft tissue. The growth here is root pressing into stone. Root grinding, micron by micron, year by year, against the mineral walls of its own city. Every pillar is a slow engine. Every wall surface is a place where growth exerts pressure continuously against something that resists it, and the resistance and the pressure together make, across millions of points of contact, a sound that is individually below the threshold of perception but collectively, in aggregate, a constant low grinding that fills these sealed chambers and has nowhere to escape to, and so it accumulates.

It is the sound of the place eating itself into its final form.

There are also cracks. The cracks happen rarely — perhaps one significant crack per chamber per decade — but the chambers are old and the walls are old and the root is indefatigable, and a crack in this sealed space sounds like something structural and alarming even though it is ordinary, even though it has been happening at this rate for as long as the place has had walls. After a crack, a series of smaller sounds follows: the creak of root settling into the gap, the click of small debris falling onto the root-floor, a brief increase in the grinding as the disrupted tissue compensates. Then everything returns to its baseline roar.

Occasionally: a deep tone. It rises from below, through the floor, through the soles of your feet before it reaches your ears. A long note, almost musical, almost intentional. The geothermal below the city reaches into certain resonant cavities in the root-work and excites them as air in a pipe organ is excited. The city plays itself. The note lasts between thirty seconds and several minutes, then fades, then returns after an interval you cannot predict. There is no other music here, has never been any other music here, but this music has been playing continuously for longer than any conscious being has been present to describe it as music.

---

## The light

There is no light.

There is truly no light. Not the faint red of infrared warmth, not bioluminescence, not a single photon making its way to you through any gap in the root-mass above. The place where the city was built was sealed over, gradually, by the extending canopy of the surface growth, and the surface growth sealed over centuries ago, and the seal has held.

This is not uncomfortable to the plants. None of the plants in here conduct photosynthesis. That entire metabolic pathway has been lost, fully replaced, along some evolutionary timeline that required such patience as to be almost theological. The plants that live in total darkness do not miss light. The concept is not available to them.

If you had been here from birth you would navigate by texture. The root-floor is smooth where foot-traffic — if there had been foot traffic — would have worn it smooth, rough-ridged at the edges where no pressure has been applied in centuries. The passages to other chambers are marked by a change in airflow, a slight increase in the geothermal warmth that circulates between rooms, a change in the sound-quality as the acoustics shift. You would know the geography entirely through your body.

In the absence of light, scale becomes uncertain. A pillar you can almost reach around might be six feet tall or sixty; the ceiling you sense above you might be three meters up or twelve. The sound suggests the chambers are moderately sized — the reverberation has the quality of modest enclosed spaces — but the sound has been modified by the root covering on every surface, and root covering absorbs differently than stone or earth, and so the acoustic inference is unreliable.

The darkness is not heavy. It is not threatening. It is simply the condition of the place, as ordinary to the place as air. The place does not register its own darkness any more than you register the particular color of ordinary daylight. The darkness is just what here is.

## The temperature and the coolness

Cool. Not cold, not the bite of frost, but the sustained lower temperature of deep enclosed space that has had no sun on it in long memory. Somewhere between twelve and fifteen degrees Celsius is the best estimate; the temperature varies slowly with the geothermal cycles, a degree or two over the course of what would be seasons if seasons applied, and the plant life has calibrated to this variation and grown so accustomed to it that the slow seasonal shift in temperature is part of the mechanism the plants use to time their own — not reproduction exactly, but the equivalent, the slow process by which new root extensions commit to growing in one direction rather than another.

The coolness is consistent. It is the most stable thing about the place. Temperature is the place's anchor.

There is no wind. There is the faint circulation of warm air rising from below and being replaced by cool air from the sides — a convective draft so gentle it would not stir a single loose fiber on the moss-ceiling — but nothing that constitutes wind in any meaningful sense. The air simply circulates, slowly, carrying the mineral vapor upward to where the roots can use it, returning cooler and depleted to the stone below. This has been happening for a very long time. The air is old. The air feels old. Not stale exactly but *finished*, like the air has been through every process it has to go through and is resting now between cycles.

## The construction beneath the construction

Here and there, if you know how to read the root-geometry, you can find where the original city was. A doorway, now sealed with growth but still a doorway in outline — the arch of it preserved in the way the root chose to bridge the gap, replicating without understanding the structural logic of the arch, producing something that looks almost exactly like ashlar stonework in its proportional arrangement. A plaza, identifiable as a place where the root-floor is slightly lower and slightly smoother, where the original paving stones created a flat surface and the root adopted that flatness as its own. The central chamber, which was probably a market or a gathering place, identified by the density of root-pillar arrangement at its edges, which were probably walls of more substantial construction than the interior passages.

The people who built this place used simple tools. The marks of those tools are preserved in some places: the edge of a chisel on a stone block that the root has grown around without displacing, the grooves of a drill in a ceiling plate, the score-marks of a measuring line on a pillar base. Everything is hand-made, everything is hand-scaled, everything is built with the assumption that building is a physical negotiation between a person and material, that tools are extensions of hands, that the work proceeds at the pace that hands permit.

The people were not stupid. The city is well-organized. The passages are logical, the chambers are varied in size for different purposes, there are what appear to be cisterns — though they have been dry for ages, and the root has colonized them entirely and turned them into its own deep reservoirs of whatever the root stores instead of water. There are what appear to be sleeping spaces: smaller chambers, lower-ceilinged, warmer due to their position above the primary geothermal channels, arranged in clusters off the main passages. There are what appear to be workshop spaces: chambers with slightly higher ceilings, with arrangements of root-covered platforms at heights that suggest work surfaces.

The original construction was probably stone and timber, locally sourced from whatever was above and outside, shaped by hand and by simple metal tools or pre-metal tools — the question remains open, the tool-marks are ambiguous. The buildings were densely arranged, touching each other, sharing walls, the urban density of a place where building material was difficult or where the climate above required shelter at scale. Which it apparently did, or they preferred enclosure, or there were other reasons that are no longer available.

What they were not prepared for — or perhaps what they were entirely prepared for, perhaps what they invited, perhaps what they hoped — was the root.

The root came from the surface. The city was always embedded in ground that hosted surface growth, and the roots of that growth descended over decades into the soil below the city, and then into the foundations, and then into the floors. This was not catastrophic. This was slow. This was slow enough that if people were present they could have stopped it at almost any point in the first few centuries. They chose not to, or they left before it became a question, or — the thought that visits and doesn't quite leave — they stayed until the root was their infrastructure, until they were living within it rather than despite it, until the choice to remove it became incoherent, became the equivalent of choosing to remove your own bones.

And then they were gone, and the root continued, and here it is.

## The strangeness

The strangeness is not dramatic. It doesn't announce itself. It is the strangeness of a place that has followed its own logic long enough to arrive somewhere that external logic can't entirely account for.

The spiraling leaf-patterns on the pillars are too regular. They follow a Fibonacci arrangement with a precision that suggests, to a certain kind of paranoid attention, that the plants are computing something. But they are not computing anything. The computation is the algorithm of growth under constraint, nothing more, nothing more intentional than a crystal lattice. It is not strange in the way that intelligence is strange. It is strange in the way that a very old, very settled pattern starts to look like intention simply by virtue of its completeness.

There is no water here, has been no water here for so long that the word *drought* ceased to apply ages ago — drought implies that water should be there and isn't, and should doesn't apply, and isn't is just the condition. But the root-pathways trace precisely the lines where the original cisterns were, where the original drainage ran, where the original water supply entered the city. The roots remember water they never touched. They mapped a hydrology that existed before them by finding its traces in the mineral chemistry of the stone, and they grow along those traces as if following a guide, as if something passed this way once and left an instruction.

The geothermal tone — the long organ note from below — varies in pitch very slightly over decades. Not randomly. In a slow cycle. The cycle has been measured, probably, if anyone were here to measure it, and probably it would reveal a period related to some deep oscillation in the rock below, some tidal resonance, some thermal cycle. But the effect of the cycle from inside the sealed city is that the pitch of the fundamental tone descends for years and then rises for years and then descends again, and the rate of descent and ascent and the interval between them is very close to — but not exactly — a musical relationship. Almost a fifth. Almost, for decades, a sustained minor second. The place is almost resolving to something and never quite getting there.

This has been happening since before anyone was here to notice. It will continue after.

---

## What it feels like to be here

The place is not hostile. The place has no relationship to you at all, which is different from hostility and in some ways more disorienting. You are not expected. You are not unwelcome. You are simply not a category the place has any provisions for.

The root will grow around you if you stay long enough. Not aggressively, not as a trap, simply because the root grows around everything at its own pace, following pressure and chemistry, and you are a presence that creates both. In a century you would be incorporated. In several centuries you would be a shape in the root, a pocket of different density, eventually dissolved into the substrate, unrecognizable as the thing that entered.

The darkness is absolute. The sound is constant and low and everywhere. The coolness sits on your skin like a cloth. The air you breathe is old and dry and carries the mineral flavor of the chemistry the root runs on — not unpleasant, metallic and slightly floral, a smell that has no human referent. The floor under your feet is solid, cooler than expected, faintly gritty where root-debris has crumbled and not been moved.

Around you, in all directions, in total silence: the constant, almost inaudible, cumulative sound of a city making its final adjustments. The grinding of root into stone. The occasional crack of something giving way that was always going to give way. The long, circling, almost-resolving organ note rising from the deep rock and filling the sealed chambers and going nowhere.

The place has been here for a very long time. It will be here for a very long time more. It has settled into what it is. It is finished in the way that only ancient things get to be finished — not ended, but complete, still proceeding, unhurried, running down no clock.

You are a brief and slightly bewildering interruption in an enormous patience.
