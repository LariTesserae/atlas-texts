# The Understory at Night

---

The canopy doesn't let in stars. This is the first thing to understand. There is no sky here, not in any functional sense — only successive layers of leaf above leaf above leaf, the highest branches somewhere beyond knowing, and between you and them: darkness that has texture, darkness that is almost a material.

You are standing — if standing is the word — in a space that is simultaneously vast and claustrophobic. Open in the sense of a cathedral is open: ceiling implied, walls far off, but the air itself filled with presence, column upon column of trunk and root and hanging vine so dense that moving forward requires negotiation. The forest extends in every direction far past what you could traverse in a day, valley-sized, hill-sized, the rough dimensions of a county you've never heard of. But none of it wide open. All of it *full*.

---

The ground underfoot is new.

This is the peculiar thing, the thing your body knows before your mind catches up: the world here is young. Not ancient and worn and dark with accumulated meaning. *Young*. The soil is loose and dark and gives under weight with a freshness, like bread just risen. Root systems haven't yet wrestled each other into arthritic compromise — they reach out clean and white into soil that hasn't been claimed, hasn't been eaten down to mineral patience. Where a tree has fallen — and trees fall here constantly, you will understand why — the stump is pale and bright, the exposed wood still resinous, the splinter-edges still sharp enough to catch skin.

The forest is not old growth. The forest is something else: growth in a state of happening. Every trunk you press your hand against is fighting upward *now*, in the ongoing present tense, competing for a slice of whatever light exists somewhere above those layered canopies. The vines aren't draped languorously across the scene. They move. Not metaphorically. They *reach*, and if you have been here long enough — an hour, two — you can see where they have moved since you arrived, a degree of arc you can detect against the pale bark they're climbing.

This place is not wilderness settled into itself. It is wilderness in the act of becoming wilderness, racing to fill whatever space the world has made available to it.

---

The moisture is a mood, not a condition.

There is no river — or perhaps there is, somewhere, you might find it in half a day's walking if you could walk in a straight line, which you cannot. What there is: the air holds water at the threshold below rain. Condensation beads on every surface and on every surface of every surface — the underside of leaves, the moss on the bark, the back of your own hand if you hold it still. This is not mist, not fog, not precipitation. It is more intimate than any of those. It is the air simply unable to keep the water anymore, releasing it slowly and constantly against whatever it touches.

The result is that everything glistens in the dark, and the darkness itself is not quite uniform. The water-drops on leaves and webbing and fur catch whatever faint light sources exist here — bioluminescence, mostly — and scatter them at angles, so that there are constellations underfoot and at shoulder height and all around at whatever height a web happens to be strung, which is all heights, every height.

The ground drains laterally through this new soil rather than pooling, so you don't sink, but the litter layer beneath your feet holds its moisture, holds the coolness of recent water, and the warmth above it — that general vegetal warmth of the tropics at night, the residual heat of photosynthesis and decomposition and so much animal metabolism all crowded into one space — creates a constant movement of air through the lower strata. Just above the ground, always, a slow river of warm-cool, warm-cool moving past your ankles.

---

About the sound.

This is the thing that fills you so completely that it stops feeling like sound after a while and starts feeling like pressure, like weather, like a change in the composition of the air itself.

The fauna here have overwhelmed the ecological carrying capacity of every reasonable model. You sense this before you can count — the numbers are wrong. The number of insects in a cubic meter of air is not a number that resolves into individual insects; it resolves into a *quality*, a density of wing-flutter and carapace-click and mandible-work that produces a base note, sub-sonic in its pervasiveness, felt in the chest. Above this: frogs. Hundreds of species of frogs, in competition for acoustic territory they cannot actually establish because the acoustic territory is already full, and so each one calling louder, at a different frequency, at a different rhythm, producing — not cacophony exactly, because cacophony implies chaos you can sort if you try, and you cannot sort this. This produces something more like a new kind of silence, an overwhelming of the silence faculty.

Above the frogs: insects in a higher register, crickets and katydids and things that have not been named, species racing to fill available niches in this new young place, the whole system still negotiating what roles will exist. Among them and above them: birds calling in the dark. Not the sparse night-calls of temperate forests, one owl, one nightjar, the occasional curt alarm of something disturbed. All of the birds are calling. The birds have not reduced their activity for nighttime. The birds are responding to the competition, to the density, to the constant movement. And then: larger mammals moving through the mid-canopy, the crash and branch-snap of things that weigh real weight pushing through a forest that is dense enough to slow them, the vocalization that accompanies territory-assertion, mating, conflict, warning, and whatever passes for conversation.

You cannot think in complete sentences for very long. The sound interrupts the mind before thoughts close. You learn to exist in the present moment because the present moment is already more than enough to attend to.

---

There is life on every surface and in the air between surfaces.

The trees are colonized from root-flare to branch-tip. The bark is a stratigraphy: first the lichen-layer, gray and rust and pale green, then above that the moss-layer, then the liverwort layer, then the bromeliads anchored at branch-angles like small bowls, catching the condensation-water, holding their own ponds above the ground, their own ecologies of water-flies and tiny frogs and invertebrates that have evolved to live only in bromeliad-cups and nowhere else. From the branches: orchids in colors you cannot see in this darkness but would not believe in light. From the orchids and the bromeliads and the mosses: hanging roots, their own architecture, a second branching system below the first, ghost-white, growing downward.

Between the trees: the insects. The insects. They move in visible masses through the lower strata — not a swarm, because swarming implies a unitary behavior, and what these are is more complex: competing columns of ants crossing competing columns of ants, beetle populations moving through fallen wood, moth populations in their navigational spirals through the middle air, clouds of gnats ascending and descending with the thermal currents from the warm floor. On your skin: immediate contact. Within seconds of standing still you are being assessed, explored, recruited into the ecology in minor ways. Nothing malicious. Nothing personal. The forest simply has not learned yet to leave you alone.

The mammals: numerous and restless. Small ones skitter at the base of every trunk. The understory is seething at knee-height with rodent movement, marsupial movement, the low rapid threading of things that have not yet learned to fear large presences because large presences are new here too, and the learning is still happening. In the mid-canopy: primates, some kind, moving in what you take to be groups but the groups keep intersecting with other groups and the intersections are loud.

The amphibians coat the wet surfaces. This is not an exaggeration. The north face of every trunk, the underside of every broad-leafed plant, the curved surfaces of every bowl-shaped structure: frogs. Clinging. In colors that would matter in daylight. Small ones and large ones and ones in between, in the various sizes of the various niches of the various insects that compose their various diets, the whole system calibrated against itself in ways that are still, here, being calibrated in real time.

---

There is movement everywhere and it is not peaceful movement.

The forest is not serene. The forest is not in equilibrium. The forest is actively competing with itself in a way that produces constant change at every scale: a vine that has just reached and overtopped a smaller tree, a tree that has just died and is beginning its fall in slow motion — you can hear the root-work tearing, a low percussion below the frog-sound — and all the species that live in the dying tree beginning to move outward from it in radiating waves, displacing other populations in adjacent trees, who displace others, a cascade of small relocations moving outward from the falling tree like ripples.

This turbulence is not catastrophic. It is the normal state. The forest has been like this every hour of its young existence and will continue. There is no toward-equilibrium. The new species arrive faster than the existing species can adapt to their arrival. The vegetation grows faster than the animals can track it. The animals reproduce into the new space faster than the vegetation can build structure for them. Everything overshoots; everything corrects by overshooting in the other direction. The forest breathes in pulses you can almost measure if you stay long enough and are very still, which is difficult to be.

---

Here is what is unusual, the thing that edges past the merely extraordinary:

The trees *listen*.

Not metaphorically. There is a sensitivity to large vibration — footfall, voice, the crash of a mid-canopy branch — that produces a response in the surrounding vegetation visible on a timescale of minutes. A mimosa-like response but not limited to the touch-sensitive species, distributed somehow through the whole community, or through enough of it that the effect is general. When something large moves through, the immediate vicinity of plants makes small adjustments — leaflets angle slightly, petioles shift degree by degree, the orientation of broad leaves changes as though tracking.

They are not tracking. There is no central processing. It is emergent from something in the soil perhaps, from the mycelial network that in this young forest is already dense (the fungi came first, before the trees; the trees grew from the scaffolding of the fungi; they are still partly fungal in their relationship to each other), some vibration-sensitivity distributed through that network that produces coordinated response without coordination.

The effect, standing in the dark with the sound pressure of ten thousand species pressing in on you from all sides, is that the forest knows you are here. That it is turning its attention, in some fashion that has no mind behind it, toward whatever you are. That you are being incorporated into its model of what this place contains.

You are new here too.

So is everything.

The forest doesn't mind.

---

Later, the condensation water collecting on leaves above you reaches critical mass and falls — not as rain, as a fat singular drop striking your forehead with force — and the sound of it hitting you is, absurdly, audible through all the rest of it, because your brain has learned to filter for the immediate and the immediate is always this, always the next thing pressing close, and the forest is so full of next things that there is no room left in you for anywhere else.

This is the understory at night.

You will not remember it clearly. The density of sensation defeats linear memory. What will remain is something more like a weather-state: the feeling of being inside something alive and early and overwhelming, something that hasn't finished deciding what it will be, with you inside it, also being decided about.
