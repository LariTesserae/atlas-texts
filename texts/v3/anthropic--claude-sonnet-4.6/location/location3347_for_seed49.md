# The Wet City at the Bottom of Everything

---

Start with the sound, because the sound arrives before anything else can.

It is not a single sound. It is every frequency of sound that a warm, saturated, densely-packed, continent-spanning city can produce, all at once, all the time, without pause. There is the low tectonic moan of infrastructure shifting under its own accumulated weight — pipes and conduits and load-bearing columns of a material that has no good name in any language you know, something between poured ceramic and slow glass, something that was engineered and then engineered again, recursively, at scales your instruments can't quite hold steady. There is the percussion of condensation: water sheeting off every surface, water finding every crack, water falling from higher tiers of the city onto lower tiers in curtains thick enough to lose a hand in. There is machinery — but *machinery* is too small a word. There are processes running here that happen to produce sound as a byproduct, enormous coordinated operations whose purpose you cannot immediately determine, whose rhythm suggests intention but whose scale suggests something more like weather. There is the fauna. And there are the voices, or what function as voices, rising from everything that moves.

You cannot think clearly here for the first hour. The sound is a physical fact. It pushes on your chest. It has texture.

---

Now: the terrain.

The city is low. This is the first and most important geographic fact. It occupies a basin so large that its edges are below the visible horizon in every direction — you cannot see the rim from anywhere inside. The floor of the basin is ancient, settled, elaborately weathered. Whatever rock underlies all of this has been here long enough that it stopped being purely geological and became something else: a foundation layer so thoroughly colonized by construction over so many generations of building that it is no longer clear where bedrock ends and earliest infrastructure begins. The oldest layers fuse. Core samples, if you could take them, would show strata of stone giving way to strata of poured substrate giving way to strata of something crystalline and deliberate, then more stone, then more building, a palimpsest going down farther than current drilling technology has chosen to explore.

The surface — the surface you walk on, the level that functions as *ground* — is wet. It is always wet. The city produces its own weather (there is enough of it; there has always been enough of it), and that weather is warm and humid and tends toward rain. Precipitation cycles through the upper districts every few hours, a rhythm the inhabitants have built around, engineered around, given up trying to stop. Warm condensation rises from the lower streets into the cooler air of the upper tiers and comes back down as water. The streets at ground level are never dry. The drains are enormous — you could lose a vehicle in one — and they are always running, always audible beneath everything else, a deep subsonic gurgling that you feel in your knees.

Water seeps from the walls. The walls of this city — the walls of its oldest surviving structures, which are still in use, still inhabited, still serving purposes both original and accumulated — weep. Not metaphorically. They have been here long enough that groundwater moves through them in understood pathways, and in places the builders have simply accepted this and built channels into the walls themselves, directed the seeping into useful flow. In the oldest quarters the walls are dark with perpetual moisture, slick in the low places, and the stone — if it is still stone — has taken on the rounded, settled, unhurried quality of material that has arrived at an arrangement with its circumstances.

There are no plants. There is almost no plant life anywhere in this city.

This is the first unusual thing.

---

A city this warm, this wet, at this scale — ecology should be everywhere. It should be taking over. In a warm, saturated, continent-spanning basin, vegetation should be aggressive. The absence of it is a fact about the city, not about the plants. Something here outcompetes everything photosynthetic, or replaces what photosynthesis provided, or the city was built in an era and manner that made plant colonization impossible and has maintained that condition through mechanisms you are still working out.

What grows instead is fungal, mostly, in the biological sense — pale shelf-like formations on the undersides of bridges, something soft and gray that accumulates in corners and breathes with the rhythm of humidity, colonies of organisms that are doing something metabolic but doing it quietly, doing it without chlorophyll or visible hunger, doing it in colors that suggest they are processing something the city produces. They are not incidental. In places they have been incorporated into the architecture. They fill gaps in walls with a substance that is more structurally reliable than mortar. They are, perhaps, understood. Perhaps cultivated. There is no clear line here between what is natural and what has been allowed.

There are also, in certain districts, formations that are clearly neither animal nor fungal nor plant but which grow — which *accrete* — in ways that look purposeful. Crystalline structures budding from the joints between structural elements. Filaments of something metallic, or metallic-adjacent, spanning gaps between buildings in arrangements that carry current. The city has been modified at the nanoscale for long enough that the boundary between the built environment and the living one, between technology and organism, is a question that locals find slightly quaint. Of course it grows. Of course it responds. The infrastructure has been iterating on itself for generations. What did you expect?

---

The fauna are abundant, and they have adapted completely.

They are not, for the most part, large. Large fauna do not thrive here — too little light in the deep streets, too little unoccupied space, too much noise and motion and the constant low-level electromagnetic signature of infrastructure operating at its own incomprehensible complexity. What thrives here is quick, social, small to medium, capable of navigating a three-dimensional built environment at speed. There are things moving in the upper airways of the city — warm air currents between the tall structures carry them, and they ride those currents in groups, in formations that suggest communication. There are things in the drains. There are things living in the wall-channels alongside the weeping water, feeding on the fungal colonies, being fed on in turn by something that moves too fast to see clearly in poor light. The fauna are not unusual, exactly. They are very specifically adapted. The city has been here long enough that it has had fauna for long enough that those fauna have been thoroughly shaped by the city, and the city has been shaped by them in ways neither party fully accounts for.

You will hear them constantly. You will hear everything constantly.

---

The scale of the city is the scale of a continent, and this means the city has internal geography.

There are districts so distant from each other that the technology of travel between them is itself a significant engineering domain. There are regions of the city that have developed distinct microclimates — the city's self-generated weather patterns vary by sector, and the built environment has responded to those variations, and the responses have further shaped the weather, iteratively, over the long time the city has been occupied and occupying. Some districts run hotter. Some run with more rain. There are sectors where the humidity has been, by various layered engineering decisions, held somewhat lower — where the walls are drier and the ground is almost not wet — and those sectors feel alien in a way that is hard to immediately name.

The city does not have an edge in the ordinary sense. It thins. It becomes — gradually, over a span of territory that would itself constitute a major metropolitan area by any other measure — lower-density, newer-built, more obviously under construction, more obviously straining outward. The perimeter of the city is, at any given moment, in the process of becoming more city. This is a process that has been ongoing for longer than reliable records extend. Whether it will stop is a question that produces different answers depending on who you ask and what they believe the city is *for*.

---

Inside the city — inside the core, the settled dense heart of it — no space is unoccupied.

This is the second most important geographic fact. The packed density is not simply a matter of buildings standing close together. The vertical space between buildings is occupied. The space beneath elevated structures is occupied. Conduits run everywhere — pipes and cables and pathways for things that move too fast to categorize, and between and around the conduits, more conduit, more infrastructure, and between those, the things that live in the spaces that infrastructure creates. Every surface is a surface *for* something. The walls carry current. The floor carries water. The ceiling — when there is a ceiling, in the covered districts — carries the weight of everything above while conducting information through a crystalline lattice that might be structural, might be computational, functions as both, can no longer be separated into its original purposes because its purposes have multiplied past the point of clean accounting.

You move through this place by making decisions at very short intervals. There is always something immediately in front of you. Always something occupying the space you were about to occupy. Always a negotiation — with the infrastructure, with the fauna, with the other inhabitants, with the city's own continuous low-level reorganization of itself.

The city is turbulent in the way that a river is turbulent: it has direction, it has coherence, it has been doing this for a very long time, but within that coherence every specific thing is in motion. Deliveries of something arrive and are immediately absorbed. Structures that were in one configuration yesterday are in another configuration today, not because they have been demolished and rebuilt but because they are the kind of structures that reconfigure. The city's technology includes materials and systems that are responsive, adaptive, that treat the built environment as an argument to be continuously revised in light of new information. The new information is always arriving. The revision is always underway.

---

The light is ordinary light, and this is strange in its own way.

Given everything — given the density and the weather and the self-modifying infrastructure and the warm wet press of the whole enormous thing — you might expect the light to be strange. Filtered through pollution, or through the electromagnetic fog of so much active technology, or simply blocked by the sheer volume of built material stacked above. But the light here is managed. This is another of the city's long-evolved competencies. Light is considered a resource and its distribution is an engineering domain like water or power or acoustic management (though acoustic management is clearly the one where the city has, over the generations, accepted the scale of the challenge and chosen to simply live with the noise rather than solve it). Somewhere in the high reaches of the uppermost structures there are systems — and systems managing those systems, and systems monitoring those systems, iterating and adjusting — that ensure light reaches the lower streets in approximately the quantity a warm-spectrum ordinary day would provide. Not sunshine. Not quite. But warm, consistent, full-spectrum luminance that arrives from above and reflects off the wet streets and the wet walls and fills the street-level world with a quality of light that is, despite everything, pleasant.

The wet surfaces make the light multiply. Everything gleams. Everything reflects everything else. You can see your own face in the side of an old conduit if you lean close and the light catches right.

---

The smell you would notice, if you were there, is warm water and old stone and ozone and something organic from the fungal colonies and something metallic from the infrastructure's continuous self-maintenance and, beneath all of that, the very specific smell of old occupation — the smell of a place that has been inhabited for so long that the inhabitants and the place have exchanged chemistry with each other until they share it. Cities, given enough time, come to smell like the people who live in them. This city has been given more than enough time.

---

There is a quality to very old, very large, very settled things that is distinct from the quality of new or small or unstable things. It is not peace — peace implies the absence of motion, and nothing here is still. It is more like — sureness. The city is sure of itself. It has been here through enough that its existence is no longer a question it entertains. It will continue. It has the particular unhurried confidence of the very large and the very old, even as it churns and revises and generates its own weather and fills every available space with more of itself and makes noise you cannot think through.

Beneath the sound, if you find a moment of relative quiet — a corner where two noise sources cancel, a maintenance corridor sealed behind a massive door, a place where the engineering has accidentally produced a pocket of reduced amplitude — you can hear the water in the walls.

Just the water, moving through stone that has accepted it, moving toward wherever it's been going for longer than you have existed, moving without urgency, finding its level.

The city breathes around you. You are very small inside it. You are not unwelcome.
