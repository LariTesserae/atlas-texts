# The Fen of Breathing Machines

---

Begin with what you can see, which is not much.

The light here is the color of pewter dissolved in water — not darkness exactly, but a refusal of brightness, a light that has been used before and returned slightly wrong. It comes from no direction. Or from every direction at a height too low to illuminate anything properly. The sky, if this is sky, is the underside of something enormous and wet. Clouds that have forgotten they are clouds. A ceiling of vapor so dense it generates its own condensation and releases it continuously, steadily, without drama, as a fine mist that is not quite rain and not quite fog and coats every surface with a film that runs in thin threads downward, always downward, finding every groove.

The ground accepts this water the way very old things accept attention — without surprise, without resistance, having been through it so many times that reception has become structure.

---

Stand somewhere in the middle of it — the middle being a concept that doesn't quite apply but will have to do — and try to take it in as a whole. This is the first difficulty.

The landscape is packed. That word does not capture it. *Saturated* comes closer. Every cubic meter of this place is occupied by something: low stone formations, the flanks of which weep continuously and run dark with mineral-laden water; the grey-black shells of organisms that might be mollusks and might be something that convergently resembles mollusks; pools that have no depth you can determine from the surface because their surfaces are covered in a membrane of what appears to be iron-depositing bacteria — rust-orange, blue-grey, iridescent in a way that should require more light than is available; and then the things you cannot immediately categorize.

The things you cannot categorize are everywhere.

They are the dominant presence.

They are — you turn this over for a long time before accepting it — *industrial in nature*, but they were not built. They grew into industry, or industry grew into them, or the distinction is not operative here. They are large. They are low to the ground, everything in this place is low to the ground, pressed down by something — age, gravity, atmospheric weight, some principle that resists verticality. They have the form of engines: chambers, conduits, apertures, pistons of calcified or chitinized material that move, slowly, continuously, driving fluids through themselves and into the surrounding matrix. Stacks that vent steam in rhythmic pulses. The steam is warm and the warmth is shocking against the cold and it dissipates into the ambient mist within seconds, contributing to the general wet everywhere.

These are living machines or they are mechanical life or they are a third thing that the distinction between life and machine was invented specifically to avoid naming. They cover the fen floor in dense clusters. They interconnect via structures — tubes, channels, what appear to be roots or conduits or both simultaneously — so that the whole field pulses with a single sluggish coordination, the way organs coordinate inside a body. You are standing inside a body. Or you are standing in a fen in the grey cold light and there are a great many strange organisms that happen to resemble industrial infrastructure.

You hold both possibilities.

---

The age of this place is the thing that most resists comprehension.

Erosion is the process that reveals age, and here erosion has done everything it is possible to do. The bedrock of this landscape — visible in long low outcroppings everywhere, pushing through the fen floor like bones through skin — has been reduced to its final statement. It has no sharp edges anywhere. There are no angles less than about 120 degrees on any exposed stone. The rock is rounded into itself, into shapes that language wants to call organic but that are simply the shapes that physics selects when given unlimited time and access to moving water. Millions of years. Perhaps more. The Precambrian wore surfaces down like this. The surviving landforms of the very early Earth — if any survived — might look like this. 

The stone is dark: basalt-black and greenstone-grey and in places a deep dull red where iron has been going slowly, slowly to rust for longer than most rock units even exist. The surfaces are slick and gleaming with their film of water, and wherever water runs in defined channels it has deepened those channels into smooth troughs, into grooves, into eventually tubes and caves in the stone. The whole substrate is riddled through with these passages. You can hear water moving through them from below — a low, continuous subterranean sound that has been interpreted by every nervous system that has ever come to stand in this place as a sound of breathing. Long, slow, geologic breathing. The earth inhaling. The earth exhaling. The breath of something on a timescale that makes the concept of breathing almost metaphorical.

The landscape has settled into its final form. Or rather: it has settled into a form that it will maintain for as long as the conditions persist, and the conditions feel as though they have persisted and will persist. There is an equanimity to the erosion that is almost peaceful. Almost.

---

The sound.

The sound is why peaceful isn't quite right.

This place is loud in a way that takes several minutes of presence to fully register because no single sound is sharp. Everything is continuous. The machine-organisms — the industrial fauna, call them what you will — produce a combined working noise that is felt as much as heard: a low-frequency grinding and pumping and venting that resonates in the ribcage. Piston-rhythms at irregular intervals. Pressure releases. A sound like a boiler room where all the boilers are biological and all the biological things have become boilers. Individual venting events send brief hisses and groans across the fen that are loud enough to make you turn toward them, expecting something dramatic, but there is nothing to see — just steam, just the grey form of another cluster of organisms engaged in their processes, settling back into the general rumble.

Over this: water. Water everywhere in audio. The continuous mist generates no sound itself but every surface it touches is running and those surfaces run into pools and those pools spill over into channels and those channels run into the passages in the rock and the passages in the rock carry it away with that perpetual breathing-sound. The pooled surfaces don't quite splash — nothing here is energetic enough to splash — but they ripple continuously from the falling mist and from some process of the organisms that occasionally disturbs them. A low, complex, total water-sound.

Then the fauna.

The fauna is the loudest part, which is counterintuitive given what the fauna is. Animals — if these are animals — in cold climates are often quiet. These are not quiet. They are loud the way machines are loud: not by choice, not by communication, but as a byproduct of what they are doing with energy. They call, they screech, they produce sounds that have no analog in familiar wildlife but that the nervous system interprets as mechanical in origin. And there are so many of them. The fauna density in this place is extraordinary, which makes a certain ecological sense: where there is abundant energy being processed and vented by the machine-organisms, there are organisms that have evolved to intercept that energy.

They crowd every available surface. Every shelf of stone. Every flank of every engine-creature. They hang from structures. They pack into pools. They occupy the ground between the larger organisms in such density that the ground itself appears to move when you focus on it at close range. They are adapted to cold. They are adapted to wet. They are adapted to industrial noise and industrial atmosphere and they are adapted — this is the part that takes time — to a world in which the boundary between biology and mechanism is not a boundary.

Some of them are building. Or extruding. Or metabolizing in a way that produces structure. The structures they produce resemble components of larger machines. The larger machines, examined closely, appear to contain regions that are clearly living tissue. The tissue appears to be generating the mechanical components from within. You are watching, in real time, the process by which life becomes industry and industry becomes life and the cycle has been running so long that neither term refers to a stable category.

---

But here is where the place breaks with what is normal.

Observe: a pool. Size of a room. Surface covered in the iron-bacteria membrane, iridescent blue-orange-black. Rippling. Now watch the surface of the pool carefully.

The ripples are traveling in two directions simultaneously, and the two sets of ripples pass through each other without interference, which is normal, waves do that — except that where the two sets cross, the crossing points are not darker but *brighter*, briefly, with a pale cold light that has no source. And the crossing points are not randomly distributed. They form a grid. A regular grid. The grid shifts slowly, tracking something, and if you stare at it long enough you begin to perceive depth in the surface of the pool: not depth downward into water, but depth in some other direction that vision is not designed to report, and the depth appears to contain something receding into it.

This is not the only impossible thing.

Watch the mist. The mist falls. Of course it does. Mist falls. Except that in the mist, directly above two of the engine-organisms, the mist is falling *upward*, slowly, a reversal of about a kilogram of water vapor per second, drawn up into the vent-columns by — by something that does not appear to be the vented steam, which is moving normally, which crosses the ascending mist without interacting with it, which passes through it the way the wave-sets pass through each other.

Observe: a stone, of the rounded basalt type, at the edge of a pool. The stone is being slowly, steadily consumed by the red rust-chemistry that operates on all the iron-bearing rocks here. Watch one spot on its surface for long enough. The rust is advancing. But the advancing edge does not advance continuously. It advances in discrete jumps, and between jumps, it recedes slightly, and then advances further, and the net motion is forward, but the recessions — the recessions go somewhere. The rust that recedes does not dissolve into the water. It goes somewhere else. Into some other version of this moment. The stone is simultaneously at several states of erosion, and what we see is an average.

And the fauna. The fauna moves normally, mostly. But in very dense clusters — and the clusters are everywhere dense — the individuals at the center of the densest packing have begun to be, occasionally, briefly, *partially elsewhere*. Not absent. Not vanished. Somewhere the word *elsewhere* is doing more geometric work than it should. An organism the size of a large rat, covered in filaments of modified keratin that have the iridescence of oil on water, sits at the center of a cluster of sixty of its own kind, and its edges are uncertain, and there is, faintly, a second version of it offset by about eight centimeters to the left that is slightly less opaque than it should be, and the two versions are moving together in a coordination that is almost but not quite synchronized.

The more you look at this place the more of these violations you find. They are distributed throughout the fen in no pattern you can discern. Or in a pattern that the perceiving apparatus you have brought with you is not equipped to perceive. The machine-organisms may perceive it. They may be organized around it. They may, in fact, be producing it — or filtering it — or the violations may be why they are here, the reason a world evolved them, because a world that regularly produces these kinds of structural inconsistencies needs something to process them.

---

Stand still for a moment and let the place settle around you.

Cold. The cold is real and immediate and clarifying. Not killing cold, but the cold of wet stone and metal in a space that does not receive sun. Your breath is visible. It rises and joins the general mist and is indistinguishable from it within seconds, and perhaps there is something uncomfortable in that — in the ease with which what comes out of you becomes part of the atmosphere of this place.

The low elevation means horizons. There are horizons in every direction, distant ones, where the fen floor curves very slightly upward — almost imperceptibly — and the edge of the visible world is a silhouette of the engine-organisms' forms against the pewter sky. The silhouette is irregular and dense and producing steam and the steam rises and becomes sky and you are at the bottom of a bowl, essentially, a bowl that extends for many kilometers in every direction and is full of this: the living industrial fen, the packed wet machine-biological matrix, the water running everywhere over stone that has been running over stone for so long that the stone has given up resisting and become smooth and offered itself.

You cannot see sky properly. The sky is the underside of the vapor mass. But occasionally, briefly, the vapor thins in a patch, and through the patch you see — grey. More grey. Whether that is sky or something else is not determinable. On one occasion, through one such patch, you see something that might be a star, which makes no sense because this is overcast and it appears to be day, or what passes for day here, and yet there it is: a point of light through a hole in the mist, in the wrong position, and then the mist closes.

---

The rules of this world, as best they can be stated:

Water goes where water goes, which is mostly down, but not exclusively. Erosion has reached its terminus: the forms that exist will persist; no new forms are being created by erosion, only smoothed further, further, toward the spherical ideal that erosion approaches but cannot quite reach because the water keeps moving and keeps depositing and keeps reshuffling its mineral load. Life and mechanism are the same process observed at different scales. Energy moves through the ecosystem by pathways that include what would elsewhere be called technological infrastructure but that here grew from nothing, from chemistry, from the long elaboration of chemistry into complexity into machinery. The organisms are not using tools; they are tools; they are users; the distinction requires a boundary the evolution of this place declined to establish.

The violations of physical consistency — the upward mist, the simultaneous wave-states, the partly-elsewhere creatures — are not random. They concentrate near the densest activity of the engine-organisms and they have been happening for a very long time; the stone around the most active clusters is marked with patterns that could be erosion artifacts and could be records of repeated anomalies in the same locations, accumulated over the long life of this fen, scripted into the rock the way footpaths are scripted into soil. This place has always been strange. Or it has been ordinary for long enough that strangeness accreted to it the way minerals accrete to submerged objects: slowly, from the outside, layer by layer, until the original surface is entirely covered and what remains is an object whose shape is entirely a product of what has happened to it.

---

The mist falls. The piston-organisms pump their slow beats. The water runs through the stone below and the stone below breathes. Something large and filamentous and chrome-grey shifts its position among the cluster of its kind, its edges slightly uncertain, its processes engaged in something you cannot name, in this old, cold, loud, impossible, fully-occupied place, where everything that is here has been here for so long that being here has changed what it means to be, and the violations in the structure of things are just one more kind of weather, and the weather is continuous, and the world continues.
