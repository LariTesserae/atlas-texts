# The Plateau of Settled Things

---

## First Approach

You do not climb to this place. Climbing implies that something rises sharply enough to be climbed, and this plateau has long since forgotten sharpness. The edges arrived at their current angle ten million years before anything lived on this world capable of noticing angles, and they have not moved since. What you do instead is walk, and walk, and then find yourself no longer below. The transition is gradual in the way that sleep is gradual — there is no moment you can identify as the crossing, only the retroactive understanding that you are now somewhere different.

The plateau is vast. This requires emphasis. Its vastness is not merely geographic; it is meteorological, phenomenological, almost gravitational. The place has internalized its own scale to the point where it generates consequence — its own cloud systems that have circulated above it for so long they have developed something like habit, its own barometric character that differs at the center from the edges not as variation but as *climate*, its own version of morning that arrives differently here than it arrives at lower elevations, as though the sun has to make a separate decision about this place each day.

The stone beneath everything is the color of very old ash. Not charcoal, not slate, not grey in any of the familiar ways — ash specifically, which is to say: something that was once different, that underwent heat, that arrived at this color through transformation rather than simply by being formed this way. The plateau was not always a plateau. One assumes it was mountains once, or something even stranger, and that time has worked on it the way water works on soap — patiently, without malice, until the original form surrendered.

## Ground

The surface is not flat in the way a floor is flat. It is flat in the way a sentence is true — roughly, with local exceptions, with the flatness emerging only when you step back far enough. Up close, the ground is a complex of low features: cracked pans of stone separating from themselves along lines that formed when the last major catastrophe occurred, which was a very long time ago. The cracks are not raw. They have been there long enough to collect soil, and the soil has been there long enough to collect moss, and the moss is thick and particular.

The moss is important. It is the lushest thing here, and lush is relative to the cold and the sparse, so understand: it is not the lushness of jungles, not overwhelming, not wet-breathed and heavy. It is the lushness of things that have had an enormous amount of time to perfect themselves within severe constraints. The moss grows in the cracks, in the low places where condensation gathers in the hour before dawn, in the sheltered sides of any rock that presents a sheltered side. It is extraordinary in its detail — if you look closely, which you must, because nothing else competes for your attention at close range, you see structure within structure: tiny fronds within fronds, miniature forests with their own miniature clearings, dew suspended on filaments so fine that the dew appears to be floating in mid-air above the moss, attached to nothing, obeying only local rules.

Between the moss-filled cracks and the outcrops and the low ridges of exposed bedrock, there is open space. Much open space. The density is sparse, and you feel it. You stand in the middle of the plateau and things are *there* — vegetation, rock formations, the scattered arrangements of the settlement — but they are far apart, and the space between them has weight. Not emptiness as absence but emptiness as material.

Some of the rock formations are harder to explain than others. Most are straightforwardly geological: resistant cores left behind as softer stone was removed by wind and water and time, standing now as low inselbergs, rounded knobs and humps two or three times a human's height. These fit the world. But then there are others — formations that the eye processes as geological and then, after a moment, cannot hold in that category. A stack of stones that could be a balanced natural column and could be something else; a series of low walls that the erosion pattern seems to support but which follow a geometry that erosion does not usually follow. The plateau, in its extreme age, has entered a condition where the difference between what was made and what arrived becomes unclear. Not because it was always unclear, but because the time elapsed is sufficient to erase the distinction. The made things have been colonized by time until they are natural. The natural things have been shaped by time into configurations that look intentional. The planet itself has become ambiguous.

## Water

Rain comes intermittently, as though the clouds above are considering it, reach a tentative conclusion, rain for a while, and then reconsider. When it rains, it rains sideways, because at this elevation the wind is always doing something, even when it seems still. The drops are small and cold and do not make the sound you expect on the stone — they make a lesser sound, a sound like the memory of rain, like someone in the next room doing something quiet with their hands.

The water runs to the cracks. It pools in the low places among the moss. It does not stay long — the cold dries it more slowly than warmth would, but the wind accelerates that drying, and so the water exists in pockets that appear and disappear and appear again on a cycle tied to the clouds, which are tied to the circulation patterns of a place large enough to have its own weather, which are tied to nothing obvious from here.

In a few locations, the rock has developed concavities — broad, shallow basins worn by a process that must have involved water flowing more continuously than it does now, perhaps more rain in a previous era, perhaps glaciation, the specifics long predating any record. These basins collect standing water when it rains: cold, clear, minimally deep. You can see through them to the stone beneath. The stone beneath appears close but distances are unreliable in this water — the basins are slightly deeper than they look, and the clearness is absolute enough to function as distortion. Things in these pools appear to be almost touchable, nearly reachable, and yet reach in and you find more water than expected before your fingers close on stone.

Condensation forms on everything during the hours before dawn. The moss collects it first. Then the stone collects it. Then, in ways that take longer to notice, the built things collect it — the wooden posts and the stacked-stone walls and the stretched materials overhead all becoming briefly wet in the dark, shining faintly when the sky lightens.

## The Settlement

There is a settlement. It is distributed across the plateau in a pattern that would be difficult to call organized from the ground but which, if one were somehow elevated further, would resolve into coherence — a network of structures following the logic of the terrain, seeking shelter from the prevailing wind direction, clustering around the better water sources, spreading apart where the ground is stable and the rock is good for building upon.

The structures are made of what is available. Stone is available; stone is used. Not cut to precision — the tools that shaped this stone were simple, hand-held, accomplishing the work of hands multiplied by hours and years of effort. The stones are fitted to each other through selection and arrangement rather than shaping: stones of compatible shape stacked in combinations that produce stable walls. The walls are thick, because thick walls hold warmth and because the builders understood that thickness is its own argument against the cold. They have been built long enough that some of them have become difficult to distinguish from the natural outcrops, for the same reason the worked stones anywhere on this plateau become difficult to distinguish — time, and moss, and the patient work of surfaces becoming continuous with their surroundings.

The roofing is stretched material: skins or heavy woven cloth or something that sits ambiguously between the two, supported on frameworks of poles lashed at the joints. Some of the poles are pale wood, carried up from the lower elevations or sourced from whatever grows at the margins of the plateau where conditions permit something taller than moss. Some are bone, because this place — despite currently hosting very little in the way of animal life — has a deep history, and in the deep history there were animals large enough to provide structural-grade bone, and those bones have been used so long they are now polished by handling into something resembling very old ivory.

Between structures: paths, or the suggestions of paths, worn into the stone by passage over long time. The stone where people have walked is darker than the surrounding stone, or lighter — it depends on the specific surface — and in either case the difference marks the routes. The paths converge at certain points: at the water basins, at a central area that is not quite a plaza but functions as one, at a cluster of structures that seem to have a different purpose than shelter, their interiors kept dark, their thresholds marked by arrangements of small stones in patterns that repeat across all of them.

The built environment is sparse but not abandoned. It is inhabited-sparsely, which is different. The population sufficient to maintain and use these structures is small relative to the scale of the plateau, small relative to the size of the settlement itself, which has accumulated over many generations of people with low population but long continuous presence. The structures outnumber the people currently using them. Some have been empty for years. Some have been empty for decades. This does not make them ruins — ruination requires discontinuity, and here the continuity has been maintained. An empty structure that is still maintained is something other than a ruin.

## Sounds

This is a quiet place. But quiet at this scale and in these conditions is not silence — it is a particular soundscape with silence as its foundation.

The wind is the constant, and it is not loud. It passes over the plateau and around the stone outcrops with a tone in the low registers that you feel as much as hear, a tone that has been continuous for so long that the life here — human and vegetable and geological — has calibrated itself against it as a baseline. When the wind pauses, which it does briefly and unpredictably, the quiet beneath becomes apparent, and it is a different quiet than the quiet during wind: deeper, more specific, the kind of silence that makes your own body seem audible.

Rain, when it comes, adds the small percussion already described. The moss absorbs most impact and produces little sound; the open stone rings more clearly; the stretched roofing materials of the structures make a variable rhythm of small impacts that sounds something like a language but produces no meaning — or produces no meaning that can be recovered by someone who did not grow up listening to it.

Very little else. There are no animals making noise. Something moves through the moss sometimes — whether this is the moss itself, adjusting to some slow biological process, or something smaller than is easily visible, or a current of air along the ground finding its way through structure, is not always clear. When it happens, it produces a sound like paper or like the turning of a page: dry, brief, directional.

The people, when present, speak quietly. It may be that the place trained this quietness into them, or it may be that people who chose to live at this elevation and at this sparse population density were already disposed toward quiet. Their voices do not carry far. When you hear speech, you understand that you are close to someone.

## Light

The light comes at an angle.

High elevation angles the light. Dawn and dusk here are longer than at lower elevations — the sun takes more time to clear or approach the horizon relative to an observer this high, and so the transitional light, which is the softest and most particular light, persists longer here than most places. The plateau spends a significant fraction of each day in golden-to-pink transitional illumination, the kind of light that gives everything a surface-attention, that makes the moss look like it is lit from within, that turns the stone from ash-grey to amber-grey to something that does not have a simple name.

During the middle of the day, the light is clear and cold and shadowless in the open areas, because clouds at this elevation scatter it before it arrives. The clouds that the plateau generates through its own circulation do this — they are not overhead constantly, but they pass through often enough that direct sun is the exception and diffused bright is the norm. The effect is that colors appear saturated without appearing to be in sunlight: the pale grey-green of the moss, the ash-stone of the ground, the dark of the water basins, the stretched-material roof-tones of the structures all hold their colors as though lit from a source that has no direction.

At night, if the clouds clear — which they do sometimes, in cycles — the stars are closer here than they should be, or seem to be, which is an elevation effect and also something else. The something else is difficult to specify. The stars at this elevation have a character that stars at lower elevations do not. They are brighter, yes, and the atmosphere between them and the observer is less, yes, and there are more of them visible, yes — all of these are true and none of them fully account for the impression that looking up from the plateau at night is not quite the same activity as looking up from elsewhere. The sky here looks back in some way that sky elsewhere does not.

## The Processes

What makes this place active, despite its silence and sparseness, is the multiplicity of processes running simultaneously, most of them slow enough to be invisible at any given moment but producing, in aggregate, continuous change.

The stone is still eroding. Extremely slowly, in the end-state of a process that began when the original uplift began, but eroding still. The crack networks grow by microns per year. The rounded outcrops lose their surfaces in grains. The basins deepen. In another ten million years, the plateau will be lower. This is proceeding now, under your feet, indistinguishably from stillness.

The moss is growing. The moss is always growing — outward at its edges, upward in its fronds, into the gaps between its existing colonies, and in some locations through the rock itself by chemical process, acids produced by the organism finding their way into crystal boundaries in the stone. The moss shapes the stone. The stone shapes the moss. This has been reciprocal for longer than any distinction between them makes sense, and the current state of both reflects every prior negotiation.

The clouds circulate. This is visible and can be watched: they form at the plateau's own elevation, pushed by temperature differentials between the plateau surface and the air above, and they move in patterns that repeat on cycles of hours and days but never repeat exactly, a system with deep structure and irreducible contingency. The clouds produce rain selectively — some areas of the plateau receive more, and the vegetation there is thicker, and the stone there is wetter, and the built things there are mossy in ways that structures elsewhere are not. This differential is a kind of slow writing, a record of where the clouds have been landing their attention across the years.

The settlement is doing something that is harder to describe as a process but that functions as one: it is expanding and contracting simultaneously. New structures are added where need and material coincide. Old structures are incorporated into new ones, their walls becoming the walls of something different, their roofing replaced, their purposes revised. The settlement breathes in a time scale that makes individual human lives only slightly longer than a breath. What it is building toward, or whether building toward is the right framing for a place this old that has presumably already arrived at itself, is unclear.

## The Weirdness

And here is where care is required, because the place does not announce this.

At this elevation and this age and this scale, the plateau has been through more than elapsed time accounts for. The erosion scores it correctly — it is at its final geomorphological state, or very nearly, the stone as low as it will be before the next major geological event resets everything — but the *finality* of this condition seems to have worked some change in the nature of the place that is not straightforwardly physical. The plateau has been *settled* for so long that it has developed, one might say, opinions.

Not in the human sense. Not in any sense that generates communication or intention in recognizable forms. But the place has characteristics that new places do not have, and some of those characteristics are not entirely explicable by geology, ecology, or the human presence.

For instance: the shadows here fall at angles that do not always match the light source. This is subtle and is not constant — for hours at a time, shadows behave normally, and then a moment occurs in which a shadow falls slightly toward the sun, or in which your own shadow is longer than it should be at this hour, or shorter, or angled degrees off. If you stop and examine this, it self-corrects while you're watching. If you accept it and continue, it continues.

For instance: the water in the basins sometimes contains reflections of the sky that do not match the sky above. Not dramatically — not a different sky, not different stars — but the clouds in the water are arranged differently than the clouds overhead, as though the water is reflecting a sky from a slightly different time, recently past or near future, the exact temporal displacement unclear. The people of the settlement drink this water without evident concern, which suggests either that they have accounted for this or that they have been here long enough not to notice it anymore.

For instance: distances between the stone outcrops are not consistent. You can walk from one to another and count your steps, and then walk back and count differently, with neither measurement obviously wrong. The outcrops are where they are. The distances between them simply do not fix. This is not an illusion in the sense that illusions are accidental or correctable — the ground between the outcrops is exactly as much ground as you cross when crossing it, and you cross different amounts at different times. The plateau contains more or less interior space depending on when you measure it, or on some condition of measurement that is not yet understood.

For instance: the built things of the settlement seem to have been arranged with knowledge of the previous point. The paths are laid in such a way that they are always the same length, regardless of what the space around them does. You walk a path from structure to structure and the distance is what it always is, even when the surrounding space has been generous or withholding with its dimensions. The people who built the paths knew something about this, or intuited it, or discovered it empirically over generations, and built accordingly. The paths are reality anchors. Staying on them is a practical strategy here, not merely a social one.

For instance: the noise of the place, already described as quiet, sometimes produces sounds that are not sounds in any production sense — not the wind, not the rain, not the stone or the moss or any identifiable source — but something that arrives at the edge of audibility and occupies a frequency range that is more felt than heard. This is infrequent. It correlates with nothing obvious. It is not alarming to the long-term inhabitants because they are long-term inhabitants, but it is notable to a newcomer. It sounds — the best approximation — like the tectonic plates of a planet becoming aware of each other. A mutual recognition of enormous surfaces, subterranean, transmitted through rock too old to be surprised by anything.

## The People Here

They are few, and they have been here long enough that few is what they have always been. Population density here is not a symptom of decline — it is a feature of a livable equilibrium at altitude, in cold, with limited water and a landscape that requires careful working. They are not primitive in any condescending sense; they are primitive in the accurate sense that their technology is early, their tools are hand-tools, their fabrication is manual, and they have developed that technology to extraordinary refinement within its constraints. The lashing that holds their pole-joints together is braided in patterns that take years to learn and produce a joint of impressive resilience. The stone-fitting of their walls is the result of centuries of accumulated knowledge about which stones fit with which other stones and how to find the right stone in a given outcrop rather than forcing the wrong one. Their weaving, wherever there is weaving, is dense and close and made from materials that are only available here, that have no analogues below, and that produce textiles with properties — warmth, water management, dimensional stability — that reflect an entirely local knowledge base that has been developing for a very long time.

Their relationship to the weirdness of the plateau is pragmatic. They have a vocabulary for it, which is not the same as an explanation for it, but vocabulary is sufficient for managing. They know which conditions precede the shadow misalignments and adjust their activities accordingly. They know which basins have more temporally displaced reflections and which are more reliable. They know the paths, and they know which paths are most reliable when the space is being generous or withholding.

There is a practice — calling it ritual is an imposition of a category that may not fit — in which certain members of the settlement walk to the specific outcrops that have the most uncertain geometry and remain there for a period of time. Not doing anything visible. Present. This appears to stabilize the surrounding space for a time, though causality is difficult to establish. Perhaps the space stabilizes coincidentally while someone is there. Perhaps the presence itself matters. The people seem to believe the latter but do not explain it and are not certain that explanation is the appropriate response to this particular phenomenon.

## What the Place Is

The plateau has arrived at itself. This is the most important thing. Everything else — the cold, the lush moss in its cracks, the water appearing and disappearing, the sparse settlement of people with their hand-tools and their practical relationship to reality's local fluctuations, the clouds of its own making circling overhead — all of this is what arrival looks like after this many years of going.

The place has the quality of a very old face. Nothing more to conceal, nothing more to prove, no aspect of its character that has not long since been exposed and worn smooth and accepted. The active processes within it — the ongoing erosion, the biological growth, the meteorological circulation, the subtle reality fluctuations that may or may not count as a process — are not efforts toward anything. They are what it does. This is what doing looks like when becoming is finished.

To come here from somewhere younger is to feel the youth of where you came from as a kind of noise. Everywhere else is becoming something. The plateau has become.

Whether that makes it peaceful or unsettling depends on what you came looking for and how much you were attached to the idea that the future will be different from now. Here, that attachment gets tested. Not destroyed — tested. The light still changes. The rain still comes and goes. The moss grows. The people move along their stabilizing paths.

But the sense of *toward* has left this place, if it was ever here. And in the absence of toward, what remains is the specific, fine-grained, highly detailed texture of what simply and finally *is* — the ash-grey stone, the dew on the filaments, the subterranean recognition of enormous surfaces, the shadow falling wrong, the child of the settlement learning the path with her feet before she knows what the path is for.

---

*Far off, at the plateau's edge — which is also, from the plateau's center, just a feature of distance, not a boundary you can see — the cloud system performs its circulation. It will come back through here in a few hours. It always does. It is one of the things this place has come to count on, and counting on is, at this level of age, essentially the same as love.*
