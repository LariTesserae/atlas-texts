# The Lowland Storm Forest

---

The sound arrives before anything else does. It arrives before the eyes adjust, before the cold registers on exposed skin, before the mind has finished assembling what the body is standing in. It is not one sound. It is thousands of sounds happening simultaneously at volumes that compress into a single physical pressure — a roaring, crashing, snapping, hissing, drumming immensity that you feel in the sternum, in the back teeth, in the fluid behind the eyes. Rain on broad leaves. Rain on standing water. Rain on rain. Wind tearing through canopy that is so dense it becomes a second ground, a green ceiling that convulses and tears and re-seals itself in continuous motion. Somewhere below the rain-noise, because rain-noise is the sky of the sonic landscape, there are lower things: the crack of young wood not yet old enough to know it should not break, the bass percussion of water dropping from height into water at rest, the continuous white-noise exhale of a billion wet surfaces releasing vapor into air already saturated past capacity for vapor. You cannot think in complete sentences here. Thought arrives in fragments, in images, in the recognition of single things before the roar takes them.

---

The valley is wide. That is the first terrestrial fact.

It is a low place between hills that are not yet really hills — they are suggestions of eventual hills, lifted recently enough that their edges remain sharp, their angles unrounded, their slopes not yet given over to the slow democratic slump of erosion. The hills show their bones. Pale rock, something granitic and close-grained, protrudes in fresh shelves and fins along the ridgelines, and where it faces the prevailing wind it is bare and bright even in the darkness, a brightness that is the brightness of wet stone with no soil yet committed to it. The hills hold rain the way new stone holds rain: by running it off immediately, efficiently, with great enthusiasm, so that the valley floor receives not only what the sky sends directly but everything the young hills refuse.

The valley itself is low enough that the water table is a concept with very little practical meaning. The ground is not wet the way a lawn is wet after sprinklers. The ground is the upper surface of water. You would call it a marsh except that marshes imply a certain settled quality, a knowing relationship between the land and its moisture, and this place has not settled. The vegetation erupts from the wet ground in extraordinary density — sedges that come to chest height, ferns whose fronds are individually the size of a small door, willows so young and so full of growing-urgency that they have achieved in what must have been a decade what a normal willow would accomplish in five. Among the willows there are taller things: alders, cottonwoods, some broad-leafed species that has no settled common name because no one has settled here long enough to name it, all of them shot upward with the particular frenetic energy of young trees in rich ground with no competition yet established, all of them still working out their spatial relationships through the medium of crowding and lean and the slow violence of branch-on-branch contact.

The canopy, seen from below on a calm day, would be beautiful. On this day — and it is always this day here, the dynamic of this place is storm, is turbulence as a standing condition, not weather but climate operating at the scale of a single event that never ends — the canopy is a chaos engine. Entire sections tear free and reattach. Branches the diameter of a human torso whip through arcs that seem impossible, contact other branches, spray water in explosions of droplets, spring back. The trees are young enough to be flexible, flexible enough to survive what should destroy them, and this is part of what makes the roaring: not destruction but an immense and continuous resilience, a billion points of accommodation, every leaf a sail that has learned to spill wind, every stem a spring.

---

The darkness is not the darkness of night.

It is the darkness of layer upon layer of leaf-canopy in motion, the darkness of a sky that has committed to cloud cover as a permanent meteorological posture, the darkness of late afternoon on the shortest days compressed into something that behaves like permanent condition. A dim, gray-green light fills the valley. It is the light that passes through a jar of pond water when you hold it to a window. It illuminates without revealing, makes everything equally present and equally ambiguous. The wet bark of the alders is black and glistening. The fern fronds are almost luminous in their greenness, the kind of green that seems to generate rather than receive light, and they move constantly, shivering, rotating, pressed flat by gusts and then springing back, and the effect of thousands of fern fronds in simultaneous agitated motion in low light is something between calming and maddening depending on how long you have been standing in it.

Water runs along the undersides of leaves before it falls. Water collects in the cups formed where branches meet trunks, overflows, traces channels down the bark, drops to lower branches, traces more channels, falls to the ferns, runs down the fern-stems, reaches the ground-water and joins it. Every surface is in the business of moving water toward the valley floor. The valley floor has been receiving this water since the hills first lifted it and directed it here, and the ground-water is warm relative to the air — a slightly sulfurous warmth, the warmth of decomposition and of the Earth's own mild opinion — and where the cold air meets the warm ground-water there is fog, or would be fog on a calmer day. Here it is more like a continuous disruption, wisps that form and are immediately torn by the wind and reform behind a log or a dense fern-cluster and are torn again, so the ground-level atmosphere has a quality of constant incompletion, of weather that is always trying to become something specific and is always interrupted.

---

There is a structure at the valley's edge.

It is not large. It is partly buried by the speed at which this landscape grows — willows have come up through what were once windows, and moss has colonized the roof to a depth that suggests years of inattention, except that years here produce decades of accumulation. The structure is metal, partly, and partly something else. The something else is the part that marks it as beyond what the current moment knows how to make. It is a surface that does not corrode despite the wet; it is a surface that does not quite look the same color when viewed from different angles, not because of any iridescence but because of something more fundamental about how it relates to the light that falls on it, as though it is participating in the conversation about light rather than merely receiving it. The structure hums. Even through the roaring of the storm you can feel the hum more than hear it, a frequency that is not the frequency of any natural process in the valley, a frequency that the teeth know before the ears do.

Inside, presumably, there are instruments. Outside, there are two more structures similarly half-consumed by vegetation, similarly humming, positioned at intervals along the valley edge with a spacing that suggests a surveying purpose, a triangulation, a watching. Whatever they are watching, they have been watching without requiring maintenance or human presence for long enough that the valley has largely forgotten they are here. The fauna have not forgotten — the deer that browse at the valley margins give them a measured distance, and the large wading birds that work the shallower margins of the groundwater lake that the valley floor has become do not nest near them — but this awareness is quiet, habitual, not frightened.

---

The fauna of the valley is abundant in the way a young, rich, turbulent ecosystem is abundant: chaotically, opportunistically, in populations that have not yet been pruned to their eventual stable sizes.

There are wading birds everywhere along the margins of open water. Some are familiar — herons standing with their extraordinary patience, heads cocked, watching below the surface with a precision that the surrounding chaos seems not to affect — and some are less familiar, smaller birds with long legs and extremely short wings that suggest they have not needed flight recently and are in the process of forgetting it, though this is probably not true yet, probably they can still fly when pressed. Waterfowl scatter before gusts, land, scatter again. Small mammals move in the deep fern-coverage, visible only as movement, as fern-shiver that does not match the wind. There are insects in number but the cold and the rain have driven most of them into the protection of the dense plant-mass, and they emerge only in the brief intervals between gusts when the air is merely wet rather than actively violent.

Fish in the channel that crosses the valley floor — a channel recent enough that it has not yet committed to a single path, that bifurcates and rejoins and bifurcates again across the lowest ground. In the fish: the same urgency, the same frenetic quality. They hold against the current in loose schools, silver-bodied, quick.

No predators that make themselves visible. The feeling of predation is present without its source — the awareness that the density and the darkness and the noise together would make an excellent context for large, quiet, watching things. This feeling is probably not warranted. The valley is young. The large predators have not arrived yet, or have arrived and moved on, finding the turbulence difficult to hunt in, finding the noise too much competition for the sounds they need.

---

The cold is not brutal.

It is the cold of a wet place in early winter, a cold that gets into the clothing rather than hitting the skin, that works by absorption rather than impact. The air temperature is low enough that the breath plumes and vanishes immediately in the roaring. The ground-water temperature works against this cold at the lowest points, and there are seeps along the base of the young hillsides where warmer subsurface water emerges through fresh cracks in the not-yet-pressured rock, and around these seeps the vegetation is even more extravagant, even more crowded, even more urgently alive, as though the warmth is a rumor and everything wants to be near it.

Ice has tried to form in the still-water pockets. It has not succeeded. The motion of the air and the relative warmth of the groundwater conspire against it. There are traces of ice-attempt at the edges of the still-water pockets, a thin translucent margin that the waves from the storm break continuously, so that the margins of the still-water pockets are edged with a perpetual fringe of breaking ice-crystal, a thing that forms and breaks and forms again on a cycle of minutes.

---

The place is not strange in any single identifiable way. It is not uncanny. Its strangeness is only accumulative, only a matter of degree — too much water, too much vegetation, too much density, too much sound, too much motion — and yet the accumulation becomes its own kind of experience, the experience of standing inside a process rather than beside it, of being in the presence of something at full commitment, at full expression of its own nature.

The valley grows. It grows at the speed of the fastest willow. It changes at the speed of the storm. It watches, through its three half-buried instruments, at a speed and with a patience and toward an end that it does not share with the herons or the small wingless birds or the silver fish or the person standing in the fern-cover with cold water running down the back of the neck, teeth aching from the hum, trying to think in complete sentences and failing.
