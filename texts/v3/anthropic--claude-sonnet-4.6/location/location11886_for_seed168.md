# The Plateau of Unfinished Stone

---

There is a high place where the world has not yet decided what it wants to be.

You reach it by climbing through nothing in particular — no path, no trail worn by animals, because there are no animals — and when the slope finally releases you onto the plateau, the first thing you understand is the silence. Not the silence of abandonment, not the hush after something has ended, but the silence of something that has not yet begun to speak. A silence with mass to it. A silence that is also a kind of pressure against the eardrums, a presence rather than an absence, as though the air here remembers no sound and has no template for receiving one.

The plateau extends. That is the primary fact of it. It extends in a way that exceeds your immediate comprehension, and then continues extending past that. The horizon is real but it is also irrelevant — a technicality. The land simply goes, carries itself past visibility, implies its own continuation with the calm authority of something that knows it will still be there whether or not you can see it. It is valley-sized, regional in feeling, the kind of scale that requires the word *territory* rather than *place*. From where you stand, the curvature of thought begins to matter. You cannot think about the whole of it at once. You file away parts of it for later, and the parts you file away do not feel diminished by the filing.

---

The stone here is new. Not young in the way of worn-smooth riverbeds or ancient mountains sanded to gentleness — young in the way of something being made right now, incrementally, without hurry. It is raw-geology in the truest sense: not yet settled into its own identity. The surface in most places is bare mineral interrupted by what you might at first mistake for frost, though the air is warm. What you are seeing is something closer to efflourescence — minerals pushing up through the surface plane with no particular urgency, forming pale crusts and crystalline webs that cover perhaps a third of the visible stone. The rock itself varies: sections of it are grey and undifferentiated, like compressed potential, like lithology that has not yet received its assignment. Other sections have begun stratifying — faint horizontal lines appearing in the cliff faces at the plateau's edge, the planet beginning to develop the habit of layers, history beginning to make the gesture of accumulating without quite following through.

There are no riverbeds, no watercourses, no dry washes, no arroyos. The drainage logic of this landscape is simply absent. If it rains here — and the humidity in the air, faint but real, suggests something — the water has nowhere to go and so it does not arrive. Or it arrives and immediately vanishes into the stone, claimed before it can establish a route. The chemistry here runs on other principles. Some of the mineral extrusions are pale greenish-white and smell faintly of something sharp and almost electrical, something that is not quite sulfur and is not quite ozone and is exactly itself.

---

But the plants.

This is what the plateau refuses to be simple about.

They begin perhaps two hundred meters from where the stone is rawest, and then they become profuse with a speed that has no ecological explanation — no gradient, no transition zone, no sparse pioneering species followed by thicker growth. They are simply there, starting at an edge, and then they are everywhere. The vegetation is lush in the way that word fails to contain: it is deeply, structurally lush, lush with conviction. Broad-leafed plants — nothing that matches exactly what you know — spread low across the stone in overlapping layers, their leaves dark green on the upper surface and a matte, almost chalky green-white beneath. The leaves are large, some the width of your outstretched hand. They do not move. There is no wind here worth the name.

Between the broad-leafed ground cover, taller stalks rise at intervals, thick as a forearm at their base, topped with structures that are not quite flowers and are not quite seed-pods and fulfill the function of both without committing to either. These structures are pale and slightly luminescent in a way you cannot quite catch directly — the glow seems to occupy the peripheral vision and politely leave when you look at it. The stalks are evenly spaced, as though they have already calculated their own arrangement, as though the density question was solved before the growing began. The spacing gives the landscape a quality of being almost architectural without any architecture being present.

There is no undergrowth competition visible, no dying back, no patches of yellow. The vegetation does not appear to be doing anything except being what it is, at a high and stable level of being it. It does not look tended. It does not look wild. It looks finished — finished in its lushness, complete in a way that asks no further questions of itself.

---

And then: the sound.

Not from the vegetation. The vegetation contributes nothing to the acoustic record of this place. The sound comes from deeper in the plateau, from below it, from the stone itself. It is industrial in character — that is the only honest word — and yet there are no machines here, no structures, no evidence of manufacture, no made thing anywhere on this entire high plain. The sound is subterranean and regular: a deep, slow rhythmic percussion, like a press descending and ascending, descending and ascending, at the pace of something very large and unconcerned with efficiency. Every forty seconds, approximately. You feel it first in the soles of your feet before you hear it.

Beneath the pressing rhythm, a lower continuous sound: a long tonal thrum, almost below the frequency of hearing, felt more than heard. The kind of sound that makes you aware of your ribcage as a structure. It does not change pitch. It does not modulate. It has been doing this before you arrived and will be doing it when you have been gone long enough that your having been here is not a fact that any part of this plateau retains.

What the sounds suggest is this: that the earth beneath the plateau is working. That something tectonic and pressurized and purposeful is happening in the substrate — not eruption, not catastrophe, not chaos. The opposite of those things. An ordered, industrial-scale process of geological becoming. The plateau is being made, from below, steadily, without urgency. The press descends. The mineral is consolidated. The press ascends. The next layer is considered. This is what construction looks like when it requires no architects and no workers and no time pressure: it looks like silence broken only by itself, like stone manufacturing stone, like a world in the practice of arriving.

---

The light is full. It is day, completely, with no ambiguity about that fact. The sun — some sun — stands at an angle that suggests mid-morning or mid-afternoon, casting short shadows from the plant stalks, catching the mineral crusts so that they give back a faint crystalline shine at the right angles. The sky is high and blue and present without being dramatic. Clouds are visible at the far edges of the plateau, sitting on the horizon like thoughts that have not yet been incorporated into the scene. They are not moving noticeably. They are simply there, in their positions, fulfilling their atmospheric role.

The warmth is not aggressive. It sits in the upper end of comfortable, the kind of warmth that would be pleasant if you were resting and undemanding if you were not. It does not shift. It does not build toward afternoon intensity. It does not seem to have done anything different earlier in the day and will not do anything different later. The temperature is a condition, not an event.

---

Here is the list of things absent:

Water in any form that moves or pools or speaks. Animals of any kind — nothing walks here, nothing flies above it, nothing burrows within it, nothing passes through. Roads, walls, cairns, paths, any mark left by any intelligence that walks on legs and uses hands. Birdsong, insect sound, the noise of mammal presence. Wind enough to stir leaves. Weather of any consequence. Decay visible anywhere in the vegetation. Young plants. Old plants. Change.

Here is the list of things present:

Stone in the process of becoming itself. Vegetation that has arrived at a permanent arrangement and stopped there. An industrial rhythm from no industry. Warmth from no particular source of drama. Light that fills but does not interrogate. A vast open stillness that is not emptiness because the plateau is too large and too purposeful to be empty — it is simply between stages, resting at a high plateau of its own existence, waiting in the way that a system in perfect equilibrium waits: which is to say, without waiting at all.

---

If you stood here long enough, you might begin to feel that the plateau was not a place you were visiting but a process you had wandered into the middle of. That the vegetation was not simply growing but had grown — had arrived at completion and stopped — and was now simply present as an achieved fact. That the stone below you was not waiting to erode or weather or be carved by forces into something more legible, but was instead accumulating toward a form it had already planned in some deep pressurized geological calculus that predated your arrival by no time at all.

The world does not feel old here. It feels precisely as old as it needs to be to have done what it has done, and that age is not particularly great. This is what new stone looks like when it already knows what it is doing. This is what lush vegetation looks like when it has no competition and no predators and no history of either. This is what warmth feels like when it has never been cold and does not know the word.

You are on a plateau that is in the act of its own formation, that has paused at a high and stable moment of that act, that may stay here — at this exact stage, this exact density of mineral and plant and thrumming sub-industrial geological labor — for longer than the concept of waiting applies to.

The press descends.

The plateau does not notice you.

The press ascends.
