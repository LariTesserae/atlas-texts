# The Drowned Plateau

---

The water here is not deep in the way oceans are deep. It is deep in the way sky is deep — meaning there is no bottom you are falling toward, only an immensity you are suspended inside of. The plateau sits at elevation, high enough that on a clear day — if clear days still happened — you would once have seen the curvature of the coast from its rim. But there is no rim now that means anything. The water came up and kept going, and the plateau is simply where it stopped being quite so total.

Here the surface lies somewhere around forty feet above the old stone. Forty feet of cold, pale, moving water, lit from above by a sun that reaches down in long angled columns, wavering and slow, like light that has forgotten what it was supposed to illuminate.

The light is normal in its intensity. This is one of the stranger facts of the place. You might expect the underwater world to be dim, green, obscure — and in the deep places it is those things. But up here on the plateau, the water column is shallow enough that the light gets through almost fully. It is slightly diffused. It has a quality like overcast noon — present, even, shadowless in most directions, casting only faint suggestions of shadow where the columns strike the stone at angles. Everything is visible. Everything is clear. You can see a very long way.

And what you see, mostly, is nothing.

---

The plateau extends — the word *extends* does not capture it. The plateau *is* a dimension. It stretches in all directions past the limit of vision, which is itself extraordinary given the clarity of the water. You can see, perhaps, three or four kilometers in any direction before the pale columns of filtered light simply dissolve into a generalizing blue-grey haze. In all that volume: stone, water, light, and very little else. The stone floor — old granite, basalt intrusions, younger sedimentary sheets that haven't had time to compress fully — lies far below, and the ceiling of the world is the membrane of the surface, visible as a trembling mirror when you look up. Between those two planes: almost nothing.

The emptiness has a physical quality. Not the emptiness of a room that has been cleared, or a landscape that has been abandoned. Something more fundamental — the emptiness of a place that the world has not yet filled. As if the water rose too fast and the things that should live here haven't arrived yet. Or are on their way. Or decided, reasonably, not to come.

The fauna is here, though. The fauna is very much here — but the fauna contributes to the emptiness rather than reducing it, which requires explanation.

---

They are large.

Each one is large enough that its presence registers as *region* rather than *creature.* Shapes that you perceive first as geological features — a darkening in the water at distance, a slow compression of the light columns, a change in the way currents move — and only later, and only gradually, resolve into the understanding that the darkening is moving, that the compression has intention, that the current change is wake.

They are not fish. They are not cetaceans or cephalopods or any category with a name you would recognize. They are vast filtering animals, something like manta rays enormously scaled, something like jellyfish architecturally inverted, something like the idea of a creature that evolved to process water on a continental scale. Their bodies are nearly translucent — you can see the light shift through them, the columns of sunlight bending and elongating as they pass through the flesh, so that each animal is also a lens, projecting distorted patches of light onto the stone floor far below. They move by mechanisms that aren't obvious — no fins working, no pulsation of jets, just a slow perpetual glide, banking with terrible patience.

There are many of them. But *many* at this scale means: several visible at any given moment as distant darknesses, a few closer shapes taking minutes to resolve into full comprehension, and occasionally one passing close enough that its passage is an event. The shadow of it. The pressure change of it. The way sound alters in its vicinity.

And now we should talk about the sound.

---

The sound is why you cannot think clearly here, at first.

It is not loud in the way explosions are loud. It is not sudden or percussive. It is deafening in the way the ocean is deafening when you submerge your ears at the shore — a total presence of noise that fills every frequency simultaneously and leaves no cognitive room for anything else to exist.

The sources are these:

*First:* the animals themselves produce sound on a scale that is not really sound, which is to say the vibrations they generate are so low in frequency that they are felt as pressure more than heard as tone. They communicate — if it is communication — in subsonic ranges, in frequencies that resonate inside the bones of the plateau itself. The granite vibrates. Standing on any solid surface, you would feel it in your teeth. The calls travel thousands of kilometers and reflect off the far edges of the plateau and return, layered on top of the direct signal, and the whole of the plateau's water column is at any moment threaded through with the superimposed songs of hundreds of these animals, overlapping, refracting, creating standing waves and dead zones and places where two frequencies cancel into a silence so precise it feels manufactured.

*Second:* the machinery.

---

There is machinery here.

It is scattered — this is the right word. Not built in any coherent fashion. Not maintained by anything obviously present. Scattered like things are scattered when they've been dropped from a great height, or deposited by a process that has no interest in arrangement. There are structures that look like they were once above water: platforms, derricks, the upper sections of things that extend down into the sediment below — industrial infrastructure of an earlier era, now simply *here,* partly corroded but not fully, kept in a strange equilibrium by the cold water and by something else that is harder to name.

The derricks still function. This is the unusual thing. Not in any way that seems to have a current purpose — they are not extracting anything, not producing anything, not connected to any visible power source. But they run. You can see the motion of them through the water, mechanical arms cycling through their sequences with a regularity that is more unsettling than chaos would be. Some are anchored to submerged platforms. Some float freely, kept at depth by ballast arrangements that suggest design, though what kind of design and by whom is not apparent. They are electric, or were — lights still burn on some of them, yellow-white industrial floods pointing in directions that illuminate only open water and the occasional passing belly of a vast animal overhead.

The sound of the machinery adds a mechanical layer to the biological infrastructure of sound. Gears turning, chains running through their guides, pumps cycling fluid through circuits with no apparent intake or output. The metal is young — this is one of the signs that the plateau is recently emerged, or that the machinery was recently installed, or both. Not the rusted red-orange of long exposure but a surface corrosion, a first oxidation, still showing the color of the original steel beneath it. The edges of the cut metal are still sharp. The welds still show the texture of their making.

The machinery runs its sequences without variation. Every derrick cycles at its own rate, and those rates do not change, have not changed, will not change. The equilibrium of this place is total. Nothing here is moving toward any other state. The cold keeps it. The depth keeps it. Something in the nature of this particular plateau keeps it.

---

The floor of the plateau, where you can see it, is stone as stone gets when it's been expressed from the earth's interior and hasn't yet had time to be anything else. Basalt columns in some sections, still showing their hexagonal organization, the geometry that magma falls into when it wants to be efficient about cooling. Granite sheets cracked by pressure into large angular blocks. Almost no sediment yet — the currents across the plateau surface are steady enough that silt doesn't accumulate, so the stone is exposed. Clean. Hard. The fractures where one plate section meets another are sharp-edged, the kind of sharp that disappears over geological time, still present here because geological time hasn't had a chance to do its work.

There is no plant life on the stone. No algae. No moss. No kelp anchored to the basalt columns. The cold is part of this. The relative newness of the environment is part of this. Something about the sonic pressure the animals generate may be part of this — there is research to suggest that sustained low-frequency vibration at the intensities present here would interfere with the cellular structures of plant reproduction. Whatever the cause, the stone is clean. Mineral. It is a world of its own kind before biology arrived to complicate things, and it has been returned to something like that state.

There is, in a few of the sheltered places — in the lee of the machinery bases, in gaps between large block sections of granite — a growth that could be bacterial mat, or could be something closer to the thin edge of fungal organization. Dark, flat, almost invisible unless the light catches it at an angle. It does not move. It does not obviously grow. It is simply there, having found the conditions it needed, in whatever narrow margin the conditions allowed.

This is the only concession the plateau has made to organic complexity, apart from the vast, gliding, light-bending animals that inhabit its water column.

---

If there is weather — and there is weather, this being a place vast enough to generate its own — it occurs at the surface, which is to say it occurs in another world from the one you inhabit below. Looking up, you can sometimes see the membrane of the surface roughen and distort, see the even ceiling of light-from-above break into something more turbulent. The light shifts. There are periods of dimming that might correspond to cloud cover, or to the sun simply having moved — impossible to tell from below. The animals respond to these changes, which is how you know they are real changes and not an artifact of perception. Their glide paths shift. The low-frequency sound alters, not in volume but in character, the way a conversation alters in tone without becoming louder. They are attending to something you cannot see.

The surface storms, if that's what they are, produce no perceptible wave action at the depth of the plateau. The water column absorbs everything. What reaches the stone below is: nothing. Equilibrium. The same cold, the same even light (slightly shifted), the same pressure of sound.

---

The cold is not the cold of suffering. It is the cold of the deep ocean — functional, total, below what warm-blooded biology prefers but above what water becomes when it considers becoming something else. The water at this elevation would, in another climate, be surface water with some thermal variation. Here it is uniformly cold from the surface down. The warm water that would ordinarily stratify on top doesn't warm enough to stratify. It sinks. Mixes. The plateau's entire water column is one temperature, and that temperature is just cold enough that your hands, if you had hands, would begin to lose precision after a certain duration of exposure.

The machinery is unaffected by this. Or has been designed for it. Or doesn't have the option of being affected.

---

There is one more thing, which is the hardest to describe and may be the most essential.

The vast animals do not appear to eat.

They move through the water in their filtering fashion, their structures open to the passage of water through them, and the water passes through — you can see it happening, see the slight displacement pressure on either side of each animal as water enters the leading structure and exits somewhere along the trailing margin. But the plateau's water is empty enough that what they are filtering is unclear. There is nothing visible to eat. The water column has no bloom of plankton, no concentration of particulate matter, nothing that would sustain a creature of this size through normal biological mathematics.

And yet they are healthy — as far as you can tell, which is only as far as their slow, patient, continent-crossing glide suggests. They have the look of things that have found exactly the conditions they require. They are not starving. They are not searching. They glide their slow, enormous glides through the empty water and the even light, and the machinery runs its cycles below them, and the stone is cold and bare and young, and the sound of all of it fills the water from surface to floor in a continuous roar that you stop hearing as sound eventually and begin to understand as simply the nature of the medium you are inside.

This is what the plateau is: a world that has found its arrangement and intends to keep it. The water rose. The animals came. The machinery continued. The stone stayed young. And now it holds, in the way that things hold when they have found the precise shape of their own equilibrium and there is nothing left in them that is capable of wanting anything different.
