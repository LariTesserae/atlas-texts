# The Condensation Cities

---

Begin with what you feel before you see anything, because there is almost nothing to see.

Cold. Not the clean cold of altitude you might expect — not the bright-sky, thin-air cold of a mountain in sunlight — but a wet cold, a cold that enters through the skin and keeps moving inward, a cold with the patience of something that has been waiting a very long time and has nothing else planned. The air is not quite fog and not quite rain. It is something between the two that has never been given a name because naming it would require staying here long enough to agree on anything, and nobody stays long enough for that. Droplets form on the backs of your hands without falling from anywhere. The atmosphere is simply condensing onto you. It is condensing onto everything.

You are high. The ground — and it is barely ground yet, more a suggestion of ground, a proposal the rock is making to itself — drops away in every direction into a darkness that has no visible floor. You are somewhere on a shelf or buttress of something young. The geology here is *nascent*. That is the only word. This stone was not here, in any meaningful sense, very long ago. The formations that jut upward through the mist are not yet sure of their own edges. They have the quality of things deciding what shape to take. Stress fractures run through them without breaking them. Mineral seams bleed different colors of wet: ochre, gray-green, something that is almost blue in the near-dark. The rock weeps. Every surface weeps. Water does not fall so much as it *arrives*, materializing at any point it pleases, running along a face of stone as though following instructions, pooling in dimples and embryonic basins that will not, for ten thousand years, look like what they are becoming.

The darkness is not total. That is important to understand early. If it were total, you could simply call it night and wait for morning. But there is no morning this description is building toward. The darkness here is a permanent condition with exceptions. The exceptions are what you need to look at.

---

Light comes from the city.

The city — or the thing that is functioning as a city, or that was a city once and may be a city again, or that is a city in ways that require different vocabulary — occupies the space around you in layers. Above, below, lateral, receding into the mist. It is not lit by anything so simple as electricity or fire. The structures produce light as a secondary effect of what they are doing, the way a transformer hums as a side effect of moving current, the way a hot pipe ticks as it cools. The structures are doing something, and light happens while they do it. It comes from junctions in the materials. It comes from the gaps between components that were designed to be precisely as close together as they are, no closer, and in that exact gap some process operates that emits, among other things, a dim blue-gray luminescence that does not illuminate so much as *indicate*. It says: *here.* It says: *this is where something is.* It does not help you see.

The architecture does not have a single grammar. Portions of it are old in ways the rock beneath them is not — the stone was still deciding what it was when the first structures were placed on it, and those structures look like they knew that, like they were designed to anchor in material that had not finished forming. Roots — not plant roots, structural roots, metal or something like metal, something that learned from metal and improved on the idea — reach into cracks in the new stone and participate in the stone's formation. They have been there long enough to be included. The rock has grown around them. This is, perhaps, the oldest part of what is here.

Other portions are not old at all. They are, in some sense, happening. Additions accumulate against older surfaces in ways that do not look like construction — no scaffolding, no workers, no equipment in any form you recognize. The material seems to deposit itself the way mineral does, layer by slow layer, following gradients only it can perceive. This is also, you realize, construction. It is construction the way a coral reef is construction. It is happening too slowly to observe in any given moment and too surely to mistake for anything else when you look away and look back, over years or decades, over the long slow patient time this place operates inside.

Everything here operates in slow time. The city's pulse, whatever it is, has a period you cannot feel — like trying to sense the rotation of the planet through your feet. You know it is moving. You cannot catch it moving.

---

The fauna make the sound.

This is the revelation you have been moving toward.

The noise here is enormous — not violent, not painful, but vast and omnidirectional and continuous, a noise that has the same relationship to silence that the darkness has to light: it is the ground condition, the baseline. It comes from every direction including up and down and from directly inside your ears somehow, as though the atmosphere itself is vibrating at just below the frequency that would damage you.

The fauna.

They are everywhere. *Everywhere* is not hyperbole. The structures of the city are populated the way the ocean is populated — not with things moving through a neutral medium, but with things for whom the medium and the city both are *substrate*, *habitat*, *body*. Things cling to every surface. Things move through the gaps between surfaces — slowly, as everything here moves slowly, but continuously. Things have built their own structures against the city's structures, and those additional structures have been built against and added to and built against again until the distinction between what was placed by design and what has accreted by life is not a distinction any more.

The shapes are difficult. You do not have good analogies. Many of them are soft in some parts and rigid in others, like things that have not yet decided what they are, which is appropriate for a place where the geology has not yet decided what it is. Many of them are large — unexpectedly large for something you are this close to. They move the way tides move, which is to say: they move, but only suspicion tells you so, only the fact that what you see when you look away is different from what you see when you look back. Many of them produce light, secondary light, light as an artifact of process. Their bodies run with it, faintly, in patterns that shift too slowly to be called dynamic.

The sound they make is collective and ambient and categorical and does not resolve into individual voices, or does — but the individual voices are each themselves layered, each one a structure of sounds the way the city is a structure of structures, sub-signals nested inside signals, the whole thing dense and saturated with itself. It is not communication you can parse. It may not be communication in any sense you would recognize. But it is not random, either. It has the quality of something organized — not organized with you in mind, organized toward some other purpose, continuous and slow.

---

You cannot see far. The mist and the darkness collaborate to give you a radius of perhaps forty meters in which shapes are visible, and beyond that a zone of suggestion where large things are definitely present, and beyond that nothing. But you know the city extends. You know it from the sound, which does not diminish in any direction. You know it from the light-scatter in the mist, that faint bioluminescent ghost of a glow that is coming from further away than you can see. You know it the way you know a continent exists when you are standing on a beach: not from seeing it but from the fact of it, the sense that the scale you are part of is larger than your senses can report.

The scale is regional. Town-and-valley-sized, if the town grew across a mountain face, if the valley were vertical, if the mountain's stone were still becoming itself. The city layers upward and downward on this young rock face, and the organisms layer through the city, and the water moves through all of it — through channels that were designed and channels that erosion-in-progress is just beginning to suggest and channels that the organisms have, over their slow time, worn smooth with passing.

Water is everywhere in the structure. It is not a problem they have solved; it is a resource they are using. The seepage from the weeping rock is directed, in ways too subtle to immediately see, toward purposes. It runs downward — gravity is operating normally, this much is conventional — and is, somewhere below the reach of your light, doing something. The organisms near the water-flows position themselves differently from organisms away from water-flows. The structures near water are different from structures away from water. Water is load-bearing, in some sense. Water is structural.

---

There is nothing green. There is nothing that was ever green. No plant has been here. No soil is forming — soil requires time and the biology that produces it, and the biology here produces something else, though what that something else is in the long term is not visible yet. The rock is raw and new and wet and the structures sit on it and the organisms move through it and there is nothing gentle about any surface. Everything is mineral or made-from-mineral or made-to-imitate-mineral or made-from-processes-that-occur-in-mineral so deep and so long that the distinction has stopped mattering. The cold keeps everything sharp. Nothing rots. Nothing softens. There is no decomposition, only accumulation and the slow slow work of water on new stone.

The strangeness of it — the *unusual* quality that sits at the edge of wrongness without crossing into it — is not in any single feature. It is in the combination. Cities are built by something, and you can see that this was built, that intelligence or something functioning as intelligence made decisions about where structures go and how they connect and what they are made of. But the scale of time in which those decisions were made is the scale of geology, not the scale of lives. The organisms are not clearly the builders or clearly the built-for. The city is not clearly the city of the organisms or the organisms clearly the inhabitants. They have been here together so long in such intimate contact that the categories have blurred, which is perhaps the oldest story in the world — the one about what happens when two things are in contact long enough.

---

Stand still for a while. The mist deposits on your shoulders. The cold works inward. The noise, which seemed overwhelming, begins to have texture — not meaning, exactly, but texture, the way a forest canopy has texture when you've been looking at it long enough to stop seeing green and start seeing the individual.

Something large is moving, very slowly, on a surface about twenty meters above and to your left. You can tell it is large by the quantity of secondary light that shifts when it moves. It takes several minutes to cross from one junction in the structure to another. It does not appear to notice you, and you do not entirely trust this, because something operating on timescales this slow might be noticing you in ways that will not be legible for a while yet.

The new rock beneath you makes a sound. Not often, and not loud, but it makes a sound. A sound like something settling, like something finding a position it might stay in for a few thousand years. The city above it does not respond, or responds in ways that will not be visible for longer than you are going to be here.

The water arrives on your face. The darkness holds. The sound continues, enormous and intricate and not for you. The city extends in all directions past the edge of what you can know.

This is not a place in the process of becoming something else.

This is a place in the process of becoming itself.

It has been doing this for a long time.

It has a long time left.
