# The High Town

---

The town sits in a fold of the plateau, not quite on the ridge and not quite in the valley below — settled into a shallow depression where two long slopes meet, like a palm cupped upward. You come to it by a path worn pale by feet across centuries, and the first thing you notice is how still everything is.

Not the stillness of emptiness. The stillness of something that has finished becoming.

---

The buildings are stone, of course. Stone from here, from this place specifically — you can tell because the color is continuous between the walls and the ground beneath them, the same blue-grey that grades into the hillside, the same faint iron-rust in the mortar seams as appears in the exposed ledge at the edge of town. Someone quarried close. Someone didn't want to carry things far. The walls are thick, finished with a roughness that suggests hand tools rather than any machine precision — you can see the chisel marks on the dressed corners, small parallel strokes, patient work. The blocks fit tightly, not perfectly. There are gaps that have been chinked with smaller stones, and those smaller stones are gripped by lichens, grey-green and flat, which have been there long enough to look like decoration.

The streets — if they are streets — are narrow and irregular. They were not planned so much as allowed to happen between the buildings, which were themselves placed where the ground was flattest, where a natural shelf in the bedrock offered something to build on. Two carts could not pass one another without one giving way. Three people walking abreast would fill the lane entirely. The paving, where there is paving, is uneven flagstone, dark with absorbed damp even when it hasn't recently rained.

---

It hasn't recently rained. But the damp is here regardless.

The plateau collects cloud. Not dramatically — there is no low ceiling pressing down, no dramatic fog. The light today is even and diffuse, the sort of light that comes when clouds are overhead but thin, when the sun is present as a general brightening rather than a source. This light doesn't cast hard shadows. Everything is visible, nothing is dazzled. The wet-grey of the stone is its truest color in this light. You see it as it is.

But the moisture is real. It lives in the air as a coolness at the back of the throat. It beads on the iron of the hinges — simple hand-forged things, unadorned, slightly rough where the hammer finished — that hold the shutters to the window frames. It pools in the low spots between flagstones, thin bright pockets no deeper than a thumbnail, reflecting the pale sky back upward. On the north faces of walls, where sunlight rarely reaches, the stone is almost perpetually wet, darkened to near-black at the base, drying to grey only in the upper courses where wind gets at it.

Condensation runs without urgency. A slow bead down the side of a clay pot left outside a doorway. A dark streak below a roof-edge where water collects and drips, has always dripped, has left a stain that is simply part of the wall now.

---

The town is small.

You can see most of it from the high point where the path crests before descending to the main lane. Thirty buildings, perhaps forty — it's hard to count because several share walls, lean against one another, have grown into adjacency over time. At the center, if it has a center, there is a slightly wider space, not quite a square, where the lanes converge. A well there, stone-ringed, with a wooden cover worn smooth as old skin. The cover has iron fittings, the same rough hand-forged quality as everything here, and a simple wooden crane arm for raising the bucket, pivoting on a pin.

Beyond the cluster, the buildings thin and stop. The ground resumes. The plateau continues. There is no clear edge, no wall or boundary marker — the town simply ends where the last building is, and past it the grey-green slope reasserts itself, cropped low by altitude and weather, scattered with small plants pressed flat against the ground.

---

Those plants.

This high, this cool, they are survivors more than flourishers. Low rosettes of something with thick leaves, waxy-surfaced to hold what moisture they can. Tight cushion-mounds in the cracks of the bedrock where a little soil has gathered. A few taller grasses, sparse, growing in isolated clumps rather than continuous sward — you can count the individual blades at the margins of a clump and they are not many. Between the plants is stone, or thin mineral soil the color of slate, pressed hard. Nothing grows densely here. The vegetation seems curated by difficulty down to its essential forms: whatever is left is what could stay.

In the protected angle where two walls meet and the wind doesn't reach, something woodier has established itself — a low shrub, grey-barked, leafless or nearly so, with a few small dark leaves still holding on. It grows slowly here. It has been growing for longer than it looks like it should take to reach this size. Its roots are deeper than you'd expect.

---

The animals are mostly absent, or mostly hidden, which comes to the same thing in terms of what you experience.

A bird once, heard before seen — a short dry call repeated twice and then not again. You don't see it. The call came from somewhere among the buildings and didn't say where. Later, near the well, a small pale moth settles on the stone rim and folds its wings and stays there. That is all. No dogs. No livestock audible. If there are inhabitants keeping animals somewhere, the animals make no sound today, or the sound doesn't carry.

The town gives the impression of sparse occupation rather than abandonment. There is a quality to genuine abandonment — a specific kind of weathering that looks like neglect, doors off hinges, accumulated debris of wind-blown things. Here the doors are on their hinges. The shutters close properly. The well has its cover. Things are maintained, but quietly. Someone is in the habit of maintenance without drawing attention to it.

---

The sounds that exist are small.

Wind, but not much wind — a low-frequency presence felt as much as heard, rounding the corners of the buildings without urgency. The occasional acoustic result of stone contracting in the cool air, a sound so slight it might not be sound exactly, more like the sense of the material settling into itself. The bead of water off a roof. Your own footstep on a flagstone, brief, absorbed quickly. There is no echo here. The sound leaves and doesn't return.

From somewhere interior, once, a sound of something being worked — a rhythmic scraping, very faint, that suggests hand-tool against material, though the material is unclear. Wood being planed or bone being shaped or stone being smoothed. It happens for a while and stops. It does not start again.

---

The dominant quality of this place is equilibrium.

Not stasis in the way of death or abandonment — not the frozen quality of a thing stopped mid-motion. Something more complete than that. This place has been working toward its current form for a very long time and has arrived. The erosion of the plateau, the wearing of the paths, the darkening of the stones, the growth of the lichen to its present size and shape — all of this has reached a kind of balance. The lichen will grow another millimeter over decades. The path will deepen imperceptibly. But the pace of change is so slow it is functionally indistinguishable from permanence, from the outside, in a lifetime.

The town fits its ground as an old callus fits the hand that formed it.

---

Standing in the wide place by the well in the even diffuse light, cool air moving slightly against your face, the town arranged in its irregular way around you — the narrow lanes, the thick walls, the hand-forged iron, the low plants in the cracks, the pale moth on the stone — you have the feeling of being inside something that has reached its conclusion. Not ended. Concluded. Found its form. The way a sentence, when it is right, needs nothing added or taken away.

It is very quiet. The sky is bright without sun. The stone is the color of the sky reflected in shallow water.

Nothing is about to happen. This is not a waiting. This is simply what it is.
