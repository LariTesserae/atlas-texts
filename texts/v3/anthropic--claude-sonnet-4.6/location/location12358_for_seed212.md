# The Verdant Insistence

---

Begin with what you notice first, which is not a thing but a *pressure.*

The air does not merely touch you. It *leans.* Something about the density of the green — the absolute, geological totality of plant life filling every cubic meter of space from ground to whatever passes for sky — creates an atmosphere with mass. You breathe it the way you'd breathe cloth. It is warm in the way that living bodies are warm: not solar, not radiated from stone, but metabolic, exhaled, the warmth of ongoing process. The heat is *purposeful.* Someone is making it.

Underfoot — if there is an underfoot, and this is already a question worth sitting with — the substrate is not soil. Or rather: soil was here once, recently, in the way that a cocoon was once a caterpillar. Something has replaced it. A continuous mat of root-fiber, pale-beige shading to deep reddish-brown, woven so densely that it has achieved the structural properties of timber without becoming timber. It flexes. It yields two or three centimeters under weight, then pushes back. The ground has *opinions* about where you stand.

---

## The Architecture of Green

This is a city. Or it was. Or the city was eaten and the eater has taken its shape.

Look up: the canopy closes not in layers but in tiers, each tier a distinct civilization of plant life. Closest to the ground — maybe four meters overhead — the broad palmate leaves of something like a monstera but larger, obsessively larger, tile against each other like scales on a fish or shingles on a roof. Light falls through them in moving coins and shafts, in a constant trembling aperture, because nothing here is still. The leaves breathe. You can see them breathe. Their stomata are wide enough to observe individually if you're close, and you are close, because density here means that you are always close to everything.

Above that tier, columns of vine-trunk, centuries-old by their diameter but geologically young by the freshness of their wood — you can smell the newness in them, the almost alcoholic sharpness of recently-opened cells, the way cut grass smells but richer, more complex, with undertones of something animal. The vines have claimed the load-bearing work. They form vertical members, rough natural pillars, twenty and thirty meters tall, rising toward a second canopy that exists as a nearly solid vault of interleaved branches. The vault is not static. It moves. The whole architecture *rolls* and *shifts,* a slow churning, branches extending and contracting, forking and rejoining, the pace just below what you'd call fast but far above what you'd call slow. It is on a middle timescale, the timescale of weather or of bread rising, visible change requiring only the patience of minutes.

Between the vine-columns, the shapes of old construction persist. A wall: still standing, its original material — block, brick, something quarried — now entirely replaced by thick woody growth that has taken the wall's exact dimensions and maintained them for its own reasons. The corner is still a corner. The angle is precise. Plants do not usually do this. Plants do not usually have reasons. But here, there is the continuous suggestion that they do, that you are moving through a city that is being *remembered* by the life that replaced it, that the street grid is preserved in the root-mat below your feet because the plants found it useful to maintain the concept of *streets,* and have been doing so ever since whatever happened here happened.

Doorways gape. They lead into chambers of greenery: hollow spaces, surprisingly dry inside, sealed by walls of living growth, their dimensions roughly those of rooms in buildings, chambers sized for human occupation though humans are not here. In these rooms, condensation drips from the inner surfaces, slow irregular dripping into small pools, dark water on living-fiber floor. The moisture sits in pockets like this. Outside it is intermittent: the air is humid but not wet, occasionally a fine rain falls from somewhere inside the upper canopy, released from accumulated condensation by whatever movements disturb the leaves above, and the rain lasts thirty seconds and stops and the ground absorbs it almost instantly, the root-mat drinking without ceremony.

## Sound

You have not yet addressed the sound, and you have been avoiding it, because the sound is the thing that makes the place hardest to describe without your description tipping into inadequacy.

It is deafening. But it is not any sound that deafening usually means.

There are no machines. There are no engines or waterfalls or explosions. The sound is the sound of *living material in constant motion at massive scale.* The creak and tick of wood under tension, multiplied by millions of instances per second until it becomes a continuous roar with texture, with frequency range, with something that is almost — that is — *rhythmic,* though the rhythm shifts before you can identify it. The leaves: at this density they make percussion. When the canopy moves, and the canopy always moves, the sound is that of rain, of hail, of waves, of a crowd. The vines rub against each other and make sounds at the edge of music. Long low moans as giant members flex. High thin hissings as small stems slide against leaf surfaces. Pops and cracks as growth tears through surfaces that have momentarily solidified.

And underneath: a *grinding.* Felt as much as heard. The root-mat doing its slow catastrophic work against whatever remains of the old substrate below, roots forcing themselves between molecules of stone, root-pressure splitting mineral structure, the geological patience of plant expansion concentrated here and made hyperactive, made *urgent,* so that the work of a century is done in a year and the work of a year in an hour.

The sound system of this place is enormous and it is coming from everywhere at once and it fills the sinuses and the chest. You feel it. Your teeth pick up frequencies. You cannot think in complete sentences inside it. Something about cognition requires quiet margins that this place does not permit.

## Movement and Turbulence

The correct word for what is happening here is *growth,* but growth does not usually connote what is happening here. Elsewhere growth is a slow archive of change, only evidenced in retrospect, documented by time-lapse. Here growth has crossed into the register of *action.* Of *event.*

Watch a stem. Any stem. Give it three minutes. It has moved. Not swayed — moved, translated, extended in a direction that was not its direction before. Tendrils, and there are tendrils everywhere, curl and uncurl with the speed of fingers. Leaves reorient. The flat surface tracks something — light, pressure, chemical signal — and pivots to face it. A vine end probes the air and then strikes at a surface and adheres and then thickens at the point of adhesion over the course of twenty minutes into a grip that could hold weight.

The city-shape is being revised continuously. The wall-shaped mass that retains the memory of a wall is also extending. New growth pushes from its upper edge, lateral, seeking contact with the nearest structural element, the nearest fixed point. When it reaches one, it will bind to it, and the angle between them will be colonized, and a new surface will exist where no surface was. This is happening in many places simultaneously. The space is being *subdivided,* new partitions growing between old ones, the resolution of the plant-city increasing, rooms becoming rooms-within-rooms, streets becoming passages becoming tunnels. The architecture is elaborating itself faster than you can track it.

And not only elaborating. Also *failing.* Also *collapsing.* Under the pressure of new growth, old structures give way. A vine-pillar that has become too heavy tears loose from its canopy attachment and swings, massive and slow, crashing through two tiers of leaf-cover, taking other material with it, opening a wound in the green that lets direct light through — burning white light, much brighter than the filtered light elsewhere — and in the wound you can see the cut ends of several dozen broken stems already beading with fluid, already beginning the first motions of regrowth, sealing their cuts, reaching. Within an hour the wound will be half-closed. Within a day it will be difficult to find.

## The Rules of the World

There are a few. They feel like discovered physics, like facts about this place that would be true whether or not anyone came to notice them.

**First rule:** Animal life is absent. This is not death — there are no carcasses, no bones, no evidence of departure. The category of fauna appears not to have operated here. Insects, birds, anything with locomotion of its own choosing — nothing. The pollination is done by other means, or has been redesigned, or the plants have found each other sufficient. There is a loneliness that would be present if loneliness required a subject to feel it, but there is no subject, and so instead there is something structural — a *shape* of loneliness in the ecosystem, the missing-animal-shaped hole in the web.

**Second rule:** The plants are not communicating in the way metaphors about plants usually mean. They are not *like* they are communicating. They are *communicating.* Root-mat chemical signaling at speeds that are too fast for mycorrhizal networks as understood. The response of one section of growth to an event somewhere else in the system is too prompt, too coordinated. When the vine-pillar fell, the regrowth around the wound started not in seconds but in the same second. The root-mat registered the impact and the response was simultaneously distributed. There is no center, or there is no center that can be located, or the center is the same thing as the whole.

**Third rule:** The weirdness is not dramatic. It does not announce itself. It accretes. You have been here for some time now and you are beginning to have difficulty locating yourself in three dimensions, which should be a function of your vestibular system and your vision and the steady presence of a horizon, but there is no horizon, there is only green, and the green is always moving, and the reference points you used to fix your position have moved. The passage you arrived through is no longer where it was. Or it is where it was but where it was is no longer where you think it was. The architecture has revised itself around your navigation. Whether this is accident or intent is the question that you keep approaching and that keeps not having an answer.

**Fourth rule:** The young stone still exposed in places — fresh mineral surface where root has recently pried through — has a quality that fresh stone never has. It is lit from within. Dimly. The light is greenish and follows no obvious source. When you look directly at it, the light is not there. In peripheral vision, it is unmistakable. The stone is doing something it should not be able to do, and the plants appear to have been using it for this, appear to have been growing toward light-sources in the stone rather than light-sources in the sky. This inverts something. This makes the sun an afterthought.

## Moisture

In the early afternoon — identified by the angle and warmth of light diffusing through the canopy, no sky visible to confirm it, just a general brightening — it rains inside the vault.

The mechanism is this: the heat rises and reaches the dense closed upper canopy and is held there, and the moisture transpired from ten million leaves collects in the sealed upper air, and when enough has collected it condenses on the leaf surfaces and falls. The rain is warm. It is the temperature of breath. It falls for perhaps four minutes, a fine mist that becomes briefly a true rain, that taps on the broad lower leaves in irregular patterns that could be a message if you were inclined to read it as one, and then stops.

The ground, which is to say the root-mat, which is to say the living floor, absorbs the rain in the way that dry foam absorbs water: silently, quickly, with evident relief. Small pools form in the low places, in the hollows of old street intersections where the root-mat has dipped. Dark water, no sediment, the color of very weak tea. It holds still for a few minutes and then it is gone.

This is the only water. It is enough. The system requires exactly this much, has calibrated itself to produce exactly this much, or has calibrated its production of rain to exactly what it requires. The system is taking care of itself. You are watching a metabolism. The scale of a valley and the function of a cell.

## What You Are Left With

There is no name for this place. Places are usually named by the people who persist in them, and there are no people here, and if there were ever people here their naming has been consumed along with their buildings.

The green continues past sight in all directions. It is not a jungle in the sense you may have carried into this reading — that word implies a certain chaos, a certain indifference, a terrain that has not noticed you. This place has noticed you. Not as a predator would, not as a host would, but the way a very large system notices a small fluctuation: as data, as perturbation, as something to be incorporated into the next revision.

The sound is permanent. The warmth is permanent. The motion is permanent. The light — that soft, adequate, diffuse light of an overcast warm afternoon that seems to come from the green itself as much as from any sky — is permanent.

Something is being built here. Something is being built here at speed, at terrible and gorgeous speed, and it is not finished and it will not be finished because the building is the point, the process of building is the condition of the place, and what it is building toward is more complexity, more interlocking, more — you find yourself thinking — *more conversation,* though between whom and about what you are not able to say.

You will not find your way out by walking. The architecture will not cooperate.

But something in the root-mat, something in the chemical conversation running at impossible speed through the whole woven living substrate of the place, knows where the edge is. If you sit still, which is very difficult in the noise, which requires an effort that the place seems almost interested in watching you make — if you sit still and let the root-mat learn the particular chemical signature of your weight and your warmth and your exhalation —

It might show you.

Or it might not.

It might decide you are more useful here, as a fixed point, as a new piece of anchor, as a thing for the new partitions to grow between.

The tendrils are already interested. They have been interested since you arrived. They are patient in the way that fast-growing things are patient: they do not need to wait long.
