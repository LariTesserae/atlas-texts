# The Murmuring Flat

---

Begin with the sound, because the sound arrives before anything else does.

It is not a roar. Roaring implies a single source, a throat, a direction you can face into. This is closer to what you would get if you took every surface in the world and set it vibrating at a slightly different frequency — a dense, layered, total hum that enters through the sternum before it enters through the ears. Standing still in it, you feel the bones of your jaw begin to sympathize. Your molars ache faintly. The sound has texture: beneath the foundational drone there are clicks, there are the thin wet sounds of things moving against things, there are percussive bursts that rise and dissolve before you can locate them. There is something that might be a call — high, reedy, almost harmonic — that surfaces every few minutes from somewhere out in the middle distance and is immediately absorbed back into the larger noise. The silence underneath all of this, if it exists, is so far down as to be theoretical.

Now: where you are.

---

You are standing — or you would be standing, if the ground were the kind of thing you could stand on easily — at the edge of what was once, very long ago, a wide river delta. The river is mostly gone now, or rather it has distributed itself so completely that it no longer qualifies as a river. What remains is a low, enormous plain, flat enough that you can see a long way in every direction without seeing anything rise above your waist. The horizon is a smear. The sky above it is the color of a bruise that has reached its greenish-gray final stage — not threatening, exactly, just old. There are clouds but they are low and slow and carry the quality of permanence, as though they have been stationed here and have stopped expecting to move.

The ground itself is mineral. This is important to understand early, because nothing here is biological in any sense your body recognizes as kin to itself. The substrate is a fine dark silt — compressed over centuries into something denser than mud but not quite stone, cracked into large irregular tiles by cycles of wetting and near-drying that have been occurring since before there was anything to witness them. The cracks are old. Their edges have softened. Water stands in the lower ones, a shallow black gleam, and where two cracks intersect there are sometimes small pools a handspan across and two fingers deep, perfectly still, perfectly opaque, reflecting nothing because the light is too thin to give them anything to work with.

The whole plain is damp in a way that isn't quite wet. The air is cool — not cold, not the cold that bites, but the cool that settles, that sits on your skin like a damp cloth — and moisture moves through it constantly in ways that stop short of rain. Condensation forms on every surface. The dark tiles of compacted silt are darker on their upper faces than on their sides, perpetually freshened by this near-rain that is not quite rain. Sometimes you feel individual droplets on your face and look up and cannot identify their source. Sometimes a mist moves through at ankle height, thin and purposeful-seeming, going somewhere you can't identify.

---

But you would not notice the ground for very long.

What you would notice — what prevents you from noticing much of anything else — is the fauna.

Dense is not the right word, but it is the direction of the right word. Overwhelming suggests a catastrophe, a sudden inundation, and this is not sudden and has never been sudden; it is simply the state of the place, the way the place has always been, the equilibrium it reached long ago and settled into with complete indifference to whether anything found it remarkable. Every square meter of this plain is occupied, fully, by things that move.

They are small, mostly. This is important. A place this dense with large things would be chaos, would be impossible. But the things here have found the scale at which maximum density becomes possible — they are the size of a hand, of a thumb, of a grain of rice, of something smaller that you can only perceive as motion at the edge of your vision. They move across the tiles in columns and whorls and in patterns that look almost deliberate from a height you don't have. Up close, each individual thing is its own problem: legs that don't correspond to any number you expect, bodies that are the color of wet stone and are therefore almost invisible against the wet stone until they move, which they are always doing. There are things with shells and things without shells and things where you cannot determine where the body ends and the environment begins.

They are not insects. They are not crustaceans. They have features that suggest both without being either, and this is one of the ways the place is strange without announcing its strangeness — you keep reaching for a category and the category keeps sliding away. Some of them have structures on their upper surfaces that catch the faint light and scatter it faintly, not iridescent exactly but somewhere in that direction, which means that the whole surface of the plain, if you could somehow rise above it, would appear to very softly glimmer with the aggregate of millions of these small light-scatterings. From the ground, the effect is subtler: a kind of aliveness to the air just above the tile surface, as if the ground is breathing in a very fine, very rapid way.

---

And then there are the larger ones.

They move through the small ones the way weather moves through weather — not ignoring them exactly, not predating on them in any legible way, just occupying a different layer of the same space. They are the length of your forearm and carry themselves low, their bodies nearly touching the tile surface, their movements deliberate and slow in a way that contrasts with the constant quick motion of the smaller things around and under and occasionally over them. They have what you might call heads but only in the sense of a direction they face. Their heads are down. They are attended by disturbances in the surrounding small-thing population — a clearing that opens ahead of them and closes behind them, a bow wave and a wake in the living surface of the place.

There are hundreds of them visible at any one time, in every direction, at varying distances out toward the smeared horizon. When you watch one for a long time you begin to understand that it is following something — a crack in the tile, a path in the condensation, some trail or signal laid down in a medium you have no organ for perceiving. They do not appear to interact with each other. They do not appear to notice you. This is perhaps the most unsettling thing: the complete indifference of a place so full that there is nowhere to put your foot without displacing something.

---

Out in the middle distance — and middle distance is all there is here, because near and far are both just more of the same — there is a standing water. Not a pond. Too irregular, too shallow, too infiltrated by the mineral plain to be a pond. It is a lens of water maybe thirty meters across, a centimeter deep at its edges and perhaps a few centimeters at its center, connected to the crack-network so that it breathes with the slight pressure variations of the plain. Around its edge the density of small things is higher still — they layer on each other there, they overlap, they stack — and the sound at its edge, if you could isolate it from the larger sound, would be the most concentrated, thickest version of the place's general noise: a clicking, a wet percussion, something almost like language if language could mean anything this alien to utterance.

The water itself is not still. Things move through it that you cannot see clearly in the low light, that disturb its surface in V-shapes that spread and intersect with other V-shapes until the whole lens is a complex interference pattern that reforms and dissolves and reforms.

The mist that moves at ankle height comes from places like this, or goes to them; you cannot determine which direction it is traveling, and suspect that the direction changes.

---

The light is something to talk about, because there is light — but just barely.

It does not come from a sky that is luminous or from a sun that is visible. It comes from the sky in the way that color leaches out of old things: slowly, all at once, without a source. The plain is visible, but only in the way that things in a very large room with a very small window are visible — with effort, in gradients of dark gray and darker gray, the wet tile surfaces slightly lighter than the crack-water, the moving things slightly lighter or darker than the tiles depending on the angle. Your eyes adjust, because eyes always adjust, and what you find after adjustment is a landscape that is nearly monochrome but not quite — there is a very faint warmth in some of the biological surfaces, something that might be bioluminescence so faint it registers more as a quality of the air than as an actual glow. Or it might not be. The light here invites this kind of uncertainty.

In the low near-dark the sound becomes more dominant. In the low near-dark the movement of things across the plain becomes less a visual fact and more a felt one — a vibration in the silt coming up through the soles of your feet, a pressure-change in the air as columns of small things shift direction en masse, a warmth that isn't warmth exactly but that you notice the absence of when something large moves away from you.

---

The rules here are not posted but they are legible if you stand still long enough.

The first rule is that everything is occupied. There is no empty space at ground level — not the cracks, not the pools, not the surfaces between. The occupation may not be visible to you but it is there, and your presence is a displacement, a dent in the system that the system is already working to fill back in around you. If you stood here long enough, things would begin to use your feet as surfaces.

The second rule is that this has been this way for a very long time. The tile-cracks are old. The paths the large things follow are old. The relationship between the layers — the smallest things, the mid-sized things, the armspan things in the pools — has been calibrated by long contact into something that looks, from outside, almost cooperative. Not cooperative. Settled. The word for it is equilibrium, but the kind of equilibrium that is not peace — it is the balance of a thing under enormous sustained pressure from all sides at once.

The third rule is that the sound does not stop. Not at any point. Not when you have been here so long that you stop registering it consciously. It continues beneath your threshold, and when you leave — when you move far enough away that it is no longer audible — you will feel its absence as a specific physical vacancy, a place in the chest where something was and now is not, and you will find yourself in the silence somewhere else listening for the click and hum and wet percussion of a place this full of itself, this settled into its own strange fullness, this indifferent to every category you brought to it.
