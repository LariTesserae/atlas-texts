# The Cistern

---

You are inside something that was made.

That is the first thing you understand, before your eyes adjust, before you can name any particular feature: the geometry is too consistent, the walls too deliberately curved. Whoever or whatever built this place built it with repetition in mind — the same intent expressed again and again until the structure became its own kind of nature.

---

The space is not large. Perhaps forty paces across, though the water makes distances difficult to trust. It is a cistern, or close enough to a cistern that the word arrives and stays. Circular. High-ceilinged — the ceiling vaults upward to a closure you cannot quite see in the dimness, though you sense its presence the way you sense the top of a well when you lean over the edge: a certain quality of air returning to you. The walls are stone, and the stone is old beyond the ordinary meaning of old. It has been worked — you can see this in the regularity of the blocks — but the working was done so long ago that the distinction between made and mineral has almost fully collapsed. The mortar has become indistinguishable from the stone. The joints have filled with their own mineral patience, with calcite deposited by centuries of seeping water, and now the wall reads as a single continuous surface interrupted only by the faintest memory of seams.

This is not ruin. Ruin implies deterioration, and this place has passed through deterioration and come out the other side into something more settled: a final form, like a stone that has tumbled in a river long enough that every edge is gone.

---

The water is everywhere.

It fills the lower portion of the cistern to a depth of perhaps four feet — you are standing on a ledge, a walkway that circles the interior at roughly waist height above the surface, and the walkway itself is slick with mineral film and a thin, continuous sheen of overflow. The water is warm. Not uncomfortably so, not the aggressive warmth of a hot spring, but the warmth of something that has been enclosed a long time, that has reached equilibrium with its container. It is the temperature of living things. If you trailed your hand through it you would feel almost nothing, because the gradient between your skin and the water would be so small.

Below the surface — and you can see below the surface, faintly, because the water is extraordinarily clear — the cistern continues. The walls go down. The floor, if there is one, is somewhere beneath the limit of the available light, which is almost nothing, which makes the depth feel infinite. This is almost certainly an illusion, but the feeling is real enough to stand on its own.

The surface of the water moves. Very slowly. There is a circulation, a long rotation, a single massive eddy that takes minutes to complete one turn. You can track it by watching small particles suspended near the top — mineral flecks, the cast-off of walls — moving in their patient arc. Something is feeding this rotation, some inflow somewhere below that you cannot identify by sound because there is almost no sound, only the softest possible suggestion of water against stone, a sound so quiet it might be your own pulse making shapes out of silence.

---

The light is almost nothing.

There is a source — somewhere above, somewhere in the vault's closure, there is an aperture, a hole or a gap in the construction, and through it comes not light exactly but the memory of light: a faint, blue-gray luminescence that reaches the water's surface and then diffracts and dies. It is enough to see by in the way that the last minutes of dusk are enough to see by: you can perceive the room's general shape, the presence of the walls, the surface of the water. Detail requires you to lean in, to hold still, to let your eyes build their picture slowly.

The stone, where the light reaches it at the right angle, has the color of old teeth, of chalk submerged too long. In places it is encrusted with the white and cream mineral deposits that accumulating water leaves behind — formations like frozen dribbles, like the inside of a geode stretched thin. They are everywhere. They coat the lower sections of the wall in particular, where the water has been touching and retreating and touching again over time measured in geological increments. Some of them have formed small shelves, narrow horizontal ridges, that break the wall's curve into layers. This gives the interior a stratified quality, like the walls of a canyon that have recorded their own history.

In the darkness below the waterline, shapes are visible but barely. The walls continue, marked by the same formations. And there are things attached to those walls, things that have colonized the lower stones: creatures, or the forms that creatures take when warmth and stillness and time have been available in abundance.

---

They are quiet. This is the first and most important fact about them.

They move, but slowly enough that the movement is not immediately obvious — you have to give your attention time to recalibrate, to accept the pace at which this place operates. Then you begin to see it. A pale form shifting fractionally against the stone. A cluster of something repositioning itself over the course of minutes. They are pale, these animals, or they appear pale in the near-dark — soft bodies, many of them, crustaceans and mollusks and things that have no obvious common name, things that have evolved or simply persisted in the warmth and dark. They coat the lower walls in overlapping layers. They are the density here: not plant life, nothing green, nothing photosynthetic, but a rich animal density, an abundance of small slow bodies pressed into every available surface.

Some are fixed. Some move. They filter the water. They consume each other in arrangements so gradual and small that it barely registers as predation. There are things that look like barnacles but are not quite, structures with delicate feathered extensions that emerge from time to time and sweep the water and retract. There are pale gastropods moving in tracks that would take them hours to complete. There are things you don't have names for — soft, whitish, vaguely segmented, clinging to the rock in clusters of six or seven, their edges barely distinguishable from one another.

None of them make noise, or the noise they make is below your threshold. The density is animal but the affect is mineral. They are so slow, so quiet, so thoroughly present that they feel like a feature of the stone rather than a population living on it.

---

The walkway you stand on is the only evidence of intention at human scale. It is a hand-width wider than feels comfortable, just enough to move along if you press close to the wall and proceed with care. The material is the same as the rest — cut stone, or formed stone, or stone that was once cut and has since become its own thing — and it has the same coating, the same mineral glaze, the same feel of surfaces that have been touched by water for a very long time and have made their peace with it.

At intervals along the walkway there are niches in the wall. Small ones, just large enough to hold a clay pot or a lamp. Some of the niches contain things: remnants, traces. A shape that might once have been an oil lamp, now fully calcified, fully incorporated into the mineral record of the place — its original form suggested but no longer recoverable. In another niche there is something that might be a carved figure, a votive or a marker, similarly consumed. The things placed here were placed here by hands, by people who had intentions, who wanted to remember something or honor something or simply mark the fact of their presence. The water and the time have turned their objects into geology.

The hands are gone. What they left is almost gone. What remains is the shape of what remains.

---

The warmth is even. There are no drafts. The air is saturated — not oppressively so, not steaming, but fully humid, close, the air of a sealed space that has been sharing breath with a large body of water for long enough that the two have become the same thing. You breathe it and it is like breathing the cistern, becoming briefly part of the process.

The smell is mineral and faintly animal and mostly water. It is not unpleasant. It is the smell of depth, of enclosure, of something that has been kept.

---

The sound.

You have to be very still.

There is the slow water, the patient almost-audible circular current. There is the occasional soft contact of a gastropod against stone, a sound so small it could be imaginary. There is the faint mineral clink of something depositing, somewhere in the dark, another fraction of a millimeter of calcite. There is your own breathing, which in this space sounds enormous, intrusive, embarrassingly fast.

And then, below all of that, or perhaps beneath is the wrong direction — perhaps woven through it, perhaps constituting it — there is a very low, very slow sound that is not quite a sound, that you feel in the sternum more than hear. A throb. A long, warm pulse. It has a period of many seconds. It is probably structural, probably the cistern breathing with temperature differentials, with the slow cycle of whatever makes the water warm — but it feels, if you let it, like the heartbeat of the place itself, or the place's memory of having had a heartbeat when it was being made, when the stones were being cut and fit and the hands were still here.

---

The rotation continues. The pale creatures continue their patient work on the walls. The mineral formations continue growing at their impossible slowness — another fraction of a millimeter by the time you have stood here as long as you can stand.

Nothing is urgent. Nothing is coming. The place has arrived at what it is going to be and is now simply being it, indefinitely, in the warm dark, full of water and quiet life and the residue of intention, settling further into itself with every long pulse of whatever passes for time down here.

You are the fastest thing in the room.

You are, probably, the most temporary.
