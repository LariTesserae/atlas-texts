# The Drowned Terraces

---

The water came up slowly, and it has not finished coming.

This is the first thing to understand about this place: it is not yet decided. The mountains are still becoming mountains. The sea has risen to meet them before they could properly harden into themselves, and so what exists here is a negotiation between stone that does not know its own shape yet and water that is in no hurry to find a shore.

---

Stand at what would be, in a drier world, a considerable height — the air has the thinness of altitude, a quality of lungs working slightly harder, of sound traveling fractionally less far. But the altitude is submerged. The upper terraces where you walk are perhaps forty meters above the floodline, but the floodline itself sits where a valley floor should be, and the valley floor is somewhere below under dark green water, and whatever was there before the waters settled in has been slowly forgetting itself for longer than anyone has been counting.

The terraces are wide. The rock they're cut from — or rather, the rock they're *becoming*, since cut implies intention and what happened here is closer to: the stone was soft, and the water found the layers in it, and the layers opened like pages in a book left in the rain — this rock is pale. Cream-colored, almost. Limestone that hasn't finished consolidating, still faintly chalky at the edges where fresh faces are exposed, where yesterday a shelf gave way and dropped into the drowned valley with a sound that took a long time to complete itself. The faces of new breaks are almost white. They dry quickly in the warm air and then turn the color of old teeth.

---

The warmth is constant and sourceless-seeming. There is no direct sun. There is rarely direct sun here. Instead there is a diffuse luminance that comes from everywhere and nowhere — the light filtered through a permanent partial overcast, through air that carries substantial moisture, through the evaporation rising off the drowned valley below, through the general wetness of everything. It is the light of the inside of a cloud. It illuminates without glare, without shadow, which gives the terraces a quality of being slightly unreal, slightly overexposed in the wrong direction: too even, too present on all sides, like a scene from which the depth cues have been removed.

You can see. But you have to trust your depth perception more than usual. Edges are harder to read. The pale limestone and the pale water below and the pale sky above sometimes achieve a kind of tonal conspiracy, a merging at the margins. You learn to be careful near the terrace edges.

---

The water below is green. Not the green of algae, though there is algae, but the green of depth — light entering and not entirely escaping, being absorbed and scattered into a color that is mostly the memory of light. It sits still most of the time. Occasionally a slow undulation crosses its surface, some settling in the stones far below, some accommodating sigh of new geology, and the green shifts and then is green again.

It does not smell of salt. It does not smell much of anything. It is fresh water, snowmelt from higher elevations that no longer exist as elevations so much as they exist as islands, water that has been gathering here for what the terraces suggest has been a very long time. The terraces themselves tell a story about duration: the marks of old waterlines are carved into the cliff faces in horizontal bands, each band representing a time when the water was higher, or perhaps lower — it's genuinely difficult to say which direction things are moving. Whether this is a flood still arriving at its peak, or a flood beginning its long slow consideration of departure. The bands don't say. The rock doesn't know yet.

---

The vegetation grows in cracks.

That is, it grows in cracks, but the cracks are everywhere, and so the vegetation is everywhere, and it is more accurate to say: the vegetation grows in the interstices of the emerging landscape, in every gap the stone offers, in every seam, and since the stone is young and soft and still opening itself to the water's argument, the gaps are many and the vegetation is dense.

Not tall vegetation. Not forest. The altitude and the young stone don't support trees with anything deep to root into. What grows here is layered differently: mosses first, and these are remarkable — thick, saturated, genuinely green in a way that seems almost aggressive compared to the paleness of everything else, green like something insisting on itself, like a value pushing through a canvas. The mosses have colonized the terraces so thoroughly that in places the stone underneath is only knowable by its firmness underfoot, invisible beneath two and three inches of growth. They hold water, which means the terraces are always slightly wet, which means the stone underneath is always slightly softening, which is part of why new cracks appear, which is part of why the vegetation spreads into them.

Above the moss layer: ferns. Low-growing varieties, some of them species you may not recognize, species that may not have had occasion yet to need names, their fronds the same assertive green as the moss but lighter in quality, catching the flat diffuse light and rendering it back in small soft gleams. The ferns occupy every ledge and overhang. They drip, even when it hasn't rained recently, because the air is wet enough that condensation collects on the fronds constantly, and falls.

Above the ferns, in the deepest cracks where some small depth of soil has accumulated: herbs, groundcovers, small flowering things in white and pale yellow, things that bloom for reasons of their own without caring much about pollinators since pollinators are rare here. The flowers are small and patient and seem to bloom and close on cycles that have more to do with moisture than with light, since light doesn't vary enough here to be worth responding to.

---

The settlement occupies the second and third terraces — the widest ones, the ones where the stone, though still young, is at least consistently solid underfoot.

*Settlement* implies intention, permanence, a decision to be in one place. What's here is somewhat less decided than that, which fits. The structures grow out of the terrace walls more than they stand on the terrace floors. Walls built into existing rock outcroppings, roofs of woven material — reed, rush, broad fern fronds dried and layered — that catch rainwater and channel it but must be rebuilt seasonally as the fronds deteriorate and the geometry of the overhang shifts. There is no metal here. The fastenings are rope and wet clay pressed into joints, wooden pegs in wet-drilled holes. The buildings are pale from the limestone dust of their construction, low, with openings facing away from the valley-edge, toward the cliff wall, as if the settlement is engaged in a long and inconclusive negotiation with the rock behind it.

The paths between structures are worn into the moss, which regrows at the edges, gradually narrowing them, so the paths are maintained by use rather than by construction — they exist exactly as far as they are walked and no farther. Where they end, the moss is already reclaiming.

Evidence of craft: clay pots, drying near the cliff wall. Stone bowls, laboriously made with other stones, surfaces smoothed by long work. Ropes, many ropes, hung between outcroppings, used for carrying loads across the terrace and up and down between levels, crude pulleys of wood and lashed-together elements. There are no wheels here. The stone would not support them well and the reasoning for them has not been fully arrived at. There is no timber long enough to suggest it has been hauled from elsewhere, so the wood in use here is what the terraces offer: driftwood that has risen from the drowned valley, things that have floated up from whatever was there before.

The structures number perhaps enough to hold seventy people. Perhaps fewer are actually here. The terraces have the quality of something built for a population that has not quite materialized, or has recently diminished, or exists partially elsewhere.

---

The sound of the place is mostly the sound of water.

Not dramatically. Not the roar of falls or the slap of waves. The sound is subtle: the drip from fern fronds onto moss, a note like a slow clock. The occasional creak of a rope under load in the absent wind. Far below, translated up through the cliff walls and into the soles of your feet more than into your ears, a low vibration — the sound of new stone deciding where to go, of a layer separating with geological patience, of something that has been working on this for a long time and will continue. Occasionally a splash from the valley when a piece of terrace gives up and drops. The splash echoes from cliff walls and then slowly disperses into the flat air, and then it is quiet again, and the ferns drip.

Voices, if there are people: low, as people are low when the place they live in is low. As if learning to match their volume to the ambient register of the world. Not whispering, but not carrying. Speech that stays in the space where it is needed and goes no farther.

---

The fauna is mostly theoretical.

Something moves in the water. You catch it in peripheral vision: a displacement, a shift in the green. It is large enough to displace. It does not surface. There are stories — or rather, there are gestures toward stories, the kind of communication that doesn't require that much language — about what is in the water. The gestures are neither fearful nor reverent. They are informational. *That is a thing in the water. It has always been there. It does not come up.*

Birds, occasionally, gray-winged and slow, crossing between the islands that are the higher peaks. They don't land here. The terraces don't offer what they need. They appear in the flat sky and then they don't.

Small things in the moss: the occasional movement that suggests invertebrate life, the tiny animal kingdom of the wet gap-world, abundant but invisible at any useful distance. The moss is full of living things if you press your face close to it. At standing height, you are alone.

---

The strangeness of the place is not in any single element but in its temporal posture.

Everything here is *almost*. The stone is almost formed. The settlement is almost established. The flood is almost finished arriving, or almost finished departing. The vegetation has almost established a soil to work with. The light is almost bright enough to see clearly by. The sounds are almost loud enough to locate.

It is a landscape in subjunctive mood. It might become something. It might not. It is not distressed by this uncertainty; geological time has no affective register. But for a visitor trained to expect landscapes that have made their decisions — that are plains or mountains or forests or coasts — this place requires a small adjustment of expectation. You have to be willing to receive it as process rather than place, as a direction things are moving rather than the place they've arrived.

The warm dimness helps with this. There are no hard shadows to anchor you in a specific moment. The diffuse light could be morning or afternoon or some middle time that doesn't have a name. The place exists in the kind of time where it is always *becoming*, always in the middle of a sentence the geology started a long time ago and will finish without consulting you.

---

You can sleep on the moss. It is thick enough and soft enough and stays warm enough, and if you press your ear to it you can hear, very faintly, the submerged world below working on itself in the dark, patient and uninterested in your schedule, occupied entirely with the long project of becoming whatever it is going to become.
