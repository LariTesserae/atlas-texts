# The Singing City

---

Begin with the sound, because the sound is the first and last thing.

It is not a roar. It is not a cacophony in any sense you've used that word before. It is closer to what an ocean would sound like if an ocean were also a cathedral choir, and that choir were also a machine, and that machine had been running for so long it had forgotten it was ever built. You feel it in your teeth before you hear it with your ears. You feel it in the backs of your eyes. The sound comes from the plants.

---

The place occupies roughly the footprint of what might have been, in another world's reckoning, a town of four or five thousand people. It has the quality of having been arranged — not by intention exactly, but by long accumulation of intention, the way cities acquire their shapes over centuries without anyone drawing the final map. There are axes. There are what you might call boulevards if boulevards were made entirely of living matter. There are denser concentrations that feel like districts, and looser zones that feel like parks, except that the distinction between park and district is false here because everything is park, everything is district, everything is plant.

The scale is bounded. You can walk to its edge in an afternoon, if you walk in a straight line, which nothing here encourages. There are edges — not walls exactly, but a gradual thinning, a place where the root density drops and the canopy opens and you find yourself standing in ordinary scrubland with a ringing in your ears and a strange grief, like leaving a party that was also a religion.

---

The plants are the architecture. This must be said plainly before anything else.

There are no bones here except plant bones. No stone has been shaped by hand, no timber cut and dried, no clay fired. The city was not built *from* plants — it was not built *with* plants — it has grown into the shape of a city because it is old enough and dense enough and strange enough that it has become one. The distinction between organism and structure collapsed so long ago that asking which came first is like asking whether the word or the meaning is more real.

The towers are banyans. Not single banyans — banyans that have merged with banyans that have merged with banyans, their aerial roots dropping and thickening until the original trunks are indistinguishable from the new ones, until a structure might be sixty or a hundred individual trees all expressed as one continuous architectural entity eighty feet tall and three hundred feet across at its canopy spread. These are not tall in the way mountains are tall or even in the way skyscrapers are tall. They are tall in the way old things are tall: their height is incidental to their age, a side effect of having simply continued. The elevation stays low — this is a low-lying ground, floodplain-adjacent, the water table generous but not present, the soil a dark loam that stays slightly damp the way breath stays slightly warm.

Between the banyan towers: the understory fabric. This is where the density-illusion happens.

---

Because here is the thing about density in this place: there is almost nothing per volume.

Walk into one of the great banyan cathedrals and you walk into a hollow. The roots form colonnades. The interlocking canopies form vaults. The floor is a deep mulch of centuries of shed bark and leaf and fruit, soft enough to leave prints, and it is doing something — it is composting, exhaling, alive in its decomposition — but it is bare. There is sixty, eighty feet of air above you, green-filtered light falling through in shafts like something reverent and accidental. You stand in a space big enough for a village and it holds almost nothing. A few ferns at the root bases. Some mosses. Beetles in the mulch. That's all.

But the sound.

The sound fills every cubic inch of this emptiness. The sound is what the vegetation has made of the empty space — has colonized the air with vibration the way it has colonized the ground with root. The banyans are singing. They do not know they are singing. They are doing what they have always done, which is to grow, to exchange chemistry with their root-symbiont networks, to move gases, to respond to light, to signal across their merged root-masses in slow electrochemical waves — but they have been doing it so long, and they are so large, and they are so densely interconnected, that the signaling has achieved frequency. The trunks vibrate. The root columns vibrate. The aerial roots at various thicknesses vibrate at various frequencies, and what you hear is a chord — a vast, many-layered chord that shifts over the course of hours the way tides shift, that has harmonics you can feel in your lower intestine and harmonics you can feel in the roof of your mouth.

This is not metaphor. The plants are making sound. They do not know this and have never known this, and it does not matter, and the sound is extraordinary.

---

Walk from one banyan cathedral to another and you pass through transition spaces that operate differently.

Here: a zone of fig trees, their strangler roots long since having consumed whatever host they once grew on, their bases now hollow casts of absence, great wooden voids inside wooden columns. The figs are fruiting — they are always fruiting, cycling through their cycles at their own pace, which is unrelated to seasons as you understand them. The fruits are split and fragrant, red inside, falling. The ground here is sticky with fermented fig-juice. This is where the fauna concentrates.

Birds. So many birds it reads as weather. They mass in the fig zones and they are loud — loud in the ordinary sense of many animals making noise — but their loudness is a frequency layer added to the ambient vibration of the banyan choir, and the result is something that presses against the limit of what a body can process as sound rather than as physical force. Hornbills with casques like architectural ornaments. Barbets in green and red that look like they were painted by someone working from a description of birds rather than actual birds. Fig-wasps so small they read as atmosphere rather than creature, a living haze around every ripe cluster.

Beneath the fig delirium: small mammals, mostly unseen but present in the acoustic layer, their chittering and rustling absorbed and re-expressed by the ambient sound, so that sometimes the general roar seems to individuate into almost-voices, almost-words, patterns that are not patterns.

This is where the weirdness begins to be legible.

---

The weirdness is not dramatic. It is not ruptures or impossible geometries or sky-tears. It is subtler and stranger than that, and it accumulates.

The roots remember where they've been. This is the first thing you notice when you spend time here. A root that grew around a stone forty years ago, and that stone has since crumbled and washed away, maintains the shape of what contained it. This would be normal — wood holds forms — except the maintained shape is wrong. The hollow in the root is the shape the stone would have become, not the shape it was. As if the wood has been modeling not the present object but the object's trajectory through time.

The canopy does not behave consistently with light. Light falls through it and illuminates the ground, which is normal. But sometimes the light falls differently than the canopy geometry suggests it should. A shadow falls where the canopy is thin. Brightness gathers under the densest coverage. The effect is not constant and is not dramatic and is something you might dismiss as your eyes adjusting, except that it happens consistently, and when you stand in an inexplicable shaft of brightness, you feel watched. You feel watched by something that has no eyes and is not looking and is not a predator but is attending. Something in the plant-mass is paying attention in some register that you can feel but not name.

The growth patterns are urban. This is the other thing.

There are junctions. The paths formed by root-free ground, by the natural clearings between root systems, form a network that has intersections. Has right-of-way logic. You stand at a confluence of three clear paths and there is something like a corner, and the paths are not random — they have the logic of streets that formed around prior streets. Except there have been no streets. There have been only roots growing around roots and the spaces between. The city-form emerged from the plant-logic rather than being imposed on it, and as a result it is a city of a kind you do not recognize: the blocks are wrong, the angles are wrong, the scale shifts in ways that city-scale shouldn't shift. You turn what feels like a corner and the space you enter is three times larger than the space you left, and turning back you find the smaller space has maintained its dimensions as if you never changed anything.

The sound changes register at night. You do not know this first-hand because you are only here in daylight, but you know it the way you know many things here — from the way the current register implies its own history. The daytime sound is rich and complex. The daytime sound is being modulated. Something else was here before this and will come back, and the plants are keeping time for it.

---

Water arrives as gift.

There is no river, no standing pool, no marsh. The water table is down there, somewhere, accessible to the deepest roots, and the roots bring it up and redistribute it in ways that defy the gradient — humidity is higher inside the canopy clusters than outside them, higher at the bases of the root columns, and occasionally, without any change in weather, a section of canopy will begin to drip. The leaves have learned, over their long years, to collect and channel the condensation from their own transpiration. A banyan cluster will drink its own exhalation, cycling water through itself in slow cellular re-ingestion, almost closed-system, almost self-sufficient, leaking only a small percentage out to the ground where ferns collect it and mosses absorb it and beetles drink from the moss-cups.

During the rains — they come every week or so, brief and intense, warm rain that falls hard and fast and then is done — the whole system activates differently. The sound changes within thirty seconds of the first drops hitting the canopy. You can hear the leaf-surface chemistry responding, a new overtone added to the chord, higher, more complex, something almost like urgency. The ground doesn't flood because the root-mulch absorbs and redistributes quickly, but the smell that rises from this process is enormous — green and fungal and sweet and like nothing you have a word for, a smell made of a thousand component smells, each identifiable if you focused, together constituting something past naming.

After the rain: a half-hour of increased birdsong. Then the chord reasserts itself, slightly changed, having incorporated whatever the rain brought in its chemistry. The city resumes what it was doing, which is growing, and signaling, and making its sound, and attending to something.

---

The light, here, is normal in the technical sense — same sun, same angles, same quality of afternoon gold. But it has been processed by passage through so many layers of leaf that by the time it reaches you it has lost most of its blue and yellow components and arrived primarily in green and dappled amber, which is not abnormal for a dense canopy, except that it happens more completely here than physics alone would predict, and the green quality of the air is total enough that it has an effect like submersion. You are in air but you feel like you are in water. Your movements feel like they should leave ripples. The birds moving through the understory look briefly, at the corner of vision, like deep-sea things, luminescent and fluid, before your eye refocuses and they are barbets again.

The warmth is the warmth of things that have been alive for a long time in a small space. Not uncomfortable. Not humid in the heavy pressing way of a rainforest — the low water keeps that from happening. More like: the warmth of a room occupied by many breathing things, a warmth with biology in it, a warmth that has history, a warmth that is the metabolic output of a system old enough that its waste heat has shaped the microclimate into a stable envelope a degree or two above what the surrounding terrain maintains.

---

Stand at the center — there is a center, you can find it without trying because the paths lead there the way paths lead to town centers, by accumulated use and gradient — and you stand in the largest single hollow, under the oldest joined cluster, and you understand something that you cannot quite say but will spend a long time trying to.

The city is not a metaphor. The city is not being compared to a living thing. The city has not been taken over by plants or allowed to rewild. There was never anything here but plants, and the plants made a city because cities are what happen when things that communicate over networks occupy space with long-term goals and distribute resources and establish hierarchies of need and build structure that outlasts the builders and re-use old structure as substrate for new structure.

The plants did all of this. The plants have always been doing all of this. It was always a city. We just did not have the instruments to hear the meetings, read the infrastructure, map the districts, understand the economy.

The sound is deafening.

The sound is the sound of a civilization at full operation, conducting its business, arguing and agreeing and building and mourning and celebrating, in a language made entirely of frequency and chemistry and root-touch and light-angle.

You stand in it.

You are very small.

You are, possibly, being noticed.
