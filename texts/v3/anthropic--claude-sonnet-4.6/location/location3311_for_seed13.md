# The Terrace City

---

The plateau rises from the lower world without apology. It simply ends the sky at a certain elevation and dares you to climb to it — and when you do, when your lungs have adjusted to the thinning and your legs have stopped their complaint, you find that the plateau has been here long enough to forget it was ever anything else. The rock is pale limestone, almost white in the noon light, fractured in clean vertical lines as though some slow force pressed it upward recently and the cracks haven't had time to round their edges or fill with soil. The faces of the escarpments are sharp. Run your finger along a ledge and you'll find it leaves a line. The stone is new in the way a tooth is new: formed somewhere deep and dark and pushed into the light, not yet worn to comfort.

The plateau is broad — broad enough that standing at its center you lose sight of its edges in three directions, see only the far rim to the north where the land drops away and the lower country spreads itself out hazily below. The distance between you and anything is real distance. Walk an hour and you will still be on the plateau, still among the same pale stones and the same short grasses and the scattered dark shapes of trees, still breathing the same cool air that smells of mineral and wet wool and something indefinably high.

---

Cool is the wrong word only because it suggests comfort. The temperature here is cool the way silence is quiet — as a fact, not a quality. Midday brings no warmth worth the name. The sun moves across a sky that is almost always a deep, saturated blue at the zenith, paling at the horizons to something closer to grey, and it delivers light without heat in the way a lamp in a cold room delivers light: faithfully, without obligation. Shadows are hard-edged and stay that way. Your breath is not quite visible but almost. By mid-afternoon your fingertips remind you that they exist.

The moisture comes and goes. There is no river here, no lake — or rather there is a river, but it is not the kind you'd call a river at first glance. It runs in a shallow braided channel across the western edge of the plateau, perhaps eight meters wide at its widest and in places only a meter, threading between bars of white gravel and pale sand, cold enough to ache if you stand in it. In the dry weeks it retreats to a trickle in the deepest groove of its bed and the gravel bars bleach out in the thin sun. In the wet weeks — which come unpredictably, two days of low cloud and light persistent rain — it swells and murmurs to itself. Between these states the plateau exists in a condition of intermittent dampness: the rock faces grow dark patches where seepage finds its way through, the soil in sheltered hollows holds moisture days after the last rain, and in the early mornings condensation collects in the cup-shapes of the younger stone formations, drops of water gathered overnight in depressions not yet worn shallow, sitting there bright and still until the sun takes them.

This is enough. This intermittent, distributed, careful moisture is enough for the vegetation, which is remarkable.

---

The trees are not tall but they are dense in their individual forms — each one a fully occupied structure, branches tight and interlocking, leaves small and waxy and dark green, the kind of leaf evolved to hold what it gets. They grow in clusters, never evenly spaced, following the invisible lines of underground water and sheltered microclimates. Between clusters is open limestone pavement and grass, and then another cluster, and the rhythm of open-and-occupied gives the plateau its characteristic texture from any distance: dark islands in pale stone. Up close each cluster is its own world. The canopy is low, two or three meters, and beneath it the light is filtered and green and the ground is soft with years of accumulated leaf litter, the only soft thing on this plateau. Mosses. Ferns in the wetter pockets. Small flowering plants that send up yellow or white blooms for a few weeks in the growing season and spend the rest of the year as unremarkable rosettes pressed against the rock.

In the open spaces between tree clusters the grasses are short and grow in tussocks, each tussock a distinct object with clear space around it — not a lawn, not a meadow exactly, but a collection of grass-beings each going about its own business in proximity to others. Some of these tussocks are enormous, knee-high, ancient-looking, their centers dead and brown while new growth rings their edges. Others are young, small, bright. The grass is the same cool green as the sky is blue: factual, unambiguous.

---

Then the city.

It has been here long enough that calling it the city feels redundant, like calling the plateau the plateau. It is simply what this high flat place contains. It occupies perhaps three or four kilometers of the plateau's central region, spreading from the low ridge at its heart outward in a pattern that looks organic from above but reveals, on walking through, a strict internal logic: terraces. Everything is terraced. The city is a terrace city, and it is a terrace city because the limestone breaks naturally into horizontal layers and the people who built it understood this and worked with it rather than against it.

The buildings are limestone. Of course they are — it is the only material here in quantity. But the quality of the workmanship is extraordinary given the tools available, which are: other pieces of limestone, harder varieties, shaped into picks and wedges; wood from the plateau trees, hard and slow-growing; rope made from grass fiber, twisted and braided with patience; no metal worth the name, or only the beginnings of it, small iron objects traded from elsewhere or found in the occasional seam, used sparingly, kept carefully. With these tools the builders have cut limestone blocks to a regularity that is not perfect but is consistent. The joints between blocks are tight. A fingernail fits in some, not in others. The walls have been standing long enough that the newer-cut stone faces have begun to age toward the color of the natural plateau, though where a wall has been recently repaired or extended the fresh cuts gleam white against the older grey.

The terraces step down from the central ridge in broad shallow steps. Each terrace is perhaps twenty to thirty meters deep and the drop between them is two to three meters, held by retaining walls of dry-laid limestone that are among the finest things here — massive, slightly battered back toward the hillside, the courses of stone laid with a care that communicates a belief that this wall must last. And it does last. These walls are the oldest visible structures in the city and they show it: lichens have colonized their faces in grey and orange rosettes, and in the joints where small amounts of soil have accumulated, tufts of grass and small ferns have taken root and are slowly, very slowly, working their way into the stone. This is the only erosion visible: biological, incremental, patient. The stone itself is still sharp beneath the life.

The buildings sit on the terraces in rows that face outward over the terrace edges, so that from the outside the city presents a sequence of facades stacked above one another, each floor of the city looking out over the rooftops of the floor below. Windows are small — the cold demands it — and the lintels over doorways are massive single slabs of limestone hauled here by means that left no permanent record. The roofs are flat, because rain is intermittent and because flat roofs are useful: things dry on them, children play on them, the lowest-angle sunlight reaches them first in the morning and people stand on them then, warming what can be warmed. Parapets around the roof edges are low, not for safety but for windbreak.

The streets between building rows are narrow and paved in small limestone cobbles, fitted by hand. They are not quite level — the plateau is not quite level — and they slope slightly in directions that have been figured out over generations to lead rainwater away from doorways and toward the channels cut into the terrace-edge walls, down through stone-lined conduits to the braided river below. This drainage system is the most sophisticated engineering in the city, a network of deliberate channels and sumps invisible to anyone who isn't looking for it. It works. After rain the streets are wet for an hour and then damp for an afternoon and then dry, the water gone to its proper places.

---

It is mostly quiet.

This is a fact that surprises, given the settlement density. A town of this size — thousands of people, surely, given the building count — should make more noise. But the stone absorbs less than you'd think, and yet the city is quiet. The explanation is the people themselves: this is a culture or a place or a moment in which loudness is not the default. Labor is quiet. Commerce is quiet. Conversation happens in the registers of consultation rather than announcement. You hear, walking through the streets:

The sound of stone tools on stone. A rhythmic tapping, sometimes, from a workshop — unhurried, methodical, each strike placed with care. It stops. It starts again.

Footsteps on cobbles, approaching, passing, receding.

Wind in the narrow streets, low and intermittent, pressing and releasing.

The small sounds of animals — and there are animals everywhere, of the kinds that have agreed to live near people: certain birds nesting in the wall joints, a particular dove whose call is three notes descending, repeated without variation or hurry; dogs lying in patches of sunlight on the broad terrace walls, not sleeping, simply resting, watching; goats somewhere on the lower terraces, their bells sounding in ones and twos and then stopping.

From the open plateau beyond the city's edge, when the wind is right, the braided river's sound: not rushing, not loud, a cold murmur like a thought you had earlier and can almost recall.

---

The fauna of the plateau is abundant in the way that high places can be abundant when the pressures of the lower world are absent: not diverse, but deeply present. The birds are numerous, adapted to the cold, the cliff-nesters and the open-ground foragers alike. There is a raptor — large, dark-backed, pale-bellied — that hunts the open spaces between tree clusters and has learned that the city's terrace edges make good perches. It sits on the highest parapets sometimes and regards the streets below with the complete attention of a creature that has never needed to learn patience because it was born with it. Smaller raptors work the tree clusters. Ground-nesting birds weave between tussocks on the open plateau, their eggs the exact color of pale limestone and nearly impossible to find.

Mammals: the goats are partly domesticated, a gradient between fully wild and fully kept, and the ones at the wild end of the gradient graze out on the open plateau at distances that change with the season. There is a small deer — high-shouldered, short-legged, built for the plateau rather than the forest — that comes to the city's edges at dawn and dusk, nibbling at the grasses growing in the terrace walls, unafraid but careful. Rodents in the walls. A mustelid of some kind, sleek and fast, seen in the early morning before the city wakes.

Below the rock surface, in the cave systems that the plateau's limestone conceals: bats, in large colonies, pouring out at dusk in ribbons over the city. This is the daily spectacle. The bats leave their caves in the escarpment face and stream upward over the terraces, thousands of them, in a ribbon that undulates and expands and then disperses into the cooling air, and the sound of it is a high thin rustle, not quite a sound, more a texture. It lasts perhaps ten minutes. Then they are gone into the dimming sky and the plateau is quiet again.

---

Here is the unusual thing, the thing that requires more than a paragraph and cannot quite be explained:

The city does not change.

Not slowly. Not in the way that cities change slowly, generation by generation, old buildings replaced with new, the street plan gradually shifting. The city does not change. The wall repaired in your grandmother's time looks exactly as it did when your grandmother repaired it. The tree that fell against the eastern building in the year of the great storm has been removed, as it was removed then, and the building patched, as it was patched then, and the patch is indistinguishable from the wall around it because the stone aged to match in the way stone does — except here it aged immediately, overnight, as if the new stone wanted to be old. The children playing on the rooftop are playing the same game the children were playing when their grandparents watched from below. Not the same game in the sense of tradition. The same game.

No one finds this troubling. The word for it in whatever language this city speaks might translate as right-ness, or completion, or the state of a thing having arrived. The plateau has a quality of stasis that is not death and not sleep but something more like saturation — the way a sponge that has absorbed all it can absorb is still, not because it is empty but because it is full. The braided river runs in its familiar channels. The dove calls its three-note descending call from the same wall joint it has always called from. The raptor on the parapet has always been there. The people move through their days with the unhurried certainty of people who do not expect tomorrow to be different from today, and are not saddened by this, because today is sufficient, and tomorrow will be today again, cool and bright and full of stone.

The buildings are sharp-edged because the stone does not erode here. The stone does not erode because time is moving through this place the way water moves through certain dense materials: present, acknowledged, but not doing what it usually does.

Stand on the highest terrace at midday. The light is direct and clear and falls equally on everything. The far rim of the plateau is visible to the north, hazy with distance. The tree clusters are dark islands in pale stone. The river is a silver thread to the west, cold and talkative in the morning quiet. The bats are in their caves. The dove is calling. A dog raises its head, looks at you, looks away. Somewhere, the sound of stone on stone.

It has always been like this. It will always be like this. This is not a threat. This is the plateau telling you what it is.
