# The Delta Engine

---

The water came before anything else had names.

It comes still. It has not stopped coming. This is the first rule of the place, maybe the only rule: the water does not stop. It arrives from three directions simultaneously — from the sky as warm, close rain so dense it erases the boundary between air and surface; from the ground as upwelling, as seepage, as the slow hydraulic insistence of pressure finding its ceiling; and from somewhere lateral, somewhere just past the edge of vision, where a wall of brown-green water stands maybe forty feet high and advances at the pace of a walking man and has been advancing for as long as anyone has been here to watch it. It never arrives. The wall is always forty feet high and always the same distance away. This is not a comfort.

Underfoot — if you can call it that, if the soft, sucking substrate of new delta deserves a word as confident as *underfoot* — the land is barely land. This ground was not here recently. You can tell by its consistency, which is the consistency of something still becoming: silt so fresh it hasn't decided on a color, cycling through pale gray and ochre and a bruised near-violet depending on what the light is doing, which is hard to say because the light is never doing one thing. The banks, where banks exist, are young and crumbling-forward, not eroding away but growing, extending into the water in fingerlike formations that form and collapse and reform on a schedule that has no period. A bank collapses; three smaller banks emerge from the collapse. The world here is not old enough to have committed to its own geography.

The vegetation has taken this as an invitation.

---

It is wrong to say the plants are growing. Growth implies a pace. What the plants are doing is *insisting*. Reed-structures forty feet tall push through the water's surface in stands so tight their hollow stalks form walls — not metaphorical walls, actual walls, barriers you could lean against, pressing back — and within those walls the water is a different color, stiller, older, already tending toward the murky biological richness that older wetlands carry. The reeds are themselves colonized. A moss with no common name — call it damp-fur, call it living felt — coats every surface above and below the waterline, and the moss is being colonized by liverworts, and the liverworts are being colonized by grasses that have given up on soil entirely and grow rootless, suspended, their threadlike attachments reaching into the water column like questions. 

Floating mats of vegetation extend across every open water surface large enough to hold them. Some are a few square feet. Some are several acres. The largest ones — and there are at least three visible from any raised position, each the size of a small neighborhood — have developed topography of their own: shallow pools forming in their depressions, secondary reed-growth establishing itself on their ridges, as though the mat is practicing at being land and getting better at it. These mats are not stable. They move. They move with the water's motion, yes, but they also move *against* it — the mats have directions, purposes, vectors of travel that don't correspond to the current, and they come into contact with one another and when they do the collision is audible, a long, resonant, vegetable-grinding that joins the broader noise of the place.

The broader noise of the place is hard to describe because it is not one thing.

---

It is the rain, which is immense and continuous and falls with the energy of water that has been held up and now is not, a sound like applause from a crowd that will never stop, a white roar with texture in it, pockets of heavier fall and lighter fall making a kind of rhythm that almost resolves into pattern and then doesn't. 

It is the engines.

The engines are not supposed to be here. This is clear because of the newness of everything around them, the rawness of the delta-land that looks like it emerged last month, and the engines look like they have been here for decades. They are not a consistent technology. Scattered through the reed-stands and half-sunk into the silt, there are: a turbine housing the size of a barn, black iron, riveted in rows, its single enormous exhaust pipe pointing downward into the water rather than upward into the sky, producing a constant low-pressure boiling that makes the water around it churn and steam; a row of diesel pumps in a line that curves away into the reeds and out of sight, each pump connected to the next by pipes that have grown over with the moss but are clearly still pressurized, thrumming at a frequency you feel in your chest; a generator building half-submerged, its cinder-block walls still above water, the door hanging open, the interior glowing — not with electric light, not exactly, but with something that was electric once and has become something else, a light that moves too slowly for electricity, that pools and drains within the space of the building like water itself. 

These structures are not related to one another. They are not parts of a single industrial system. They seem to have arrived separately, from different eras, different purposes, different understandings of what engines are for. What they share is: they are running. All of them. Whatever they are doing, they are doing it continuously, noisily, without apparent fuel source, without apparent output except for the noise and the heat and the strange effect they seem to have on the water nearest them, which moves differently near an engine — more purposefully, as though the water has accepted a direction.

There are no animals here. This is the second rule of the place.

---

You notice the absence gradually, the way you notice an undertone. There are no birds, though the reed-stands are exactly the habitat — the ideal, textbook, near-perfect habitat — for breeding wading birds, for grebes and rails and the whole secretive ecology of the tall-grass margin. The insects are gone too. There are no insects on the surface of the water, no water-striders, no whirligig beetles in their tight panicked circles. The vegetation has no parasites, no galls, no leaf-miners. The mats of floating plant-life carry no passengers. The place is biologically immense and biologically singular — one continuous vegetable kingdom with no division between one plant and another, no competition, no negotiation, just the single insisting fact of growth in all directions simultaneously.

The absence of animals is the loudest absence. You feel it as a pressure, the same way you feel the pressure of the waiting wall of water, always at the same distance, always forty feet high, never arriving.

*Why no animals.* You ask this question and the question joins the noise and the noise absorbs it.

---

The sky is not a sky.

Or: it is a sky in the same way that this ground is ground — technically, provisionally, still working on it. The rain falls from a ceiling of cloud so low and so complete that there is no sky above it, no suggestion of sky, no conceptual room for sky. The cloud is warm. The rain is warm. The steam rising from the turbine building is warm and joins the cloud and the cloud is perhaps producing itself, cycling the warmth upward and releasing it again as more warm rain. The whole system — water falling, water rising as steam, cloud thickening — is a closed circuit and has been for longer than seems possible for something this young, this new, this recently-emerged.

The light within this system is the light of deep cloud, of permanent overcast, but it has been changed by the machines. Near the generator building, the slow electrical pooling affects the air for several hundred feet, a luminescence like bioluminescence but colder, not biological, the light of some process running past its intended endpoint. Farther from the machines, the light is the color of warm water held up to a window, that translucent amber-brown, a color that doesn't cast shadows so much as it softens them, makes them uncertain. Everything in the middle distance becomes approximate.

---

Here is what the place is doing, when you watch it long enough:

The delta is building itself. Not slowly, not in the geological sense, but actively, in real-time, visibly — silt accumulating into banks that become ridges that become the suggestion of islands, the reed-mats docking against these new formations and adding their own mass, the whole system moving sediment upward, landward, toward something like solidity. The delta wants to be land. It is doing everything it can to become land. And the water wants to remain water — or rather, there is so much water that the land cannot get far, the banks collapse, the islands are reabsorbed, and the system starts again.

The engines are helping. This is the conclusion you come to. The engines are helping the delta become land. The turbine pushing hot water downward through its misaligned pipe is doing something to the silt below, sorting it, compacting it through some hydroacoustic principle you don't have the physics for. The diesel pumps are moving water away from the new banks, not pumping it out of the system but redistributing it, accelerating the process of exposure and colonization. The generator building's slow light is doing something to the moss, something that makes it grow faster than moss should grow, fast enough to be visible, to be *watch-able*, the felt-texture of it advancing up a cinder-block wall at the speed of a minute hand.

Who built the engines. When. For what original purpose. These questions do not have local answers.

---

The sound changes.

It doesn't diminish — it never diminishes — but it shifts in character, several times a day, according to no clock. Sometimes the rain-sound comes forward and the engine-sound recedes into it, and the place feels purely natural, purely this extraordinary thermodynamic excess of water and warmth and green insistence. Sometimes the engines come forward and the rain-sound becomes background, and the place becomes something industrial, something purposive, something pointed at an outcome. In these periods the light from the generator building intensifies and the slow pooling of it spills farther across the water's surface.

And sometimes — not often, but often enough to expect — both sounds come forward together, the rain and the engines arriving at the same amplitude simultaneously, and in those moments the air changes pressure and the floating mats change direction and the wall of water on the horizon shudders, oscillates, and for a moment appears to be closer, and then the moment passes and it is forty feet high again and the same distance away.

During those moments, the plants grow fastest. You can see the reeds extending upward, inch by inch, a visible fact, a counter-argument to every slow thing.

---

What does it smell like.

Green is not a smell, but this place would convince you otherwise. The smell is the smell of green taken to an extreme, past pleasant into something physiological: the sharp aldehydes of fast-growing vegetation, the methane-sweetness of new silt colonized by bacteria, the metallic overtone of the engines which is not quite rust and not quite hot metal and not quite electricity but all three in a compound the place has invented for itself. The rain has a smell, which rain doesn't usually have — warm rain smells of the cloud it came from, and this cloud smells of itself, of altitude and condensation and the long cycle it has been running.

Underneath everything, occasional and concerning, the smell of ozone. As though somewhere in the closed circuit of this system, in some conversion of energy that the machines are performing, lightning is being generated and used internally, never escaping upward.

---

The rules of the place, collected:

The water does not stop. The wall does not arrive. No animal enters and if an animal entered it would not stay. The machines run. The delta is always in the process of becoming land and never completing the process. The light pools. The sound is always plural, always massive, always just at the threshold of being parseable into meaning and then — not. The plants grow at the speed of argument. The sky is a ceiling and the ceiling is warm and the warmth is a loop. The ground is new. The ground has no memory. The ground is inventing itself and is serious about this.

The place is not hostile. This is important to say. It is not welcoming either — welcoming implies you were expected, and the place has no frame for the concept of a visitor. The place is absorbed. The place is doing something, has been doing something, will continue to do something, and the something involves water and silt and warmth and engines and reeds and the slow-fast logic of a system that wants to become more itself, denser, more insisting, more present, more land, more water, both, neither, the third thing.

The wall of water, forty feet high. Always the same distance. Patient with an patience that has nothing to do with waiting.
