# The Woven Interior

---

Begin with the floor, because the floor is where the strangeness is most legible.

It is woven. Not metaphorically — the ground beneath your feet is a mat of interlaced vegetable fiber, thick as a man's palm, pressed flat by years of pressure and warmth until it has taken on the qualities of something geological: striated, load-bearing, faintly resonant when you step wrong. The individual strands are as thick as rope in some places, as fine as hair in others, plaited by hands — many hands, across what must have been a great patience of time — into patterns that repeat but never quite exactly, the way a song has verses. Reed, rush, split wood, dried vine. The joins are irregular. This was made without metal. The tools that made it left their character in the making: blunt edges, patient pressure, the occasional missed beat in the weave where a craftsperson's attention drifted or a strand snapped and was knotted back.

There is no soil beneath. Or rather: what lies beneath the mat is more mat, older mat, mat going down through successive generations of flooring as though the builders never demolished anything, only laid new work over the settled old. The place is built on itself. It has always only ever been built.

---

The walls are close.

This is a small place. Perhaps thirty feet across at its widest point, and that wideness is not a room so much as a slightly generative loosening — the space exhales a little here before contracting again into corridors and alcoves and dense, leaning shelving that functions as wall whether it was intended to or not. The overall shape resists description as a single geometry. It is more like the floor plan of a creature's thought: purposeful locally, labyrinthine overall.

Everything is made of the same several materials rearranged. Wicker panels, dense-woven, serve as walls. Lashed-pole frames support the roof, which is also woven — a tighter weave overhead, almost watertight, the pattern different from the floor's, the light coming through it in a fine green-gold diffusion as though filtered by leaves, though there are no leaves directly above. The filtering is structural. The weave was calculated, by eye and trial, to let in this quality of light and no other.

The warm air inside is almost perfectly still.

---

Now the plants, because they are everywhere, and they are the shock of the place.

Given that there is almost no water — given that the world this place exists within seems to operate on a different chemistry, something drier and more mineral, something without rivers or rain in the usual sense — the vegetation inside is an affront to expectation. It crowds every surface. It does not merely decorate the built structure; it has become structurally integral to it, or perhaps the built structure was always integral to it, a scaffold for growing.

Thick-leafed succulents grip the wicker walls with modified root-adhesives, their leaves storing whatever moisture they metabolize from — from the air itself, from the breath of the room's inhabitants, from something more obscure. Vines have been trained along the pole framework with such care, over such time, that they have now fused with the lashings, the cordage threaded through and through with living wood until you cannot tell where the craft-object ends and the organism begins. Low-growing mats of something moss-adjacent — though not moss, the leaves are articulated, jointed, each one a small architecture — fill every horizontal surface that isn't actively being used as floor.

The green is not uniform. It is dense, layered, the colors ranging from the blue-green of something waxy and sealed against moisture-loss to the bright yellow-green of something young and metabolically urgent, recently unfolded. Here and there a small rosette has gone to flower, and the flowers are modest: four petals each, dull ochre, pointing inward like cupped hands. No scent, or a scent so subtle it only arrives in certain moments, an almost-sweetness that might be the wood.

The lushness is the result of a closed system. Water is nowhere and yet the plants thrive because the humidity is managed — not by machinery, nothing so late as machinery, but by design: the density of the vegetation itself, the tight weave of the roof, the bodies of the fauna, the slow transpiration of leaves into an interior volume that does not exchange much air with outside. Whatever is exhaled is reabsorbed. The chemistry here is different enough that this is possible. Or the chemistry is the same, and what differs is a physics of patience, a dryness that is not absence but storage.

---

The fauna is many and small.

This is important: nothing here is large. The scale of the place prohibits it, and perhaps the scale of the place was chosen to prohibit it, or perhaps the fauna scaled themselves to the place over the slow time of their residency. The relationship goes in both directions.

Insects in the loose sense — jointed, many-legged — move through the leaf-layers in numbers that are only visible if you are still for long enough. Their colors tend toward the green-brown of the plants against which they rest. They are not camouflaged exactly; they are continuous with the surfaces they inhabit, as though the distinction between animal and substrate was negotiated loosely and some of them came out partly plant, or the plants came out partly animal, or both, and no one has yet determined which. They move slowly in the warm still air. They communicate, if they communicate, by contact — touch, the pressing of one body against another in a syntax that leaves no sound in the air.

Other things: a family of small, pale lizard-adjacent creatures that live between the layers of the floor-mat, audible only as a faint scritching when they pass under your feet, and even that sound absorbed almost immediately by the dense fiber. Something that clings to the ceiling in the darker corners, motionless enough to be overlooked, its outline soft, its breathing (if it breathes) imperceptible. A bird — or a bird-shaped animal, the wings are wrong, folded in a way feathers wouldn't explain — perched on a high crossbeam, asleep or performing sleep. Its chest moves. Its eyes are closed. It has been there a long time.

---

The quiet is not the quiet of emptiness.

It is the quiet of saturation. Too much is present for sound to travel far. The wicker walls absorb it; the living plant-matter absorbs it; the layered floors absorb it. What you hear is therefore only what is generated very nearby: the small settling of the structure as the warmth of the day moves through it, contraction of fiber and wood so slow it registers as a sequence of isolated, gentle ticks rather than movement. The occasional passage of a small body through leaves. Your own breathing, which you become aware of because it is among the louder things in the room.

There is a sound that might be, if you attend to it very carefully and do not frighten it away with attention: the sound of the place growing. The slow cellular work of photosynthesis and extension, the vines' tendril-tips exploring along the poles at a rate measurable only across days, the matted floor-plants thickening infinitesimally toward the next layer that, in some generation's time, will be buried and become substrate. It does not make a sound you can hear. But you hear it anyway, eventually, as a quality of the silence: full, directional, ongoing.

---

Here is where the reality begins to negotiate with itself.

The weirdness arrives slowly, as the place becomes more itself.

The first thing is the shadows. They do not correspond, exactly, to the light. This is most visible at the edges of objects — the wicker wall has a shadow that falls not away from the light source but at a slight angle to where it should, as though there is a second light source that is not visible, or as though the shadow is remembering a different time of day. The shadow of the sleeping bird on the crossbeam is larger than the bird. The shadow of your hand, when you hold it up, has more fingers.

The second thing is the plants' growth. It is slow — it is very slow, the dynamic here is patient, measured — but it is not incremental in the way growth should be incremental. Attend to a leaf tip for long enough and what you observe is not continuous movement but a series of *states*, the leaf occupying one position and then, at some interval too long for the eye to catch but too short for the mind to deny, occupying another. The vine on the eastern pole has, in the time since you arrived, extended what appears to be a half-inch of new growth. This should take days. You have been here an hour. You check your sense of the time and find it uncertain, unreliable.

The third thing is the insects. Watched individually, they are simply insects. Watched as a community — and this requires a kind of unfocusing, a willingness to take in the whole wicker wall at once — they form patterns. Not metaphorical patterns. Literal ones: arrangements that shift in response to some signal you cannot perceive, hundreds of small bodies repositioning in unison to make a shape that holds for a moment and then dissolves. The shapes are not symbols you recognize. But they are symbols. The syntax of their touching is a language of the wall, the wall a page, and the message, if it is a message, is for someone with eyes differently made.

The fourth thing is structural and hardest to account for.

This place should not be able to hold what it holds. The volume of the interior — if you try to calculate it, if you try to hold the floor area and the ceiling height simultaneously in mind and multiply — does not account for the depth of some of the alcoves, the length of some of the corridors, the density of what grows in spaces you passed through and did not look directly into. The built environment is larger inside than outside. Not dramatically — this is not the fantasy of infinite interior. The discrepancy is modest, maybe fifteen percent, maybe twenty, the kind of thing you might attribute to faulty spatial memory. But you checked the outside before you came in. You counted your steps. The inside is bigger. By exactly as much as is needed to hold everything that is here.

---

The warmth is body-temperature, or close to it.

This is the most intimate fact about the place. Not hot — not the punishing warmth of a greenhouse in summer — but the warmth of being held, the warmth of a room that has been lived in continuously, the residual heat of accumulated life. It comes from the vegetation's metabolic radiation, from the small fauna, from the woven walls that trap it, from the layers of old flooring below which old warmth still slowly dissipates upward.

It is possible that the place is warm because it has been inhabited for so long that the warmth has become architectural, laid down in the material like a quality of the fiber itself. The old mats, layers deep, have always been warm. The warmth is now structural.

You could sleep here. This might be why it was built.

---

But it was also built recently. This is the paradox the young erosion score insists on.

The edges of the cut reeds are still sharp. Where poles have been trimmed, the wood is pale, unweathered, the cut face not yet darkened to the color of the surrounding material. The knots in the cordage have not yet taken on the permanent set that old knots have; they retain the memory of having been tied, the rope still wanting, slightly, to return to its previous form. There are places where a newly-added section of wicker wall meets an older one, and the new section is a slightly different weave pattern, a slightly different color, the dried plant material not yet harmonized to the interior's particular brown-gold.

The place is very old and was also just made. The vegetative layers suggest decades. The sharpness of the craft-edges suggests days. This is either evidence of rapid, unusual growth, or of time operating differently in here than the shadow-discrepancy suggests, or of something else, something without a name, some property of fully-made places that have been made in the same location by the same methods over and over until the made-ness accumulated into a kind of geological stratum, a constructional deep-time, and the new work laid on top inherits the age of everything below it even as it remains, in its materiality, fresh.

The place does not know how old it is.

---

You are standing near the center, which is not exactly the geographic center but is the center nonetheless — the spot where the light from the woven ceiling falls most evenly, where the insects are thickest on the walls, where the bird on its crossbeam is directly above you and its too-large shadow falls, more or less, on your feet.

The place is not watching you. That framing is wrong, too predatory, too paranoid. The place is *including* you, which is different and in its own way more demanding. The vegetation leans infinitesimally in your direction — not toward light, which comes from above, but toward you, toward the slight moisture gradient of your exhalation, toward the warmth of your surface, toward whatever signal your presence broadcasts in the grammar of this interior.

You are being incorporated.

This is slow. You have time.

The walls are warm. The floor accepts your weight with a faint, responsive give. The silence continues being full. Somewhere beneath your feet, in the compacted dark between the mats, something small is moving at the pace of something that has nowhere particular to be.

The shadow of your hand still has more fingers than you do.

You notice this and wait to feel afraid, and instead what arrives is something more like recognition — the sensation of understanding a rule you did not previously know existed, a rule that has always been true, a rule that is true right now, a rule that will continue to be true long after you have been fully woven in.
