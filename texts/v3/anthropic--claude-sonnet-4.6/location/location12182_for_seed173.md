# The Lock

---

It is a chamber. It has always been a chamber.

The walls are iron — not the bright iron of a forge but the iron of a century of submersion, iron that has taken on the color and texture of the riverbed it replaced. Riveted plates, each the size of a barn door, bolted to curved ribs that arch overhead like the inside of a whale. The rivets themselves are domed with rust the color of dried blood, and where they meet the plate, mineral water has stained fans of orange and umber outward in every direction, so that the whole wall looks like a map of a delta seen from above, or like the burst vessels in an old man's eye.

The floor is iron too, but you can't see it. Four, five feet of water stand in here, cool and dark and slightly green with the particulate life that has made its home in the stillness. The water level is almost exactly where water levels always are: just below the lower hinge of the upper gate.

There are two gates. That is the logic of a lock.

---

The upper gate is closed. It is always closed, functionally — both leaves of it have swollen into their sills over decades of disuse, the timber facing swollen and married to the iron frame behind it, and what was once a controlled seal has become a permanent one. Water still comes through, though. It always does. It seeps through the grain of the old wood in a dozen thin streams, each catching the light differently — some sheeting flat against the gate face, some arcing out into the chamber in parabolic threads before breaking apart into drops. The sound of these is the primary sound of the place: a continuous white complexity, the overlapping patter of water that has fallen a foot or two onto the surface below, and below that the lower register of whatever is pressing against the gate on the other side, the weight of the upper pound, tons of canal held back by swollen wood and iron dogs and the fact of the thing having worked for so long.

The lower gate is open, or open-ish. One leaf has been cranked back to something like forty degrees and frozen there. The other leaf is gone entirely — its upper pintle sheared at some point, the leaf pivoted down and away and now lying half-visible on the floor of the lower pound beyond, a dark shape under moving water, hosting a civilization of freshwater mussels.

Through the open leaf, you can see the lower canal stretching away — or rather you can see a few yards of it before the dimness takes it. The surface out there catches more light than the surface in here, being exposed to the sky slot between the embankments, and so it appears as a brightened rectangle framed by the gate opening, the water out there restless with current and the small negotiations of surface tension.

---

The light inside the lock comes from two sources.

There is a single electric lamp mounted high on the wall to port — a cage lamp, industrial, the kind with a wire guard over the bulb. The bulb itself is old enough that the filament glows orange-warm rather than white, and this warmth falls across perhaps a third of the chamber before exhausting itself. The lamp is connected by conduit to somewhere outside. It hums. The hum is consistent and low and almost below notice, but once noticed it becomes part of everything, a ground tone under the water sounds, a sign that something, somewhere, is still running.

The second source of light is the gate leakage itself — each thread of water from the upper gate is briefly luminous where it catches the lamp, a shifting lacework of brightness that plays across the opposite wall and the water surface both. This light is not still. It moves with the water's movement, with pressure fluctuations, with the passage of anything through the air.

The result is a chamber that is dim without being dark, that is full of motion without being turbulent. You can see reasonably well in here once your eyes settle. You can see the far wall. You can count the rivets on the gate.

---

The fauna.

This is where it gets complicated.

The water is thick with life, but life of a patient, sessile, low-drama kind. The mussel colonies have extended from the lower pound into the lock itself, up the iron walls to the waterline and a few inches above it, where they sit in their damp safety and filter whatever the water brings them. They are everywhere that iron meets water. They have been here long enough that the oldest individuals have died and their shells have been colonized by younger ones, and by algae, and by the small freshwater limpets that have found this a workable niche. The walls below the waterline are upholstered in living matter — not lushly, not thickly, but consistently, the way lichen upholsters a north-facing stone.

The fish are less numerous but present. There are perch — three or four that you might see at once, holding in the green shadow near the lower gate, barely moving their fins. There are gudgeon working the floor. Occasionally something larger passes through from the lower pound, a pike or a large bream, moving with the unhurried certainty of an animal that has no real predators here, and the perch scatter slightly and then reassemble.

There are eels. You won't see them during the day but you'll know they're here. The sediment on the floor carries the signs of their passage.

Above the waterline, on the ledges formed by the gate machinery housing and the tops of the buttresses, birds have taken up residence. Mainly jackdaws. There are perhaps eight or ten individuals who treat the lock as their territory, roosting in the angle where the lamp conduit meets the wall, foraging the waterline for invertebrates, quarreling over nothing with the intensity that jackdaws bring to everything. Their calls are the loudest thing in the chamber — sudden, percussive, echoing sharply off the iron and returning changed. When two of them dispute a ledge, the sound bounces between the walls until it's hard to locate, until it seems to come from the iron itself.

A grey wagtail picks the waterline below the gate leakage, stepping neatly over the mussel shells, entirely unbothered.

---

The machinery.

Each gate had a rack-and-pinion mechanism, hand-operated, mounted on the top of the gate beam. On the surviving gate leaf, the mechanism is still there — a cast-iron housing the size of a small suitcase, a long iron bar for a handle, a brass plate (now green) with the maker's name and a date. The gearing inside would still turn if you put your weight on the handle. The teeth are worn but not stripped. There is something almost insistent about the quality of the manufacture, the sense that this was built to outlast whoever built it and has done exactly that.

The sluice paddles are both non-functional. The lower sluice housing has corroded through in one corner and now leaks a continuous stream regardless of paddle position, adding its voice to the general sound. The upper sluice paddle cannot be raised; its rack is seized. But these failures are old failures, failures that the place has long since accommodated. The lock doesn't try to be a lock anymore. It has become something else: a chamber, a habitat, an acoustically rich iron room partially filled with cool green water, doing its settled thing.

---

The age of this place is not hidden. It is the main thing.

Everything that was going to corrode has corroded to its stable state. The iron walls are not rusting further — they have reached the equilibrium form of iron-in-water, the red-brown surface layer that protects what's underneath, the same way a bronze statue greens and then stops. The wood of the surviving gate leaf is not rotting further — it has hardened past a certain point, become something denser than wood, almost like stone in feel if you press it. Even the biological communities on the walls have the look of having been there for a very long time, the pattern of colonization too orderly, too settled, to be recent.

The chamber does not feel ruined. Ruins imply a fall, a before-and-after, a sense of interrupted purpose. This is past that. This is a place that has been exactly what it is for long enough that what it is has become natural — the water sounds and the jackdaw sounds and the orange glow of the lamp and the slow perch and the streaks of mineral stain running down the iron like the brushwork of something patient and vast.

It is not picturesque in the way that ruins are picturesque. It is not asking to be mourned.

It is a room. It is a room that water lives in. The machines that made it a machine are quiet, or nearly quiet — only the lamp hums, only the continuous leak of the sluice adds its note to the water's complexity. The industrial fact of the place is undeniable and present, in every rivet and every plate and every cast fitting, but the industrial moment has passed and what remains is the form after the function, the shaped iron space persisting because iron persists, because the embankments that hold the canal hold it still, because the water that found its way in has stayed.

The sound of falling water, in an iron room, is very loud. You would not think, before you entered, that it would be this loud. The echoes of the jackdaws and the continuous white rush of the gate leakage and the hum of the lamp and the percolation of current through the lower gate add up to something that fills the chamber completely. There is no quiet corner. Every surface is live.

You stand on the gate beam, or on the iron walkway bolted to the lock wall, with the water four feet below you and the orange lamp and the moving light of the water playing across the iron above, and the jackdaws are doing whatever jackdaws do, and the sound of the place is all around you like a substance, and you understand that this particular convergence — the water, the iron, the animals, the age, the dim warm light — has not been arranged. It has accumulated. One thing after another, for a very long time.

That is the difference.
