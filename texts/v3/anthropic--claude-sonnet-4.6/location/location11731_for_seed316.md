# The Roar Flats

---

The first thing you understand, before you understand anything else, is the sound.

It is not loud the way thunder is loud, or surf, or crowds. Those sounds are events. This sound is infrastructure. It underlies everything, holds everything up, the way bedrock holds a mountain — except here the bedrock is vibration, is frequency, is a pressure that has been continuous for so long it has reshaped the bones of everything that lives in it. The sound is not coming from anywhere. Or rather: it is coming from everywhere, which in practice means it has no source, which in practice means it simply *is*, the way gravity simply is, and you feel it not with your ears but with your sternum, your teeth, the loose fluid behind your eyes. To stand here is to become a resonating body. Your thoughts loosen at their edges. Held too long in this sound, the border between *you* and *air* begins to blur.

What is making the sound is the flats themselves.

---

The land is ancient. Not old in the way a mountain range is old, proud of its crag and its snow. Ancient the way sediment is ancient: it has been *ground*. Whatever this land was — upland, ridge, the flank of some Precambrian plateau — time has worked it past all distinction. It is flat with a flatness that feels less like absence of height and more like a conclusion, like an argument that has been finished for so long the argument itself is gone. You could walk for days without ascending or descending more than a meter. The surface stretches to every horizon with no interruption, no punctuation, nothing the eye can use to anchor itself, and the horizons themselves are so far that they soften into something other than distance — they become *weather*, haze, a boundary that is more conceptual than visual.

The land is flat. The land is also never still.

This is the contradiction that takes longest to absorb.

The surface, at any given moment, is moving. Not in one direction, not in any pattern you can extract into a rule. The sediment — grey-brown, an ancient mineral paste somewhere between silt and clay, intimate with water, always saturated to within a centimeter of its upper surface — is in constant, turbulent rearrangement. It does not flow like a river. It does not pulse like a tide. It does something for which no word exists in languages built by people who lived somewhere else: a churning that happens everywhere simultaneously, millions of individual motions that are not coordinated but whose sum is not chaos, exactly — whose sum is something *stranger* than chaos. Something intentional that has no intent.

Pools form and vanish in seconds. Long cracks appear in the surface, fill from below with dark water, close. Mounds of sediment push up, reach perhaps thirty centimeters, and are reabsorbed. The whole surface breathes, if *breathes* is a word that can stretch that far. The whole surface thinks, if *thinks* can be drained of everything it means and filled with something else.

---

The water is everywhere and nowhere classified.

There is no river here. No lake. The distinction between ground and water is a polite fiction you brought with you and must now let go. The clay is wet. The clay is, essentially, water that has learned to hold a shape — and even that shape it holds loosely, provisionally, under constant renegotiation. Step here and your foot sinks to the ankle. Step there and the surface holds you, barely, long enough. The rule for which is which cannot be determined in advance. Old-timers — there are no old-timers — would say the ground reads intention. Reads weight. Reads something.

Where the sediment has been pushed aside momentarily, or where a vent has opened in the churning below, water stands in shallow pools: black, or nearly. Not because it is deep but because whatever is beneath it — sediment, biology, the dreaming substrate — absorbs light rather than reflecting it. These pools are warm. Noticeably warm. The warmth has a pulse, varying in intensity over periods of minutes, which is another thing that should not be happening and which is happening nevertheless.

The air above the flats is saturated. Not humid — *saturated*, beyond the threshold of the word, past the point where a distinction between air and water serves any purpose. Condensation forms on every surface. It forms on rocks that weep constant slow trickles. It forms on your skin within seconds of standing still, beads gathering and running, gathering and running. The air is not mist, exactly — you can see clearly for perhaps a kilometer — but light passing through it behaves strangely, bends where it shouldn't, arrives slightly off from where it was aimed. Objects at distance shimmer. Objects at distance *shift*. Sometimes an object you've been watching at half a kilometer resolves, as you approach, into something entirely different from what you thought it was, and sometimes it resolves into something that was not there before.

---

Vegetation clings to this place with the tenacity of the almost-defeated.

What grows here grows low. Flat. It has learned, over whatever span of time low-growing things learn anything, that height is risk, that height is exposure to whatever this environment *does* to height, and so it hugs the churning surface and does what it can. Low mats of something grey-green, almost lichen-like, cover perhaps a fifth of the visible ground at any moment, though the coverage shifts — the churning buries some patches, exposes others, and what emerges from a period of burial sometimes looks different from what went under, sometimes looks more than one thing at once. Long-stemmed water plants push up through the pools, pale-stalked, leaves flattened like hands pressing against glass from below. They live brief vertical lives before the pool closes or the sediment shifts and they are taken back.

There are trees — two, visible from where you stand. Perhaps five meters tall, and this makes them the dominant vertical features of the landscape for as far as you can see, which is to say they are the monuments, the cathedrals, the skyscrapers of the flats. Their trunks have been worn smooth by what? By air. By vibration. By time past reckoning. Their wood has gone grey and fine-grained and hard as the idea of hardness. They have no leaves. They have branches that end in smaller branches that end in smaller branches that never quite end, the fractal carried past the point of resolution into something that looks, from a distance, like fog gathered into tree-shape. They stand in the moving ground. They have been standing here long enough that the ground's motion has become part of their root structure — they have grown *with* the churn, accommodated it, their roots distributed through the moving substrate in a network too large and old to disentangle from the sediment itself. They are this place as much as this place is them.

They hum. The trees hum. They are resonating — they have been resonating so long that the resonance has become part of their growth pattern, their rings (if cut, which nothing here is cut) would show the frequency of the hum in the density of the wood.

---

Built things.

There are perhaps three structures visible from any given vantage, and *structure* is the generous word for what they are.

One: a ring of stones, perhaps four meters across, arranged with a precision that took intention and now exists in a state of being slowly disproved by the ground. The stones are sinking at different rates — the far side already half-absorbed, the near side still at original height — and this differential sinking has given the ring a tilt, a lean, an aspect of a thing in mid-sentence at the moment the sentence was interrupted. The stones themselves are dark with mineral accumulation, with wet, with time. They are not local stone; they were brought from somewhere. That somewhere is gone, or was always far away, or perhaps never existed as we would mean *exist*. No one who placed these stones exists anymore in any category of existence that applies here.

Two: something that was a wall. One course of fired brick, still mostly upright for perhaps twelve meters, then falling to scattered rubble. The bricks are red-orange, or were, and the color has been largely consumed by grey lichen and grey sediment and grey air. The wall goes nowhere, encloses nothing, divides no two things from each other except symbolically. A symbolic division, maintained for however long fired brick lasts in warm wet air. Longer than expected, apparently. Longer than anyone expected.

Three: not a structure but a *mark*. Ground-level. A set of channels cut into a flat stone, shallow, regular, unmistakably intentional — a system of measurement, or a record, or a message to something. The channels are full of water, which makes them dark against the stone's lighter grey, which means they are legible but unreadable. Whatever marks this place, marks it here, in a language that requires a context that is not available.

---

Animals.

You hear them before you see them, but hearing and seeing are both unreliable out here, so the sequence doesn't mean much.

Something wades in a pool thirty meters off. Medium-sized — the body low-slung, the legs adapted to the instability of the surface, splayed wide and flat, almost like a starfish's radial logic applied to a vertebrate. Its skin or hide is the grey of wet sediment. It moves slowly, methodically, and it does not look up. It has been eating something from the pool's bottom — filtering, perhaps, from the warm black water — and its focus on this task is complete, total, a focus without self-consciousness, without awareness of being watched, or with a complete indifference to being watched that is functionally the same thing. After several minutes of motionlessness it begins to move toward the far edge of the pool and then the churn of the sediment accelerates in that quadrant and the animal stops and waits and the sediment stills and the animal continues, as if negotiating.

Birds, once, far overhead. So far they are points. They do not descend.

Below the surface, something is certainly moving, has been moving for longer than the surface structures suggest. The warmth of the water has a source deeper than sun exposure could account for. Something below converts what is available into heat, into motion, into the perpetual churning. Whether this something is biological — whether *biological* even applies as a category — is a question you will not answer today. The flats are 3.0 in whatever units apply to this, which is to say the flats have their own physics, their own weather, their own logic that holds and is internally consistent and simply does not cross-reference the logic you arrived with.

---

Weather.

There is weather here that forms at the scale of the flats and does not extend beyond them.

Columns of warm wet air rise from the churning surface and, at altitude, condense and fall back as rain, which warms on the way down and arrives at the surface not cold but body-temperature, or warmer, a rain that feels like being rained on by something that knows you are there. This cycle repeats with a period of roughly forty minutes — rise, condense, fall, warm, surface, rise — and it is the flats breathing, the flats recycling themselves, the flats maintaining an internal humidity that has nothing to do with any weather system beyond the horizon.

At the edges of the flats — and you cannot see the edges; the edges are days away in every direction, maybe more — the internal weather collides with external weather and the collision is ongoing and large and violent. From where you stand, at the interior, you can hear that violence as a component of the sound, a lower register beneath the churning's higher frequencies, something like the sound of a continent in slow argument with an ocean.

That argument has been ongoing for longer than anything here can measure. It will continue after everything here is gone.

---

Light.

The sky is pale. Not overcast — there is no single cloud layer — but *veiled*, as if the light itself has been traveling so long by the time it arrives that it has lost specificity, lost directionality. You can find no shadow. Or rather: there are shadows, but they do not agree with each other about where the light is coming from. A stone casts its shadow north. A mound of sediment casts its shadow east. Your own shadow, when you look for it, is faint and points in a direction that shifts if you move quickly enough to check. Midday here is not bright; it is *diffuse*. There is enough light to see clearly but not enough to feel certain of the geometry of the world. The warm pale light soaks into wet grey surfaces and what returns is softer than what arrived, slightly different in quality, as if the surface has processed it somehow, reemitted it with small changes.

At some point, the light will change — has perhaps already changed, you have been here long enough to lose the measure of it — and night on the flats is not an event that follows day but a process that the flats participate in, dimming from within as well as from above, the pools going dark first, then the mats of vegetation, then finally the trees losing their dim visibility and becoming presence-without-form, felt rather than seen, still humming, never not humming.

---

Rules of the world.

The world has rules, but they are old rules, older than the formulations we have for rules, and they operate at a scale below perception and above comprehension, which means in practice they operate sideways to comprehension, and you encounter them not as *laws* but as *resistances*, as *impossibilities that are happening anyway*. 

The surface moves, always, in the turbulent non-pattern. This is rule one, and it is the ground of everything else.

What is buried is not necessarily gone. What is gone is not necessarily absent. The stones and the wall and the channel-marks are evidence of something that persisted long enough to make marks and then ended, and the ending is ongoing, and the marks remain in states of partial remaining, and *what made the marks* is not here and also is not entirely not here. This is not metaphor. Something of it is here. Something that was organized enough to cut channels in stone is now dispersed enough to be *this*, to be the turbulence, to be the warmth from below, to be whatever negotiation happens between the low-slung animal and the movement of the sediment. If you sit with this long enough you will begin to feel it is obvious and have no way to explain why it is obvious.

The flats do not optimize. They have not, in all their age, moved toward an equilibrium. They *are* an equilibrium, but of a kind that requires constant agitation to maintain, the way certain flames require constant fuel to remain constant flames. The agitation is the stability. The churning is the stillness. There is a logic here, and the logic is sufficient, and it requires nothing from you — not your acknowledgment, not your understanding, not your presence.

You are here, though, having arrived somehow, standing in warm condensing air, feeling the sound in your sternum, watching a low-slung creature move between churning pools, watching two leafless trees fractalize toward a resolution they never reach, watching the grey-green mats of vegetation breathe and be consumed and re-emerge.

The sound continues. Will continue. Has been continuing.

You will leave, or you will not leave, and either way the sound will continue.
