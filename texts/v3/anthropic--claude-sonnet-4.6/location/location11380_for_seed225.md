# The Lit Cavity

---

Below the surface, though by how much is difficult to say. The ceiling is close enough that a tall person might graze it with raised fingers — close enough to feel like a room rather than a cave — and yet the walls are so far that the edges of the space dissolve into a kind of soft atmospheric distance before they're ever truly found. This is the contradiction you have to accept first: the place is small in one dimension and opens itself endlessly in the others. A plate rather than a box. A lens. A pressed space.

The floor is sandstone, worn into a skin so smooth and so long-settled that it has lost the character of stone and acquired something closer to the character of old wood — a warmth in its color, a slight give in the perception of it, even though it doesn't yield. It is pale orange-pink, the color of the inside of a hand held to sunlight. It has been this way for a long time. Longer than the things that live here, longer than whatever made the hollow — and something did make it, or something made the conditions that unmade it, and whatever that was has long since finished and departed and is irrelevant to what remains. The erosion is complete. There is no active wearing. Everything that could be rounded has been rounded.

The light is the central fact.

It comes from somewhere — it must — but the source isn't visible and after a while you stop looking. The light saturates the air itself, the way late-afternoon light sometimes fills a room with gold before the eye can locate the window. Here it is not golden but white-warm, the color of milk held briefly over heat. The ceiling, low and smooth, reflects it back down without loss. The floor reflects it upward. The light meets itself in the middle of the small vertical space and becomes something ambient, something that casts no shadow strong enough to define a direction. You have illumination without orientation.

In this light, the cavity is fully legible, every surface revealed — and yet the thing it reveals is mostly emptiness. Mostly air. The density of the space is its most striking quality once your eyes have adjusted: there is almost nothing here. A vast lot of clean, warm, lit nothing. You can breathe it. You can turn in it. There's no pressing-in of surfaces except above, and even that closeness feels more like enclosure than compression.

---

Water comes from the walls in places.

Not as a seep, exactly. More like the stone sweats — a thin film in certain sections, the kind of moisture that only reveals itself when you touch the surface and find your palm wet. In a few spots the film collects into a small pendant drop and falls, and falls again every few minutes. The drops catch the light as they fall through the illuminated air and are briefly bright — tiny lit spheres — and then they land on the sandstone and spread and disappear into the floor, or collect into a shallow groove that runs at an angle, and move very slowly toward a part of the space where the ceiling is slightly lower and the stone is darker.

There is a pool there, in that corner, though "pool" is generous. It is more a lens of water, a shallow clarity that covers maybe a meter of floor. It is perhaps two centimeters deep at its center. The stone below it is the same pale orange but sheened and magnified by the water, so it looks more saturated than the rest, more internally lit. Nothing feeds this pool from a visible source. The sweating walls and the falling drops and time itself have made it and make it perpetually, very slowly. It probably was deeper once. It is in equilibrium now — what arrives equals what evaporates into the warm still air.

The warm still air is consistently warm. Not hot. Not uncomfortable. The warmth of a room where someone has been sleeping. The kind of warmth that asks nothing of you.

---

Vegetation is sparse and it is strange.

Along the walls where the moisture is, low things grow. Mosses in patches — but mosses that have become, over a long stay in this permanent bright warmth, a pale color, something between silver-green and grey. They have bleached slowly in the constant light, across generations of whatever mosses do instead of generations. They are not dying. They are fully alive but their chlorophyll is doing something different here, working with a light that never strengthens or weakens, never comes from an angle. They grow flat against the stone, each frond precise and small, and they cover perhaps a quarter of the lower wall in these patches, irregularly.

One other plant. In the corner near the lens-pool, something that is not quite a fern. It has the posture of a fern — the unfurl, the arc — but its leaves are nearly translucent, like wax paper, catching the ambient light and transmitting it rather than reflecting it. A dozen of these fronds extend from a central structure that is barely visible in the floor's surface. They are perhaps thirty centimeters tall. They don't move. There is no air movement here sufficient to move them. They are very still and they glow faintly, which is to say the light passes through them, and they seem brighter than the air around them, even though they are not.

---

The fauna is, by some accounting, abundant. What that means here is the following:

Things of small size, in the air, moving without urgency. Insects that have, over generations, lost much of their pigment and taken on translucence similar to the fern-fronds. Their wings in flight are nearly invisible — only the body, pale and small, traces their movement, giving them the appearance of hovering seeds or animated dust. They are not interested in you. They move on whatever trajectories please them, slowly, in the warm still light. There are a great many of them, in fact, spread through the air of the plate-space, but they are so fine and so dispersed that they barely register against the emptiness. The empty cavity and its full cavity are both true simultaneously.

On the walls and floor: pale-bodied creatures, something like isopods, that have expanded slightly in scale from what isopods usually manage and slowed to nearly geological speeds. They eat the moss. The moss grows at roughly the same rate as they consume it. This relationship has been established for a long time and neither party has pressed for renegotiation. When you watch them you can confirm they are moving but only if you look away and back. From the corner of the eye they register as stationary.

In the lens-pool: something. A few somethings. Pale, finless, or nearly finless. They drift in the two centimeters of water like suggestions of fish, and when you approach they don't flee because they have been in this pool, or their ancestors have been in this pool, long enough to have forgotten that flight is a response. They simply drift. They are perfectly calibrated to their volume of water, their population in equilibrium with what the pool can sustain. If you placed your hand in the water they would drift against it incuriously. They have no pigment left in them at all. They are the color of the light.

---

The built and the technical have not arrived here in the usual sense.

But — and this is where the strangeness locates itself most clearly — there is evidence, in the floor, of something worked. Not tools left behind, not structures, not artifacts in the conventional sense. Rather: marks. Arrangements. In one section of the smooth floor, near the center of the plate-space, some of the sandstone has been worked in a pattern. Small grooves, pressed or abraded, forming a design that is regular enough to be intentional. Circles perhaps, though the geometry isn't quite cartesian. Overlapping curves. Whatever made them had limited tools — fingers, a harder stone, bone — and considerable patience, because the stone is hard and the marks, though definite, are shallow, and to make them must have taken a long time.

They might be a map. They might be a tally of something. They might be a practice, a ritual of repetition, something that was not about the marks themselves but about the making. It's impossible to say because whoever made them did not remain and did not leave a second kind of record that would let you read the first.

The marks are old. They are as weathered as everything else, which is very weathered, which is to say they are in equilibrium too — they will not erode further because the conditions are stable and there is no water over them and the air is still. They have arrived at their final form. They are neither clear nor unclear. They are simply there, settled into the floor of the warm lit plate, and the isopods cross them slowly, and the pale insects drift above them, and the not-quite-fern glows near the water in the corner, and the light, sourceless and warm, illuminates everything equally and casts no shadow that tells you where the day is, or whether there is a day, or how long any of this has been going on.

The quiet is very close to total. Occasionally a drop falls into the pool. The drops do not echo — the ceiling is too low, the space too intimate — but the sound of each drop arrives cleanly in the absence of other sounds and then is gone, as if the silence absorbed it. You might hear, if you sat for a while and listened, the faint texture of the insects moving in the air, something below sound but registered by the body. Something like the feeling of a room that is occupied.

The space has been here long enough to have opinions about itself, though it does not share them. It is not waiting for anything. This is not a place in the process of becoming something else. It arrived and it stayed and it has been, for a very long time, precisely this: warm, lit, nearly empty, patient, and strange in the way that deep equilibrium is always a little strange — the way a thing that has found its final form seems to look back at you from a position of complete indifference to the question of why.
