# The High City

The plateau is so large it has its own weather systems.

Stand at its center and the edges do not exist for you — they are somewhere beyond the curvature of the world, theoretical places where the stone eventually runs out and the air falls away into the valleys below. You cannot see those edges. What you see instead is more of the same: the grey-tan surface of the rock, the low press of structures, the cold sky leaning down. The horizon is merely where more plateau begins. It has always been this way. The word *edge* is nearly mythological here, spoken the way other peoples speak of oceans.

---

The plateau sits at significant altitude — the air is thinner than it should be, although the body adjusts and forgets this, and only strangers feel the headache that comes with it, the slight unsteadiness, the sense that something is being subtracted from the world. The cold is not dramatic cold. There is no blizzard, no theatrical freeze. It is the cold of a place that never fully warms, where the sun — when it comes — delivers light more than heat, where stones in shadow hold their chill through noon and release it back into the night air unchanged. The ground temperature, six inches down, is constant. The people know this and use it.

---

The formation is young, geologically speaking. This matters.

The stone has not yet had time to round itself. Where the plateau surface cracks — and it cracks frequently, in long fissures that run for hundreds of meters — the edges of those cracks are still sharp enough to cut a hand laid carelessly against them. The rock cleaves with an almost willful precision, as though it has opinions about how it should break. Larger formations that jut from the surface — and there are many, the plateau is not flat but ridged and hummocked and interrupted — show fresh faces where frost has recently pried slabs free. Those faces are pale, almost white, compared to the oxidized grey-brown of the older surfaces. You can read the age of a fracture by its color. The people read this the way others read weather signs.

The plateau is still, very slowly, rising. The mountains that ring it — visible on clear days as a darker mass against the sky, on other days absent entirely, swallowed by cloud — are being pushed upward by the same forces. The people do not conceptualize this. The idea of land moving is not part of their framework. But they have noticed, over generations, that certain old survey marks no longer align with certain landmarks. They attribute this to the landmarks moving. Which is, in a sense, correct.

---

Water is a negotiation.

There is no river on the plateau, or rather the rivers are young and intermittent — they exist seasonally, running in the fissures and along the base of the ridges when snowmelt comes or when the rains arrive from the southwest. These channels have not yet carved themselves deep. They are shallow, wandering, uncertain of their courses. In wet season the fissures run with cold grey water that tastes of mineral and frost. In dry season they hold only dampness, dark patches of moisture on the stone, slicks of condensation in the morning hours.

The condensation is the real economy of water here. At night the temperature drops enough that moisture precipitates on every horizontal surface — on the flat stones, on the thatch and bark of the rooftops, on the wooden frames of the drying racks, on the skins of the animals asleep in their enclosures. By morning, before the weak sun burns it off, there is a visible film of water on the world. The people have built accordingly: the rooftops of the structures slope inward, not outward, channeling runoff to central cisterns cut into the rock. Every household has its cistern. The cutting of cisterns is a fundamental craft, taught early, refined over a lifetime.

There are pockets of standing water where the surface is depressed and the substrate is dense enough to hold it — small, shallow lakes of pewter-colored water that reflect the sky perfectly when the sky is clear and become invisible when the sky clouds over, indistinguishable from cloud. Wading birds work these pockets in season. The water is cold enough to ache.

---

The settlement covers a remarkable area.

Not because any individual structure is large — they are not — but because the settlement has spread and spread across the plateau over many generations, following the lines of available stone and the locations of the water-pockets and the microclimates that exist in the lee of the ridges. It is a city only in the sense of scale and density; in character it is a thousand villages that have grown until they touched each other and could not be told apart. There are no grand avenues. The pathways between structures are organic, the result of people walking the same routes until the rock wore smooth beneath their feet. Some of those worn paths are very old. The smoothness of the rock under foot is one of the few things here that is not sharp.

The structures are stone, because stone is what the plateau provides. The walls are dry-laid, fitted with considerable skill — a skill that has had generations to develop — without mortar, because mortar requires materials the plateau does not readily offer and because, more importantly, the builders long ago discovered that mortared walls crack when the rock beneath shifts (and it shifts; slowly, but it shifts) while dry-laid walls simply resettle. The walls breathe and adjust. They are thick — a full arm's length thick in most cases — for insulation against the cold. Windows are small and shuttered.

Roofing is the challenge. Timber is scarce at this elevation. The sparse vegetation runs to low scrub, to tufted grasses that grow in the cracks, to a few stunted and wind-shaped trees that exist in the most sheltered places and are never cut for lumber because there will not be more. Roofing is therefore a patchwork: flat stone slabs where they can be managed, which is often; stretched hides cured and waterproofed with fat and ash; woven grass mats layered thick and replaced in season; occasionally, in the oldest and most central parts of the city where trade has brought things from below, a few actual planks, smoothed and precious, handled with care.

The density of the settlement means that structures share walls, that the alleyways between them are narrow enough to touch both sides with outstretched arms, that the city generates its own warmth in its interior spaces — not enough warmth, never quite enough, but perceptibly more than the open plateau beyond the settlement's edge. People understand this and build accordingly, extending inward rather than outward where they can.

There are levels. Not many, and not systematic — this is not a city of towers — but the ridges and humps of the plateau have been built over and around and upon, so that in some places a structure's roof becomes another structure's floor, and you move through those parts of the city in a kind of vertical tangle, ascending or descending through other people's spaces by common consent and long established right-of-way. Those vertical passages are well-worn too, the handholds polished.

---

The life of the plateau is dense, and it is — relative to what one might expect of cold high stone — surprisingly various.

The fauna have sorted themselves into ecological roles with the patience that comes from long cohabitation. The primary large animals are a kind of heavy-coated bovine, kept by the people for milk and fiber and occasional meat, and a smaller, quicker animal — like a cross between a goat and something more uncertain — that manages the steeper ridges and the areas too broken for the bovines. Both animals are present in enormous numbers, managed in large communal herds that move slowly across the plateau surface following the seasonal emergence of the tufted grasses.

The birds are where the abundance really declares itself. The plateau is on a migration route, and in season the sky is thick with travelers — enormous V-formations crossing from horizon to horizon, too high to identify, present for days at a time. Resident species are numerous: raptors that work the thermals along the ridge faces, ground-nesting birds of many sizes that pick through the grass-cracks for insects and seeds, wading birds at the water-pockets, scavengers that follow the herds. The raptors are large. They have found, at this altitude, an abundance of prey and few competitors, and over generations they have grown into it. A person working alone in an outlying part of the settlement watches the sky from habit.

Small mammals: abundant beneath the surface, in the fissures and in burrows cut through the shallower substrate. They come out in the morning to sit in what sun there is. They are eaten, and they eat the grasses, and the raptors eat them, and the system is dense and running and old.

Insects in summer, enough of them to be audible — a low background presence, felt more than heard, a vibration at the edge of perception that disappears in cold weather and returns each year as a kind of announcement.

---

The tools.

The bone-handle, the stone edge, the fiber cord. These are the primary materials of making. Metalworking is known but marginal — small amounts of trade metal come up from below the plateau via the routes that also bring the occasional timber plank and the dyes that produce the bright threads woven into otherwise dun-colored clothing. The metal is used carefully: blades, primarily, and needles, and the occasional pin or fitting. It is not wasted on construction.

Stoneworking is the primary craft and it is highly developed. The ability to read a stone's grain, to find the fault line that will let it cleave cleanly, to shape a block to fit a specific gap in a specific wall — this is a full expertise, a life's study, and its masters are recognizable by the wear on their hands and by the quality of the walls they have made and by the way they look at any new surface, any newly fractured face, with the particular attention of someone reading a language.

Weaving and cordage are the other central crafts. The fiber from the bovine coats is long and dense and, once processed through a series of combing and spinning steps, becomes a textile that insulates well enough to be the difference between survival and its absence. The processing equipment is everywhere: the drop-spindles hanging from doorways, the simple frames for weaving, the carding tools, the bone needles large and small. The craft is not gendered in any absolute sense, but there are patterns of specialization that shift by household and by season.

---

The soundscape is mostly the planet's own.

Wind is the dominant sound, and it is not loud wind — the plateau's enormous flatness means there is little to catch and amplify the air's movement. It is a persistent low register, a presence rather than a noise, felt slightly in the body. When it drops entirely — which it does sometimes, in early morning or just after sunset — the silence it leaves is striking, the kind of silence that has weight.

The animals contribute: the low sounds of the herds, a distant aural texture when they are close, a smear of lowing and footfall. The birds, more various — the raptors are mostly silent or cry briefly, but the ground birds maintain a scattered commentary through the day, and the migrants in season call to each other in the vast flocks with a sound like something tearing, but gently, far away.

The city sounds are: stone tools on stone, the careful ring of a good strike. Voices, low and nearby, not carrying far. Children, more audible than adults. The movement of animals in enclosures. The scrape of a shutter. Water moving in the morning channels, when there is water. The sound of a large city going about itself quietly, without hurry, because the cold discourages hurry and the altitude thins the impulse to shout.

At night: very little. The cold closes things down. There is the wind in its low register. Occasionally an animal sound from somewhere. The settlement's interior spaces murmur with sleeping bodies and the sounds of small fires banked for the night. Beyond the settlement, the plateau offers only itself — the rock, the grass, the dark water-pockets, the cold stars overhead in their extraordinary clarity, the occasional call of a nocturnal bird working the darkness for small mammals.

---

The change here is slow enough to be nearly imperceptible within a human life.

The plateau rises its fraction of a millimeter per year. The fissures extend, slightly. The city grows at its edges, one wall at a time, one household extending into available space. The herds move through their seasonal patterns, the same patterns, more or less, as the generation before. The migrants come and go on their own rhythms, which are the rhythms of the planet's tilt and the temperatures of places far away and unimaginable.

A person born here and living their full life would see: the repair of walls they saw built. Children who became old people. The slow migration of the herds' preferred grazing ground, following some subtle shift in the grass distribution. The deepening, by a hand's width, of a water channel. These changes are real but they are legible only against the backdrop of a whole life.

The stones will still be sharp at the end of that life. The sky will still lean down. The wind will still find the gap between the structures and move through it, looking for somewhere warmer, not finding it, continuing west.
