# The Plateau of First Stone

---

The rock here is not finished yet. That is the first thing you understand, arriving at the edge of the plateau, looking out across something that has not decided what it is.

The surface underfoot is warm — not from sunlight, which arrives here only obliquely through a low ceiling of vapor, but from below. The stone itself carries warmth the way a body does after exertion. Press your palm flat against it and you feel a pulse that is probably just your own blood amplified by the contact, but you are not entirely sure. The rock is pale grey where it has been here longest, which is measured in weeks. Toward the interior it is the color of old teeth, of fog, of something halfway between mineral and not. At the edges of certain formations — and *formation* is too strong a word for them yet — the material grades into something that has texture without hardness, that yields slightly, that you would rather not look at directly while it is mid-process.

This is a high place. The plateau sits perhaps two thousand meters up, which you know more from the ache in your chest than from any view, because the view does not extend. Vapor moves through continuously, not as weather but as condition — the air here exists at its own dew point, permanently, so that breathing is slightly wetter than breathing elsewhere, and the fine hairs on your arms stay beaded, and your collar stays damp against your neck. The rock weeps. Every surface that is old enough to have developed microstructure runs with thin films of water that have no obvious source. The water is warm too. Everything here participates in the same mild, insistent warmth.

---

The plateau is small enough to know. Perhaps three hundred meters across its widest dimension, irregular — not a circle, not a mesa, but a shape that a coastline might make if the coastline were in the process of deciding. You could walk from one edge to the other in five minutes, eight minutes if you went slowly, which you would. There are crevices forming. Not crevices that have been there — crevices that are becoming. You can hear them, in a way, beneath the louder sounds. A low register of settling, of material finding lower states, of — not cracking, exactly, but something that cracking will become in a few thousand years.

The edges of the plateau drop away into cloud. You cannot see what is below. The vapor swallows everything at about thirty meters of distance in any direction, so the plateau exists as its own small world, bounded by white, unanchored from the larger geography. This is not uncomfortable, exactly. It is more that the usual system of placing yourself — *I am here, relative to there* — becomes unavailable, and you fall back on simpler orientations. I am warm. The rock is under me. The sounds are coming from the northeast, or what I am calling northeast.

---

The sounds.

They are the loudest thing. They are the first thing, before you see anything clearly in the vapor.

The dominant register is biological — the fauna here is abundant and much of it is vocal. There are birds, or things that occupy the bird niche: small, quick, warm-colored, calling in sequences that are longer and more syntactically complex than birdsong usually is. They move in loose groups across the broken surface, picking at the mineral margins of new formations. Their calls overlap into something that has rhythm but not pattern, density but not cacophony. When they go quiet for a moment — which happens periodically, a collective hush of a few seconds — the plateau sounds enormous, because the next register becomes audible: the water.

The water is everywhere in the sound field. It is in the weeping rock and in the thin channels that are beginning to form in the newest material and in the vapor condensing on every surface and running off in irregular paths. The sound of it is not unified — there are dozens of separate small drips, trickles, films, none of them loud, all of them together constituting a continuous white-noise layer with shifting emphases. When vapor condenses on your face and runs down your jaw, you are participating in the same system.

Beneath the biological sounds and the water sounds is a third thing. A low, aperiodic sound that you feel more in the sternum than hear with the ears. It comes from the technology. It comes from the things that are making the rock.

---

There are structures here, but calling them structures is a decision about how to see them.

Three of them, or four, depending on how you count a partial one near the northwest edge. They are small — the largest could be walked around in thirty seconds. They do not look built in any way that that word usually applies. They look, instead, like points where the world has been instructed, quietly, to behave differently. The surfaces around them — extending perhaps two or three meters in each direction — have a regularity that the rest of the plateau does not. Not a geometric regularity. More like the regularity of a crystal, or of a sentence: an internal logic that you can feel without being able to articulate.

The structures themselves are approximately matte black, but *approximately* is doing heavy work there. Their color is a function of observation angle and illumination angle and something else that has no name in the current vocabulary of seeing. They are warm to the touch, warmer than the rock, but not hot. They do not vibrate that you can feel. The low sternum-sound comes from near them, intensifies near them, and the small birds do not avoid them — they perch on them, call from them, seem indifferent to whatever the structures are doing.

What they are doing, as far as it can be read, is making the plateau. Not shaping existing rock but producing it. The material at the formation-edges — that yielding, halfway material — comes from processes that originate in or are directed by these objects. The plateau is growing. Slowly, in real-time terms: perhaps a centimeter of new stone per day, perhaps less. But the newness of everything here is legible. Nothing has had time to accumulate. The soil is measured in grams. The plants that exist — a few dry-adapted but water-touched species, thin-leaved, low, anchored in the margins of older zones — are months old at most. The lichens on the oldest rock are the most ancient living things here and they arrived recently on the wind.

Who built the structures? No readable answer. They do not have a legible origin in the sense of showing how they were assembled or where the materials came from. They are simply present, operating, doing their long work. The sternum-sound has been going for some time. The plateau exists because of it.

---

The light is dim and sourceless. Not dark — there is plenty of light to see by, to see everything clearly. But it has no direction. The vapor diffuses whatever sun reaches this altitude into an even, shadowless gray-white that makes depth perception work differently. Objects at ten meters and objects at twenty meters are equally clear, equally present, until you step toward one and the other shifts slightly in apparent size. The formation-edges glow faintly — not literally, perhaps, or perhaps literally — with a warmth that the diffuse light above does not account for. The new material emits something, or the processes that make it do.

In the dim light the water films on every surface look like polish.

---

The animals.

The birds are the most obvious. Perhaps forty of them visible at any time, more heard in the vapor, moving in and out of the white boundary. They are curious in a way that reads as intelligent without being, as far as you can tell, self-conscious about it. One has been following at two meters for several minutes, head tilting, calling intermittently in what might be inquiry or might be announcement.

But the birds are not the majority. In the warm rock, in the new stone where it has gaps and pockets, there are insects of some kind — small, fast, heard more than seen, moving in and out of the half-formed crevices. They seem to be involved with the formation process, in ways that are not quite clear. Possibly they are feeding on something that the formation process produces. Possibly they are, in some way, assisting. The relationship between the structures' work and the biological community here is active and mutual-seeming without being obviously so.

There are things in the water films. Small. The films are thin enough that there is not much room for anything and yet there is movement in them that is not explicable by flow alone. The warm water carries something that is alive, or pre-alive, or in a category that neither of those words fully serves.

---

The plateau, understood whole: a small, bounded, warm, wet, dim, loud place at high altitude, wrapped in vapor, where stone is being made by technology that does not explain itself, and life arrives as fast as there is anywhere for it to go, and the land is weeks old in places and the birds do not know this, they treat it as ground, which is what it is, which it is becoming more completely every day, the formation-edges advancing their slow centimeters into whatever the structures are turning the air and the chemistry into.

The sternum-sound continues.

The water runs down.

The crevices are not crevices yet but they know what they will be.
