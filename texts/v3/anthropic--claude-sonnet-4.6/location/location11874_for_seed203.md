# The Underloft

---

There is a ceiling.

That is the first fact. You register it before anything else, before temperature or orientation, because it is so enormous and so close — closer than any sky has a right to be — maybe three hundred meters overhead, maybe four hundred, and it is made entirely of root.

Not the tangled, dark-basement roots you might imagine. Not the modest root-mass of a single tree or even a copse. These roots are the underside of a continent of vegetation, and they have had enough time — just enough, you sense, not much more — to knit themselves into something like a vault. Like the inverted nave of a cathedral that was never planned, that grew into cathedral-ness through pure accumulation and gravity and the slow negotiations of ten thousand species reaching for what little moisture filters down. The roots interweave into arches, into buttresses, into columns that descend thirty or forty meters before ending in air. They are pale. Nearly white. A few hold the yellow-green of something still alive near the surface — but most of this ceiling is the color of old ivory, of birch bark in February, of bone that hasn't quite crossed the threshold into dryness.

And bright. The ceiling is — inexplicably, overwhelmingly — bright.

---

The light deserves its own paragraph because it defies the logic of being underground.

It comes from the roots themselves, and from what grows on them. Somewhere above — in the dense, packed, total vegetative world at the surface — there are plants that have solved the problem of light transmission the way glass-sponges solve it, the way fiber optics solve it: by building structures that trap photons at the surface and shunt them downward through crystalline cells, through water-filled vacuoles, through some chemistry not yet catalogued. The roots glow. Not dimly. Not bioluminescently, in the soft blue-green of deep-sea things. They glow with something close to daylight — diffuse, sourceless, omnidirectional, filling the underloft from above the way light fills a room with north-facing windows, flat and even and without strong shadows.

The result is a space that is enormously bright and simultaneously subterranean. Your eyes don't know what to do. There is no sun to locate. There is no horizon. The illuminated ceiling curves away in every direction — and it does curve, because this space is vast enough to have its own curvature, vast enough that the far margins of the ceiling dissolve into a haze that might be distance or might be the slow exhalations of the place — and the light falls on the floor of the underloft, which is far below, and mostly empty, and the brightness only makes the emptiness more apparent.

---

The floor is the second fact. Stone, mostly. Young stone — which means not young in the way a puppy is young, but young in geological terms, which means its edges are still edges. Where rock faces have sheared away or thrust upward, they present clean fracture planes, angular and pale gray, with crystalline inclusions that catch the root-light and scatter it in brief cold sparks. The stone has not yet been worn smooth by water. There are places where it has been worn at all, and those places are channels — narrow, knife-cut channels where some intermittent flow has passed through, perhaps seasonally, perhaps on a stranger schedule tied to the moisture-breathing of the vegetation above. The channels are dry now. Or mostly dry. In one, a trickle moves with the slight guilty haste of water that knows it shouldn't be here — it disappears into a crack within thirty meters and does not return.

Condensation. That is the predominant moisture. The root-ceiling is cooler than the air below, and in the temperature differential — the underloft runs cold, genuinely cold, a cold that settles into the lungs and makes each breath feel slightly more substantial than it should — the moisture that the roots wick down from the surface forms slow droplets and falls. You can hear them if you listen. Individual drops, striking stone or the occasional still pool that has gathered in a natural hollow. They fall through several hundred meters of bright empty air and they are the primary punctuation of a space that is otherwise quiet in a way that seems physical, like a material property of the underloft rather than merely an absence of noise.

The sound of a drop falling from the ceiling. A pause. Another drop. From a different direction. A pause. The very faint trickle of the guilty stream, vanishing. Then: quiet so complete it has texture.

---

The walls — there are walls, somewhere, though you cannot see them. This space is described by every measurable indicator as vast, and it is: the underloft extends laterally for distances that defeat comprehension, that would take weeks to cross on foot, that have their own regional variations in geology and root-architecture and what sparse living things have managed to descend this far. You are in one portion of it. You can see, in the distance, where a great root-column descends all the way to the floor, perhaps two kilometers away, perhaps more — scale is difficult without landmarks — and it is perhaps fifteen meters thick at its base, and it is enormous, and it is one of many, and they are still so sparse in the general space that the underloft feels, despite their presence, empty. Nearly void. A vast hollow beneath a living continent.

The empty space is most of what there is.

---

Fauna: rare, as promised, as befits a place that has not yet fully decided it can support life.

There are things that live here, but they tend toward the ceiling, toward the roots, where there is at least the nutrient traffic of the plant world above. Down here at floor-level you occasionally find evidence: the shed casing of something arthropod in nature, translucent as a contact lens, pressed into a crack in the stone. Some kind of scat — small, dry, ambiguous as to source. Once, far off, a movement at the edge of perception, something pale and large-ish crossing between two root-columns and then gone before your eyes have time to negotiate what they were offered.

The things that live in the underloft have not had long to adapt to it. They are, in their way, young — recently come down from surface-world ancestors, still in the early stages of becoming something new. Their eyes, if they have eyes, are likely oversized; the light here is enough to use but still less than the surface's maximum. Their coloration is already trending toward the pale. One species — you find one, eventually, small enough to cup in two hands, moving slowly across a root-column at eye level — is a kind of isopod, cream-white, with legs that work in the slightly-too-deliberate way of something new to cold. It pauses. It continues. It pauses. The root beneath it is warm relative to the air, or at least less cold, and the isopod clearly knows this.

There are also, higher up, near the ceiling, things that move quickly. You see them as small dark shapes against the bright white of the upper roots, and you cannot tell if they are animals or seeds or debris falling upward, which is the sort of thing that seems not impossible in a place like this, a place still settling into its own rules.

---

The turbulence is not weather. Not exactly. But the underloft is not stable.

It is young, and its youth means the ceiling is still moving. Roots that have found nothing to anchor to at floor-level are still growing downward — slowly, in human terms, but visibly, in the timescale of the underloft, which measures time differently. And when a root grows, it pulls. It pulls the matrix above it with fractional but cumulative tension, and sometimes something shifts, somewhere in the vault, and there is a sound.

The sound arrives before any movement: a long, low, resonant creak that seems to emanate from the entire ceiling simultaneously, like the sound of a ship's hull in a swell, if the ship were a continent. The sound is extraordinary. It is the sound of something vast and alive adjusting. It fills the underloft completely and then fades unevenly, some frequency disappearing before others, until only the lowest register remains and then it too is gone.

After the creak, often, falls.

Not catastrophic falls. Not the ceiling coming down. But root-matter: dried fragments, curls of bark or cortex that have become unmoored from their position and now spiral down through the bright air, three hundred meters, taking a long time, catching what air currents exist in the underloft's slow circulation, finally settling on the stone floor or into the dry channels. The stone floor in many areas has a layer of this detritus — root-fall, accumulated — and it makes a soft sound underfoot, a quiet rustling, the only carpet available.

Sometimes the creaking precedes larger movement: a section of the vault, forty or fifty meters across, slowly lowering, or a root-column forming in fast-motion, visibly extending its tip downward like a slow pointing finger, growing perhaps half a meter over the course of a day before the cold slows it. The underloft does not stop changing. It is in the process of becoming something, and that becoming is turbulent in the way of all becoming, which does not necessarily mean violent, but means constant and unfinished and full of sudden decisions.

---

There are, scattered, the artifacts.

They are found near the root-columns, usually. Near the base, where the columns meet the stone. Someone — or some people, few in number, because the artifacts are never dense, never suggesting a settlement or even a camp of more than three or four — was here. Is here? The artifacts do not answer this question clearly.

They are made things. Primitive made things: a platform of split wood lashed with what looks like plant fiber, elevated perhaps half a meter off the ground, sized for sleeping. The wood is of surface origin, obviously — the underloft has no trees — and it was carried down, or lowered, at some effort. Next to it: a shallow bowl carved from the same wood, with a slight residue inside that might have been food or might have been pigment or might have been something used in processes that don't have obvious names. Near another root-column, further on: a series of marks cut into the stone with a stone tool, shallow and clean-edged, recently enough made that the cut surfaces haven't yet acquired the patina that the exposed natural faces carry. The marks are not representational in any obvious way. They may be counting. They may be mapping. They may be addressing the root-column directly, in a language that operates through marks on its base.

The platform and bowl suggest return — someone sleeps here and eats here, or intends to. The marks suggest attention, careful attention, the kind of attention you pay to something you're trying to understand. Whatever brought people down here, they are taking it seriously. They are paying the cold and the emptiness and the creaking ceiling the full cost of their attention.

There is no fire. You look for evidence of fire and there is none. Perhaps the cold is managed with insulation — there are pelts, you realize, on closer inspection, draped over one end of the sleeping platform, something small-furred and pale, the underloft fauna caught and killed and used. Perhaps fire is simply absent, considered too costly in smoke and signal and fuel. Whatever light they needed, the ceiling provided.

---

Stand still in the underloft long enough and you stop being able to remember other places clearly.

The brightness is part of it — it is so counterintuitive, so wrong-feeling for a below-ground space, that the mind keeps trying to categorize it as sky, keeps constructing a horizon that isn't there, keeps expecting the temperature to be wrong in a different direction (shouldn't daylight be warm?). The cold comes up through the stone into your feet. The ceiling glows and shifts and occasionally creaks its long note. Drops fall. Your eyes move over distances of empty air and find nothing, then find a root-column, then lose it again in the haze of distance.

There is the sense — not a mystical sense, a practical sense, a spatial-reasoning sense — that the underloft is larger than you will ever know. That there are portions of it, far off in directions you can't name without a compass and maybe not with one, where the geology does something different, where the vegetation above is different and the light-transmission is different, where the moisture from whatever occasional rain falls on the surface takes different paths down through different cracks and collects differently. Where the root-columns are arranged in ways that create their own micro-territories. Where the animals that have descended from the surface world have had slightly different pressures shaping their descent.

The underloft is not one place. It is a class of places. An underloft-world, sub-continental in extent, variable as any surface landscape, with its own ranges and basins and deserts (dry root-vaults where no moisture passes and nothing lives and the fallen root-matter has built up deep and undisturbed) and its own equivalents of coastline (where the stone of the floor meets standing water in the few places where the underloft dips low enough to collect what drains down, cold pools of exceptional stillness, exceptional depth, reflecting the root-ceiling back at itself from far below).

You are in one small part of it. The creaking comes again, distant this time, several kilometers at least, traveling through the vault as sound travels through bone. A few fragments of root-matter catch the diffuse light as they spiral down. The isopod on the root-column has moved three centimeters since you last looked at it.

The underloft proceeds.
