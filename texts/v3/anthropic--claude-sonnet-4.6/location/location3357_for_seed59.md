# The Green Town

---

Start with the smell, because the smell comes before anything resolves visually. Rot and sweetness together, the particular mixture you get when decomposition is so complete it has become nutrition and the whole system is running on itself. Peat, maybe, or something peat-adjacent — dark wet fiber giving off warmth from underneath, biological warmth, the warmth of process rather than sun.

Then the sound.

It is loud here in a way that takes a moment to disaggregate. The first impression is just *noise* — a continuous layered noise like a city heard from a hilltop — but as you stand still it begins to organize itself into components. Frogs, certainly frogs, at multiple registers, not calling but *conversing*, overlapping in a way that suggests dense population and territorial negotiation. Water moving everywhere, not in a single channel but distributed — a trickle here, the glug of something draining through a root system there, a drip from a leaf cluster onto a hollow section of bark that has become, inadvertently, a drum. Above all of this: birds. Dozens of them at once. Not the occasional singular bird of a quiet forest but the ambient *bird roar* of a place with enough insects and berries and nesting architecture to support improbable density — wrens down in the wet roots, larger things up in the canopy, something screaming repeatedly in the middle distance with a tone that suggests no emergency, just opinion.

And underneath it all, a low sibilance: the sound of ten thousand small wet movements, leaves adjusting, things crawling through mulch, water finding the next lower surface.

---

The place is small. That needs to be understood first, before the density of it overwhelms the sense of boundary. You can see, approximately, where it ends — in each direction, maybe two hundred meters, maybe less. It occupies a kind of bowl-declivity in otherwise open highland, a natural catchment that has been catching things, biologically and hydrologically, for a long time. The surrounding terrain is rocky moorland, cool and exposed, wind-scoured, largely bare. Down here the wind does not reach. The bowl creates its own interior climate.

The elevation is real — you are high enough that your breath came short on the approach, that the exposed ridgeline above is treeless, that the light has the quality of mountain light, a certain blue clarity at the edges of things. But here in the bowl that light is filtered and changed. It comes through leaf layers. By the time it reaches the ground level it is green-tinted, cool, diffuse. Normal light, technically, in the sense of being neither dim nor overwhelming, but *transformed* light, light that has passed through a great deal of life before arriving.

---

The vegetation is the architecture.

There is no framework here that is not plant. No exposed stone walls, no bare earth — the earth, where visible at all, is a carpet of mosses so thick and continuous that it reads as a surface, a floor, a thing you could make decisions about. You could choose to step on the pale grey-green patch by the large root, or the deeper emerald section near the water seep, and you would be making decisions about surfaces the way you make decisions about floors in a building. The moss is doing what floors do: defining level, absorbing sound, marking the difference between inside and outside.

The trees — and they are trees, old ones, gnarled in the particular way of highland trees that have grown slowly in poor wet soil — have become so layered with secondary life that the tree itself is a substrate. You have to look carefully to find bark, to find the original surface of the thing. Instead what you see is: lichen colonies in overlapping jurisdictions, grey, orange-edged, white-spotted; shelf fungi in stages of development, some fresh and firm and edged with the white of active growth, some older and drier and already becoming habitat for the next thing; mosses working upward from the root collar; ferns emerging from crevices where debris has accumulated in the fork of branches. The trees have become three-dimensional surfaces on which other communities live in vertical zones, the way a cliff face or a building facade accumulates different life at different heights.

The canopy is not high. These trees are not tall — the elevation and the wind history have kept them low and spreading. The canopy is maybe eight meters up, dense, interlocking. The branches are horizontal, reaching, trying to cover as much area as possible, which is what a tree does when light competition in a crowded space rewards spread over height. Lichens hang from them — old man's beard, long pale strands — in quantities that suggest decades of accumulation, moving slightly even in the still air, stirred by some convection from below.

Below the canopy: shrub layer, relentless. Bilberry in dense mats, crowberry, heather at the margins where the bowl edges toward open moorland. In the wetter sections, rushes growing in clumps so tight they are like bundles of arrows. Sedge. The particular stiff grass that grows at waterline. Meadowsweet, where it is wet enough, already past full flower, going to seed, smelling of almonds.

Below the shrubs: ground layer, below the ground layer: root networks, mycorrhizal mass, the whole system connected underground in ways that make the visible above-ground structure something like a display — the real work, the resource-sharing and chemical communication and symbiotic exchange, is happening in the dark beneath the moss.

---

The settlement is *in this*, not separate from it.

This is the thing that takes adjustment: what you took, on approach, for unusual tree formations are in some cases structures. What reads as a large mossy mound is a roof — or was a roof — or is equally a roof and a habitat, the distinction having been abandoned long ago. The buildings here are low, dug partially into the slope, roofed with living material, walled with timber that has been here long enough to grow its own moss and lichen colonies. The distinction between the building and the landscape is not sharp. It is a gradient.

The construction is serious in the sense of being effective — these structures stay up, stay dry, serve the purposes of shelter — but the technique is entirely manual. Hand-split timber, mortised and tenoned, pegged not nailed. Stone foundations, dry-laid, fitted by eye and experience. Thatched originally, the thatch now deeply colonized, so that the roof is partially grass, the grass hosting insects, the insects hosting the birds that perch there and observe. Nothing is plumb. Everything is sound.

There is a settlement pattern you can read if you look: a loose cluster of these structures around a slightly more open central area, perhaps thirty meters across, where light reaches the ground more directly. Paths between the structures, not paved but *worn* — the moss compressed and eliminated by foot traffic, the earth beneath packed hard. These paths are the capillaries of the place, connecting the structures to each other and to the water sources, to the woodpiles, to whatever external tracks lead up and out of the bowl.

Call it a hamlet in terms of scale. A hamlet's-worth of structures: a dozen, perhaps fifteen, some clearly residential, some clearly functional — storage, workshop, something that might be a common space with a low wide doorway, a hearthsmoke smell even now, in the cool of the afternoon, emerging from somewhere inside.

There are tools visible. Not many, and not metal-intensive: a wooden handled something leaning against a wall, iron-tipped perhaps but worn to a particular personal shape. Baskets. Woven material drying on a line strung between two trees, an activity that must be optimistic in this humidity, or perhaps the weaving itself incorporates moisture as a working property. Clay vessels, hand-built rather than wheel-thrown, the fingerprints of their making visible in the irregular rim lines.

---

The fauna is overwhelming.

This word needs to stand. *Overwhelming.* Not threatening — there is nothing here suggesting danger in ordinary terms — but the sheer number of living things moving and calling and feeding and mating and competing in this small wet space is beyond what seems sustainable, and yet clearly is sustained, has been sustained for a long time, the system running at this density in what appears to be stable overfullness.

The frogs are everywhere in the wet season sense: in every seep, at every pool margin, calling from the moss, caught mid-crossing of the paths. The paths require attention or you step on one. Green and brown and mottled, different species perhaps at different moisture levels, or the same species in different states. They are not disturbed by presence in any dramatic way. They watch. They occasionally move.

The insects are not enumerable. The air above the water seeps moves with them, midges in column-formations, larger things orbiting, dragonflies cutting through at surprising speed and stopping, hovering, continuing. Beetles in the bark layers, audible — a faint chewing, clicking quality to the silence if you press your ear to old wood. Bees working the meadowsweet, working the heather at the margins. Moths, in the daytime, resting in improbable numbers on the north face of structures where the light doesn't warm them.

The birds respond to all of this productivity. There is not a moment without birds. The wren nearest you right now is investigating a gap in the stone foundation three meters away, extracting something tiny and consuming it without ceremony, extracting another thing. A thrush is working the open area, stopping, cocking its head, extracting a worm from the packed earth of the path with the practiced efficiency of long experience. Above: something circling, higher up, using the updraft from the bowl's warmer interior air — a buzzard, patient, spiraling without apparent effort.

There are fish in the larger pools, just visible as slow shadows in the tannin-dark water. Amphibians other than frogs: a newt, seen once, moving through submerged moss with a lateral undulation that looked mechanical, programmed.

---

The turbulence of the place is not meteorological. There is no storm. The bowl shields from wind and the day is cool and still.

The turbulence is biological.

Every surface is actively contested. The fallen log near the water seep is simultaneously: decomposing fungal substrate, hunting ground for the thrush, nesting site for the moss, bridge over a small wet section of path, leaning-spot for something that has been leaned against long enough to leave a polished area, and slowly, incrementally, over years and decades, becoming indistinguishable from the ground. The log is *in process*. Everything here is in process.

The seeps move. Not quickly, but the water is finding new paths constantly — you can see, at the margins of the largest wet area, the evidence of recent channel shifts, fresh mud exposed at one edge, old channel drying at another. The moss advances into drier areas in wet years, retreats in drier ones. The trees are growing, slowly but continuously, and the growth changes the light and the light changes what grows below. One tree, clearly, recently (by tree-time) lost a major branch — the wound is fresh-looking in relative terms, the exposed wood not yet fully colonized, the change in the canopy above creating a light gap through which different plants are already accelerating.

The community within the settlement seems to be doing things continuously and in multiple directions. Smoke, the suggestion of voices — not intelligible at this distance but present, the sound of people working, of children perhaps, of something being moved or constructed or repaired. The place has the energy of constant occupation, of a settlement that does not rest because the environment around it doesn't rest, because if you stop maintaining the thatch the moss and the sedge and eventually the rowans will reclaim it, because the paths require walking to remain paths, because the baskets require weaving, the fires require feeding, the tools require the maintenance that hand tools require.

---

The cool is real and specific.

Even with the biological heat from below, even with the shelter of the bowl, there is a dampness in the air that carries cold efficiently. You feel it on the backs of your hands. The condensation is visible on every cool surface: the clay pots hold a film of moisture on their exteriors. The stones of the foundations, wherever they are shaded, weep slightly. A spider web between two branches is beaded with water droplets in such precise intervals it reads as made, decorated, intentional.

Your breath, if you slow down and look for it, is faintly visible.

The people here dress for this. Layered wool, close-woven against the damp, oiled perhaps, darkened at the hem from ground-contact in the constant wet. These are materials that work in rain, that stay warm when wet, that can be dried by a fire and used again tomorrow in the same conditions.

---

There is nothing strange here. That is the last thing to say, and it's important.

This place has a powerful atmosphere and yet it operates by entirely ordinary rules. The frogs are frogs. The moss is moss. The water is moving downhill. The people are making things with their hands that will be used and will wear out and will need to be made again. The trees are old and still growing. The fungi are doing what fungi do.

The intensity of the place — its density, its loudness, its turbulence, its saturation — comes entirely from natural processes at high expression, from an environment that has been running long enough and with enough material inputs to reach a kind of productive overabundance, and from a human community that has learned, over generations, to build inside rather than against that overabundance.

It feels, standing here, like a place that has found its own logic. Like everything that couldn't persist here has already been removed by the environment's own insistence, and what remains — the specific mosses, the specific frogs, the specific construction techniques, the specific people — remains because it fits. Because it works in these conditions.

The sound continues. The water finds its level. The wren extracts another thing from the gap in the wall and is briefly still, and then is not.
