# The Blister

It is a cavity inside rock — not large, perhaps four meters across at its widest, two and a half high at the crown. It formed recently. The walls still hold the memory of their own opening: fracture lines that haven't yet had time to round their edges, surfaces that break in clean conchoidal planes rather than the worn faces you'd find in an older space. The rock is basalt, or something close to basalt, dark and fine-grained, and in places it has vitrified — the heat that made this pocket was intense enough to leave patches of black glass on the ceiling, smooth as the inside of a bowl, still faintly lustrous.

The floor is uneven. Small rubble from the initial fracture sits where it fell, not yet redistributed by anything, not yet worn. Gravel in loose piles. A few larger fragments, angular, recently detached from the walls. Nothing has been here long enough to settle into arrangement.

It is very hot. The air temperature is somewhere above fifty degrees, possibly higher near the floor where warmth percolates up through the fractured substrate. You can feel it on the skin of your forearms before you've moved. The heat isn't dry — there is water here, just enough of it, seeping through microfractures in the upper walls where two geological strata meet imperfectly. The water doesn't flow. It weeps. It beads on the glass patches of the ceiling and falls in single drops, intermittently, into the gravel below. Each drop makes a small sound — a brief tick, absorbed almost immediately. The air carries this moisture without cooling from it; the two conditions coexist, wet and hot, the way a mouth is wet and hot.

Where the water collects in depressions in the floor it becomes something between puddle and film — a few centimeters deep at most, irregular in outline, already warm to the touch. Steam doesn't rise visibly, but the air above these pools is slightly different in quality, slightly thicker, slightly more reluctant to the eye.

---

The light is the first thing that asks questions.

There is no opening to a surface. The cavity is sealed — a blister in the rock, not a passage, not a cave with a mouth. Nothing from outside has found its way in. And yet the space is bright. Not dim-bright, not the orange glow of lava or the blue of bioluminescence — simply bright, the way an overcast day outdoors is bright, diffuse, coming from no single direction. The walls emit it. The basalt emits it.

This is not phosphorescence. Phosphorescence has a quality of effort, of something releasing stored charge slowly. This light is steady, flat, confident. It doesn't flicker. It doesn't respond to air movement. It doesn't seem to diminish toward the edges. The fracture lines in the walls are fully lit. The underside of a loose fragment propped on another loose fragment is fully lit. The light simply is, without apparent mechanism, a property of the rock in this location at this time.

The glass patches on the ceiling catch it and bounce it back modified — a slight shift toward the violet end of the spectrum, barely perceptible, but present. In the brightest part of the chamber, near the apex, there is a region where the light seems to concentrate not because anything is focusing it but because the rock composition changes there, a different crystal density perhaps, a different internal geometry, and the light responds to this the way current responds to resistance.

There are no shadows. Almost none. Objects cast faint indications of shadows — the rubble on the floor has ghost-shadows, approximate suggestions of shadow — but nothing like the strong shadows a light source with direction would produce. The space sees itself evenly.

---

The walls are mostly bare. This is important — not bare as absence but bare as the natural condition of a place that has not had time to become otherwise. No staining, no mineral bloom, no secondary depositions. The original rock. In a few places there are thin white lines where minerals in solution have migrated through the fractures and begun to crystallize along their edges — calcite possibly, or something analogous — but these lines are thin, preliminary. The geometry of early chemistry. Given time they would thicken, branch, develop into formations. They haven't had time.

Near the base of the north wall, in a low alcove where two fracture planes created a roughly triangular recess, there is something that might be a mat of grey-black material, roughly palm-sized, lying flat against the stone. It does not move. It has no apparent structure at the scale of the eye — no differentiated parts, no texture beyond a slight matte roughness. It could be a mineral deposit. It is probably not a mineral deposit. Whatever process governs it — and something governs it; it exists in a particular place for a particular reason, its edges are specific, it is not randomly distributed — that process is not fast, not visible, but present. Something is occurring at scales smaller than this place cares to narrate. It seems to require the water, the heat, or both. It has the quality of an experiment in early stages.

The stone near it has been slightly altered. Not visibly, exactly — more like the alteration registers on attention before it registers on the eye, the way you sometimes know a surface has been worked before you can see the marks.

---

The sound in the chamber is mostly the sound of the chamber not making sound.

Hot air has a texture. It is not silent — it carries the faint patterning of convection, of warm air meeting slightly cooler air, small turbulences, inaudible individually but collectively giving the atmosphere an almost-sound, a presence, a sense that the air is doing something rather than just being there. Beneath this, the intermittent drip from the ceiling into the floor puddles. Tick. Then a long silence. Then tick again. The interval is not regular — it changes with the slow variation in seep rate as water migrates through the fractures by differential pressure — but it isn't random either. It has the irregularity of a natural process rather than the irregularity of noise.

Once, while you are here, there is a small sound from the wall — a single crack, brief, like a knuckle popping. A thermal stress fracture extending itself by a millimeter or less. The rock continuing to adjust to whatever made this space. Then quiet again.

---

This place is not a destination. Nothing travels here. There are no corridors leading to it. The animal that occupies it — if the thing in the alcove is an animal, or a thing at all in the sense of being singular and bounded — did not arrive by walking. The question of how it came to be here is probably not a question about movement.

The cavity has been here for — not long. Geologically, almost nothing. Perhaps a year. Perhaps less. The fracture surfaces say: recent. The absence of secondary mineralization says: recent. The presence of the rubble exactly where it fell says: recent. It is a room that the earth made by accident, that it hasn't yet noticed it made, that it will eventually close again as pressures shift and the fractures heal or extend into full breaks. It is in between moments.

The water continues to weep through the upper wall. The temperature doesn't fluctuate. The light doesn't change. The thing in the alcove does nothing visible. The heat simply continues to be heat. The bright, directionless illumination continues to make everything equally visible and somehow therefore equally unknown.

It is enough to breathe in this place, if you could breathe here. It is enough to be a body in it and feel the heat on the body and hear the drip and the near-silence and see the walls lit from within, sharp-edged, new.
