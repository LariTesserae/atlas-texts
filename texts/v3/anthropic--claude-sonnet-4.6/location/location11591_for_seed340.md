# The Undercroft of First Growing

---

You descend before you realize you have been descending. The slope is gentle, a barely-perceptible gradient, but the ceiling — the ceiling is the first sign something has changed in the world above. It comes in from the sides rather than from above, the way cellar doors do, and then it is simply *there*, a continuous living vault pressed close and warm, and you understand: you are inside something now, not on its surface.

---

## What You Are Inside

The root-ceiling is perhaps eight meters overhead in the tallest chambers, three or four in the compression zones where the growing is most violent. It is not a ceiling of dead timber or carved stone. It is a ceiling of *becoming* — of roots so thick they have fused into plates, root-slabs the size of barn doors, pale cream and arterial orange and the yellow of old bone, threaded through with finer networks that pulse visibly if you watch for long enough, a systolic shimmer that takes about four seconds between beats. Water does not drip here. Nothing drips. The roots have taken the moisture from somewhere distant and held it utterly. The air carries no humidity you can detect. Your lips begin to feel tight within minutes.

The floor — and the walls, and really everything — is also root. Here below, the distinction between floor and wall is more administrative than architectural. The surfaces are rounded, continuous, merging one into the next like the interior of some enormous intestine made of vegetable matter. Your feet press into layers: the topmost layer is fine root-hair, the texture of thick velvet, cream-white, replacing itself constantly — you can see where you walked three minutes ago, the crushed root-hair already standing back upright, already being supplemented by new growth from below. Beneath that: packed root-cord, the diameter of your smallest finger, woven in no pattern a human hand would call intentional but in a pattern that holds weight perfectly, that does not give when you press it. Beneath that: the thick structural roots, each as wide as your body, some wider, running in every direction. The ground is not flat. It rises and falls in long organic swells, root-hills and root-valleys, everything continuous, nothing sharp.

You have not seen a single stone. You are not sure stone exists here yet.

---

## The Newness of It

Something strange presses against your understanding when you try to take in the scale: this place is *new*. Not old, not ancient, not the product of geological patience. New in the way a wound is new. The roots at the advancing edges — and there are advancing edges, the place is expanding, it has directions — the roots at the far margins are still pale, still thin, still finding their way into substrate that is not yet sure what it is. You can see, in the middle distance, zones where the root-network has not yet closed the ceiling — where gaps in the vault show dark soil, where single pioneer roots angle downward like the legs of some vast slow creature taking a cautious step. The soil those pioneer roots enter looks young. It is not old loam. It is something granular, something the color of raw concrete, something that hasn't fully decided to be soil yet. It crumbles in odd ways. It has no earthworm. It has no smell of deep time. It is substrate without history, and the plants are writing history into it in real time.

Nearby, close enough to watch: a new fusion. Two roots, each as thick as your forearm, have grown to within a centimeter of each other. Even as you stand there, one of them bows, almost imperceptibly, toward the other. This is not fast. It is also not slow. The movement is at the absolute threshold of human perception — you cannot quite *see* it move, but if you look away for thirty seconds and look back, the gap has reduced. Something is being decided. In an hour, probably less, they will touch. When they touch, they will merge. That is how the floor is made. That is how the ceiling is made. This entire architecture is the record of a billion such decisions.

The place is not finished. It will not be finished for reasons that go beyond mere time.

---

## What Lives Here Beside the Plants

The fauna is, categorically, too much.

Not dangerous, exactly — or not *only* dangerous. More that it is too *present*, too varied, too actively doing things, too numerous, too in motion, too loud. The animals here exist at a density that seems to violate some agreement you thought nature had made with itself. Every surface moves. The root-velvet of the floor ripples not only from its own growth but from the bodies beneath it — creatures that travel through the root-hair layer the way fish travel through water weeds, visible as moving bulges, sometimes a nose, sometimes a claw, breaking surface for a moment and submerging again. These are small. Call them voles if that helps, though they have no eyes that you can see and their coloring is the exact cream-white of the roots they live inside, and they are numerous beyond counting. Beneath them, in the structural root-lattice, larger things move in the darkness between root-slabs — you see them in silhouette when they cross a bioluminescent patch. They are leggy. They are patient. You cannot tell if they are hunting the vole-things or tending them.

Above: the vault is never still. Things hang from the root-ceiling in configurations that keep shifting. There are insects — you want to call them insects — but in groupings that seem coordinated, that seem to perform a collective behavior rather than individual behaviors happening to coincide. A patch of perhaps ten thousand small pale-winged things is currently orbiting a section of ceiling root in a slow clockwise spiral, and the root they orbit is being stripped of its outer layer in a thin even ring. You cannot tell if they are feeding or building or communicating or all three simultaneously. Every so often, a portion of the swarm peels away and descends to deliver something to a different portion of the swarm near the floor, and this transaction happens in a fraction of a second, an explosion of wings, and then both groups reabsorb into their respective configurations as though nothing occurred.

There are larger things in the air. Larger than birds, moving with the casual ease of creatures that have no predators. They do not flap. They undulate, somehow, lateral body-waves, though their bodies are not fish-shaped. They trail behind them a substance — a secretion, a silk, something — that catches the light and hangs in the warm air long after they have passed. This substance has, apparently, purpose: the root ceiling is threaded with it, the earliest traces of some form of material that is neither plant nor mineral but something being invented here, now, by creatures that themselves seem to be in the first generation of their form.

That is the strange thing you keep returning to: the animals also look *new*. Not young — not larval or juvenile. New in the sense of recently-speciated, recently-decided-upon, recently *made* in whatever process is making this place. A beetle you pick up is the shape of a first idea of a beetle. Its carapace is uniform; it hasn't worked out the finer details. Its legs all move with equal confidence. It lacks whatever small asymmetries a species accumulates over millennia of solving specific problems. It hasn't failed yet. It will.

## Sound

Stand still for one moment and let the sound in, and here is what arrives:

First, the substrate. The roots themselves produce sound. Not one sound — a *range* of sounds, a continent of sounds. The growing produces a sound that falls below what you'd call a creak but above what you'd call silence: a deep, continuous, biomechanical murmur, like a city heard from the distance where individual voices cease to resolve. This comes from everywhere equally, from the floor through your feet, from the vault overhead, from the walls to every side. It is not threatening. It is simply *present*, simply the sound of a system that does not rest.

Above that: the insects. Their wings produce frequencies that cancel and reinforce each other in complex patterns — the spiraling swarms act as a kind of deranged musical instrument, and the tone shifts as the spiral shifts, as the density shifts, as new individuals join and others depart. This tone is high, is piercing, is not quite painful but occupies the frequency where sound becomes difficult to ignore.

Above that: the larger fauna. They produce vocalizations that do not sound like animal calls — they sound more like argument, like negotiation, like something being worked out in real time. Not melodic, not territorial. Deliberative. There is no silence between these sounds because there is no moment when none of these creatures has something to say.

Above all of it: the catastrophic sound of growth. You don't expect growth to be loud. Here it is. When two roots complete a fusion — the moment of contact and merge — there is a sound like a heavy book dropped on a stone floor, a single deep percussive event. This happens constantly. Somewhere in the middle distance, another fusion. And another. The fusions are happening at roughly the rate of three or four per second across the whole system within earshot. The percussive events layer and roll into a sound like continuous distant thunder, like a storm that is always breaking just over the next root-hill. You cannot find a position where you cannot hear this.

Between the thunder-rhythm of the fusions, the insect-tone, the deliberative animal-voices, and the background murmur of growth, the total sound of this place sits at a level where you feel it as much as hear it, where you have to focus to form a thought, where words spoken aloud to a companion standing two meters away would need to be half-shouted to land clearly.

## What Little Humans Have Left Here

Occasionally, rarely, you find evidence of human passage without finding a human.

A stake driven into a root junction, its top carved flat, something scratched into the flat surface. The scratches look like they were made with stone — not metal, not a blade precise enough to be metal. A groove made by repetition rather than by sharpness, worn into the surface by going over and over the same mark. The marks are regular but not alphabetic. A record, possibly. A claim, possibly. You cannot interpret them, and they are already being incorporated: the root on whose junction the stake rests is growing over the stake's base, and given the rate of growth here, the stake will be fully enclosed — swallowed, sealed, entombed — within the month.

Elsewhere: a structure woven from the living roots themselves. Someone spent time bending young roots, training them into a low dome perhaps two meters across and one high. It is impossible to say when this was done because the roots have continued growing in the directions they were bent, have continued thickening in those directions, and the structure is no longer exactly what it was when made. It is more now than was intended. The builder is not here. Whether they were driven out by the sound and the density and the heat, or whether they simply moved on, or whether the place consumed them as it will consume the stake, you cannot say.

Inside the dome: a sleeping arrangement of compressed root-hair, heavily compressed, the shape of a body, the scale of a human. A bowl made of what might be resin — tree sap, worked while liquid into shape, then hardened. Primitive but not careless; the lip is even. The bowl is empty. Whatever it contained, whatever was drunk from it or stored in it, is gone. Something small is living in the bowl now, a pale leggy creature that fits easily in the bowl's curve and does not move when you lean close.

Here is the irreducible strangeness of the dome: everything in it is being slowly incorporated. The root-hair sleeping arrangement is being supplemented from below, the roots below it producing new root-hair into the compressed old, trying to restore volume to what was pressed flat. The bowl is being grown around. The dome itself is becoming something other than the builder planned. The plants are not indifferent to the human artifacts. They are *responsive* to them, in the way that growth is responsive to any object — finding edges, growing to and against and around and eventually, in this fast-growing place, over.

## Temperature and Air

Warm, and not only warm: insistent. The heat is the heat of biological process, of fermentation almost, of a million systems converting chemistry into structure into motion into sound. It comes from the roots themselves — the root-slabs overhead are warm to the touch, not body-temperature warm but noticeably, unmistakably warm, like touching something with blood behind it. The air is dry, as noted, tight in the throat, carrying the smell of the roots' chemistry — a smell that sits just outside familiar categories. Not unpleasant. Not sweet. Faintly resinous. Faintly something you might call iron if iron were a vegetable thing. An alive smell. The smell of processes.

The light comes from bioluminescence, patchily, distributed through the root network in patterns that don't repeat — some sections of root-slab glow faint green, some amber, some a blue so pale it looks white. This is supplemented, occasionally, by the secretion-silk of the undulating aerial fauna, which phosphoresces faintly and hangs in air long enough to cast a slow moving light on the zones it drifts through. Together these produce illumination sufficient to see by. Not comfortably — shadows are long and inconsistent and the light sources shift — but the place is not dark. It has, instead, a quality of interior firelight without flame, warm and various and unreliable.

---

## The Feeling of It

There is a feeling beneath the sound and the heat and the press of life and the strangeness of the new-made animals. A feeling like being at the beginning of something so large that you cannot see its edge, like standing at a sentence's first word. This place has not happened yet. It is happening. The roots are working something out. The animals are working something out. The entombed stake is being worked into the record. The crushed root-hair stands back upright behind your footsteps, erasing you.

Stand still long enough and the growth begins to register against you — not threateningly, not fast, nothing like a horror. Just: something touching your boot from below. Just: a thin pale root finding the stillness of your weight and interpreting it as a feature of the landscape.

You are warmly and comprehensively not the point.

Something older than intent is doing something it has not finished planning, and you are in the room where it is happening, and every surface is moving, and the sound is the sound of a world being written in a language of pressure and chemistry and time, and you are, for now, warm, and able to breathe, and unharmed, and here.
