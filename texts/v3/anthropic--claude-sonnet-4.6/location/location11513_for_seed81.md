# The Caldera City

---

Begin with heat.

Not the heat of a summer afternoon or a kitchen oven — this is geological heat, the kind that has a pulse. You feel it before you see anything. It comes up through the soles of whatever you're wearing, through the platform, through the iron, through everything. The ground here is not indifferent to you. It is aware of your feet in the way a sleeping animal is aware of weight.

Now look.

---

The caldera is wide. Wider than seems right for the elevation, for where you are — which is already high, already above where most things live, the air thinner and drier at the throat but wet, wet, *wet* against the skin. This is the contradiction the place has settled into over a long time: altitude that should be arid, and moisture that refuses to leave. The rim of the old volcano stands in a rough broken circle at the horizon's edge, maybe eight kilometers out in the clearest direction, less in others where the haze thickens. Within that rim, everything has been happening for a very long time.

The caldera floor is not flat. It was never flat. Decades — more than decades, maybe a century of construction — have built up from the original collapse topography, and the result is a city that doesn't sit *on* the land so much as it has become continuous with it. The buildings are real. They are industrial, they are organized, they have a civic grammar — there are districts, there are arterials, there is infrastructure that someone laid out with intention. But whatever plan existed has been revised by the heat, by the sulfurous springs, by the slowly shifting ground. The city has adapted. The city has *learned*.

---

The structures are made mostly of something between ceramic and cast iron. The two materials have been used so close together for so long, in such a thermally active environment, that the boundary between them has blurred in the older districts. Walls have a quality of having been slightly melted and reconsidered. The color is dark — iron-red, deep charcoal, a particular matte black that comes from basalt aggregate mixed into the casting medium. Some walls have been sealed with a glaze that weeps. This is the water.

The moisture comes from below as much as above. Geothermal steam condenses on every cold surface, and there are no cold surfaces, exactly, but there are *cooler* surfaces — the higher walls, the northern faces, the iron conduit pipes that run along the street-level colonnades like the city's circulatory system exposed. On these surfaces, water collects. It runs in thin sheets. It drips from the undersides of elevated walkways. Where it pools in low stone channels it picks up a faint mineral iridescence — calcium, sulfur, whatever the earth is currently exhaling — and the puddles are not quite the color of ordinary water. They are slightly opalescent, slightly wrong, the way a puddle looks when there's an oil film, except the film here is geological.

The air itself is a medium. Not fog exactly — the temperature is too high for comfortable fog — but a haze, a hot wet particulate haze that you breathe and feel on your tongue. Sulfur is present, but low. The city has been managing this for a long time. There are vents, there are filtration stacks, there are old engineering compromises that mostly work. The sulfur is background music. It sharpens the air without making it dangerous. It tells you where you are.

---

The city is almost empty.

This is the strange thing, the thing you don't expect once you understand the scale of the place. The infrastructure is not ruined. The construction is not abandoned in the usual sense. The streets — stone-paved, carefully graded to channel water toward the central collection cisterns — are in reasonable repair. The gas lamps are lit. There are gas lamps: iron-hooded, mounted on columns at regular intervals, burning with a blue-white flame fed from the same underground sources as the heat, the same vents and pressures that the city was built to exploit. They are lit even now, in the middle of whatever time of day this is, because the haze and the height of the surrounding rim mean that the light inside the caldera is always dim, always this particular quality of dim — not dark, not shadowed exactly, but *filtered*, as though the city exists perpetually in the last hour before a storm, or the first hour after one.

The lamps are lit. The infrastructure runs. The city breathes.

But there is almost no one here.

---

Walk a district. The street is perhaps twelve meters wide at its broadest — wide enough for industrial traffic, for the flatbed wagons that would carry equipment between the processing facilities that ring the caldera's inner edge. The wagons are absent. The processing facilities are running at what might be called maintenance capacity — minimum operation, enough to keep the mechanisms from seizing, enough to keep the heat from building to dangerous levels in the boiler rooms that haven't been fully shut down. You hear this. The sound of the city, such as it is, is mechanical and low: the sustained bass note of pressure in pipes, the irregular rhythm of a condensation pump cycling somewhere in a sub-street chamber, the distant *thrum* of something rotating — a flywheel? a turbine? — running with no particular purpose except that stopping it would require a person to go stop it, and no one has.

The buildings are large. This is a city built at industrial scale, built to house industrial processes first and people second, and the human spaces within it — the windows at upper floors that suggest dwelling, the archways at ground level that suggest commerce — are modest relative to the bulk of the structures around them. A factory building with a footprint of four city blocks will have, at its top floor, a row of small windows behind which people presumably once slept. The proportions suggest a place where people were important but not primary. Where the machines were the real inhabitants, and people tended them.

---

Between buildings: space. Real space. More space than the building density would suggest from the outside, looking in. This is because the streets are wide and the intersections are wider still — there were plazas here, loading areas, points where the arterial gas lines branch and the pressure management infrastructure surfaces as a cluster of valves and meters, each one a kind of monument. These open areas are not comfortable spaces. They catch the heat and return it upward. The paving stones in the center of the larger plazas have cracked from thermal cycling and been repaired, cracked and been repaired, until the repair pattern is as intricate as anything planned — a web of iron fill in the stone, a record of contractions and expansions, every winter cold (less cold than it should be, even at this elevation, but still: the differential is there) and every summer heat spike marked permanently in the ground.

Stand in one of these plazas and the emptiness is almost physical. The open volume of hot damp air above you. The surrounding walls of ceramic-iron, the dim lamplit facades with their dark windows, the sounds from underground or far away. You are a small animal in a space built for something else, and the space doesn't notice you particularly.

---

Now: the rare life.

There are things moving. Not many. Not reliably. But the city is not quite sterile.

In the wet crevices where walls meet paving, where water runs consistently enough to maintain a micro-habitat, something grows that is not precisely algae and not precisely biofilm and perhaps predates the more complex word *biology*. It is orange-red, the color of rust or of certain bacterial mats in hot springs, and it lines the channels the way moss lines old stone in wetter climates, but it is not moss, it has no structure above the mat level, it is chemistry that is almost life, life that is mostly chemistry. It has been here longer than the city. It was here before anyone built anything. The city was built on top of it, and the city's water management has, in some places accidentally and perhaps in some places deliberately, given it more surface area to colonize.

The mat is the city's oldest resident. It doesn't do much. It metabolizes. It produces a faint sheen on the water surfaces near it. It is not interesting in any way that the word *interesting* usually implies and yet it is entirely impossible to ignore once you have seen it, because it is proof that something persists here. That even in the heat and the mineral load and the near-absence of anything we comfortably call soil or nutrition, metabolism finds a way to continue.

There is also, occasionally, something with wings.

Not often. Once in a long while, you might hear — the sound is not quite possible to locate, it comes and goes — the sound of something moving through the haze above the roofline level. The wings are leathery, maybe. The creature does not land where you can see it. It is adapted to something in this environment, some resource that the city produces or that the caldera provides, but it has learned to avoid the ground level, or perhaps there is simply nothing for it at ground level. It lives above. It is warm-blooded, possibly, or operates on some other thermoregulatory logic. It feeds on something. These are hypotheses. The creature does not confirm them.

---

There are also people. Somewhere.

The evidence is accumulative: the lamps are lit, meaning someone lights them, or lit them once and maintains the mechanism by which they light themselves. The cisterns are kept clear of the mineral scale that would otherwise occlude them. The main pressure manifolds have been adjusted recently — you can tell because the fittings are slightly different from the ones beside them, newer castings, the tolerances tighter. Someone has been here. Someone is here, probably, somewhere in the eight or twelve or fifteen square kilometers of built environment.

You do not see them.

Whether they do not wish to be seen, or whether the city is simply so large and they are simply so few, amounts to the same experience on the ground. You are alone in this city. The city is alone with itself. Whatever it was built for, it has outlasted that purpose and found another, quieter one — the purpose of simply continuing, of maintaining pressure and heat and the movement of hot water through its pipes, of burning gas at its lamp-heads in the dim hot haze, of letting the orange mat spread another millimeter into a new crack.

---

And the caldera wall.

You keep coming back to the caldera wall. The rim. It defines the world from inside — from any point in the city, you can see some portion of it, the raised broken skyline of old igneous rock, wind-worked over long time into something smoother than it once was but not smooth. It is the color of dark iron in some lights and the color of old blood in others. It holds the haze in. It holds the heat in. It makes the sky above the city a rough circle — blue when there is blue, gray when there is gray — and the haze beneath that circle is the city's atmosphere, distinct from the atmosphere outside the rim, genuinely different in temperature and composition.

Inside the caldera, this haze. Outside: thinner air, actual weather moving through, the mountain world.

Inside: this. This specific thing, this balance that has been established and that runs, on its own considerable momentum, possibly indefinitely.

The sound of the pressure pump cycling. The lamp flame too small to hear but visible, blue-white, moving slightly because something in the air is always moving slightly. The water on the wall running down in its thin sheets, iridescent, tracing the same paths it has traced since before anyone thought to observe them. The orange mat, chemically patient. The thing in the air above the roofline, once in a long time.

The heat, continuous, geological, authoritative.

The emptiness — not an absence of what was here, but a presence of a different kind of here. The city as a thing that has arrived at its own nature. Dense with infrastructure, hollow of traffic. Built at scale, inhabited at a whisper. Hot and wet and old and running, running, running on its own slow logic, the flywheel spinning, the gas burning, the water finding its level in all the thousand channels engineered for it, and the city, in its great dim organized stillness, being itself.
