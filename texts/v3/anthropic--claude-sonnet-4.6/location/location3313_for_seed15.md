# The Cistern

---

The water is warm.

That is the first thing — not warm the way a bath is warm, not warm the way you might expect from a sealed underground room where heat has nowhere to go, but warm in a way that feels intentional, almost parentally so. The kind of warmth that makes you stop wondering whether you are safe and simply accept that you are. It sits at perhaps thirty-two degrees, maybe thirty-three, and it does not vary by more than half a degree from one end of the chamber to the other, which is a remarkable fact about a body of water this size, though you would only recognize it as remarkable if you had a thermometer, and you do not.

The place is small. That matters. It is not a cavern, not an underground lake, not one of those vast flooded grottos with cathedral ceilings lost in darkness. It is a room. A made room. Perhaps eight meters across at its widest point, though the shape is irregular — more ovoid than circular, the walls bowing outward in some places and pressing inward in others, following no strict geometry but following *something*, some logic of craft that was worked out incrementally by hands rather than calculated in advance. The ceiling is low: you could stand on the bottom, if the bottom were accessible, and reach up and touch it. The ceiling slopes, and there is one end where it dips close enough to the water's surface that only a narrow band of airspace remains, a breathing gap of perhaps thirty centimeters between water and stone.

The walls are cut stone, but cut is almost too precise a word. Worked stone. Dressed stone. Each block was shaped by hand tools — you can see this in the marks left behind, the small parallel striations where a chisel was driven at a consistent angle, the occasional asymmetric gouge where a tool slipped or the stone proved harder than expected. The blocks fit together with a tightness that was clearly labored over, that was the result of setting and resetting, of grinding surfaces against each other until they seated properly, of packing the joints with a pale mortar that has not yet fully cured, that still shows, in places, a slight tackiness. The room is new. The room was made recently. The mortar is pale because it has not had time to darken. The edges of the stone blocks are still sharp where they meet, have not been worn smooth by water and time. Everything about the fabric of this place speaks to recent completion, to a construction project that has just — perhaps days ago, perhaps a week — been declared finished and filled.

The floor cannot be seen. That is the water: the water fills the room from floor to ceiling except for that breathing gap at one end, except for the narrow shelf at that same end where the floor rises slightly above the waterline and there is a small landing of perhaps two square meters. The floor, as best as you can intuit it, is also cut stone, also mortared, slopes very slightly toward a central drain whose iron grate is visible just below the surface at the shallow end, casting a small grilled shadow on the pale stone beneath it. The drain does not seem to be draining. The water level is stable.

Because the room is full of water, you hear the water constantly. This is the loudness of the place, and it takes time to understand, because at first you expect a flooded room to be quiet — water as damping, water as silence. But this water is active. There is an inlet, somewhere in the wall near the ceiling at the far end, a pipe or channel through which new water enters, and it enters with force, with a continuous rushing sound that echoes against the stone walls and the stone ceiling and comes back to you changed, multiplied, layered over itself. The echo is not long — the room is too small for a true echo, what you get is a very fast repetition, a kind of thickening of the sound — and the result is a noise that fills the room completely, that has no silences threaded through it, that is simply always present, always the same, always *loud*. The rushing of new water into the chamber. And then the secondary sounds it produces: the slap of water against the walls near the inlet, the lower churn as currents move through the body of the room, the occasional small surge that sets ripples crossing the surface toward the landing where you stand, and the sound those ripples make when they reach the wall behind you, a small rhythmic lapping that is quieter than everything else but somehow more distinct, more countable.

The light.

There is a great deal of light, which is the other thing you would not expect, and its source takes a moment to identify. Torches, but not — not standing torches, not sconces. Rushlight holders, yes, but the holders are submerged, built into the walls below the waterline, and the light they hold is sealed behind thick glass panels set flush with the stone. Someone engineered this. Someone solved the problem of putting light underwater with glass and some kind of sealant — pitch, perhaps, around the edges of the frames — and the lights in the walls glow from within the water itself, diffused by the water, scattered by the water, so that the light in the room is not directional. It comes from everywhere. The water conducts it, redistributes it, so that there are no shadows. The warm glow of rush-flame, yes, with all its yellow and amber tones, but spread through the mass of water and returned to you as something softer than flame, something that does not flicker at the surface because the surface cannot see the individual lights clearly, can only see the aggregate. The ceiling is illuminated. The far wall is illuminated. Your own hands are illuminated when you hold them up.

The water is slightly not-quite-clear. Not dirty. Not turbid. But there is something in it that gives it a faint mineral quality, a very slight opacity, so that objects at depth are slightly softened at their edges, slightly hazed. The stone blocks of the far wall, three or four meters across the water, are visible but not perfectly sharp. The iron grate over the drain is visible but its details resolve only when you look carefully. This is not a flaw in the water — it has that quality of something intended, of a material property that was understood and perhaps worked around and perhaps accepted.

In the water: very little. This is what the sparseness means in this room. There is no growth on the walls — the room is too new, the stone has not had time to develop anything. The water does not contain fish. It does not contain any biological presence that you can see. What it contains is the water itself, the light moving through the water, and in one corner a coil of rope tied to an iron ring set into the wall, presumably for lowering things or steadying persons who need to enter the water for maintenance. The rope is pale and fibrous, has absorbed some water, hangs half-submerged in a loose coil. There is also, resting on the landing beside where you stand, a wooden-handled tool — a kind of long-handled scraper, for cleaning the walls below the waterline — that has been leaned against the stone and left there. Both objects feel functional. Both objects feel like they were set down by someone who intended to return.

No one returns while you are here.

On the landing, there are no other living things. No insects in this sealed room. No moss in the joints — the mortar is too new. There are, along the back wall above the waterline, three plant cuttings that someone has pressed into a crevice between blocks, stem-ends wedged into the mortar gap, leaves hanging forward. They are alive but just barely — cut plants, slowly drying, though slowly because the air in here is humid from the water. Some herb, perhaps. Flat leaves, pale green. They may have been placed there as some gesture, ritual or practical, or simply because someone could not bring themselves to throw them away. They will be dead in a few more days. They are not thriving. They are present.

The activity of the room is hydraulic. Constantly hydraulic. The water enters. The water circulates. The water is warm and seeks to remain warm, and through the walls there is the very faint transmission of outside temperature, the slight differential that drives a slow convection through the room — warm water rising at the center, cooler water sinking at the walls, a pattern that is invisible but that you can feel if you hold your hand still in one place for long enough. The surface of the water is not glassy. It is slightly, constantly in motion: the inlet current, the convection, the small surges and reflections. It catches the light from below and returns it to the ceiling in shifting patterns, soft caustic lines that move slowly across the dressed stone above you, the only movement in the room that is visible from the landing, the ceiling's slow light-writing that never repeats exactly.

The mortar smell is present but faint — that alkaline, slightly dusty smell that has been overlaid almost entirely by the smell of water, the smell of wet stone, the faint mineral character of whatever the water carries. It is not unpleasant. It smells clean in the way that stone and water are clean. It smells like something that has been made for a purpose and is now fulfilling that purpose.

This is a cistern. This is a water-storage chamber, built under some building or plaza above, built recently by workers with chisels and mortar and patience, filled now and running, maintaining itself, loud with its own incoming supply, bright with its submerged lights, warm as a kept room, containing almost nothing except what it was made to contain. It is functional. It is, within its function, complete.

You stand on the small landing and the warm mist settles on your face and the ceiling moves with light.
