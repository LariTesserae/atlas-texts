# The Undermaze of Pale Chalk

---

Below the surface — not by much, as these things go. The ceiling is thirty feet up in most places, sometimes sixty, occasionally pressing down to graze the tops of the highest structures at twelve. It is not a cave in the conventional sense. The word *cave* implies enclosure, narrowness, the drama of constriction. This is something older than that word and more patient. This is a place where the rock above has simply stayed, neither risen nor fallen, for so long that staying has become its fundamental character. The ceiling is chalk. Pale, almost cream, almost the color of old teeth. Smooth in the way that only millions of years of nothing-happening can make a surface smooth — not polished, not worn, but *settled*, the way a face settles in very old age into a final arrangement it no longer has the energy to change. 

Here and there the chalk ceiling weeps.

Not dramatically. A trickle. A bead of water gathering at a rougher patch and releasing itself every forty seconds or so in a drop that falls and vanishes into the activity below. Other spots are merely damp, dark patches of moisture that don't quite drip but breathe their humidity into the air below. The atmosphere carries that moisture: you feel it on the backs of your hands before you understand that you feel it, a coolness that isn't quite cold, the temperature of a stone cellar kept slightly damp, the temperature of the inside of a cloud that has lost most of its ambition. Perhaps ten degrees above what you'd call properly cold. The kind of cool that makes exertion comfortable and stillness slightly uncomfortable.

Light comes through the chalk.

This is the first unusual thing.

Not through holes — through the chalk itself. The rock has thinned over these unmeasurable ages to a translucency that passes the sun's full force above as a diffuse, pervasive, directionless brightness. There are no shadows because there is no clear source. Everything is illuminated from above, from all points above equally, in the way that a sheet of paper held to a window glows. The chalk breathes light. It is not blue-white or yellow-white but a specific warm cream-white that seems related to the chalk's own color, as though the stone is generating light from itself, fermenting it slowly out of its own ancient substance. You can read easily. You can see to the far walls without effort. The visual environment is not dramatic, not spectacular, but it is *clear* — clear in the way that overcast days are sometimes clearer than sunny ones, every surface revealed without the violence of direct angle. Nothing hides. Everything is softly obvious.

---

The space is large. Not infinite-feeling — the far walls are visible — but regional in scale, the way a city district is regional: you could walk for twenty minutes and still be within it. The floor is chalk too, but where the ceiling stayed smooth and still, the floor has been worked, consumed, rebuilt, worked again across so many generations of habitation that the original rock surface is perhaps six feet below what your feet actually touch. What you walk on is a stratum of construction, of layered history, of everything that was built and fell and was built on top of. The archaeology of the floor, if anyone cut a cross-section, would read like a dense text in a language that shifted form every century: pressed clay, crushed chalk-rubble mixed with animal fat and hair, more clay, a period of flagstone laid in irregular courses, more chalk-fill, the remains of a fired-brick layer that didn't last, chalk again, and finally the present surface, which is the floor as it currently is — uneven, pale, scuffed and worn in tracks where movement concentrates, scattered with the debris of daily use.

Because there is daily use. Constant use. Turbulent use.

---

The city — it is a city, or has become one over durations too long to have been planned — does not look like a city from above, which is the only way you might see it clearly given the brightness and the openness of the space. It looks from above like an anthill rendered in pale materials by a craftsman who knew the anthill but had never seen a clean line.

The structures are chalk-block and chalk-brick and chalk-daub, almost all of them, occasionally with timber salvaged from wherever timber can be gotten — and in this below-world, timber is rare enough to become structural treasure, used in load-bearing roles and then used again as the structures are rebuilt around and above it. The buildings are not tall: two stories at most, often one, sometimes merely walls defining an activity-space open to the ceiling. They are packed together in the center of the space and scatter toward the edges where the density thins and single structures sit with gaps between them and then there is just floor and wall and the strange intimacy of the rock.

The architecture has the organic quality of things built without drawings, where each new addition responded to the previous one and accumulated error over centuries until the whole is a labyrinth of accretions that somehow works. Walls don't meet at right angles because no one was enforcing right angles. Streets — the gaps between buildings — are body-width in places and then open suddenly into a small square. Everything has been rebuilt so many times that the style is illegible: there is no style, only the result of hand-work repeated until the hands' preferences become the place's preferences. Door frames are rounded because hands rounding door frames is easier than cutting a clean corner in chalk. Thresholds are worn to curved depressions. Rooflines are varied and accidental.

What everyone uses for tools is visible in the forms: stone tools, primarily. Bone tools. Antler, where it can be obtained. There is no metal that isn't traded-in from somewhere else and thus too valuable to use freely. The marks of stone-cutting are everywhere in the chalk structures — a particular kind of roughness, a chipping-texture rather than a sawing-texture, the mark of percussion work and patient scraping. Some walls have been smoothed by rubbing, by hands over years, by the application of finer stone to coarser, by the paste of wet chalk rubbed into dry chalk until a surface emerges that approaches finish. The city is built by hand in the most direct sense: by unmediated human contact between body and stone, over and over, for a very long time.

---

And yet it pulses.

The quiet is what registers first, wrongly, as stillness. The sound environment is genuinely reduced: the chalk walls and chalk ceiling absorb instead of reflecting, and the absence of wind in this underground space means that the normal scattering of ambient noise doesn't accumulate. You have to listen specifically to hear things. But what there is to hear, if you listen specifically, is *motion*. Constant motion. Feet on chalk-floor, many feet, creating a soft percussion that is always present below everything else. The movement of bodies through narrow passages. The knocking of objects. Somewhere, rhythmic stone-on-stone: someone working. Somewhere else, a sound that might be water running along a cut channel, directed, used. Animal sounds — and there are animals — pressed to their quieter registrations: the scratch and scrabble of something small in a wall cavity, the sound of breath from larger animals in their pens.

The fauna of this place are diverse in an evolved-relationship way, the way that human settlement attracts and selects for everything that can use it. There are the small pale things: insects adapted to the chalk-filtered light, pale brown or cream-colored themselves, moving in their purposeful ways through every crack and crevice in the construction. Beetles of several kinds. Crickets that don't sing, or sing below audible range, or have lost the habit in a place where quiet is the norm. There are rodents — chalk-colored coats on most, after however many generations of selection in this pale bright world — moving in the walls and beneath the floor-layers, emerging when emergence is advantageous and retreating when it isn't. There are larger animals that are here because people keep them: something goat-like, but adapted and small, that can work through small spaces and find the pale fungus that grows in the wetter patches near the dripping ceiling. Something avian, domesticated, that perches on rooflines and watches the lit chalk ceiling with an attention that suggests it understands the light but not the ceiling. There are the things people eat. There are the things that eat what people leave.

And the fungal growth: this is the only biology that qualifies as wild, as non-managed, as existing for its own purposes without human intention. It is not extensive — the parameters of this place are not generous to it — but it persists in the moist zones, the ceiling-drip zones, growing flat against chalk in formations like shields or hands or pressed flowers, the color of old cream and dusty amber. It is not dramatic. It is there.

---

The turbulence is not in the landscape, which is ancient-stable and has no further erosion left to offer. The turbulence is in the city's interior life, its daily metabolic rate.

Something about this place — the enclosed but spacious, the bright but underground, the cool and damp and old — has selected for people who move. Who move *a great deal*. Who conduct their affairs in a state of constant physical transaction. The open squares, when you find them, are markets in continuous session: not markets that open and close but markets that are permanent states of the plaza, negotiations that have been ongoing so long that specific trades are simply episodes in a long conversation. Goods change hands quickly here because goods *have* to change hands — the economy of this underground city requires circulation the way the body requires circulation, and stillness is pathological. There is no sense of leisure in the movement, but also no sense of panic: it is the movement of an anthill, purposeful and continuous, where the purpose of each individual is legible and the purpose of the aggregate is not legible but is clearly being served.

The turbulence expresses itself in small ways: a dispute that flares in a narrow passage, voices raised in the absorbed-by-chalk way that makes even raising-voices somehow muffled, resolved with gesture and the exchange of something, continuing. The ongoing reorganization of materials through the streets as things move toward where they are needed. Children who run because children run, adding their erratic vectors to the flow of adults. Someone repairing a wall while people move around them, the work happening in the stream of traffic, not pausing it. The continual sound of stone working stone from somewhere, always from somewhere, the city always in the process of rebuilding itself at the margins.

---

The walls of the space — the actual cave-walls, the chalk edges — are old in a different way than old usually presents. They have not been much worked; there is no incentive to expand into the rock when the floor-space is sufficient. Instead they have accumulated: not construction, but attachment. Things are leaned against them, stored against them, in some cases built against them where a wall's face is flat enough to use as one side of a structure. In the moister zones near the walls, where the chalk breathes the most water, the walls are slick and the fungus makes its most ambitious growth. Some walls bear markings — not decoration, exactly, but the accumulation of marks that have been made over long time for many reasons: a scratch to mark a measurement, kept and added to; a sign that once meant something specific and now means only that it is there; the sooty outline from fires lit against the wall in an earlier era of the city, before fire-management was built inward and the walls were given their current role as background.

The far walls fade from view not because of distance but because the diffuse light, even at its brightness, cannot make fine detail legible at three hundred feet. They exist over there as pale forms, generically cave-wall, the boundary condition of the world.

---

What this place knows about itself:

It knows it is old enough that the question of who built it first is not a question anymore, having passed beyond the reach of any tradition into the status of geological fact: it was here before memory, and memory was organized to fit it rather than the other way. It knows that its ceiling will continue to filter light as long as the chalk above remains and the sun remains above the chalk, and neither of these conditions seems presently threatened. It knows that the water that drips will continue to drip, that the channels which direct water to where water is needed will need maintenance in the way that all things need maintenance but will not need reimagining. It knows that it is full of life, full of motion, full of animals and people and the things that grow in corners, and that all of this will continue at the same rate tomorrow that it continues today, as it continued yesterday.

What it does not know is whether it is a finished thing or an ongoing one.

The evidence points both directions. The erosion is so complete that the place has the quality of things that have found their final form — nothing left to wear away, nothing left to break down to a simpler state, settled into its deepest stability. And yet. The people move. The goods circulate. The buildings are perpetually and incrementally rebuilt. The city is in continuous motion even when the rock around it has reached its terminal stillness.

Perhaps this is the answer: the rock is finished and the life within it is not. Perhaps they are two different kinds of time happening in the same space. The chalk breathes its ancient light down from above, patient and final, and below it, in that pervasive cream-brightness, the warm-coated animals and the pale-fungused walls and the perpetual market-motion of the streets enact a kind of time that is not geological, that is not final, that does not know how to settle.

The drop falls from the ceiling every forty seconds. The floor absorbs it.
