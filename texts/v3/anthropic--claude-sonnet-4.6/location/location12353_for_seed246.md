# The Worn City

---

Something was built here. That much is certain. The rest requires patience.

---

Start with what the ground tells you. It is flat in the way that only very old things become flat — not leveled by intention but worn to it, ground down across an interval so vast that the distinction between hill and valley has been dissolved into memory. The plateau sits high, genuinely high, where the air has a thinness that makes distances unreliable and breathing slightly deliberate, each breath a small negotiation. But the high place has been here so long that it no longer feels dramatic. It does not *rise*. It simply *is*, the way a fact is, the way an old scar is. The stone underfoot is pale grey, almost white in places, smoothed to near-glass by processes that finished their work long before anything now living was born. The original texture of the rock — whatever it was, granite perhaps, or some harder nameless ancestor of granite — has been erased. What remains is the abstract of stone. The idea of a surface.

And on this surface: the city. Or what the city has become, which is harder to say.

---

The structures are low. This is the first surprise. You might expect ruins to collapse *downward*, to fall into their own footprints and leave rubble, chaos, a drama of destruction. But these have done something else. They have *settled*. The walls, where walls remain, are knee-height at most, sometimes only ankle-height — pale lines in the stone that describe rooms, streets, thresholds, the outlines of what once organized life here. They are made of the same material as the plateau beneath them, which raises the question of whether they were ever separate from it, or whether the city and the mountain are in the process of becoming one thing, the boundary between built and natural dissolving in the same direction as all other boundaries at this age of the world. The foundations have sunk into the bedrock slightly, or the bedrock has risen around them, and no one can now say which.

Walk through the low walls. You are walking through rooms. You are walking through a neighborhood. There are no ceilings; the sky is the ceiling. There are no doors; the openings are just places where the lines stop. The scale is intimate — you can cross what was once a dwelling in four steps, stand in what was once a square and see its edges — and yet the emptiness amplifies everything. The city is *small* in extent but the space above it is extravagant, unimpeded, a cold high sky that presses down like a hand. Between the outlines of structures: almost nothing. No furniture of the landscape, no obstacles, no filling-in. The rooms are rooms of air.

---

Now the vegetation, because it has done something unexpected with this place.

Moss has come. Not everywhere — the density of life here is genuinely low, genuinely sparse — but where it has come, it has come *completely*. In the angles of walls, in the seams between foundation stones, along the leeward edges of any surface that catches condensation, the moss has established itself in dense cushions of a green so dark it is nearly black in certain lights, nearly emerald in others. Each cushion is a precise geography: swells and depressions, miniature valleys, the topography of a landscape at a scale so reduced it fits in your palm. Touch one and your finger comes away wet. The moss holds water the way wool holds water — not pooling, not dripping, but saturated, every fiber of it a small reservoir. The plateau receives no great rain. But moisture moves through this altitude in other ways: the cold draws condensation from the air each night, and the fog that rolls through intermittently — not the warm coastal kind but a thin, cold, almost dry-seeming fog that nonetheless leaves everything it touches damp — feeds the moss's patient life.

Between the moss islands: lichen. Great flat plates of it, grey-green, grey-orange, papery at the edges, adhering to the smoothed stone as though printed there. Lichen and stone at this age become hard to distinguish philosophically. The lichen *is* a kind of stone, almost; the stone has become a kind of lichen habitat, a surface defined by what grows on it. Other plants have found the old walls' protected sides: low-growing, tight to the ground, leaves reduced to small waxy shields against the cold and the altitude. A few grasses, very short, that flower in colors almost too pale to see — white, pale yellow — and seed and die back and seed again in the thin soil that has been collecting in the wall-seams for centuries. No trees. Trees would be wrong here, would be a different story. This is a place of horizontal life, of things that press themselves against the world rather than rising from it.

---

The sound.

This is where the strangeness begins to accumulate.

The wind is the primary voice and it is not quiet. At this elevation, on this open plateau, wind is not an event but a condition. It moves across the flat stone and through the low wall-lines and it speaks in registers you did not expect. The walls, despite their ruin, despite their low height, create acoustics. The wind finds the angles of old foundations and produces tones — not quite musical, not quite language, but organized somehow, structured, as though the ruined city is performing a continuous notation of the air moving through it. A hollow moan from the far end of what might have been a street. A high thin whistle where wind crosses a gap in stone at precisely the right angle. Percussive pops and knocks where loose pebbles — very few, almost none, but some — are moved across smooth rock and strike the low walls. 

Beneath the wind: water. Not visible water, mostly, but the sound of it — a thin trickle somewhere beneath the stone, audible through a particular crack near the center of the place, water that found its way into the rock long ago and runs in channels too deep to see. You hear it as a low gurgling murmur, steady, slightly too loud for its invisible scale, as though the stone amplifies it the way a body amplifies a heartbeat. Where the moss is thickest, water sometimes gathers and runs in threads too fine to call streams, too present to ignore, and these make a small continuous whispering along the base of walls.

And the echo. Everything echoes slightly wrong here. A handclap returns changed, altered in frequency, as though the space between walls does something to sound that flat open space should not do. This is the strangeness beginning to explain itself. The city amplifies. Whatever moves through it — air, water, the occasional rare creature — it amplifies and transforms and returns to the world differently than it arrived.

---

Speaking of rare creatures.

Something moves in this place but rarely. Evidence of habitation: small tracks in the thin soil-seams, the pattern too regular for wind-work, too small for anything large. Something with small rounded feet and a cautious stride. You will not see it. It moves at night, or in fog, or in the intervals when you are looking elsewhere. Its droppings are small, dry, odorless — almost elegant in their discretion. In one of the rooms, in a corner where two wall-lines meet at a right angle and the moss has grown particularly thick, there is a hollow just large enough to suggest a den, a residence, a home inside a home inside a ruin.

Birds pass overhead. Not often. When they do — a single dark shape crossing the cold sky — the sound of their wingbeats is audible in the silence between wind-gusts, a faint percussion, and then the wind returns and they are gone. They do not land. This place is a waypoint in someone else's journey.

---

The strangeness is not spectacular. This matters. It is the wrong kind of weirdness for spectacle — no impossible geometries, no luminous phenomena, no violation of physics that you can point to and name. It is subtler and more persistent than that.

The geometry of the ruins is almost correct. Almost. The room-outlines are regular enough that you can identify them as rooms, street-enough to identify as streets, but there is something off in the proportions that resists articulation. Doorways placed where, in your understanding of buildings, load-bearing walls should be. Streets that are very slightly too narrow, or too wide, in a way that suggests a different average body than yours — not dramatically different, not alien, but *adjusted* somehow, as though calibrated to a life that was like human life but not identical to it. 

And the smoothness. The smoothness of the stone is too uniform. You expect differential wear — areas of high traffic worn more than sheltered corners, exposed surfaces eroded more than protected ones. But the smoothness is even. Everywhere equally smooth. As though whatever eroded this place touched it all at once, or touched it not from outside but from within.

The moss grows in the interiors of rooms. Not on the outsides of walls. This is the kind of detail that takes time to notice and longer to become certain of. The side of a wall-remnant that faces the open plateau: pale stone, lichen, no moss. The side that faces the room-interior, the sheltered angle, the inside: moss, damp, dark green. Every wall, every remnant, without exception. You check. You keep checking. Inside: moss. Outside: none. The city retains, still, some organizing principle about inside and outside that the wind does not observe and the lichen does not observe but the moss does.

---

Temperature. The cold is not dramatic but it is constant and it changes how everything feels. The stone is cold to the touch even in full sun — the sun here is bright, the light clean and high-altitude clear, the shadows sharp-edged and blue — and this creates the persistent sense that the place is slightly more alive at its surface than its core, that warmth exists only in a thin membrane above the stone. Your own body, moving through it, is the warmest thing present by a significant margin. This makes you conscious of yourself in an uncomfortable way. You are the heat anomaly. The moss, in its dampness, is cooler than the stone around it, which should not be possible, which is a small wrongness you add to the list.

At night the temperature drops quickly and the moisture that has been hanging in the air all day precipitates onto every surface — a thin frost on the stones by morning, which melts by mid-morning into the brief dampness the moss feeds on, and then by afternoon the plateau is dry again except in the moss-seams and the shadow-corners. This cycle runs every day and has run for longer than measurement is useful. The city is washed in it continuously, this small daily flood and evaporation, and it neither speeds nor slows the long settling that is already, for all purposes, complete.

---

What happened here is not the question this place asks. The question it asks is quieter and harder:

What is the difference between a ruin and a terrain feature? Between a wall and a ridge? Between a city and a fact?

You walk through room-shaped spaces of empty cold air and the wind moves through the same shapes and makes its organized sound and the moss holds water in the angles and the small unseen animal beds in the corner and water runs below in stone channels too deep to see, and the place is loud with wind and quiet with absence and full of life in a nearly-empty way, and old in a way that has gone past drama into something more like geometry, and the line between what was built and what simply is has been, over whatever interval this required, thoroughly and completely erased.

The city is not abandoned. It is *finished*. These are different things.

---

Stand still long enough and you stop feeling like a visitor. The wind adjusts around you. You become another feature of the surface. The moss, had you been here long enough, would begin to determine which of your sides was interior.
