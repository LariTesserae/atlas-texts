# The Dry Canopy

---

Begin with what is absent.

There is no water here. Not drought — not the memory of drought, not the cracked lakebed yearning upward for rain. The absence is older than that, constitutional, written into the chemistry of the stone itself. The rock is pale sulfate, crusted in hexagonal tessellations that were never mud, never silt, never anything that dried. They simply formed this way, mineral lattices precipitating out of air that carries just enough moisture to sustain biochemistry but deposits none of it anywhere you can see. The ground does not crack with thirst. It was always this.

And yet: everything grows.

---

You are high up. The elevation announces itself not as cold — the air is warm, close to body temperature, a humid warmth like breath — but as exposure. The horizon, where you can see it through the canopy, is not far away. This is a small place, bounded. Perhaps two hundred meters across at its widest, perhaps less. You could walk its perimeter in ten minutes if the vegetation let you, which it does not quite. The edges are unclear, not because they fade gradually but because the canopy simply becomes too dense near the rim, folding over itself, closing off. At the center where you stand the plants are more spread, more individuated, with real space between them.

Below the rim: clouds, probably. You can hear wind working over the edge with a hollow organ-pipe resonance that suggests a significant drop not far off. This place sits on something — a plateau, a spire, a formation for which there is no good word — and the something is very tall.

---

The vegetation is lush but not crowded. This is the paradox of the place. *Lush* usually means dense, layered, dripping, wall-to-wall. Here it means something else: abundance expressed through individual extravagance rather than collective mass. Each plant has room. Each plant uses that room completely.

The dominant form is something that resembles a fern only in the way that a cathedral resembles a hut — sharing a structural logic but not a scale of ambition. The fronds are enormous, three and four meters long, deep blue-green on top and a pale copper beneath. They do not photosynthesize sunlight. The sky up here is wrong for sunlight — more on that shortly — and the fronds are oriented not toward any particular light source but in slow rotation, like satellite dishes searching. They are doing something with the air. Extracting. The copper undersides are coated in a fine fuzz of glandular structures, too small to see individually but collectively giving the surface a velvet nap that glistens with some metabolic product, something between a wax and a sugar, a substance that does not dry but does not flow either. The fronds of neighboring plants do not touch. There is exactly the right amount of space. This has been established over a long time.

Between the giant fern-forms: lower growth, scattered. Pale bulbous things, knee-high, that look like gourds but are not fruits — they are the plant's main body, the fronds emerging from the top like a single crown. They sit in the hexagonal stone tessellations the way chess pieces sit on squares, one per cell more or less, and the cells are just large enough. You cannot tell whether the cells formed to accommodate the plants or the plants arranged themselves to match the cells. Both seems possible. Neither seems impossible.

Scattered among the bulbs: thin upright stalks, leafless, each terminating in a complex spiral of fine tendrils, like a question mark asked in cursive. These are doing something rapid and constant. They are the turbulent members of the community.

---

The light is dim and wrong.

The sky is not overcast exactly. It is the color of old pewter, but the pewter glows evenly, from everywhere at once, no direction, no shadows. Or rather: shadows exist but point in many directions simultaneously, a multiplicity of faint shadows around every object like a slow-motion strobe caught in still life. Something up there is radiating, diffused through a medium that scatters it totally. The plants do not cast single shadows. They cast halos of shadow.

This light is warm in temperature and cool in color, if that makes sense, if sense is what you came here for. It does not intensify or diminish in any cycle you can detect during your time here. Whether it is always this dim, or whether you have arrived at some permanent ambient state the place settled into long ago, is unknowable from the inside.

---

The sound is the thing that will stay with you.

The resonant wind over the rim is constant and loud, a deep thrumming with harmonics that shift as the wind's speed and angle shift, which they do constantly, turbulently, without pattern. It is the bass register. Above it: the fauna.

The fauna is abundant and you cannot see most of it.

What you can see: large arthropods, eight or ten limbs, dark amber exoskeletons, moving along the undersides of the great fronds with quick deliberate purpose. They are not insects — they are much too large, the size of a hand, and they move with the considered efficiency of something that plans. They communicate in percussion — they strike the copper underside of the fronds with their forelegs in complex rhythms, and the fronds transmit these sounds across their entire length, so the whole plant is a drum, and the whole canopy is a drumkit, and there are hundreds of the arthropods, perhaps thousands, all drumming at once.

The drumming is very loud. It is structured — you can hear call and response, you can hear something that might be argument or might be weather report or might be liturgy. The fronds amplify and color the strikes, each plant with its own resonant frequency, so the canopy produces a dense harmonic field that never repeats, never resolves, churning through tonal relationships with the restless energy of something that cannot settle because settling is not the point.

What you cannot see but can hear: something large, moving through the lower vegetation with deliberate unhurried weight, its footfalls felt more than heard through the sulfate stone. It has not come into view. It is circling, or patrolling, or following a path it has followed so many times the stone has begun to record it.

What you cannot see and can only suspect from the sound: very small things, everywhere, a high continuous sibilance like white noise that, when you attend to it carefully, is not random but is composed of individual elements — clicks, chirps, a sound like a fingernail drawn along a comb, repeated variations on a theme. This sound is made by things living in the glandular fuzz of the fronds. Things that feed on the wax-sugar. Things so small you could carry ten thousand of them in a closed fist.

---

The built thing is this:

At the center of the small plateau, below the tallest of the fern-forms, there is a structure made of the sulfate hexagons stacked and fitted without mortar. It is not large. It is approximately the size and shape of a beehive, perhaps a meter and a half tall, a meter across at the base, tapering. The stone is not cut — it is selected, sorted, fitted. Whoever or whatever built it understood the material: the hexagonal faces mate cleanly, the taper is achieved by choosing progressively smaller plates. It is old. The biological crusts that colonize the stone here, the same crusts everywhere on the plateau surface, have moved up the structure's sides to perhaps the third course from the top. That process, whatever speed it runs at, has been running long enough to indicate the structure is not recent.

There is an opening at the base. Dark inside. The opening is sized for something. You find yourself uncertain whether the something is larger or smaller than you. Both seem plausible depending on posture.

Nothing comes in or out during your time here. This does not mean nothing is inside.

Near the structure, set into a gap between two sulfate hexagons, is a tool. A handle of dense pale material — possibly bone, possibly a mineralized wood, possibly neither — with a flat blade attached with twisted fiber. The blade is a flake of darker stone, sharp enough still to be useful, shaped by a hand that understood flint-knapping at a level beyond the merely functional, into a form that is almost unnecessarily precise, almost beautiful. The fiber binding is dry but not rotten. It was made by something with patience and fine motor control and either aesthetic preference or a tradition that enforced precision for its own sake.

The tool has been here long enough that the glandular wax of the nearest frond, deposited by drip or by the crawling amber arthropods, has begun to coat the lower third of the handle in a slow patina. The arthropods have examined it — you can see the clean streaks in the wax where their limbs have crossed it repeatedly. They have found it interesting or useful or in the way. It has not moved.

---

The turbulence is not the weather.

The weather is the constant wind-drone, the warm dim unchanging light, the steady drumming. The turbulence is biological.

Every few minutes, one of the giant ferns enters what seems like a crisis state: the fronds begin rotating faster, the copper undersurface flushing darker, the glandular fuzz producing its metabolite visibly, a fine mist of it diffusing into the warm air. The arthropods on that plant evacuate rapidly, drums going silent on that plant while the others drum louder — response, acknowledgment, something. Then the fern's tendriled stalks, which were doing their constant rapid spiral work before, now do something extreme: they extend fully, pointing outward and downward, their tendrils opening and spreading into flat receiving surfaces, and they shudder, all of them at once, a rapid high-frequency shudder that lasts perhaps thirty seconds and then stops.

After this: the fern is still. Its fronds slow to their normal rotation. The arthropods return. The cooperative drumming resumes, incorporating the returning plant.

This happens to one plant, then another, then another. It does not spread like a wave — there is no directionality. Any plant may enter this state at any time. The rate is high enough that most minutes contain at least one such event. The air above the canopy is faintly hazed with metabolite mist, accumulated over time, and this mist is warm against the face and sweet in a non-food way, in the way that some minerals smell sweet.

The large creature below makes its circuit. The small creatures maintain their continuous sibilant chorus. The arthropods drum their negotiations or their liturgy. The fronds shudder and still and shudder elsewhere. The wind works over the rim in its low organ harmonics.

It is very loud. It has been exactly this loud, in this key, in this rhythm of crisis and return, for longer than the tool has been sitting beside the structure. For longer than the structure has been standing. For as long as this particular confluence of elevation and dry chemistry and warm dim light has existed, which may be longer than you are comfortable imagining.

---

The strangeness you feel — and you feel it, it is not metaphorical — is not horror and not wonder exactly. It is the strangeness of a place that does not need you to resolve it. The lush life with no water source you can find. The fronds drummed by creatures who may be farming or governing or praying. The hive-shaped stone thing and the fine tool left beside it, neither one explained by the other. The shuddering crises of the plants and their regular recovery. The large circling thing you never see.

This place is in a state of balance. The erosion is established, settled, nothing here is new or in conflict with itself. The strange rules governing this small high dry platform have been in effect long enough that everything here has come to terms with them, has built a life or a practice shaped exactly to them. You are the only element that has not been here long enough to understand what it is doing here.

The frond nearest to you, enormous, warm, copper-velvet below, begins its slower rotation. Its arthropods are drumming something you cannot interpret.

You have the feeling it is about you.
