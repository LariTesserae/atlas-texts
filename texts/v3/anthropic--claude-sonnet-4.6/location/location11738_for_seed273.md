# The Hanging Gardens of the Foundry Terraces

---

Start with the height. That comes first, always — the vertiginous fact of it before anything else registers. You are very high. The world below is weather. Clouds assemble beneath you in slow, indifferent formations, and from this elevation they look like something solid you could walk across, though you know better, or you think you do. The air up here has a different quality than air lower down: thinner, yes, but not cold — warm in the way that surprises people who assume height and cold are the same thing, warm because the foundry terraces generate their own climate and have been doing so long enough that the surrounding atmosphere has accommodated them, bending slightly to acknowledge their presence. Thermals rise along the stonework. The temperature up here hovers somewhere around pleasant-to-warm, and that warmth carries moisture — not much moisture, not a wet place exactly, but the kind of ambient dampness that settles on your forearms and makes your hair behave differently by midday.

Now look at what you're standing on. There is no natural substrate here. There never was — or if there was, it was consumed long ago, incorporated, melted down, repoured. Everything under your feet was made. The terraces extend in enormous stepped platforms of cast iron and fired brick and riveted steel plate, each level perhaps forty meters wide and dropping another twenty to the next, staircase-fashion, down the face of what was once a cliff or a ridge or something geologically given, though the geological given is now so thoroughly encased in manufactured material that it functions only as a ghost, an inference. You can sometimes see it in the angles — the way a wall curves slightly more than engineering would dictate, following some older contour underneath. The original shape of the place haunts the built shape.

The construction is old. Not ancient — there is a specificity to it that suggests human industrial intention rather than mythological origin — but old enough to have settled into itself completely. Rivets have gone dark and are ringed with rust-halos that have stabilized, the rust not progressing but simply present, a patina rather than a process. The brick is sooted in patterns that record decades of exhaust and weather, and the soot has been colonized in places by black-green lichens that look painted on. Everything here has been in its own state long enough that it has achieved something like ecological status — the made things have grown into their environment the way a reef grows into the sea, and you would be wrong to call it ruin, because it functions, the machinery still runs, the systems still operate, but it wears the texture of long time.

---

Walk out onto one of the upper terraces and let the density of the place settle into you.

There is a great deal happening per square meter.

The railings — and there are railings everywhere, iron pipe railings painted at some point in black or dark green, now showing through to corrosion beneath — the railings are colonized by climbing plants that have been growing along them for what must be decades, their stems now as thick as your wrist at the base, woody and insistent, sending new growth upward in bright green tendons while the older portions have become part of the railing itself, gripping, indistinguishable from the metal they grip. Vines hang from the upper edges of each terrace and brush the heads of the level below. Ferns have established themselves in the joints between iron panels, in the gaps where bolt-holes were never filled, in the horizontal ledges of every structural beam, and the ferns are large — this warm damp air suits them — with fronds spreading half a meter and more, making every beam into a soft horizontal stroke of green.

The plant life up here is improbable and therefore magnificent. It has no business being this lush at this elevation. It survives on the moisture that condenses on cold metal in the hours before dawn, on the runoff from the water-management systems, on the mist that rises when rain passes through the lower levels. The condensation drips. Small trickles find paths along the ironwork, wearing channels over years, depositing green-black algal traces along their routes, pooling in saucered depressions on the flat surfaces until they overflow and begin the path again. Plants have learned these routes. Their roots follow the water with precision. There are gardens here, in any sense that matters, though no one designed them to be gardens — they evolved into gardens, pressing into every available surface until the terraces became layered things: iron below, then the older plants with their woody rootstock, then the new season's growth above that, then the insects and the birds above that, the whole assembly reaching upward in tiers that mirror the terraces themselves.

Flowers appear unexpectedly. Small yellow things in a crack above a boiler exhaust vent. Something purple and pendulous trailing from a pipe elbow six meters off the deck. You look at the built environment and then you look again and find it full of color that wasn't there in your first accounting.

---

The machinery runs.

This is important to understand. The terraces are not a memorial. They are a working place.

Deep in the structure — and the structure has deep interior spaces, great chambers and corridors and processing floors that you move through when you descend via the internal staircases — engines turn over with a low frequency you feel more than hear. The rhythm is slow. Whatever is being produced here is produced on long cycles, not the frantic pulse of certain other industries, but a patient mechanical grinding and compressing and moving that takes its time. Conveyor systems carry materials along routes that span multiple terrace levels, the materials passing through openings in the floors and emerging at different heights, the system having a logic that is comprehensible if you follow it long enough. Pipes run everywhere. Steam does not so much vent as it breathes — releasing in long slow exhales from valves designed for exactly this purpose, the steam immediately grabbed by the warm air and dispersed.

Electricity is present but not dominant. Lights in amber and pale yellow string along the inner corridors and hang in caged fixtures from the ceiling beams. The insulation on the older wiring has gone dark and cracked. In the engine rooms, gauges with analog needles show pressures and temperatures, and the needles hold mostly steady, drifting only within their acceptable ranges. People tend this machinery — you see them at intervals, workers who move through the space with the habitual ease of people who have been here long enough to stop noticing the extraordinary nature of their surroundings. They carry tools. They consult things. They make small adjustments and move on.

The machinery is loud in the way that steady, low-frequency machinery is loud — not sharp, not piercing, but pervasive, a pressure on the ears that you adjust to and then carry without noticing, until you stop and notice it again. Layered into this baseline: the birds. There are many birds. They nest in the ironwork and they are vocal about it, and their calls make strange harmonics against the metal surfaces, the sound bouncing and arriving from unexpected directions, so that sometimes a bird seems to be calling from everywhere at once. Water drips. Something mechanical, far away, hammers in a slow pattern — one blow, a pause, one blow, a pause — and the pause is long enough each time that you begin to wonder if it has stopped, and then it comes again.

---

The fauna has done what fauna does when given a place of warmth and food and structural complexity and enough time: it has organized itself into something like community.

The birds are the most visible. Pigeons, yes, of the type that follows human infrastructure everywhere, but also larger things — raptors, apparently, because something must be eating the pigeons and the pigeons display the specific vigilance of prey animals, never quite relaxed. There are bats in the deep interior chambers, hanging in clusters from the pipe runs during the day. Insects are everywhere: in the flowers, on the water surfaces in the condensation pools, circling the amber lights in the evenings, moving through the plant canopy in a constant low traffic. Lizards sun themselves on flat metal surfaces in the south-facing exposures, utterly still for long periods and then gone. You learn to look for them after a while; their stillness makes them part of the surface until it doesn't.

Small mammals — you catch movement at the periphery, scurrying along beam edges, vanishing into holes in the structure. The structure has many holes. It has accommodated many things over its long existence and has been accommodated by them in return.

---

This is not a strange place, exactly. That's worth saying. It has the quality of something that has arrived at itself through ordinary processes, just ordinary processes applied to an unusual situation over an unusual amount of time. A built environment at extreme elevation, warm, lush, working, inhabited by machines and creatures and plants all in dense coexistence. The slight uncanniness of it — and there is slight uncanniness — is the uncanniness of *rightness*, of a place that shouldn't quite work and does, that has found its balance and holds it.

The light up here is good. High-altitude light with a bright clarity to it, the sun generous, the sky very blue where it's visible between the planted edges of the terraces. The light falls on the leaves and makes them brilliant. It falls on the old iron and makes the rust look like a deliberate finish, like something chosen. The shadows move slowly as the day turns. In the afternoon, the light angles in from the west and the whole terrace system goes amber, everything gilded, the steam from the exhaust valves lit from behind, the birds passing through the amber air on their way to wherever birds go at that hour.

Then evening comes gradually, as evenings do up here, and the amber lights inside the corridors become the dominant light, and the machinery continues its slow rhythm, and the bats come out, and the place settles into the next part of its long, patient operation.

It has been here a long time. It will be here for a long time more.
