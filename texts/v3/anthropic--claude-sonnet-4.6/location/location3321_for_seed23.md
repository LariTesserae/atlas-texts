# The Terrace Settlement of Khorveth Mere

---

Begin with light.

The light here is wrong — not in quality but in *quantity*, the way light is wrong when there's too much of it and nowhere to go. It pours from a sky that sits low and white, pressing itself flat against the high plateau like a hand against glass. No shadows run long here. They pool directly beneath their objects, hoarding themselves, giving nothing away. The sun, or what passes for the sun, fills the whole sky evenly — a white so complete it reads almost as noise. You have to squint. You have to squint constantly. This is a place built by people who learned not to look up.

---

The plateau itself is not one surface but many, stacked.

The rock is limestone of some dark persuasion — charcoal-grey, faintly blue where it's wet, which is everywhere, which is always. The plateau has spent an immeasurable time becoming itself, and it has done so in layers: terrace upon terrace, each step the result of ten thousand years of collapse and refreeze and slow dissolution, each one a slightly different angle than the one below it, so that the whole structure reads like a stack of paper badly shuffled. The edges of each terrace have been worn to lips, rounded to the softness of old soap. Water runs in thin films across the flat surfaces and then tips, silently, over the edge and into the next terrace's standing pools.

The water is everywhere and moves quietly.

Seeps emerge from fissures in the rock face. They don't pour. They don't trickle. They simply *appear*, as if the rock has decided to sweat, as if something behind the limestone is pressing forward. The thin films of water on every surface catch the bright flat sky and make the whole plateau glitter — not warmly, not like treasure, but coldly, like a surgery under bright lights, like a mirror someone left outside all winter. Between the terraces, where the walls drop short distances of three or four feet, the seeping water has deposited millennia of mineral trace, and the limestone faces there are not grey but cream and ochre and soft orange, banded in strata so fine they look drawn with a fine brush by someone with very good eyes and no other obligations.

---

The cold is real and present.

Not extreme cold — not the cold of glacier or pole — but the sustained, insistent cold of high elevation in a wet place, the cold that bypasses skin and settles into joints, the cold that makes people lean slightly forward as they walk. Your breath shows. Your breath has always shown here. No one who lives here ever sees their face without breath-cloud, even in whatever passes for summer. Ice forms overnight in the standing pools and is still half-present at midday, translucent panes floating with dark water visible beneath, and the abundant fauna of this place — we will arrive there soon — have learned to step around them, or to break them deliberately, methodically, purposefully.

---

The settlement.

It is small. From the highest point of the plateau, which is perhaps forty feet above the lowest, the full extent of Khorveth Mere is visible and can be taken in a single long look: it is roughly the size of a town square, not a large one. The geometry of the terraces has been colonized entirely, every horizontal surface built upon, and the walls between terraces have been incorporated into the building structure so that what was natural shelf has become foundation has become floor, and the limestone face below that floor has become wall, and someone has cut a window in it, or not a window but an *opening*, sized for the creatures who live here.

The construction is primitive in the sense of being direct. Stone on stone on stone. Mortar made from the pale mineral sediment of the seep-water, rich in calcium, packed into joints and smoothed flat and allowed to calcify into permanence. The buildings are mostly low — rarely more than two stories — but they are *dense*, terraced into one another so that one building's roof is another building's alley, one building's back wall is another's front wall with a shared chimney between them running up through both. The whole settlement reads as a single mineral form, as if it grew from the plateau rather than was placed on it, as if the humans here had learned to build the way the rock had always intended to be built.

Ramps rather than stairs, often. The ramps are water-cut, or begun by water and finished by hand, and they are worn smooth by the traffic of many years and are extremely treacherous. People here do not run. People here choose their steps.

No mortar has been pointed recently. No surface has been squared or leveled with any tool more sophisticated than a string pulled tight between two pegs. And yet the whole reads as deeply considered, the proportions of each space fitting the life inside it with the accuracy of long use — the accumulated intelligence of people who've had generations to understand exactly how much room a body needs to work and sleep and store what little needs to be stored.

---

The fauna is where this place becomes difficult to describe.

There are many creatures and they are busy.

Something small and dark-furred, roughly the size and general shape of a thick-limbed rabbit, moves constantly across the wet stone surfaces. It does not slip. This is inexplicable. The surfaces are wet, the wet surfaces are smooth, everything here slips — and yet these creatures move at a pace approaching a run, changing direction without decelerating, their small feet finding purchase that no surface should give. They are everywhere. In the alleys, under the ramps, along the lips of terraces. They move in the peripheral vision and stop when looked at directly. They resume when you look away.

There are birds, but they do not leave. This is the strange thing. The bright sky above should draw birds up and through, but the birds here — a species with broad flat wings and feet like fingers, dull white to match the sky — circle repeatedly, land, walk on the building tops, take off, circle, land. They make no sound that carries. Their wings beat audibly when they're close, a soft mechanical pulsing, but from any distance: nothing. The people of Khorveth Mere do not seem to notice them. The birds do not seem to notice the people.

In the water — the standing pools, the seep-fed rivulets, the thin films moving across stone — there are things. Not fish exactly. Flatter than fish. They lie in the water films, which are sometimes less than a centimeter deep, and they move with the water when the water moves. Where they go when the water tips over terrace edges is unclear. They reappear in the pools below. Perhaps they fall. Perhaps falling is not difficult for them.

---

The turbulence.

Here is what's strange: the place looks still and the place is not still.

Everything is in constant motion and that motion is quiet. The flat-white creatures on the building tops shift and shift again. The dark-furred ground animals route and reroute. The water changes its paths — the seeps strengthen and weaken according to pressures inside the rock that aren't visible, so the water films shift, the pools rise and drop, the mineral deposits change color hour by hour as different chemistry emerges. The buildings themselves are not changing but they are being changed — small repairs, adjustments, a stone here, a handful of mortar there, hands reaching into joints and pressing. The people of Khorveth Mere (who are present, who are perhaps thirty or forty in number within the visible settlement) move constantly and without apparent urgency. They are not rushing. Rushing implies that there is a difference between the pace of urgency and the pace of rest. Here there is no rest-pace. Everything moves at one speed: the speed of a place that cannot stop.

A child is redirecting a seep-channel with a piece of flat stone, pressing it into the flow so that water angles away from a building's foundation. An old person — indeterminate age, wrapped, moving steadily — is carrying something bundled across three rooftops without using their hands to balance, their arms occupied. Two people are building a low wall and they are building it quickly, without speaking, passing stones to one another with the efficiency of people who've been passing stones for so long that words would only slow the exchange.

Nothing is dramatic. Everything is happening.

---

The sounds.

The sound of water on stone is nearly all there is, and it is very quiet. Not the sound of running water — the sound of *present* water, water as material, the soft tonal hiss of it moving across surfaces in thin sheets, the occasional low note of a drip falling three feet into a pool. The birds near-silent. The animals silent. The people do not speak often, and when they do it's brief, and the words are absorbed immediately by wet stone, which is a good absorber of sound. Someone somewhere in the dense interior of the settlement is working something with a tool — a rhythmic impact, the sound of stone striking stone, but muffled, interior, reaching the surface of the settlement as less than an echo.

Mostly: the seeps. The water films. The flat sky's silence.

If you went quiet enough, stood still enough, you might hear the breathing of the flat-winged birds overhead. You might hear the cold.

---

This is a place that has been here a long time. The limestone knows it. The rounded lips of the terraces know it. The mortar in the building joints knows it, having calcified to something hardly distinguishable from the rock around it. The animals know it, having developed their impossible traction and their flat water-forms through whatever pressures produced them.

The settlement neither grows nor diminishes visibly, though nothing is still. It is not in stasis. Stasis would imply that change had stopped. What Khorveth Mere has achieved is something else: a dynamic balance so finely tuned that the sum of all its motion produces no change in overall state. Every repair offsets every decay. Every rerouted seep compensates for every shifted stone. The dark-furred animals eat what would otherwise block the drainage channels; the flat-winged birds eat what the animals leave; the water feeds the mineral deposits that make the mortar that holds the stones.

The people, moving constantly and quietly, are part of the system the way a stone is part of the system — not in control of it, not separate from it, their motion as automatic and as purposeful as the motion of water finding level.

From above, if you could see from above, all of it would appear to breathe.

---

The light, still: pressing down, white, filling the wet stone surfaces with cold luminescence. The whole plateau glitters. The breath-clouds of the people and the breath-clouds of the animals rise and are absorbed into the low white sky. The birds circle.

Nothing rests. Nothing ends.
