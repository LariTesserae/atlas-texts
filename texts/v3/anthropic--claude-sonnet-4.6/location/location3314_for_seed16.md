# The Hollow City

---

Begin with the heat. Not the heat of sun on stone — there is no sun here, or if there ever was, it has not touched this place in a very long time. This is the heat of compression, of depth, of a world pressing inward on itself from all sides. The air sits at roughly fifty degrees and does not move unless something moves it. It arrives in your lungs like a damp cloth pressed over the face, except there is no moisture — the air is absolutely, phenomenally dry. You exhale and your breath vanishes instantly, taken apart by the thirst of the atmosphere before it can curl or drift.

You are inside something. That is the first thing to understand.

---

The place reveals itself slowly in the dimness. There is light — call it amber, call it the color of old horn or rendered tallow — but it has no obvious source. It seems to seep from the walls themselves, or perhaps from the vegetation, or perhaps it is simply what vision becomes when the eyes have had time to adjust to a darkness so thorough that even this qualifies as illumination. Objects exist at about ten meters of clarity and then dissolve into warm shadow. Beyond that: inference only.

You are in a chamber. But *chamber* is too small a word for what this is, and too large. It is a room in the way that a cathedral is a room — the ceiling, whatever it is, has retreated beyond the reach of the amber glow. The walls are present but distant: worked stone, cut in large irregular blocks fitted without mortar, the joins so old and settled that moss — or something like moss — has colonized every seam and made them permanent. The floor is flagstone, or was. The flagstone is old enough that it has become floor in the way that a riverbed is floor: smoothed, continuous, the original intention of the cutting long since subsumed into what time and pressure and a thousand thousand footsteps have made of it.

This is not a new place. This is a place that has arrived at itself.

---

The vegetation is the second revelation.

It should not be here. There is no water — truly, genuinely no water, and if you lick the stone it will pull at your tongue like salt, like a mild alkaline hunger — and yet the plants have managed. Whatever biology operates here does not run on water. The chemistry is different. The plants — if they are plants — are waxy, deeply pigmented in colors that approach green but arrive instead at something more sulfurous, more mineral: the yellow of pyrite, the dull pewter-green of weathered copper. Their leaves, where they have leaves, are thick and slightly translucent, like slabs of colored glass or the flesh of aloe, but when you press them they do not yield moisture. They yield a faint, warm resistance, and a smell: metallic, almost sweet, like copper warming in a palm.

They have grown into everything.

The walls are half-consumed. Not damaged — *incorporated*. The plants have not broken the stonework so much as accepted it as substrate, winding through the fitted seams with roots like pale cable, anchoring themselves to the architecture in a relationship so long established that the stone and the root are now load-bearing together. Pull the plant and you pull the wall. The city — and it is a city, or the residue of one — has been infiltrated by its own vegetation to a point where the two systems have become a single system. This happened long ago. The equilibrium is stable. The city is not dying under the weight of growth. The city and the growth have reached an agreement.

The tallest of the plants reach toward the unseen ceiling in columns of segmented, articulated stem, branchless for several meters and then erupting into dense canopies of the sulfur-colored foliage. In the amber dimness they look, from below, like an underground forest — which is, in some sense, exactly what they are. The canopy absorbs most of the already-dim light and re-emits it at a lower register, so the floor of the city receives only what filters down: complicated, leaf-patterned, warm.

Closer to the ground, in the spaces between the column-plants, the growth is denser and stranger. Low-spreading things like succulents but larger, flattened against the flagstones in rosettes a meter across. Ropy, tendriling things that have strung themselves between the carved corbels of old archways. Something that looks almost like lichen but pulses — very slowly, barely perceptibly, once every several seconds — in a slightly brighter shade of its own color. You watch it for a long time before you are certain you are seeing it move.

There are no animals. Nothing has eaten any of this. The vegetation has had the city entirely to itself.

---

The sound.

This is where the place insists on itself most forcefully.

For a sealed, sunless, apparently abandoned underground city, it is *loud*.

The plants are responsible for part of it. Not because they make obvious noise, but because they are in constant slow process, and process has friction, and friction in an enclosed space accumulates. There is a deep, subsonic grinding that you feel in the back teeth more than hear — the roots working, the stone settling fractions of millimeters around them, the long geological negotiation of organism and architecture continuing its ancient argument. There is a soft, continuous creak from the tall column-stems, which are under tension from the weight of their canopies and the pressure of the roots below. This creak has no rhythm. It comes and goes in long, slow waves, like a ship's timber, except the ship is standing still and always has been.

But the real noise comes from the infrastructure.

The city was built by people who understood pressure and flow, even if they understood almost nothing else by our measures. They had no metal to speak of — the construction is entirely stone, ceramic, fired clay, woven fiber long since fossilized by the dry heat into something closer to mineral than organic. But they understood channels. There are channels cut into the walls at about head height, and channels cut into the floor along the edges of the wider passages, and these channels are active. Not with water — there is no water — but with something. A warm, dry, mineral-smelling current of air moves through the channel system with enough force to produce sound: a continuous, low, resonant hum that changes pitch as you move through the space, depending on how the channel geometry has been cut, depending on where exactly you are standing relative to the network. Parts of the city hum in near-unison. Parts produce discords that beat against each other in slow waves. In larger chambers, the channels produce long tones that the stone amplifies, and the whole sealed space rings with it, faintly, the way a bell continues after the strike.

The channels were not built to make music. They were built for thermal regulation, presumably — to move the heat through the settlement in controlled ways, to prevent any single area from accumulating more warmth than another, to keep the whole pocket of sealed underground space at a habitable temperature. The system still runs. Whatever drives it — geothermal pressure differential, the slow respiration of the plant-roots, something the builders encoded into the stone geometry itself — it has not stopped. It will not stop. It is in its own state now, self-sustaining, no longer requiring intention.

You walk through the channels' breath and it presses past you with the urgency of something that has been moving for a very long time and does not know how to stop.

---

Above the hum: crackle.

The dry heat does things to organic matter. The oldest of the woven-fiber structures — ceiling spans, decorative elements, the remnants of things that were once furniture or dividers or storage — have desiccated into states of extreme brittleness, and every few minutes, unprompted by anything obvious, one of them loses another small piece of itself. A crackle, a snap, a papery whisper of descent. You do not see what falls. By the time it reaches the flagstones it is powder. The powder joins the fine, pale mineral dust that covers every horizontal surface in a thin, even layer, undisturbed except by the thermal channels' breath, which has organized it over centuries into faint dune-like ripples along the floor's edges.

There is also, intermittently, a sound like an enormous door being opened somewhere at the edge of perception — a deep resonant boom, followed by a long settling groan. This happens every several minutes. It is the city's deep structure: the thermal channels passing through a section of stone that has a particular resonant quality, amplified by some accident of the chamber's geometry into something that sounds intentional, ceremonial, like a great signal being given. You wait for it. It becomes the heartbeat you time yourself against.

---

The built environment, as you walk it.

The streets — corridors, passages, ways — are narrow by any standard but especially by the standard of what the vegetation has made them. Originally they were perhaps three meters wide; now they are one, one-and-a-half, the walls consumed to the waist by the slow lateral spread of root-systems. You pass under archways that were carved with decorative friezes long since botanically obscured — you can see, in places, a carved edge, an angle, the suggestion of a repeated geometric motif, the corner of something that was once a face or an animal or an abstraction. The plants have colonized the carvings selectively, following the moisture (there is no moisture) or the mineral content of particular pigments used in the original cutting, so some sections of frieze are completely hidden and others — apparently less nutritious — are still partially visible.

The city has levels. Not many — three, perhaps four — accessible by ramps rather than stairs, the ramps worn into soft curves by long use. Each level is slightly different in temperature: lower is hotter, the geothermal source rising through the stone. The lowest level is where the largest plants grow, and where the thermal channels are loudest, and where the amber light is dimmest and most even, the shadows most complete.

The highest accessible level is coolest, which is to say it is merely hot rather than fierce. Here the vegetation is sparser, the stonework more exposed, and you can see the original construction most clearly. The blocks are enormous — too large for any small crew to have placed by hand. The builders had some system, some leverage, some organization of effort that let them work in stone at this scale. There is no evidence of metal tools in the cut marks; the edges read as work done with harder stone against softer, percussion and patience over powered force. But the cuts are precise. The blocks are true. Whatever the builders knew, they knew geometry.

On the highest level there is a space — the largest in the city — that is almost entirely clear of vegetation. The plants have not colonized it. The floor is bare flagstone, the walls bare ashlar, and in the center is a low circular platform of darker stone, a slightly different material from everything else, which suggests it was brought from somewhere else or was always here before the city was built around it. The platform has channels cut into it — smaller than the wall-channels, more intricate, more deliberate. If you put your palm flat against it you feel the breath moving through these tiny channels with a different character than elsewhere: warmer at certain points, cooler at others, a thermal texture in the stone. The builders knew what they were building here. Whatever this room was for, it was the reason for the rest.

The sounds of the city are loudest here, or rather most present: all the tones of all the channels converge, and the circular geometry reflects them back on themselves, and the room hums at several pitches simultaneously in a chord that resolves into something almost but not quite comfortable, a sonic atmosphere that keeps the mind slightly off-balance, slightly attended. Ceremonial space. Built to feel like this. Still working.

---

The unusual quality of the place.

I have been describing it as if it were simply a buried city, a sealed cave-system, an underground settlement abandoned to time and its own vegetation. But there is something else.

The plants pulse, you will remember. Slowly, once every several seconds, the brightest of them shift slightly brighter. This happens — you begin to notice — in patterns. Not all at once. In sequence. A plant in one corridor brightens, then dims. A moment later, the one nearest to it brightens. The pulse travels.

It is slow enough that you have to watch for a long time before you are certain, and then you think: it is too regular to be coincidence, and too variable to be pure physical mechanism. It is not uniform. It does not propagate in a single direction. It branches, doubles back, accelerates through the denser stands and slows in the sparse areas of the high level, as if the root-network is conducting something. Signal. Exchange. A slow biochemical language moving through the city's biological layer at a pace that would take minutes to traverse the whole settlement.

What is being said is not available to you.

But the saying of it is ongoing. The city breathes through its channels, its roots converse in light, its stones hum in chords. There are no animals. There are no people. There is no water. There is this: a system that has been running long enough to have become self-sufficient, a place that has settled so completely into its own existence that the original purpose — shelter, ceremony, home — is no longer the point. The point is what it has become.

Hot, sealed, loud, alive in its own way, dark, old.

Waiting for nothing. Not waiting. Simply occurring.

---

You leave the way you came — or in the direction you came, which may not be the same — and the city continues behind you: the hum, the crackle, the deep periodic boom of its heart, the slow conversation of light through root through leaf through stone. It was doing this before you arrived. It will do it after. The vegetation will continue its inch-century colonization of the carved margins. The channels will continue their thermal breath. The dark stone platform will continue to organize the warmth of the earth's interior into its intricate, deliberate pattern.

In some number of centuries the topmost level will be fully consumed by root and leaf, and then the second, and eventually the bottom, and eventually the chamber that contains the city will be entirely full of the yellow-copper-green of the waterless forest, and the thermal channels will still run through it, and the light will still pulse, and the sound will still rise, and the city will be less distinguishable from the organism and the organism less distinguishable from the city, and eventually the distinction will not exist.

It is already most of the way there.
