# The Undergreen

---

Begin with what you can feel before you see anything.

Moisture. Not rain — rain implies sky, and there is no sky here. Moisture as a property of the air itself, the way cold stone drinks condensation and gives it back as breath. Your sleeve is damp where it brushed the wall coming in, and the wall was made of something that had no business being a wall.

---

The space is small. That is the first surprising thing about it. You expect wilderness at this density of life to sprawl, to go on until it breaks you with distance. But this place has edges. You can feel them the way you feel the walls of a room in darkness — not by touching them but by the particular quality of the air near them, the way sound does not travel far before it is softly eaten.

It is perhaps the size of a market square. Perhaps smaller. The ceiling, if ceiling is the word, is low — six meters, perhaps eight at the peak of what must be called the nave, though nothing was built to be a nave. The shape arrived at something like vaulting by accident, by the patient insistence of stems.

---

Now the plants.

They are not decorating this place. They *are* this place. The distinction between floor and wall and ceiling has been resolved — dissolved — by decades or centuries of patient vegetable colonization. What you stand on is root-mat compressed to something almost solid, a lattice of rhizomes and adventitious roots that has accepted your weight the way packed earth accepts it, giving slightly underfoot, springy at the edges of each step. Beneath that mat: something harder. Old foundation. Poured concrete in the industrial fashion, cracked along lines that roots chose for them.

The walls — and they were walls, once, before they became this — are entirely occluded. Every centimeter of original surface has been claimed. Mosses first, as always, the primary colonizers, now so thick they hold a finger's width of water in their bodies, dark green going almost black in the near-absence of light. Over the mosses: ferns in improbable number, their fronds emerging from the wall horizontally, finding whatever light exists and flattening themselves into it. Over the ferns: vines, several species engaged in their long slow argument about priority, overlapping, strangling, sometimes fusing where two stems have grown into each other and forgotten the distinction. The vines have found the old structural members — steel I-beams, you think, though they are almost entirely sheathed now in cellulose and chlorophyll — and use them as the scaffolding they have always been.

At the top of this living wall, where the vaulting begins, something interesting is happening. A species you cannot name has learned to grow outward from the wall rather than up it. Its stems are thick as wrists and protrude horizontally for half a meter before curling upward, and from each stem hang pale tendrils — not roots exactly, not leaves exactly, something the plant invented for this specific problem of existing in near-darkness. The tendrils move very slowly in air that barely moves. They are searching for something. Whether they find it is unclear. They have been searching for a long time.

---

The darkness.

It is not absolute. Absolute darkness would be restful. This darkness has exceptions that make it worse — small phosphorescent patches on certain fungi where the wall meets the floor, cold blue-white light that illuminates nothing beyond itself, just enough to show you the shape of its own glow. Somewhere in the upper reaches, a thin line of amber, industrial amber, old streetlight amber leaking through a gap where the vegetation has not yet fully sealed the structure. It illuminates a column of air. In that column: spores, or dust, or something else, drifting upward against reason.

Your eyes adjust to a world of dark greens and darker greens, black where the shadows pool deepest, occasional silver where water runs down a stem.

---

There is water here in the way the parameters promised: intermittently, pocketwise, as evidence rather than presence.

On one wall — the north wall, you decide, though there is no north — a slow seep has organized itself into something between a stain and a stream. Water comes from somewhere above, moves down the living wall by capillary action mostly, occasionally gathering into a small rivulet that runs along a horizontal stem before dropping to the root-mat floor. The sound of this: almost nothing. A sound below the threshold of casual attention but above the threshold of silence, a sound your ears find when you stop making other sounds yourself. Drip. Long pause. Drip. The interval is not regular but it is almost regular, which is worse somehow.

Elsewhere: small pools in depressions in the root-mat, dark water, impossible to gauge depth, holding whatever lives in dark still water. You don't look too long into the pools.

---

Now the fauna, because they are here, they are everywhere, they are simply not announcing themselves.

The sound of this place is mostly quiet. But mostly quiet is doing a great deal of work. Inside the quiet: rustling that stops when you pay attention to it. The chitinous sound of insects moving through stems. A high thin frequency that might be bat echolocation or might be something a bat would not recognize as its own. 

Look long enough at any surface — the root-mat floor, the mossy wall, the hanging tendrils — and it resolves into motion. Isopods the size of thumbnails tracing their slow circuits. A beetle, iridescent even without light, standing motionless on a frond in the exact posture of something listening. Small pale worms working through the mat beneath your feet, felt rather than seen, a slight give in the floor that goes still when you shift your weight.

There are things in the upper canopy. The shape of them is hard to fix. They move when you are not watching them directly and stop when you try to look at them properly, which is either the behavior of shy animals or the behavior of something else entirely. They make no sound. Their mass is suggested by the way stems move after they have left, the slight swaying of vegetation settling back. Several of them, you think. Possibly many.

Something — one thing, distinguishable from the general presence — is following the water-seep on the north wall downward. It is taking a very long time. You have been here long enough to notice its progress but not long enough to identify what it is.

---

The built elements.

This was a factory. The evidence is in the bones where the vegetation hasn't fully covered them. The scale of the concrete pours, the spacing of the structural columns, the remnants of a loading bay on what is now the east wall — a steel door frame standing open, the door itself long gone, the frame overgrown but present, giving onto what appears to be a corridor but is more likely a digestive organ of the larger plant structure, a throat that goes somewhere.

Industrial technology persists here in ghost form. There are pipes — cast iron, some copper — running along the ceiling's original path, now entirely ensheathed in root but still carrying something. You can tell because they are warmer than the air, barely, by perhaps a degree, but in a cool space a degree is perceptible as intimacy. Whether the warmth is residual industrial heat or the metabolic warmth of a particularly vigorous vine colony using the pipe as a trellis cannot be determined from the outside.

On the floor near what was the factory's center: the footprint of a large machine. The machine is gone but the floor remembers it — a rectangular area slightly different in texture, compressed differently, where the root-mat is thinner and harder. On this former machinery pad, nothing grows. A perfect bare rectangle in the otherwise continuous green. It is the most disturbing thing in the room.

Around the perimeter of the room at roughly three-meter height, a series of iron brackets that once held electrical conduit. Some conduit remains, hanging free of the wall where the anchors have given up, curled down into the vegetation. Whether the conduit is live is a question you do not test.

---

The emergence.

Here is what is new: in the concrete floor in three places, cracks. Not the old cracks that the roots exploited long ago — those are ancient history, already healed over with biological scar tissue. These are new cracks. They are fresh in the geological sense, which means they happened in the last decade, possibly the last few years. The edges of these cracks are sharp. Nothing has colonized them yet. They are the only truly bare geology in the space.

From one of these cracks: a smell. Mineral, cold, the smell of something that has not been surface before. What is beneath this factory floor is unknown and apparently is beginning to make itself available.

The root-mat around these cracks has begun — just begun, you have to look carefully — to explore them. The finest rootlets, the exploratory kind, the ones that go first into unknown territory. In a decade the cracks will be full of roots. Right now they are bare and new and the roots are just arriving at their edges, tasting the mineral cold, deciding.

---

The weirdness wants to announce itself, but the way weirdness works here is that it has already been operating throughout everything above and is only now becoming recognizable as such.

The tendrils hanging from the upper stems: they are not random. When you look at them as a whole rather than as individual things, they have a pattern. Distributed throughout the space at unequal but not arbitrary intervals, they describe a shape. You cannot determine what shape from any single position in the room because you would need to be outside the room and above it to see the pattern whole. The sense that the shape is there is present. The shape itself remains unavailable.

The pools of dark water in the root-mat are connected. Not visibly — no channel, no runoff between them. But disturb one, drop a small object into it, and the others respond. A slight shiver. Not a wave — something else, a resonance, a communication through a medium that isn't water.

The bare rectangle where the machine stood: once per some interval — you've been here long enough to observe it once and you did not observe the beginning of it, only the end — the bare rectangle warms. Measurably, if you put your hand close to it. The warmth rises, holds for what might be thirty seconds, and then drains back below perceptible. It does not repeat while you watch for it.

The thing on the north wall following the water downward has not arrived anywhere, but you have the sense that it was not here when you arrived, which means it entered after you, which means the entrance behind you is not the only entrance.

---

Time in this place.

The active processes here are on mismatched timescales that do not coordinate with each other and do not coordinate with your own. The roots grow at root-speed, which is visible across years. The fauna moves at animal-speed. The temperature fluctuates at some cycle that doesn't match day or season — you cannot tell whether it is warming or cooling during the time you've been here. The fungal phosphorescence pulses at a rate that is very slightly too slow to be a pulse and too variable to be background.

You have been here for some duration you cannot accurately report. 

The dripping of water into the invisible pool has not changed. *Drip.* Long pause. *Drip.* The interval that is almost regular. Almost.

---

What this place is building toward, or away from, or around, is unclear. It is clearly doing something. The processes are multiple and active and the space is too small to contain all of them without them being aware of each other, and so they must be aware of each other, and what awareness means among roots and fungi and whatever is coming up through the new cracks and whatever is moving on the north wall is a question the place is entirely comfortable sitting with.

You, in it, are the emptiness. The vast space between presences.

You are what is sparse here.
