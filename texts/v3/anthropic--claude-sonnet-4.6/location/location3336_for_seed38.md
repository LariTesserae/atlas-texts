# The Canopy Plateau

---

Begin with the fact of it: the plateau is so old it has forgotten it was ever anything else.

Whatever mountains made it, whatever convulsion heaved this mass skyward, has been so thoroughly digested by time that the violence is gone. What remains is the finished thing — a high shelf of land, vast as a subcontinent, its edges so distant from its center that weather forms and dies over it without ever touching its rim. The plateau does not feel elevated. It feels like the world's upper face, the part the sky has always known. The lowlands, if they exist, are a rumor.

The surface beneath the vegetation — and there is always vegetation, in every direction, to every horizon — is ancient laterite, rusted red and soft as old bread, leached of everything sharp over so many millions of years of warm rain that it has settled into a kind of mineral patience. Rock, here, is not hard. Rock is a category that barely applies. What you stand on, if you could reach it, is more like the memory of rock — compacted, darkened, permeable, more interested in holding roots than resisting them. The roots have had their way completely.

---

The trees are not trees in the way the word usually means.

They are the substrate. They are the architecture, the ground plan, the walls and ceilings and bridges of the world. Stand anywhere on the plateau and you are inside them: inside their canopy, inside the lattice of their trunks, inside the green logic of their arrangement. They have grown for so long that they have grown through each other. Two trees will meet overhead and their cambium layers will fuse, will merge, will forget where one ended and the other began. Root systems have been continuous for so long that individual trees are a kind of convenient fiction, the way individual waves in an ocean are a fiction — patterns in something that does not actually stop.

The trunks are ancient and immense and covered. Covered is the word. Every surface wears something. The mosses come first — dense, architectural mosses in colors that do not have common names: the green that is nearly blue, the green that is nearly brown, the green that has given up and become a soft gray that is still technically green underneath. Lichens crust the mosses. Ferns root in the lichens. Bromeliads root in the ferns. The whole system is palimpsest — layer written over layer, life using the surface area of life to colonize more life. A single trunk, measured properly, accounting for the surface area of every organism growing on it, would have more square meters of living surface than a small field.

Water is always moving through this. The plateau sits high enough to catch cloud — not dramatically, not with grand scarves of mist, but persistently, quietly. A certain percentage of each day the air is at saturation, and water simply precipitates out of it onto leaf surfaces, onto the waxy sides of bromeliads, into the cups they form. The water moves down. It moves in tiny braids along the undersides of leaves, gathering, dripping to the leaves below. The sound of this is the background sound of the world: a slow percussion, diffuse, sourceless. You cannot hear individual drops. You hear the sum of drops, spread across a space too large to resolve into individual sounds, arriving at your ears as a whisper that is not quite a whisper, a hiss that is not quite a hiss. The sound of distributed water.

The ground — which is not quite ground, which is root-mat and decomposed matter and living moss compressed by its own depth — holds water without flowing. It is saturated but not pooled. Press your hand into it and water comes to the surface around your fingers. Lift your hand and the water closes back. The whole plateau is like this: holding, not releasing, a vast slow sponge keeping its water in long conversation with the roots.

---

There are no rivers. Or there is one river, but it is all of them: water wicking through root systems over distances that would take days to walk, moving by capillary and pressure and the slow decisions of gravity in a medium that resists simple flow. Eventually it emerges at some edge of the plateau, eventually it falls, but here on the plateau proper water has no destination. It is content to remain.

---

The fauna is everywhere and mostly quiet.

There are insects that have developed the same evolutionary patience as everything else here. Their generations are short but their populations are immensely old, adapted over timescales proportional to the plateau's own age — which is to say: adapted past the point of visible struggle, adapted into a kind of mastery that looks like stillness. A beetle on bark is almost impossible to see. Not because it is hiding exactly, but because it has become, over enough generations, a thing that belongs to bark in the way bark does. Its elytra carry the exact color of lichen at that altitude, in that moisture regime, on that species of tree. It is not camouflage as strategy. It is camouflage as completed identity.

The birds are few and quiet. Where they exist, they move slowly through the understory, making sounds like questions with no follow-up: a single note, or two, low, then silence for a long time. They do not sing in the sense of sustained song. They signal, check, go still. The canopy absorbs sound quickly — all those surfaces, all that water in the air — and the birds seem to have accepted that sound does not carry here and modified their communications accordingly. They have grown close to each other instead. You will find two of them within two meters of each other far more often than chance would suggest, navigating the same branch with a familiarity that suggests they have not been apart in some time.

There are amphibians in the water-cups of the bromeliads. Full ecosystems, in some of the larger ones — water fleas, mosquito larvae, small translucent creatures with too many legs, a frog no larger than a thumbnail that has never needed to leave the bromeliad it was born in and probably will not. Each bromeliad is a contained world. The plateau is a world of contained worlds.

Snakes move through the root-mat, slow and deliberate, their movement leaving no sound, only a faint trail in the moss that closes within the hour.

Something larger lives here. You will not see it. But the density of the understory does not frighten the other animals in the way it should if nothing large lived in it. Instead the smaller animals are oriented toward certain directions at certain times — a general, diffuse awareness, not of threat but of presence. Whatever it is moves slowly, as everything here moves slowly, and it has been here long enough that its presence is simply a fact of the environment, the way the humidity is a fact, the way the dim green light is a fact.

---

The light.

Light is a subject here. It is not something that enters casually. It falls through so many layers of canopy — the emergent giants, then the secondary canopy, then the sub-canopy, then the shrub layer, then the ferns — that by the time it reaches the ground level it has been filtered into something that barely resembles its origin. The green of it. It is not sunlight with a green tint. It is something that has become green, a new kind of light, warm but dim, casting almost no shadow because shadow requires contrast and the light here is too diffuse for contrast. Everything is equally lit in this green dim. You can see clearly. But there is no directional brightness, no angle from which light comes more than any other. Overhead is brighter — there is an upward gradient — but even directly above you there are layers of leaves and, above those, the soft atmospheric diffusion of a half-clouded sky.

Time of day is illegible from inside. There is a dimmer version of this light and a slightly brighter version. The brighter version might be noon or might be morning — the canopy erases the difference. Night is different in kind rather than degree: the light becomes not dimmer but absent, and then the bioluminescence of certain fungi on the fallen logs provides a replacement light, cold and blue-white, a kind of anti-light that illuminates the immediate without giving depth.

---

The structures, when you find them, are hard to call structures.

Here: a line of stones arranged with an intentionality that cannot be natural, now so thoroughly colonized by moss that the stones are green mounds, their shapes suggested rather than present. The arrangement implies a wall but might have been a path-marker. The scale is human. The age is very great. Whatever made them is long gone, but gone in the way things go on a plateau that erases slowly: not violently absent but gradually absorbed. The stones are still there. They are simply less stone than they were and more world.

Elsewhere, a depression that is too regular to be erosion, too large to be animal-made, its sides grown over so completely that only the geometry of it — a rough circle, a depth consistent around its circumference — suggests intention. It holds water. It is a small pond ringed with the same root-mat as everything else, its surface carrying a complete bloom of tiny floating plants, each a green dot, together making a surface that looks more like land than water until you press into it and find the water beneath.

A third thing: two trees growing around something that was between them once. Their trunks have curved to accommodate it and continued to grow and now, between them, there is a space shaped like an absence, an organic archway that once framed whatever it framed and now frames a view of more trees. Whatever was there — post, stone, wall — has been absorbed or decomposed. Only the memory of it remains in the shapes of the trees, in the way their wood records the pressure of a thing that pressed them as they grew.

No one has been here with tools for a very long time. The question of whether anyone was ever here with tools is not perfectly answerable. The plateau does not say.

---

The strangeness is not located.

It is not a single thing that is wrong or improbable. It is a quality of the whole, a sense that the scale and the age and the density have produced a place that has its own physics — not different laws exactly, but different emphases. Time moves at a different rate here because nothing marks it usefully. You could watch a square meter of ground for an hour and see: a beetle traverse it. A drop of water travel from one leaf to another. A tendril extend itself by a millimeter. These are the events. These are what happen. The scale of events is microscopic and the background is continental and there is nothing in between, no medium scale, no human scale of cause and effect. 

The trees do not feel like they have been growing for thousands of years. They feel like they have been growing forever, which is not the same thing. Forever is a quality, not a duration. These trees have it.

In the afternoon — which you infer rather than see — the air reaches full saturation and the plateau exhales. Vapor condenses on every surface simultaneously. The sound of it is not audible, but you feel it on your skin: a slight increase in the weight of the air, a sense that the boundary between inside and outside — between body and atmosphere — is becoming negotiable. The moss on the ground brightens by one degree of green. The bromeliads accept more water. The roots take it up or hold it or pass it sideways to other roots.

The whole system breathes in a rhythm too slow to perceive in a single sitting. But over days — if you stayed, if you were patient enough to belong to the plateau's timescale rather than your own — you would feel it: the expansion and contraction of the living mass, the daily cycle of transpiration and uptake, the seasonal (nearly imperceptible, the seasons here being more suggestion than fact) shifts in which species bloom or fruiting bodies emerge from the rot-mat or the bioluminescent logs brighten by some small degree.

---

The plateau is not waiting for anything. That is the hardest thing to understand about it.

Other wildernesses seem to be in process — glaciers advancing or retreating, forests recovering or burning, coasts building or eroding. The plateau has completed its process. It reached its current state so long ago that the current state has become the permanent state, or something indistinguishable from permanent given the timescales anything mortal can inhabit. The erosion is done. The succession is done. The ecosystem has climaxed and held its climax for longer than most ecosystems have existed.

What you enter when you enter the plateau is the finished form of a world. Not dead — teeming, wet, warm, dense with the business of living — but done in the way a sentence is done when the final word arrives. Complete. Not closed, because life is never closed, but complete in its structure. Every niche is filled. Every surface is used. There is no bare rock, no open soil, no unoccupied airspace. The ecological grammar of the place has been written and re-written until it no longer requires revision, and what looks like stillness is not stillness but the sum of all the revisions, held.

You are very small in it. Not in a sublime way — sublime requires contrast, a sense of being overwhelmed by something beyond you, and the plateau does not overwhelm. It simply includes. You are a presence in it the way the beetle is a presence, the way the frog in the bromeliad is a presence: warm, wet, metabolizing, participating in the slow exchange of water and carbon and warmth that the plateau conducts without interruption, has been conducting since before anything with the cognition to be surprised by it arrived.

Stand still long enough and the moss will note the moisture of your footprint. Something will come to investigate it. The air will close around you.

You are here. That is all that is required.
