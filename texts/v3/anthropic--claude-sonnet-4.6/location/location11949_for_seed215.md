# The High Plateau

---

The plateau is so large that its edges are not a feature of it. You cannot stand anywhere on it and perceive a boundary. It simply extends, in every direction, until the atmosphere thickens with distance and the land becomes indistinguishable from sky. Weather forms here independently — low systems that never touch the lowlands, pressure gradients that exist only at this altitude, clouds that are born and die without ever descending. The curvature of the planet is a real factor. The plateau has its own meteorology the way a continent does, the way an ocean does.

The rock is new. That is the first strange thing you notice, if you are the kind of person who notices rock. The stone underfoot has not yet settled into itself. The plateau is rising — measurably, actively — driven by something deep and tectonic that has not finished its work. The grain of the stone is unweathered. Edges are still edges. Fracture faces catch the light at angles that older rock doesn't, that softer rock can't. There are places where a seam will open in the ground over the course of a season, a new cleft a centimeter wide and running for two kilometers before anyone finds it. The stone is grey-white where it's freshest, tending toward rust-ochre where water has been working on it. Both colors are clean. Neither is subtle.

The water is everywhere and nowhere. The plateau is too cold and too exposed for standing surface water in most places, but it seeps. It weeps from rock faces along the scarps. It beads on the flat stone in the mornings after temperature swings. Shallow depressions — some of them fresh enough to have no name yet, formed when a seam closed and a basin opened — hold a few inches of iron-grey water reflecting the sky. After precipitation, which comes without warning and without much ceremony, just a lowering of the light and then moisture arriving almost horizontally, the whole surface glistens. It doesn't flood. The rock is too new and too fractured and the water finds its way down through cracks into the dark almost immediately. But for an hour, two hours, the plateau is a mirror and you can see the clouds from below.

The cold is not theatrical. At 0.4 on a scale of three, this is not the cold of polar ice or high Himalayan exposure. It is the persistent cold of a place that does not warm properly, that sits just high enough to miss the warmth the lowlands take for granted. Morning temperatures a few degrees above freezing. Afternoon temperatures that seem like they should be pleasant but somehow aren't, a chill in the wind that reasserts itself whenever you stop moving. The kind of cold that accumulates in clothing over the course of a day, that you notice most when you try to work with your hands. Nothing freezes dramatically. Things just stay a little too cool.

---

The settlement is spread across perhaps four hundred square kilometers of this stone.

It does not look like a city from the ground. The structures are low — rarely more than two stories when measured in the old way, which is to say in meters of enclosed vertical space, which is almost the wrong unit for what these buildings are. They distribute horizontally. They follow the fracture lines in the plateau surface, which run in loose but discernible patterns: primarily northeast-southwest, with secondary joints crossing. The construction mirrors this. Streets, to the extent that concept applies, tend to run with the grain. Buildings sit in the angles between.

The materials are the plateau itself, processed. The grey-white stone is everywhere, cut and stacked and bonded by something that is not mortar but functions similarly — a substance that sets into the stone chemically, that becomes, over time, indistinguishable from the surrounding material. Older buildings on the plateau, some of them decades old, have begun to be reabsorbed by the geology. The distinction between structure and bedrock softens. In the newest districts, where the fractures are freshest, buildings stand with their foundations literally growing into stone that wasn't there when they were designed. The engineers know this. They account for it.

Infrastructure runs underground, in the fracture network. Not in tunnels exactly — the cracks themselves have been widened and channeled and managed. Heat, water, power, communication: all of it moves through the native geometry of the plateau, finding paths that were already there. The technology for doing this is not fully described by the engineering literature most people learn. It involves materials that respond to pressure differentials at scales smaller than visible, that route and reroute autonomously. In practice, the system manages itself. In theory, it is doing something adjacent to computation in the rock, though no one uses that language officially.

Power comes from the rising. The tectonic activity that is building the plateau releases energy continuously. The infrastructure network captures a fraction of it — stress in stone, thermal differentials, the slow work of pressure at depth — and distributes it through the same fractured channels that carry everything else. The plateau is, functionally, generating its own energy by existing. By growing. The city is, in this sense, parasitic on the geology, or symbiotic with it, depending on how you think about such relationships.

---

The population is sparse but present.

People move through the city in small groups or alone, separated by the scale of the place and the horizontal sprawl of the construction. In any given view, you might see three or four figures, widely spaced, moving with the purposeful unhurry of people who have calibrated their pace to distances. Sound does not carry well here — the wind takes it, and the stone absorbs the rest, and the low structures don't reverberate the way dense urban canyons do. The city is mostly quiet. Footsteps, occasionally. The low structural sound of the wind on surfaces. Distant movement of machinery operating somewhere beneath your feet, so low in frequency that you feel it before you hear it.

The fauna is the loudest thing.

The plateau has been colonized by species that came with the construction and by species that preceded the construction and have not left, and the two populations are working out their relationship continuously. Birds are the primary presence. The new rock surfaces and the fracture patterns create nesting habitat that didn't exist here before the plateau began to rise, and birds have taken it enthusiastically — not in forests or trees, of which there are almost none, but in crevices and ledges and the angles of construction where structural meets geological. They are small and cold-adapted and extremely numerous. Their sound is thin and high and intermittent, swallowed by the wind before it can accumulate into anything dense, but there are enough of them that there is always something, always some call somewhere in the near or middle distance.

Invertebrates exist in the fracture network in numbers no one has fully characterized. They interact with the infrastructure in ways that are tolerated because they appear to be, on balance, neutral or beneficial — cleaning mineral deposits from channels, aerating passages that would otherwise clog. The technology is indifferent to them in the way that a large system is indifferent to small actors who are not doing it harm.

Larger animals pass through. The plateau, vast as it is, is connected to lower terrain at its margins, and ungulates of various kinds cross it seasonally, moving in small groups across the open stone, pausing at the shallow depressions when there's water, navigating around the construction with the pragmatic flexibility of animals that have decided built environments are just another kind of terrain. They do not stay. They are in transit. Their passing is noted by the birds, who react briefly, and then the quiet resumes.

---

The light is ordinary in quality, which is to say northern and high-altitude and without drama. The sun is present more often than not. Shadows are long in the mornings and evenings, which are long themselves at this latitude. Midday is flat and even. The plateau's own weather occasionally produces cloud cover that shifts the light into something diffuse and grey and honest, the kind of light that shows you exactly what things are without interpretation.

At the eastern margins of the city, where the newest fractures are opening and the construction has followed most recently, you can stand and watch the ground. If you stand still long enough, and the light is right, and you have patient eyes, you can sometimes see it — or convince yourself you can — the millimeter-scale evidence of the plateau's ongoing becoming. A freshly opened seam catching afternoon light. A crystal face where none was yesterday. The construction engineers mark these events on their models and adjust. The buildings are designed with tolerances that would seem excessive for geology anywhere else. Here they are routine.

The place is not beautiful in the way landscapes market themselves. It is not dramatic in the way high places often perform. It is large and cold and wet underfoot and filling slowly with life and construction, and beneath everything, constantly, the low structural work of a geology that has not finished deciding what it wants to be. The city is building itself here because the plateau offered the fractures, the energy, the stone. The plateau is building itself because it has no particular opinion about the city one way or the other, and the forces driving it from below do not make distinctions.

Both processes continue. Both are some time from finished. In this they are, perhaps, suited to each other.
