# The High Dark Meadow

---

The darkness here is not the darkness of a cave. It has no ceiling you can feel. You become aware of this slowly — the way you become aware of a smell only after it has been present for some time — that the space above you is open, that whatever vault exists overhead is simply not lit, or is lit by something your eyes cannot use. The air moves. Warm air, carrying the smell of wet soil and what might be jasmine, or might be something jasmine is a poor approximation of. You are, somehow, outside. The darkness is weather.

---

**The ground.**

Start there, because that is what you can verify.

The ground is ancient in a way that has passed through all the stages of being ancient and arrived somewhere else. Erosion here is a completed project. The rock beneath is not sharp, not even rounded — it is *smoothed*, in the way that the word smooth fails slightly. The kind of smooth that comes after millions of years of water and wind and the weight of everything that has grown and died and grown again on top of it, until the mineral itself seems to have given up its crystalline ambitions and settled into something more like a recommendation. Like stone that has accepted its situation.

You cannot see this stone, but you can feel it when you crouch, and you can hear how your footsteps change over it — a lower register, slightly deadened, as though the rock is thick enough that its echo has somewhere to go and doesn't bother returning.

The soil over it is thin in some places, and in those thin places the stone shows through in long, bare, low-domed outcroppings, like the foreheads of something enormous and patient sleeping just under the surface. Between these, the soil is dark and deep and holds water the way a sponge holds water — not retaining it, exactly, but in conversation with it. When you press your boot down, the ground presses back with a sound like a word being swallowed.

This is high ground. You know it from the way the air behaves — cooler at the edges, carrying pressure differently, the sense that weather happens *around* you rather than *over* you. Somewhere below there are lower places, though you cannot see them and in the dark you could not say how far down they begin. The elevation is there in the body, in the lungs, in the slight headache that started an hour after you arrived and that you have stopped noticing.

---

**The water.**

It is everywhere and mostly invisible.

When you stand still and your eyes have adapted as much as they are going to adapt — which is not very much, which is barely at all — you can sometimes see water. Not a surface of it, but a glint of it, a single-point reflection of something. There is no obvious light source for this reflection. The water reflects something the sky is doing that you cannot see directly. This is not comforting, but it is interesting.

The wetness is not dramatic. There is no river here, no waterfall. The water is more like a conviction the landscape holds, a pervasive and unhurried belief in moisture. It seeps from cracks in the old stone. It collects in the cups of broad leaves and when those leaves move in the slow warm wind, the water relocates with a sound like a whispered correction. The soil is full of it. Put your hand flat on the ground and hold it there and the ground will, after a moment, wet your palm.

There may be small standing pools. You cannot see them but twice you have nearly stepped into something that gave way differently than the rest of the ground, a sudden cold surprising softness, and you stepped back. The fauna — and there is fauna, you can hear this continuously — knows where these pools are and uses them.

The air condenses. In the warmer moments, when the slow wind drops, you can feel the moisture gathering on your face and hands. Not as rain. As a decision the air has made about where the water should be.

---

**The vegetation.**

Lush to a degree that requires renegotiating your sense of what lush means at altitude.

At lower elevations, lushness means a kind of tropical confidence, a broad-leafed assertiveness, vines going where they want. Here it is different. The plants have had time — enormous amounts of time, the same ancient-smoothed time that has worked on the rock — and they have used it not to grow taller or faster but to grow *thoroughly*. Every surface is occupied. Every crack in the old stone holds something. There are mosses here so old and so established that they no longer look like moss growing on rock; they look like a form of rock, a soft variety, green-black in the darkness, yielding to the touch the way memory yields.

The grasses — and much of what you walk through is grass, or something that grows at roughly grass height and behaves like grass in the wind — are dense. They have deep roots in the wet dark soil and they hold the ground together the way a community holds a practice together, each one reinforcing the others, the whole mat of them older than any individual stem. When you push through, they return to position slowly. They do not spring back. They *resume*.

Trees exist here but not many and not close together. This is the emptiness this place has — not empty of life, but low-density in the way a meadow is low-density, with wide air between presences. The trees you can find by reaching for them, by walking until your hand meets bark, and when you find one it is enormous around, wider than two people could embrace, and the bark is not rough so much as *layered*, a record of time kept in the tree's own body. These trees do not have visible canopies in this darkness. They go up and continue going up and you cannot find where they stop. Their presence is a column of something, a change in the air. Damp warmth collects around their roots. Small things live in the grass at their base.

There are flowers. You know this from the smell more than anything else. The scent is complex, shifting, sometimes sweet in a way that seems almost irresponsible, sometimes green and sharp. In the slow warm wind the smell moves past you in long gradual waves like very slow music. You put your hand out and find a flower once — a wide soft thing, petals like damp cloth, at head height, on a stem you cannot find when you reach down for it. The flower does not recoil. It seems accustomed to being found in the dark.

---

**The sound.**

This is the loudest thing about the place, and also the thing that takes longest to parse.

What you hear first is: a lot. A dense continuous sound that you initially interpret as one thing and then gradually separate into many things, the way you separate harmonics from a note when you've listened to it long enough.

There are insects — many, varied, a continuous underlayer of stridulation and wing-movement and things you cannot name that drone on low frequencies, so low you feel them in the chest as much as hear them. This layer does not change much. It is a ground for everything else.

There are frogs. Many frogs. Their calls are spaced — call, silence, response from further off, silence, call again — in a slow rhythm that is not synchronized but creates a distributed pattern anyway, the way rain on a large roof creates rhythm without metronome. The frog sounds are deeply familiar and at the same time slightly wrong in the register, or in the pace, or in some quality of the resonance that you can't specify.

There are larger animals. You can hear them moving through the grass. They are unhurried. Their movement is a long slow displacement, the grass parting and returning. You hear breathing, once, large and slow, from something you cannot see and that does not approach. This does not feel like a warning, from either side. It feels like acknowledgment.

The wind. Low, warm, consistent, moving through the grass in a sound that is genuinely peaceful — the most peaceful sound the place makes — like breathing out.

Water, in its seeping and dripping and rearranging, contributes a constant small percussion, irregular, never building to anything, a sound like patience.

And over or under all of this — intermittent, not quite locatable in space — something else. A tone. Not exactly a sound. The way very low organ notes in a large building are sometimes more vibration than sound, something you experience in the bones before the ears make sense of it. It comes and goes on a slow cycle, slower than breath, slower than wind. There is no obvious source. You stop noticing it and then it stops and you notice its absence, and then it returns and you stop noticing it again.

---

**The built things.**

There are structures here, or were.

You find the first one by walking into it — a wall, about chest height, that was once a corner of something. The stone it's made from is the same old smooth stone from the ground, fitted together without mortar in a way that has held for long enough that the joints have been colonized by moss and the moss itself has become structural. The wall does not feel ruined. It feels *concluded*. The distinction matters here. Ruin implies something that failed. This is something that finished.

The structure it was part of is not quite reconstructible. There are not enough walls. But there are stones that were moved, that were placed. You can tell placed stone from fallen stone even in the dark — placed stone has an intention in its position that you can feel through your hands. The foundations of whatever this was describe a small footprint, a single room or two small rooms, and the whole thing is so overgrown that you find it mostly by the change in what you're walking on, the slight regularity under the moss, the angles.

There is a second structure that is different in kind. You nearly miss it because it is very small and very dark and very still. It is an object — not a building but a device, or something device-adjacent. About the size of a hatbox. Made of something that is not stone and not metal and not any material you have encountered, but which gives the impression of all three: heavy, slightly warm to the touch, not granular, not perfectly smooth. No seams you can find. No obvious user interface. But you can tell it is not natural because it is too regular, and because it produces the tone. The long slow subsonic cycle you have been hearing is, you realize when you find this thing and the tone happens again, coming from here. Or from something this thing is in communication with. The tone happens and then stops and you wait and it happens again and the interval is, you think, roughly the same, but too long to time accurately, too long to hold in working memory precisely. Minutes. More than minutes.

The object is warm in the way living things are warm, not in the way a machine is warm. There is no visible power source. There are no lights on it. When you hold your palm flat against it, the next pulse of the tone goes through your hand as much as through the air.

You do not move it. It does not seem like the kind of thing you move.

A third artifact: a path. Or what was a path. The grass grows over it but the grass grows differently — slightly lower, slightly more compressed — and the stone beneath is worn in a long arc that you can trace for about forty meters before losing it. Someone walked here for long enough that the stone remembers. The path curves gently and then ends at nothing — not at a destination, just at a place where the pattern stops being legible.

---

**The ecology.**

The fauna is abundant but, in keeping with the emptiness of the space, not clustered. Things are spread out. Things are *here*, but across a wide area, not in any one place.

You know there are large herbivores because you find their traces underfoot — prints in the wet soil, deep and wide, with a shape that might be hoofed or might be something else, something with more toes. And droppings that are grassy and do not smell bad, which is a detail that says something about the diet and also somehow about the place. The animals themselves keep their distance. You can hear them at the edge of hearing, and sometimes closer when the wind shifts, and once you feel the ground change faintly under your feet as though something large moved, some distance away.

There are small mammals in the grass. You feel them before you see them because you cannot see them. The grass moves where something runs. A sound of quick small feet, four-footed, confident, pausing, running again. They are not afraid of you in the way prey animals are usually afraid. They pause. They seem to look at you, though you cannot see them looking. Then they continue.

There is something in the air — wings, very quiet wings, which is unusual because quiet flying usually means large wings and slow flight, and these are slow but the scale of the wingbeats seems wrong, variable, not the metronomic rhythm of a single large bird but the irregular sampling of something that moves and rests and moves. More than one. You hear them cross overhead and then you hear them land in something and then nothing.

The insects are the most numerous and make the most collective sound and are almost completely imperceptible individually. This is normal for insects.

What is not quite normal: the distribution of warmth. Animals produce warmth. In the cool dark, if you hold still and your skin is good at sensing gradients, you can sometimes locate an animal by a change in temperature — a slight warm spot in the warm air, a concentration. This happens here too often to be coincidental, and sometimes there is no sound attached to the warmth, no movement, no smell, just an area of slightly increased temperature at roughly torso height, present for a moment, then gone. Whether this is an animal, or an artifact of the technology, or something that exists in a category that doesn't have a name yet, you cannot determine. You walk through one of these warm areas and feel it on both sides of your face as you pass through.

---

**The tech.**

It is not everywhere. This is important. The device in the meadow is not surrounded by other devices, is not part of an obvious system, leaves no waste, has no attendants.

But it is not the only evidence.

There is a place — you find it by the way the air changes, a slight directional coherence to the moisture in the air, as though the condensation is being managed rather than occurring naturally — where something is happening to the water cycle. The seeping from the rocks in that area is slightly too regular. Not mechanical-regular, but modulated, the way a stream is modulated by what is upstream. Something upstream of the visible world is intervening in how this rock releases water, and it is doing so on a slow cycle, and the effect is that the moisture in that area is distributed in a pattern that, if you had light to see it and time to map it, might resolve into something intentional.

There is a smell, twice, that has no biological source you can identify — not electrical, not chemical in a familiar way, but a neutrality that is itself unusual, a pocket of air that has had something done to it that left no trace except the absence of the smell of anything. It passes. The normal smell of the meadow returns.

The tone from the device: it is, you come to believe, a transmission. Not communication in the sense of language. More like a beacon, or like the resonant frequency of a structure you can't see, a large structure, expressed as a tone because tone is what a very slow oscillation sounds like at the scale of air. If you could hear much lower frequencies you would hear more. What you hear is a harmonic. A summary. The real signal is too slow for the ear.

This is technology in the sense that mathematics is technology: it is a use of the rules of the world so complete and so economical that it looks like nature. It has been here long enough that the moss grows on the part of the device that touches the ground. The moss doesn't seem harmed.

---

**The rules of this world.**

Darkness is the ordinary condition, not the exception. Vision is not the primary sense here; the animals know this and operate accordingly. Hearing, smell, and proprioception — the knowledge of your own body in space — are more reliable. The darkness is not a problem to be solved. It is the medium.

Time here is very long. The slow things — the erosion, the growth of the moss, the wearing of paths — have been happening for long enough that the metaphors of human history are not adequate scales. Geological is closer but still short. The stone has had the time it needed to become itself. The technology has had the time it needed to become part of the landscape. These are not separate processes.

Things are spread apart because that is how they work. The abundance is real but it is not crowded. The fauna is many but not dense. The built things are occasional, not systematic. The water is pervasive but not collected. This dispersal is a law of the place, or a preference, or an equilibrium reached over enormous time. Whatever tried to be dense here either failed or evolved away from density.

The warmth is structural. At this altitude, in this darkness, with this much moisture, you should be cold. You are not cold. The warmth is ambient and sourceless and wrong in a way that you stop noticing after a while, and then notice again, and stop noticing again.

Something attends to this place. Not in the sense of an operator or a guardian. In the sense that things that would otherwise deteriorate have not deteriorated. The device functions. The water moves as it should. The balance between the wet and the living holds. Whether the attendant is the technology, or the technology is how the attendant works, or the attendant is a word for a process so slow it has no actor — this is not clear.

---

You have been here for some time. You are not sure how long.

The tone pulses. The frogs call. The warm air moves through the old grass over the old stone and the water keeps making its small patient sounds. Something large and quiet is near you, or was near you, and is now less near. The moss under your hands is damp and deep and old.

You stay still.

The place continues.
