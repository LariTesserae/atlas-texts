# The Lacunate City

---

Begin with what you notice first, which is the space between things.

The city — and it is a city, unmistakably, despite every other way it defies the word — does not press in on you. It breathes. Wide corridors of open air run between the structures, and in those corridors the ground is flat and dark, a surface that is almost geological in character, something between compacted earth and sealed stone, its color the gray-black of graphite left out in weather. The structures themselves rise at intervals that feel almost botanical, the way trees space themselves in a forest practicing self-restraint, each one claiming what it needs and leaving the rest.

There is a lot of rest.

The light is low and sourceless. Not night — nothing about this feels like night — but a kind of permanent late afternoon with the sun behind several layers of clouds you cannot see. The sky, when you look at it, is a soft pewter, granular, like felt. It presses gently downward without oppression. You can see far in every direction, which is to say you can see the city extending outward across what must be a plain or a very gradual basin, other structures catching the dim light at various distances, the whole settlement rendered in tones of slate and moss and the faint amber of internal glow, and none of it crowds you.

---

Now the structures themselves, because they are the reason for everything.

They are tall, most of them, and thin relative to their height — the proportions of reeds or candles or certain fungi that grow on nurse logs, stretched upward with a kind of effortful elegance. Their surfaces are not stone. They are not metal. They are not any material that has a comfortable name, though *grown* is closer than *built*, and *grown* is still wrong. The walls breathe — this is not metaphor. At intervals too slow to watch but too fast to dismiss as imagination, the outer surfaces shift, contracting slightly along their vertical axes, releasing a barely perceptible exhalation of warm vapor. The vapor is what creates the condensation. It collects on every horizontal surface: on the flat ledges of the structures, on the dark ground, on the broad leaves of the vegetation that has threaded itself into the city's spaces with a tenacity that the city has clearly accommodated rather than resisted.

Look at one structure long enough and you begin to see that its surface is not uniform. There are regions of it that are translucent, not glowing but admitting some deeper low luminescence, like bioluminescence in a creature that has learned to be subtle about it. These patches shift. Slowly. The way clouds move when you've committed to watching them, imperceptibly then suddenly elsewhere. You realize you are looking at information. The surface is thinking, or relaying thought, or recording something — the distinction between these may not be operative here.

The structures connect to one another via elements that are almost impossible to describe to someone who has only known bridges and tunnels and cables. They are threads, or filaments, or perhaps they are closer to roots that have gone horizontal and decided to exist in open air. They run from structure to structure at various heights, some so high they vanish into the pewter sky's softness, some low enough that you could duck under them, and they pulse. Slowly. Irregularly. Not like a heartbeat — more like breathing, the same not-quite-rhythm as the structures themselves. The filaments are dry to the eye but damp to the touch, and when you touch one — if you touch one — it continues its pulse without acknowledging you, which is more unsettling than if it had recoiled.

---

The ground-level vegetation is where the world becomes more recognizable, but not comfortable.

It is lush, genuinely lush, the kind of lush that takes a long time to achieve and implies a long time before that. The plants are almost all ground-covering or low-climbing, as though they understand that the air above a certain height belongs to other agreements. Broad-leafed, intensely green things — green so deep it approaches teal at the center of each leaf and something closer to bronze at the edges. They grow in the wide spaces between structures with the confidence of things that have not been discouraged. Some trail along the filaments, their tendrils finding purchase in ways that suggest the filaments welcome it, though welcome is perhaps another word that needs revision here.

There are mosses. This is where the dampness concentrates: on the north faces of structures, in the seams between ground-panels, around the bases of any feature that juts horizontally. The mosses are extraordinary. They are a green so bright it seems to generate its own light, which it may, though this glow is so faint it only registers when your eyes have fully adjusted to the overall dimness. They form precise edges against the darker ground-material, as though they have negotiated their boundaries carefully.

Water appears where it appears. A shallow channel in the ground, cut cleanly — not eroded, the edges sharp, the corners nearly right-angled, as though something scored this line recently and with intention. The water in it moves slowly, perhaps an inch deep, carrying nothing, trailing nothing, its surface broken only by the occasional drip from a leaf above or an exhalation from a structure's lower ventral surface. There is no smell to the water. There is almost no smell to anything here, which you notice as an absence, an unusual quiet for the nose.

Rain falls, sometimes. When it does: a few drops, scattered, like the city is not worth drenching, or like the weather is making a point about restraint. The drops are cold relative to the ambient warm, and they raise a sound on the leaf surfaces that is the closest thing to music this place produces: a brief soft percussion, irregular, lasting thirty seconds and then gone.

---

The fauna.

You should not expect much. What exists here exists at the periphery of notice: a shape that was in your field of vision and then was not, too slow for flight and too fast for imagination. Once, along the base of a structure, something the length of a hand and the color of the moss moved with a patient, low, lateral motion and then was inside the structure's base, through the surface, which accepted it without apparent disruption. This was the most direct evidence of life you will receive.

There are also the sounds that fauna make in aggregate, across a landscape, when they are numerous — the sense of a place being inhabited, that undertone of living-rustling — and here that undertone is absent, which your nervous system may interpret as alertness or as loneliness depending on your disposition.

In the vegetation, occasionally, something has been disturbed. A leaf bent in a direction that doesn't follow from gravity or wind. A channel of flattened moss leading from one ground cover thicket to another. Something came through. Something went on about its business. It had the courtesy to leave no more than this.

---

The sounds.

Quiet is the frame. Within that frame:

The structures' breathing, which is barely a sound — more of a pressure against the ears' attention, something they expect before they have reason to.

The water in its channel, which makes a sound that is almost below hearing but registers as a slightly altered quality of the silence.

The filaments, when wind moves through them — and wind does move here, intermittently, from directions that don't always accord with one another — produce something between a tone and a texture. A low, brief resonance that has no harmonic series you can resolve. It comes and goes with the gusts and does not linger.

Your footsteps on the dark ground: absorbed. Not muffled — absorbed, as if the material is more interested in sound than in returning it.

If you stand still long enough, you may hear, from deep within the nearest structure, something that resembles the cycling of large and patient machinery, but slower than machinery, more deliberate, with pauses between cycles that feel purposeful. The pauses are where the meaning, if there is meaning, might live.

---

The city's logics.

Nothing here is finished. Not in a way that suggests incompletion — more in the way that a living thing is not finished, is always in its process of being. The structures are clearly adding to themselves at some small rate. At the base of one, if you crouch and look at the interface between building-material and ground, you can see that the material is advancing, at a pace well below perception, into the graphite earth. Eating is too violent a word. *Including* is closer.

The filaments between structures appear occasionally at points where no filament existed before. You do not see them form. You look away, you look back, there is a new one, already pulsing as though it has always been there, as though memory of its absence was the error.

The city has distributed itself across a wide basin without anything like a center. You keep expecting a center — some denser clustering, some point toward which the distribution makes sense as radiation — and you keep not finding it. The spacing between structures is neither perfectly regular nor randomly distributed. It follows some logic that requires a different kind of pattern-recognition than the one you were born with.

At the edges of the city, where the structures become less frequent and the ground-covering vegetation thickens — here the city trails into the surrounding plain without ceremony, the last few structures standing in what looks like open land, their filaments reaching back toward the rest, no wall or border or change in the quality of construction to mark the transition. The city just becomes the land. The land just becomes the city. The question of where one ends is not one the place chooses to answer.

---

What the weather does.

Active is the right word, but active at a pace that requires patience to observe. The clouds in the felt sky move, and their movement changes the quality of the dim light in ways that are meaningful without being dramatic. A slight warming of the gray when the cloud-cover thins somewhere distant; a deepening of the dimness over a sector of the city that lasts an hour, then lifts. In this way the city is never quite the same as it was an hour ago, is always being revised by light or its absence.

The condensation on the structures collects, beads, runs down the exterior surfaces in thin streaks that follow the same paths every time — the exterior has learned or been built with specific channels — and drips from the lowest ventral ledges into the mosses below, which receive this moisture with the equanimity of things that have long organized their lives around exactly this gift.

Occasionally a warmer air moves through. This is the most dramatic thing that happens regularly. The warm air from — somewhere, the plain, some thermal the geology of the basin produces — comes through the corridors between structures and you can feel it on your face: not hot, not dramatic, just warmer than the ambient, like a breath from something that has been recently alive. The mosses respond. The broad-leafed ground covers shift on their stems with a patience that isn't passive. The filaments between structures alter their resonance frequency fractionally when the warm air moves through them, and for a few minutes the whole city is very quietly different in a way you couldn't point to.

Then it passes. The felt sky returns its pressure. The structures breathe.

---

You have been here a long time, or a short time; the city does not produce the conditions for time to have an obvious texture. You will leave with more questions than a landscape usually generates, which may be the point, or may be what was always true of you, and this place simply gave you room to notice it.

The space between the structures follows you. You carry a bit of the quiet. You think about the filaments for years, occasionally, without being able to say exactly why.
