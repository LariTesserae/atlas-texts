# The Sunken Works

---

The water here is not clear. It has the color of old iron, of tea brewed too long and left in the cold, a rust-brown darkness that thickens as you descend until color becomes a theoretical property rather than a real one. You are perhaps thirty feet down, though the floor is closer to fifteen — the water stacks above you, dense and pressured and *present*, not like a medium you move through but like something that has moved into you. The cold is not dramatic. It is complete. It has been complete for a long time.

---

This was a district. A processing quarter, most likely, given the piping. The buildings are low — two stories was the ambition here, occasionally three — and they crowd together the way industrial buildings do, with the specific intimacy of function rather than comfort: walls sharing walls, rooflines nearly touching, the gaps between structures measured in feet rather than streets. What were alleys are now channels, floored with silt and the slow accumulation of years, roofed with the bellies of pipes that run between buildings at every height. The pipes are iron, most of them. They have been in the water long enough to become something else — to fur themselves with layers of orange and brown oxidation, to develop the particular mineral concretions that water deposits when it has nowhere to hurry to. Some pipes have burst outward at seams or fittings. Some have simply opened with rust and hang like cut arteries, trailing filaments of mineralized debris that wave, very slightly, in currents too subtle to feel.

The buildings themselves have reached a kind of understanding with the flood. This is important. They are not *in the process of* being destroyed. They have been destroyed, and they have been submerged, and they have arrived at a state past destruction — a settled, ancient, finished state of being-underwater. The brick is original, nineteenth-century, the heavy kind, laid in Flemish bond and pointed with mortar that has long since dissolved and been replaced by colonies of organism — thin mineral films, occasional calcification, the patient work of chemistry over decades. Where windows were, the glass is mostly gone. The frames remain, iron and wood both, the iron outlasting the wood but only barely — wood here becomes a soft fibrous ghost of itself, dark and spongy, compressed by pressure and time into something that looks like wood but behaves like memory. The openings gape. Rectangles of deeper darkness.

---

You could swim through them. Through the windows, through the doorframes, through the holes in roofs where skylights were. This is the geometry now: not the linear geometry of streets and paths, but the three-dimensional grammar of submersion, where up is available, where you can pass through a ceiling and into a flooded second floor, where corridors that were once horizontal remain horizontal but above them is only water and the underside of someone else's floor.

The interiors are not empty. They are packed. Machinery still occupies the main floors — great iron frames of equipment too heavy to have moved anywhere, too solid to have dissolved. Looms, possibly. Or stamping presses. Or something specific to the processing of a material the district served. The machines are massive and patient in the water, each one its own small reef now, colonized unevenly: the horizontal surfaces accumulate silt in pale drifts, the vertical surfaces host the thin orange-brown iron-bacterium films, and in the joints and under-ledges, in the protected cavities where water stirs less, other things have taken hold. Not much. Not many. But something.

Small pale things, mostly. The fauna here is rare and specific — adapted, or simply arrived at a niche no other creature wanted. Something translucent and slow moves along the underside of a machine's main casting, gripping with whatever it grips with, feeding on whatever it feeds on. It is not quite a snail. It is not quite not a snail. It moves perhaps a foot in an hour. You would not see it move if you watched for a minute. You would only notice it was in a different place later, if you were paying the kind of attention you rarely pay.

---

The sound: almost none.

Almost none is not none. There is the distant mechanical sound — felt as much as heard — of something still operating somewhere in the district. A pump, perhaps, though what it is pumping and to what end is not clear; nothing here requires pumping. It is possible it was designed to run continuously and has simply continued. It produces a rhythm too slow to be a heartbeat, too regular to be geological: every four seconds, approximately, a deep metallic pulse that moves through the water and through the iron of every pipe and frame and hull in the district. You feel it in your sternum. You have stopped noticing it, the way you stop noticing your own pulse.

Beyond this: nothing much. The water absorbs sound differently than air. Sounds that would carry — the creak of settling material, the soft concussion of a silt-drift disturbing — die at close range. The acoustic world here is the world of contact: sound travels through things you touch, iron and brick and the pressure waves of the pulse, but the open water is a quiet place, an insulating medium, and whatever sounds the district makes to itself, you hear only the ghost of them.

---

There is kelp on the outer walls. Thin ribbons of it, dark green approaching black at this depth, barely moving. This is the vegetation: sparse, clinging to the edges of things, finding purchase where mortar has weathered away or where iron provides. It does not soften the district. It does not green it or naturalize it. It simply *attaches* — the way anything attaches to something already established, taking what is given, not transforming the host. Higher on the outer walls, where light once reached in better quantities, there are ghost-traces of heavier growth: the holdfasts remain, calcified and empty, their fronds long since died away as the water deepened or the light changed or the temperature finally dropped below what they could sustain. Evidence of a prior ecology. The current one is thinner.

---

There is a courtyard at the center of the district — not designed as a courtyard, but formed as one, where four buildings face inward and the space between them amounts to perhaps forty feet by fifty. This is the largest open space. Above it, if the water were clear, you might see something like sky — the brightened surface, fractionally more luminous than the surrounding dark. It is not clear. The brownness of the water obscures any distinction between depths. What you see above is what you see on all sides: the same graduated darkness, dense and material.

In the courtyard, a vertical pipe has corroded through its base fitting and fallen at an angle, leaning against one wall at perhaps thirty degrees. From this pipe, at the break point where it was pressurized and expelled its contents for years before the pressure equalized, there is a mineral formation — a limestone-like accumulation of calcium carbonate and iron oxide, built up in successive rings over however many years the water has been warm enough for this chemistry, then cold enough to slow it, then warm again with the mechanical heat of the pump's operation, cycling. The formation is dense and knobbed, dark orange at the outside and pale cream at the fresh growth (if growth is still happening — it may have stopped; at this temperature, the chemistry is slow). It is approximately the size and shape of a crouching person. It is not a crouching person. It does not become less strange the longer you look at it.

---

Light comes from two sources.

First: a glow somewhere beneath the surface of the silt in the largest building's floor, a diffuse bluish-white luminescence of uncertain origin. Bioluminescent organism in the substrate, possibly — a bacterial colony, or something living in the pore spaces of the silt that activates under certain conditions and has been activating, on and off, ever since the silt accumulated above it. It does not illuminate. It implies illumination. By it you can see that the silt floor exists. Nothing else.

Second: the pipes themselves. Not all of them, and not constantly. The pulse — the four-second mechanical pulse — is accompanied, in certain pipes, by a faint electrochemical flash, a phosphorescence in the oxide layer on the inner wall of the iron, visible through the corrosion-holes, for half a second every four seconds. This is the light. It is very faint. It creates, in the clustered pipes running between buildings, a rippling slow-motion traveling luminescence — the pulse moves through the pipe network at a speed you can actually track, building to building, junction to junction, the light traveling the physical infrastructure of the district — and for the half-second of each pipe's flash you can see what the pipe is adjacent to, what the pipe has become part of, the specific texture of its surface, and then it is gone.

---

The district does not feel abandoned. Abandonment implies that inhabitants were the point and they are now absent. This place does not feel like it was the point and then vacated. It feels like it has been absorbed into a longer process, a process that was always going on behind and beneath the one conducted by whoever built it. The water is doing something, slowly. The chemistry is doing something slowly. The pump is maintaining something — pressure in a system, a flow between nodes, a circulation whose purpose was industrial and now simply *is*. The pale snail-things are eating and presumably reproducing and moving their foot per hour. The bioluminescence in the silt is cycling through whatever cycle it cycles through.

The four-second pulse moves through the iron.

The cold holds everything in its specific way — not frozen, not preserving, but *slowing*, making all the processes long, making the timescale geological, making your presence here an event of no consequence, a brief warmth-trace that will be cooled within seconds of your leaving.

The mineral formation in the courtyard crouches beside its fallen pipe.

You are, probably, the strangest thing that has happened here in some time. The pale organisms do not acknowledge you. The bioluminescence does not respond to you. The pulse comes and goes, indifferent, the pipe-light traveling the network, the darkness reasserting between pulses with the confidence of something that has had the place to itself for longer than you have been alive.
