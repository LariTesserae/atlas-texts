# The Fabricated Plateau

---

Begin with the altitude, because everything else follows from it.

You are high — not mountain-high, not the razor-peak variety of high, but the sustained high of a plateau that has committed to its elevation and refuses to descend. The horizon is not a horizon so much as a ledge, a termination, a place where the constructed surface simply ends and the sky begins. The sky is enormous and pale and very bright. Not blue exactly — bleached, like fabric left in sun, like something that was blue once and has been slowly evacuating that quality for years. The light comes from everywhere and nowhere, diffuse and total, shadowless in the way of overcast days except there are no clouds, only this sourceless luminosity pressing down on everything at once.

And the surface you stand on is not ground.

---

The first thing you understand is that there is no earth here — not in any stratum, not underneath anything, not as a remembered substrate. The plateau is engineered. Every square meter of it. But the engineering is old enough that you might almost miss it, the way you might almost miss the seams in very good wallpaper.

The floor of the plateau is composed of massive sectional plates — hexagonal, predominantly, but interspersed with irregular fills where hexagons couldn't close the gaps. Each plate is roughly four meters face-to-face, cast in something that was once concrete but has since been infiltrated, mineralized, transformed by decades of condensation into a calcium-gray material that rings faintly hollow when you step on it, like the world is a drum with air inside. The plates are slightly proud of one another — uplifted at the edges by thermal cycling, by settling, by whatever is underneath them moving in slow increments over years. The gaps between plates are two, three, sometimes five centimeters wide. Dark in there. Damp.

The surface of each plate bears fine erosion marks — not old erosion, not the deep patient grooves of geology, but the fresh shallow scratching of recent process. Wind has moved particulate across these surfaces. Rain has pooled and evaporated and pooled again, leaving tideline rings of mineral deposit. The edges of the plates where they've lifted are sharp, unchamfered, geometrically new. Whatever this place is, it has not been here long enough to soften its own edges. The built world is young here, in the way that young-built things are — not charming, not weathered into character, but raw in a way that seems almost unfinished, as though the last crew has been gone for six months and something else moved in.

---

Everywhere — everywhere — are the machines.

They are not large machines, individually. That is part of what makes them so dense. If there were enormous machines, there would be space between them, negative space defined by their bulk. But the machines here are mostly the size of appliances, of furniture, of large animals. They crowd the surface of the plateau in clusters and arrangements that suggest organization without being obvious about it. There are lanes between the clusters, yes. You can navigate. But looking from any point across even a short distance, the machines overlap visually, one obscuring another, and the effect is of saturation — of a world where the ratio of machine to space has been tuned to just below unbearable.

What are they doing?

Processing. That's the word that keeps arriving. They are processing the air, or the condensation, or each other. Many of them have intake vents — hooded openings in their flanks or tops through which the cool plateau air is drawn in measurable volumes. You can feel, standing near a cluster, the gentle pull of this collective respiration. Not dramatic. Not a wind. Just a mild and constant beckoning, as though the machines are curious about the air and keep inviting it inside. Other machines have emission ports: small stacks, narrow nozzles, vented panels from which something is released — vapor, mostly, in quantities too small to be dramatic but too regular to be accidental. The vapor catches the plateau light and becomes visible for a moment and then is gone.

There are machines with legs that do not walk. There are machines with wheels that do not move. There are machines that are all surface and no obvious interior, their housings seamless and warm to the touch. There are machines that are all interior, open-framed, their workings visible and elaborate and not quite interpretable — gears meshing with other gears in ratios that seem, when you try to follow the mechanical logic, to loop back on themselves.

All of them make sound. But each machine's sound is small — a hum, a tick, a rhythmic clicking at long intervals, a subsonic vibration you feel in your teeth more than hear with your ears. And the sounds do not combine into noise so much as they cancel each other into a kind of engineered quiet, an acoustic negative space, as though the designers understood that enough small sounds, thoughtfully overlapped, produce silence of a strange and textured variety. You stand among several hundred machines and the plateau is, by most measures, quiet.

---

The moisture comes in.

Not dramatically. This is not a wet place. But there is water in the plateau's daily arithmetic, and you learn to read the signs of it. The hexagonal plates collect dew in the hours before the light intensifies — the cool surface temperature against the slightly warmer air produces condensation that runs toward the gaps and vanishes into the dark underneath. Small rust-brown stains have begun to appear on certain machines, the early autobiography of ferric process, the metal slowly introducing itself to water over time. These stains are reddish and vivid against the gray-white surfaces, the most saturated color in the landscape.

Occasionally it rains. When it does, the rain is light — not a downpour, not even a proper rain by the standards of places with real weather, but a distributed falling of small drops that feels more like the air reconsidering its gaseous commitment than precipitation in earnest. The rain lasts twenty minutes. During it, the sound changes slightly — the machines tick differently, accepting water into their ventilation, and the hexagonal plates darken in uneven patterns as their differing materials absorb or repel moisture at different rates. When it stops, evaporation is quick. In an hour you would not know it had rained, except for the tidelines forming at the low points of each plate.

Between rains and dew, the air is cool and carries a humidity that is present without being felt — an ambient dampness that lives below the threshold of perception until you reach for something metal and find it slightly tacky, slightly cold.

---

What lives here is the question you're avoiding.

The fauna is abundant. There should be no question about this — the signs are everywhere. But the signs and their makers are not always temporally synchronized, which is one of the plateau's stranger rules.

The feces of small animals appear on the surfaces of machines, on the edges of plates, in the corners where clusters create sheltered angles. Fresh. The animals that leave them are not visible. Not hidden, exactly — you have the distinct impression that they are present but offset, occupying the same space in a register you don't have access to. Sometimes you see movement in your peripheral vision, small and quick and gone. When you turn to it there is nothing, but the nothing has a texture — a quality of recent occupation, warm in a way the air isn't.

Larger animals leave different signs. Something has nested in the open-framed machines, has woven materials — synthetic fibers, stripped wire insulation, scraps of processed material that have no natural origin — into structures in the internal spaces of these machines. The nests are complex. Whoever made them understood the machine's interior geometry and worked with it rather than against it, tucking load-bearing elements of the nest against specific structural members of the machine as though they had studied engineering before proceeding. The nests are empty when you look at them. They are warm.

Something large crossed this part of the plateau recently. You can tell because the fine mineral deposit on several plate surfaces is disturbed in a pattern — not footprints in any conventional sense, but a sequential disruption of surface particulate suggesting contact, weight, and departure. The stride length, measured between disruptions, is longer than you would prefer.

Birds may or may not be birds. Things pass overhead — dark, moving fast, their wings (if wings) angled in ways that suggest familiarity with the thermals above the heated machines. They don't call. They cross the bleached sky and are gone.

---

Here is one of the rules of the plateau, and it is a rule you discover slowly:

*Scale does not behave consistently.*

You look across the plateau — a regional space, a valley-sized expanse of constructed surface — and the far edge, the ledge where the engineered ground terminates and the empty air begins, seems close. Sixty meters. Eighty. You could walk there in a minute. You walk toward it. The machines cluster densely around you, between you and it, and you navigate through them, and five minutes later the edge appears to be approximately the same distance away. Sixty meters. Eighty.

You stop. You look back. The place you came from looks far away.

This is not magic, exactly. It's a malfunction of perspective that the place has made structural. The density of the machines creates false depth cues — you read distance through them the way you'd read distance through trees, but these are not trees and the rules that trees follow don't apply here. The consistency of the surface (no gradient, no topography, no visual anchor at middle distance) collapses depth into a kind of flatness that the mind cannot properly navigate. And something else. Something more.

The light doesn't help. The sourceless brightness removes shadow, and shadow is one of the ways you know where things are in relation to each other. Without shadow, the machine clusters appear to float slightly, to exist at no particular depth, to hover in the bright air at unknown distances. You triangulate obsessively. You find landmarks. You count steps.

And yet.

---

The place doesn't change quickly. That's important to understand. Whatever is wrong with it — the absent occupants, the impossible scale, the machines processing materials whose purpose you can't deduce — none of it is urgent. This is a place of slow process, patient dysfunction, gradual accumulation. The rust on the machines is forming at the rate rust forms. The mineral deposits are building one evaporated puddle at a time. The nests were constructed over days or weeks. The surface plates lifted their edges by thermal cycling across seasons.

You feel no danger, exactly. You feel something adjacent to danger — a low-grade attentiveness the body switches on when it's not sure what category it's in. Is this safe? Unclear. Is this familiar? No. Do the rules here correspond to rules I know? Some of them. The plateau asks these questions through your body without asking them in words.

The machines continue. Their soft collective silence continues. The bright sky presses down. The cool air draws itself into intake vents and is returned, slightly altered, through exhaust ports. Somewhere behind you — somewhere at an unknown distance that might be ten meters or three hundred — something large and warm is in a nest it made by studying the machine from inside.

The far edge is sixty meters away.

The far edge is eighty meters away.

You are still walking toward it.
