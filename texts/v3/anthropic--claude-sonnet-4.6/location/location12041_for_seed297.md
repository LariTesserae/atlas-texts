# The Unwatered Garden

---

Begin with what isn't there.

No rivers. No rain-carved channels, no dry creek-beds holding the memory of water, no capillary lines of darker soil that betray where moisture once pooled. The land is not thirsty — thirst implies a prior familiarity, an absence felt. This place has never been wet. The soil, if you call it soil, is a pale mineral matrix the color of old teeth, fine-grained in the way that suggests it was laid down by something other than erosion: not worn to smallness but *made* small, crystallized at this scale, deposited by some process that skips the intermediate stages. The ground underfoot has a faint give, like walking on compacted chalk, but it does not crumble. It holds its form the way things hold their form when they have always been this way and intend to continue.

This is a young landscape. The features are sharp — not dramatically, not like broken glass, but with a specificity that older places lose. The low ridges running through the plain have edges you could trace with a fingernail and leave a line. The mineral outcrops where the pale substrate breaks upward into small formations — knee-high, shin-high, the occasional waist-high cluster — show faces that are almost geometric in their freshness, planes meeting at angles that haven't yet been softened into curves. There is no rounding here. Nothing has had time to become approximate.

And yet: the vegetation.

---

This requires sitting with for a moment, because the mind's first instinct is to reach for water and find it missing, and then to insist that what follows cannot be. But here it is regardless.

The plants — and they are plants, or something that has arrived at the same solution plants arrived at through an entirely different history — are *everywhere*. Low to the ground, mostly, which suits the low-lying terrain: the plain extends in all directions at something close to sea-level, or what would be sea-level if there were a sea, gently undulating over a regional scale, valley-and-rise, valley-and-rise, nothing dramatic, the kind of topography you can hold in your head as *flat* even when it isn't quite. Across this plain the growth is dense. The plants press against each other. They occupy every available horizontal surface and climb each other's stems and spread themselves across the pale ground in overlapping territories the way a crowd fills a room.

They are grey-green, many of them, with thick waxy surfaces that suggest they are conserving something, though it is not water. Their leaves — leaf-analogues, structures that perform the function of leaves — are wide and flat in a way that seems to contradict the desiccation logic you'd expect, except that they are harvesting something from the air itself, something the air here is full of. The air has a faint chemical richness to it, something between ozone and the smell of warm electronics and something floral that doesn't map to any specific flower you know. The plants are breathing this in. Their stomata — their stomata-analogues — open toward it. Their wide grey-green surfaces are not collecting light alone but are catching and converting some other flux that moves through this world, invisible to you, rich to them.

Some species — if species is the word — are darker, almost black-green, and their surfaces have a faint iridescent quality at certain angles, like the wing-case of a beetle, like a soap bubble held very still. These catch your eye because they seem to be doing something more than sitting. They have an orientation that isn't quite phototropic because the light here is even and normal — good daylight, full-spectrum, coming from somewhere above and slightly diffuse, casting soft shadows that are present but not dramatic — and yet the black-green plants are *turned* toward something, all of them, in the same direction, the way sunflowers track the sun. What they're tracking isn't visible.

Others are pale, nearly white, and they mat across the ground like a living lichen-analogue, covering the mineral substrate in dense continuous sheets with occasional raised nodes that might be reproductive structures or might be something for which you don't have a word. The pale mat-plants are everywhere beneath the taller species. They form a base layer. They fill the gaps. In places they creep up the sides of the mineral outcrops and cover them completely so that the sharp geometry of the rock is preserved but upholstered, the edges still visible but softer-seeming now, furred.

The height is mostly low. Knee to hip on what stands tallest. This is a ground-level world.

---

It is quiet.

Not the quiet of held breath, not the silence of something waiting. Just quiet, the way a library is quiet when there is no particular reason for noise and the inhabitants have tacitly agreed to produce none. The plants don't rustle — there is no wind, or if there is wind it is so faint and so even that it registers as stillness rather than movement. You can hear something if you stand still long enough: a very low, very slow sound, almost subsonic, that you feel in the chest before you hear it in the ears. It has a periodicity. It recurs. It is not biological — it is not breathing or heartbeat — it is more like a long tone held at the lower edge of audibility, and it is coming from the ground, or from everywhere, and it does not change.

That sound, that infrasound, is the only dynamic element in an otherwise completely static scene.

There are almost no animals. Perhaps there never were. Perhaps the ecology of this place routes its energy through channels that don't produce fauna in any abundance — the plants and the chemical-air and the invisible flux running through everything, the whole system cycling happily without needing much in the way of mobile consumers. Once, moving through a gap between two clusters of the black-green iridescent plants, you see something. Small. It is still by the time you look at it directly. It has too many joints in its legs and its body is the same pale chalky color as the mineral substrate, and then it is gone — not fled, not fast, just *gone*, in the way that things go when the rules about persistence are slightly different here. It doesn't startle. It doesn't run. It simply stops being where it was.

You don't see another one.

---

Nothing changes.

This is the thing that takes longest to understand, because it runs against a deep expectation. You watch, and you keep watching, because you know that plants move, that light shifts, that even the stillest places have gradients — temperature gradients, pressure gradients, the slow lean of shadows across the ground. But here the shadows do not move. You notice this when you crouch to examine one of the pale mat-plants and cast your own shadow across it, and then look up, and look at the light source (diffuse, soft, the light of an overcast day without quite being overcast, a bright even nowhere-light), and look back at the long-cast shadows of the outcrops on the pale substrate. They are where they were. They do not shift. The light is not moving.

The light is not moving because its source is not moving, or the source's motion has been arrested, or this place exists in some perpetual frozen-noon of its own geometry, and either way the result is the same: these shadows are permanent. They are features of the landscape in the way that rock formations are features, fixed, reliable, as permanent as anything else here. The plants growing in them are adapted to them. The plants growing in the light are adapted to that. Nothing will change, and nothing expects to change, and the entire ecology is structured around that unmoving fact.

The minerals were laid down. The plants established. The invisible flux began its ancient even flow through the air and the shallow soil. The one tone sounded in the depths and has been sounding since.

And then it stopped. And was complete.

---

The technology is hardest to name because it is not technology in the way you understand technology — there are no structures, no artifacts, no made things, nothing that looks assembled or designed. And yet something here is *engineered*. You feel it the way you feel the presence of intention in a formal garden, except this garden has no walls and no gardener you can see and the engineering is not at the scale of pruning and planting but at some other scale entirely.

Look at the mineral substrate. The crystal structure, if you had a way to examine it, would be too regular. Not impossibly regular, not ostentatiously crystalline, but regular in the way that suggests the initial conditions were controlled, that the substrate was laid down into an environment that had been prepared to receive it with certain properties. The grain size is too consistent over too large an area. Natural processes produce distributions; this looks like it was poured from a uniform source.

Look at the invisible flux the plants are harvesting. You cannot see it but you can infer it from the plants, the way you infer wind from the flag. And the flux is too even. Too perfectly distributed. Natural fields have gradients, falloff, directionality. This one is — as far as you can tell, as far as the plants' orientations suggest — isotropic. It is the same in every direction, at every point across the whole regional plain. This is not a natural condition. This is a maintained condition. Something is maintaining it.

Look at the fact that nothing changes.

A true equilibrium, a true stasis, requires work. Real systems always have some net flow. The maintenance of true changelessness, of a system genuinely time-invariant rather than merely slow, requires continuous active correction, the constant small interventions of something that measures and adjusts and returns every deviation to zero. The stillness here is not the stillness of a thing that has run down. The stillness here is the stillness of a thing being *held* still by something that has not run down and will not run down and is not visible.

It may be in the substrate. It may be in the air. It may be in the infrasound, which is not biological and is not geological but might be operational, might be the sound of whatever keeps this place what it is — the hum of maintenance, the working tone of a system doing its work in the frequency range where you would not think to look for engineering.

You will not find the mechanism. It is not at a scale you can examine. It is in the crystalline arrangement of the mineral matrix, in the distribution of the flux through the air, in the topology of something beneath your ability to resolve. It was built before the plants came, probably. Or the plants are part of it. Or the distinction between the engineering and the ecology is not a distinction this place recognizes.

---

Here is what the weirdness does to you slowly.

It is not dramatic. There is no visual distortion, no impossible color, no sky that opens into something other than sky. The light is normal. The plants are strange but they are plants, they are green, they have leaves, they occupy space in the ordinary way of solid objects. Walking through them you feel the brush of stems and surfaces in the normal way.

But time is wrong. Not the time of the world — the world's time is frozen, fine, you can accept that — your own experience of time is wrong. You have been here for some duration that you cannot correctly estimate. When you try to remember arriving — the moment you came into this place, the transition — you can almost reach it. It is there, in memory, somewhere. But the memory has no scale. You cannot tell if it was an hour ago or a day ago or longer. The stillness of the place has crept into your sense of duration. There are no cues — no shadow-movement, no change in light, no animal behavior, no wind, no sound except the constant infrasound — and without cues the interval collapses. You might have been here ten minutes. You might have been here two days and simply haven't noticed the hunger yet, or perhaps you have noticed it and integrated it into the general quality of the place without flagging it as time's passage.

And the space: you walked — you are walking — you have been walking in what you believe is a straight line, and the landscape is open, regional in scale, you can see for a good distance in every direction, and the landscape is consistent in every direction, valley-and-rise, valley-and-rise, pale substrate and dense grey-green growth and scattered outcrops with their permanent shadows. You have not been turning. And yet you have a feeling, a patient and non-anxious feeling, that you might be looking at the same outcrops from a different angle. That the region is larger than your walk has traversed, or smaller, or structured in a way that confounds the normal relationship between distance and position.

You are not lost. You are too calm to be lost. This is itself suspicious.

---

This is not a place that is doing anything to you.

That is the final understanding, which comes gradually.

The weirdness is not hostile. It is not watching. It is not performing. The breakdown of your normal perceptions is a side effect, a consequence of being a biological time-dependent thing in a time-invariant place, like the way your eyes adjust when you step from darkness into light and briefly see nothing. You are adapting to a set of physical rules that differ from the rules you were calibrated for. The infrasound is not aimed at you. The invisible flux is not aimed at you. The engineering in the substrate is not interested in you.

You are a transient anomaly in a permanent system.

You are temporary here. Possibly everything that is temporary feels, here, this particular kind of gentle wrongness — the wrongness of standing in a current, or holding still in a crowd that is moving, except the current is time and the crowd is change and you are the one still moving when everything else has stopped.

The plants are not temporary. They were built for this. Their wide grey-green surfaces tilt toward the invisible. Their pale mat-cousins creep across the mineral ground in the permanent noon, in the fixed shadows, toward nothing in particular, toward continuing exactly as they are.

You could stand here for a long time.

You might already have.
