# The Plateau of Final Forms

---

The first thing you understand, arriving here, is that nothing is still — and yet nothing has moved in a very long time. This seeming contradiction will not resolve. It simply becomes the texture of the place.

---

## Terrain

You are standing on something that was once a mountain range. Not a mountain: a range, plural, enormous, a corrugation of peaks that would have scraped the stratosphere when they were young. That was so long ago that the word "young" has become a kind of joke the rock itself tells quietly. The peaks have been ground to a tableland. The tableland has been ground to a plane. The plane has been ground further, past flatness, into something the opposite of flatness — a surface so perfectly settled into its own equilibrium that it curves ever so slightly downward in every direction, conforming to the planet's curvature rather than opposing it. The horizon, when you look for it, is very far away and very low. The world is more sphere than you usually notice.

The surface is pale. Mineral-pale, the color of bone that has gone past yellow into something nearer grey-white, nearer ash-white, nearer the color of old salt that has never touched water — because there is no water here, has not been for longer than complex chemistry required to persist. What you walk on is regolith in the old sense: the rubble-residue of all that grinding, all that slow catastrophic patience of erosion. The particles are fine. Not sand-fine but dust-fine, and they do not shift when you walk because they have been compacted by time past any normal density of compaction. They have forgotten how to drift. Each grain has found its neighbor and accepted it.

Below the regolith: massive substrate. Pale stone. Very old stone — the kind that has no grain direction anymore, that has been metamorphosed and re-metamorphosed until the original sediment that became the original rock that became the first mountains is now something that remembers nothing of its former self. Geologists would have no term for it. It is too processed. It is what stone becomes when stone has become everything it can become, and then rested.

The plateau stretches in every direction at roughly the same elevation. Roughly. There are variations — shallow basins, broad gentle swells — but these are so gently expressed that you feel them more in your legs over an hour of walking than you see them. From altitude, the plateau is nearly featureless. From the ground, you notice that these subtle undulations are everything: they are the only geography left, and so they take on a kind of enormous significance.

The elevation is extreme. The air pressure is low — not lethal, but thin enough that breathing requires slight intention. Your heartbeat is audible to you. Colors are slightly more saturated than they should be, the light behaving differently through a thinner column of atmosphere. The sky overhead grades from blue at the horizon to something darker directly above, a color that has no easy name, a blue that remembers being space.

## The Winds

This is where turbulence lives.

At this elevation, at this scale, the atmosphere does things. The plateau is large enough to generate its own meteorology — large enough that weather systems form over it that have no reference to the coasts or lowlands that might once have bordered it. (There are no coasts. There is no water. There has not been.) The air, thin as it is, moves with tremendous intention.

Winds here are not the winds you know from valleys. They are not local, not directional in any fixed sense. They are the expression of pressure systems that span thousands of kilometers and have no other surface to interact with. They are planetary winds. They arrive carrying cold from one hemisphere and deposit it here as a kind of transaction. They carry dust from the regolith — or they would, if the regolith were not so compacted — but occasionally, in the most extreme passages, you see the surface-dust lift from its long rest in thin, twisting threads that rise slowly and then vanish. Not storms. Not exactly. The turbulence is not violent in the human sense of violent. It is vast, and slow at the scale of geography, and fast at the scale of the body.

The sound of the wind is the primary sound of this place. It is nearly not a sound. It is a pressure-variation felt in the ears and chest. A deep, sub-vocal presence. Very occasionally — in the interaction of two major flows — it rises to something audible, a low register that you feel in your sternum before you hear it. Then it subsides. The silence that follows is very large.

Above the plateau, clouds form and unform in minutes. Not storm-clouds — these clouds are thin, cirrus-type, the high cold transparent kind, and they spin in patterns that don't match any local logic because they're being driven by forces with planetary momentum. You can watch them and never quite predict the next form. Sheets dissolve into threads. Threads braid and separate. The sky is, in this way, the most dynamic thing in the landscape, the one element that has not settled. Everything below has found its final form. The sky is still working.

## What Grows Here

Not much. That is the honest answer.

The vegetation that exists is the vegetation that won. What won is small and flat and very old in its lineage, though not in its bodies. There are crusts. Cryptobiotic crusts, if you needed a word from old ecology — pale grey-green sheets, barely three millimeters thick, that follow the surface contours of rocks with perfect fidelity. They have been here, in their specific relationship to specific rocks, for a very long time. Not the individual organisms: those renew, slowly. But the colony, the mat, the biofilm-organism in the broadest sense — that is settled in place as surely as the stone beneath.

Every several hundred meters, you find one. A patch of crust on the windward face of a low exposed boulder. A fringe of something lichen-adjacent on the underside of a rock where the wind passes and deposits, for minutes each day, a marginally higher moisture content in the form of vapor frost.

There are also, rarely, small plants in the old sense — vascular, rooted. They occur in the basins, in the subtle low places where the soil is very slightly deeper and the wind marginally less. They are prostrate. They do not grow upward. Nothing here grows upward. They grow outward, wide and flat, radiating from a central tap root that goes down with enormous single-mindedness into the pale substrate. The leaves are very small, waxy, grey-green. They move in the wind without apparent distress. They have been in this posture for their entire evolutionary history.

They flower. This is the unusual thing. In the brief season when the temperature crosses a threshold, they produce flowers of a pale yellow so light it is almost white, so small they require close attention to see. The flowers last two days. They are pollinated by — it is unclear. Perhaps by the wind. Perhaps there is something that visits. You would have to wait, very still, for a long time, and perhaps you would see it.

## The Fauna

Something lives here that you will not easily see.

You become aware of it through evidence rather than observation. Small depressions in the compacted regolith — not footprints exactly, more like weight-impressions, as if something has rested here and the surface has slowly memorized it. Occasionally a smear of oil-dark material on a pale rock surface, at a height that suggests something passed and brushed against it. Occasionally a sound, on the edge of audibility, that is not wind: a clicking, or a series of very small impacts, like hard material on harder. Then nothing.

The creatures that survive at this elevation, in this aridity, with this much wind, are not large. They are probably very patient. They are probably very slow, in the way that slow things in extreme environments have learned that speed is expensive and patience is free. You get the impression of something armored, or heavily cuticled, or both — something that has taken the same lesson from the environment that the rocks have taken: present the smallest possible surface, be denser than you need to be, endure.

Once, at significant distance, something moves along the edge of a low stone outcrop. It is visible for perhaps four seconds. It is pale, and low to the ground, and its motion is unhurried in a way that is either confidence or simple absence of urgency. It disappears. The wind continues.

## The Built Things

These are the hardest to describe, because they do not follow the logic of building.

They are scattered across the plateau at intervals that might be regular — the spacing is large enough that you can't quite confirm regularity from the ground. Some are visible from a distance as slight uprisings from the flat surface: angular where everything else is rounded, vertical where everything else is horizontal. From a distance you might dismiss them as geological. They are not geological.

Close, they resolve into: posts, or pillars, or structures that were once one of those things and are now something the wind has engaged with for a very long time. They are made of material that is not stone and not metal in any conventional sense — it is material that looks like it was deposited rather than carved or cast, grown into its form by some process that had very precise intentions at very small scales. The surface is fine-grained past grain, past texture, into something that your fingertip reads as continuous, the way glass reads as continuous. It is not glass. In certain light — when the sun is low and the angle is right — it shows internal structure: faint geometric repetition, a pattern within the material, regular at a scale that would require magnification to resolve but that you can infer from the way it catches light.

Some of the structures stand at their original height, or nearly — two, three meters, very narrow, tapering. Some have toppled and lie on the surface in the attitude they fell, which tells you they fell a very long time ago because the exposed contact surface has been smoothed by the same gradual abrasion that smoothed the mountains. They have had the same wind applied to them as everything else and have responded the way everything else responds: by becoming smoother, by losing edges, by becoming more themselves and less of what they were built to be.

What they were built to be: unknown. They may be measurements. They may be markers. They may be the antennae or terminals of something much larger, mostly buried, operating at a scale of time that makes their apparent dormancy irrelevant — operating now, always operating, doing something at the boundary of physics that requires no visible action. The turbulence in the upper atmosphere might be — could be — the output, or the exhaust, or the signal. There is no way to verify this. It is a thought the place seems to suggest without endorsing.

Nothing about the structures appears attended. Nothing appears maintained. And yet nothing appears broken beyond the cosmetic — the wind-smoothing of exposed surfaces, the very slight lean in those still standing, the patina of cryptobiotic crust on the downwind face of the ones that have been still long enough. Either they do not require maintenance, or whatever maintains them is present in a way that leaves no other evidence.

## Light

Normal light — this is almost the strangest thing about the place.

The sun is the sun. It describes an arc. Shadows move. This is so ordinary as to feel like a gift, here, in a landscape where everything else has passed through ordinary into something beyond. The light comes in at a low angle for much of the day because of the latitude, and when it comes in low, it performs the usual alchemy on old stone: warmth that is purely visual, a conversion of pale grey-white to amber-white to something almost gold, which then fades back as the angle steepens.

Midday is cool and clear and the shadows are short and the light is bright without being warm. You could photograph this place at midday and the photographs would look like photographs of the moon — the same flatness, the same lack of obvious scale. Without the cryptobiotic crusts, without the distant glimpse of a prostrate plant flowering, there would be no way to tell.

At the ends of the day, the long shadows reveal the surface texture that midday hides: every small irregularity of the regolith becomes visible as the light rakes across it. The subtle basins show as darker areas — not truly dark, but relatively shadowed — and the low swells catch the last light and hold it, and for a brief window, the plateau is almost dramatic. Almost.

Then the temperature drops fast, as thin-atmosphere high-elevation places do, and the sky performs its nightly procedure: blue to indigo to black to very black, and a number of stars that requires adjustment, requires sitting with, requires several minutes before you accept it as what it actually is.

---

## The Overall Law

Old things find their final form and then become the stage for something else.

The mountains became the plateau. The plateau became the surface. The surface became the substrate for crust, for patient animals, for structures built by intelligences or technologies that understood something about permanence that biology mostly doesn't — that if you build at the right scale and with the right materials, you don't build something that lasts. You build something that becomes part of what lasts.

The turbulence above is not disorder. It only looks like disorder from inside a human moment. At the right temporal scale — at the scale appropriate to this place — the cycling of the atmosphere over this plateau is a system in a very long equilibrium, a dynamic equilibrium, the kind where nothing is stable but the instability itself is stable, has always been this configuration of instability, will be this configuration for as long as the atmospheric mass and the solar input and the plateau elevation remain in their current relationship.

Which will be: for a long time.

Which is, here, the closest thing to a promise.

---

The wind picks up fractionally. A thread of surface dust lifts from between two stones, describes a small helix, and settles again four meters away, in a depression where it will remain, compacted by time, until the same process repeats in some interval that is not calculable by anything you brought with you.

You stand on the highest, flattest, oldest, emptiest, most turbulent place in the world.

It is very quiet.
