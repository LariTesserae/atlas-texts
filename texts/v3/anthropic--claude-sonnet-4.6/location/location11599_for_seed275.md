# The Undercroft Gardens of Vel Shamash

---

You go down for a long time before you understand that you've arrived somewhere.

The descent is gradual — a ramp, or what functions as a ramp, though the floor beneath your feet is not poured concrete or cut stone but compressed root-mass, fibrous and faintly yielding, like walking across the back of a sleeping animal. The walls to either side have long since stopped being walls in any conventional sense. They are root-tangles eight feet thick, pressed and woven into structural panels by decades of patient cultivation, sealed with resins the color of old amber. They hold the earth back. They hold everything back. This is important to understand about Vel Shamash: the plants are not decorating the infrastructure. The plants *are* the infrastructure.

---

The city — and it is a city, or the lower third of one — extends horizontally once the ramp levels off, opening into a chamber large enough that the far wall disappears into a soft, sourceless haze. The ceiling is perhaps forty feet above the main boulevard, though "ceiling" is the wrong word for something that breathes. It is the underside of the root system of the upper city, the surface city, and through it come pale light and occasional slow sound. Tap roots descend like stalactites. Some are as thin as wire. Others are as wide as support columns and have been incorporated into support columns, wrapped in iron collars and bearing load plates where the industrial sections begin. The light filters down through biological layers — through root-mass, through compressed soil, through something that might be symbiotic fungal film stretched thin as membrane — and arrives here cool and diffuse and honest, not quite greenish, more the color of overcast afternoon. Normal light. Light that tells no lies but volunteers no warmth.

The scale of the place takes time to absorb. You can see perhaps a kilometer before the haze softens everything into suggestion. In the middle distances, structures stand: processing facilities with their iron-pipe chimneys bent sideways to route exhaust along horizontal ducts (there is no "up" for smoke to go in the conventional sense, so it travels laterally, and the ductwork hangs below the ceiling like an industrial vine system, which is almost funny, almost not). Quiet machine-sound, low-frequency and methodical, issues from within these buildings — a rhythm like deep breathing, like a slow mechanical heart. Compressors. Augers. Some kind of rotary apparatus that cycles on, does its work, cycles off. The sound carries far in the underground air, which is dense and still.

Between the structures: space. More space than you expect. The city is *not* crowded. It was built with spacing discipline, with deliberate gaps, and in those gaps the root system above has been allowed to descend further, to branch, to find purchase in the floor. Columns of live root rise and fall in the open areas. Some have been growing long enough to split the flagstones they encountered and send new shoots sideways, seeking. This is the emerging quality of the place — the sense that the city as it currently exists is not quite what it is *becoming*. The root columns are not yet structural. The new shoots from the floor are not yet walls. The whole underground space has the quality of a sketch gaining confidence — lines that are definite in some places, tentative in others. You walk past a section of boulevard where the root-work overhead has come together into something almost vaulted, almost deliberate, the tangles interlocked into a pattern that suggests a craftsman's intention even though no craftsman was involved. Fifty meters further, the same root-work is chaotic, exploratory, just doing what roots do when given darkness and time.

---

The vegetation at high density sounds lush. Here it is something else.

The plants fill this place the way a skeleton fills a body — structurally, necessarily, invisibly except where you know to look. The root-mass walls have been smoothed on their interior faces, given a coat of mineral sealant, and on this sealed face mosses grow in patches, grey-green and damp-seeming though there is very little moisture in Vel Shamash. Very little water at all. This is the central strangeness of the ecology here, the thing that makes the botanists from the surface lean in: the vegetation of the undercroft runs on almost nothing. Some fraction of humidity pulled from the breath of the inhabitants and the machines. Some chemical exchange happening at the root-cellular level that the researchers have documented without fully explaining. The plants are, by any surface standard, impossibly dry. The mosses should be crisp. The root-mass walls should be dead. And yet — they are not. The city breathes, slowly, and the roots breathe with it.

Between structures, in the gap-spaces, the floor vegetation grows sparse and peculiar. Ground cover plants with wide, pale leaves, almost white, maximizing whatever photon ration they receive. Their texture is almost papery. You touch one and it doesn't feel like a leaf should feel — too stiff, too dry, more like touching a pressed specimen. But the color at the growing edge, where new material is still forming, is a genuine pale green. Still becoming. Everything here is still becoming.

Climbing structures occupy several of the buildings — deliberately cultivated vines trained along the iron ductwork, their root systems tapping into composted material in long troughs of pressed root-fiber mounted against the walls. These vines are the most visibly alive things in the undercroft. They move — not quickly, not perceptibly moment to moment, but the workers here can tell you: the vines that were at the third coupling three months ago are now past the fourth. They travel six inches in a good week. They are going somewhere. Nobody has decided where, exactly. The ductwork gives them a direction and they follow it. It's been suggested in several official reports that the vine system is functioning as unintentional infrastructure, that the root-grip of the vines is reinforcing the duct-joints, reducing failures. Nobody has acted on this. It remains in the reports.

---

The fauna of the undercroft is the most active thing in it, and the most diverse.

Beetles, first and foremost. Beetles of a dozen varieties moving through the root-mass, through cracks in the floor, through the packed-earth zones where the root-panels haven't sealed completely. You hear almost nothing of them individually — the sound of a single beetle in root-fiber is below notice — but the aggregate of them is a very faint, very continuous dry rustling that runs below the machine-sound like a bass note. Workers stop hearing it within a week. Visitors hear it constantly.

There are birds in Vel Shamash, which surprises everyone the first time. Small, pale — not albino, but muted, the colors of things that have spent generations under diffuse light. They arrived, or descended, sometime in the early industrial period, following warm-air currents up from the machine-heat zones, finding the root-ceiling good for nesting, finding the beetle population worth staying for. They perch on tap roots and process ducts with equal comfort. They are the most vocal presences in the undercroft, though they are not loud. Small sounds, musical enough, at intervals. They punctuate the quiet rather than filling it.

Something larger moves in the gap-spaces at certain hours. The workers call them *crawlers*, which is descriptive enough — low-slung, multi-legged, pale, feeding on the ground-cover plants and the accumulated organic matter in the floor-cracks. Not hostile. Not particularly alert. They move with a deliberate, unhurried confidence that suggests they know the layout of this city as well as its human inhabitants, possibly better, and they are not especially concerned about the industrial machinery or the pale birds or the visitors who crouch down for a better look. They have the unhurried quality of all things that have found a working system and see no reason to hurry.

---

The air of the undercroft is cool in the way that cellars are cool — stable, mineral-smelling, with something living beneath the mineral note. Not unpleasant. Not entirely plant-smell, not entirely stone-smell, but the compound of the two and the biological processes that join them. The temperature almost never varies. In summer, this is the most comfortable place in the region. In winter, it is exactly as comfortable. The machines generate heat that rises and dissipates before it reaches the working levels. The root-mass walls insulate. The soil above insulates. The temperature is simply what it is, season after season, and the organisms living here have organized themselves around that constancy in a hundred small ways.

The processing facilities run on schedules. Shift-change happens at six-hour intervals — not because there is a dawn or a dusk to mark time but because the machines require it, because the human body still carries its old rhythms even three levels below the surface. At shift-change, there is a brief increase in sound — voices, footsteps on root-fiber, the clank and sequence of machinery being handed from one shift to the next. Then the voices recede and the quiet returns, and the beetles continue their work in the walls, and the birds continue their small commentary from the root-ceiling, and somewhere in the gap-spaces a crawler is moving slowly toward the ground-cover patch it has already decided on, deliberate and unconcerned, going somewhere in the dark that is not quite dark, in the dry that is not quite dead, in the unfinished city that is still very much in the process of deciding what it will be.

---

The newest sections are the most startling. Here the root-panels have been installed within the last several years and have not yet fully compacted, not yet fully sealed. Between the fiber-courses, you can see the living interior — pale root-threads still individually distinguishable, still in motion at the growth-scale, the whole panel faintly warm against your hand despite the cool air. These walls are still growing into walls. The floor in the newest sections hasn't settled: fine cracks run across it in dendritic patterns, root-pressure from below adjusting to load-pressure from above, the two systems negotiating. Workers walk these sections carefully. Not because they are dangerous — they have been tested, they hold — but out of a respect that has developed organically (everything here develops organically) for processes that are still underway.

There is a scaffold in the newest section supporting a ceiling-panel installation, and two workers are on it, checking root-compression, making notes. They speak quietly. One points at something in the root-weave above. They look at it together. The birds watch from the ductwork. Below them, in the gap-space between the scaffold and the first completed wall-panel, a pale vine has already found the scaffold's lowest rung and begun its patient assessment of the situation.

The city is being built. The city is building itself. The difference, in Vel Shamash, is difficult to locate with any precision.
