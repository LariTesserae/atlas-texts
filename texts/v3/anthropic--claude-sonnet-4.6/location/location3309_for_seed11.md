# The Throat of the World

---

Begin with the sound, because the sound arrives before anything else does.

It arrives before sight, before understanding, before the mind has assembled what it is looking at into anything resembling a category. It is not a single sound. It is the convergence of many sounds that have each traveled enormous distances to arrive here, and they arrive compressed, funneled, amplified by geography that has spent an incomprehensible length of time becoming precisely this shape. The sound is wind that has crossed a thousand miles of open air and found, at last, the one place where the cliffs stand just far enough apart to catch it like a reed in a mouth. The sound is water — not a great deal of water, but water falling from extreme height, which turns even modest volumes into something percussive and vast, into white noise with a heartbeat underneath it. The sound is stone creaking in the thermal differential between shadow and the part of the cliff face that catches the afternoon. The sound is your own blood in your ears, recruited into the chorus whether you wanted it there or not.

You cannot think normally here. The sound reorganizes thinking. This is important to understand before going further.

---

Now the terrain.

The cliffs are not young cliffs. They gave up being young cliffs before anything with eyes existed to notice. They have been eroding since erosion was new, and they have reached a condition that is not the absence of erosion but rather something more like its completion — not smooth, not rounded exactly, but *settled*, the way a face settles in very old age into the expression it was always working toward. The rock is dark, almost purplish at the base where moisture has been wicking up through it for millions of years, fading to a pale ochre and then near-white at the upper reaches where wind does the only work that remains to be done, which is cosmetic now, which is a kind of dusting. The faces of the cliffs are not sheer. They have *character* — ledge systems and overhangs and recesses worn by water that has not flowed through them for epochs, shadow-pockets that smell of ancient mineral and something faintly organic, like the exhalation of the earth itself.

The elevation is extreme. Not dramatic in the way of newly-raised mountains, with their arrogant angles and their avalanche debris and their sense of geological ambition. This is the other kind of extreme — the extreme of something that has been standing for so long it has become an axiom. These heights are not contested. They simply are. The valley floor, far below, is not visible from certain positions along the rim because the rim itself has been so thoroughly colonized by vegetation that it presents a wall of green before it presents a wall of stone.

The gorge — because this is a gorge, or perhaps a canyon, or perhaps something wider than a canyon but narrower than a rift, something for which no single word quite does — opens at scales that shift as you move through it. From the rim looking out, it opens onto a regional expanse: other plateaus, other canyon systems, the suggestion of further terrain extending past what light allows. From the floor looking up, it closes into a cathedral. From inside the vegetation that clings to certain ledge systems, it disappears entirely and you exist only in green.

---

The vegetation is the overwhelming fact of the biological world here, and it has made itself overwhelming by solving the core problem of this place: there is not much water, but there is some, and everything living here has organized its entire existence around finding and keeping it.

The mosses are first. They are always first, in places like this. They have been first for so long that they now form the substrate on which other things build — thick, layered, stratified over time into something more like felt than moss, dark green to near-black in the wettest recesses, pale sage in the areas that dry out between rains. In the deep shadow of overhangs, the moss is frankly wet, exuding moisture when pressed, trickling when the wind shifts and drives rain at angles into the stone. On the sun-facing ledges, the moss is desiccated for weeks at a time and then, within hours of moisture arriving — whether from rain, which happens several times per season, or from the condensation that forms in the temperature differential between night stone and warm morning air — it resurrects. It is not a metaphor to say it resurrects. It is a fact. The green returns visibly, the structure rehydrates, and the moss continues being moss as if nothing happened, as if pausing for several weeks was an entirely ordinary thing to do.

From the moss, the ferns. They are not the fragile ferns of temperate woodland floors. They are ferns that have been selected, over enormous time, for tenacity. Their fronds extend from cracks in rock that seem, by any reasonable assessment, to contain nothing a plant could use. The root systems go deep into fractures, following ancient water pathways, finding the places where the rock is still faintly damp from rains that fell before memory. They grow densely where they grow, but they grow only where conditions permit, which means you move through alternating zones: bare rock, then sudden fern-thicket that reaches shoulder height, then bare rock again, then an explosion of vegetation around a seep so subtle you might not have noticed the water at all if the ferns hadn't marked it for you.

The trees are not tall. Tallness is not the strategy. The strategy is *grip*. They grow sideways from ledge systems, horizontal for several feet before turning upward, the turn seeming almost deliberate, almost like an elbow. Their wood is dense and slow-grown, rings packed so tightly they require magnification to count. Some of these trees are old in a way that does not announce itself — they look gnarled and small and almost bonsai-like in their proportions, until you notice that the limb you've been resting your hand on is as wide as your thigh, and you reassess. The bark is deeply furrowed, filled with moss. The canopy, such as it is, provides dappled shade on ledges that otherwise would be too exposed for the fern communities beneath. This is a mutualism that has been running for longer than the concept of mutualism has existed.

Higher up, toward the rim and on the plateaus beyond, the vegetation opens and changes character — lower, more sprawling, responding to the wind that is continuous up there, that has always been continuous up there. Scrub and grass and flowering plants that have compressed all their biology into small horizontal packages. Their flowers, when they flower, are vivid — yellow and deep orange and occasionally a blue so saturated it seems artificial, seems like something left behind by someone who had pigment to spare.

---

There are almost no animals. This should be stated plainly.

The place is not hostile to life — the vegetation demonstrates that life has found it, exploited it, filled every available niche. But the fauna is rare in a way that feels specifically chosen. Once, standing very still on a ledge system in the middle reaches of the gorge, you might see a raptor working the thermals above the canyon. It is distant enough to be a comma against the sky, legible only by its movement, the way it tilts and adjusts and finds the column of rising air with an economy of motion that implies complete mastery of a phenomenon you can barely perceive. It will not come closer. It has its own relationship with this place and that relationship does not require your participation.

There are insects, but they are mostly invisible — heard occasionally, or sensed as movement at the edge of vision, or found as evidence: the case of a caddisfly larva cemented to a wet stone, a hollow where something burrowed and left, a scattering of wing fragments near a crack in the rock that might be a nest-site or might be where a predation occurred.

The rarity is not emptiness. The place is not empty. But its biological density is carried almost entirely by the plants, which have expanded to fill the space that animals have vacated, or perhaps have always occupied, long before what we would recognize as animals arrived. The vegetation has the scale here. The fauna is a footnote, occasionally beautiful.

---

The structures — what little there is of them — arrive like afterthoughts.

Along the base of one cliff face, where a ledge system widens enough to be navigable, there are walls. Not many, not tall, not intact. Stones stacked without mortar using a logic that is legible once you see enough of them: the largest stones first, as foundation, then decreasing size, with flat capstones where available. The corners are done with some care. The interiors are small — a single person lying down would fill them. This is shelter-thinking, not architecture-thinking. Someone, or many someones over many generations, found this ledge and found it navigable and found that the overhang above it blocked the prevailing rain and found that small fires could be built from the driftwood that occasionally appeared at the base of the waterfalls after seasonal rains, and built accordingly. Minimally. Sufficiently.

The walls have been colonized by the moss the way everything here has been colonized by moss, which has the effect of making them look like they belong here, which perhaps they do, which perhaps is the correct reading. The interiors hold leaves and debris. One of them, the largest — which is to say, large enough for two people — has a charcoal smear on the back wall at the right height for a fire built toward the corner. The smear is old. The wood is long gone. The moss has not touched the smear.

There are other marks. Farther along the ledge, two figures incised into stone — not painted, but cut, using something hard, something that required effort, requiring the person who made them to brace themselves against the cliff face and work with what was probably a pointed stone or perhaps a piece of harder mineral found in the gorge below. The figures do not depict people, exactly. One is oval, elongated, with lines extending from it that might be rays or might be limbs or might be neither. The other is simply a repeated mark — a vertical line, then a gap, then a vertical line, repeated seventeen times. A count. Of what was being counted, the place does not say.

---

There is something else, and this is where the description must slow down, because the something else resists category.

The turbulence of this place is not all meteorological, not all geological, not simply the wind and the sound and the thermals spiraling up the cliff faces. There is a dynamism here that operates at a different register. The clouds above the gorge behave oddly — or rather, they behave exactly as clouds should when you introduce a gorge of this depth and width and orientation into prevailing wind patterns, but the result looks odd because we are not accustomed to seeing clouds form, travel, break apart, and re-form in a cycle that completes itself in less than an hour. Moisture rises from the seeps and the moss and the ferns, is caught by the wind funneling through the gorge, rises to the cooler air above the rim, condenses, becomes briefly a cloud, and then the wind shears it and it disperses and the process begins again. This means the sky above the gorge is never static. You look up and the light has changed. You look up again and the shadow that was crossing the cliff face has moved in a direction that contradicts where you thought the sun was.

The sound changes with the clouds. When shadow crosses, the thermals shift, the wind that was pouring through the gap wavers, drops a register, then reasserts. This happens continuously. It creates a rhythm that is too slow to be music and too fast to be weather, something in between, something more like breathing.

There are moments — they come without warning, they cannot be arranged or predicted — when all the moving elements briefly synchronize. The cloud above reaches a particular density. The wind through the gorge finds a particular velocity. The thermals along the western face achieve the exact speed to match the wind's pressure and for a moment the air is still — not truly still, but relatively still, still enough that you hear the water from the high seep striking the ledge below it, a sound that is always there and is usually obliterated by everything around it. A small, precise, continuous sound. Individual drops. You hear three or four of them clearly, and then the synchrony breaks, and the roar returns, and that small sound is buried again for another hour or another day.

The place keeps that sound. It is keeping it right now, underneath everything.

---

Stand at the rim as the afternoon light goes horizontal. The ochre cliffs take the light and give it back orange, then briefly deep red, and the vegetation on the ledge systems below acquires a darkness, a depth, the ferns going from green to something almost black in the shadows between ledges. The vast space of the gorge below you is filling with shadow from the bottom up, the way water fills a vessel, while the walls above still hold light. The clouds are doing their cycling. The wind is doing what it always does, which is everything. Somewhere far below, beyond hearing now, that small water continues its work on the stone, which is the only work left to do here, the only work this place still has for water, the final patient subtraction.

You are very small in this place. This is not an observation that the place makes oppressively; it is not hostile, not indifferent in the way that indifference can sometimes feel hostile. It is simply *scaled*. The place is scaled to time, to geological time, to the time it took the rock to become this settled, this finished, this final form. Against that scale, everything that arrived later — the moss, the trees, the ferns, the raptors, the people who built their small walls and counted their seventeen vertical marks and then moved on or did not — is brief. Is passing. Is a kind of weather that the stone waits out with no particular urgency.

The sound continues.

The clouds cycle.

The light continues to leave.
