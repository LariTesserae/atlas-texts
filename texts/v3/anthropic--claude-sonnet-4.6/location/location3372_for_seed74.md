# The Trellis Town

---

The town exists in a valley so shallow it barely qualifies as one — more of a long, gradual lowering of the land, like a palm turned upward. Travelers approaching from the ridge road often miss it entirely on first pass. It announces itself only gradually: a greening of the air, a change in the quality of shadow, the way the light begins arriving through something rather than simply arriving.

---

## Structure

The buildings are not exactly buildings. They were, once. The original construction is still legible beneath — stone foundation courses, brick party walls, the regular spacing of what must have been market stalls or row houses, the geometry of deliberate settlement. Someone laid this place out. Streets were planned. Infrastructure was sunk. The old drainage channels, cut from local sandstone, still run beneath everything, and you can hear them sometimes when the valley goes quiet enough: a thin, purposeful trickling, water finding the same paths it has followed for generations.

But the plants have made the place their own in a way that is not quite invasion and not quite cultivation. The distinction has collapsed over so many decades that no one here tries to maintain it.

Every roof is a growing surface. Not decoratively — not the thin sedge-and-wildflower of an ornamental green roof — but structurally. Mosses three and four inches deep have become load-bearing in a very slow sense, their accumulated mass compressing and insulating the floors below, their root-mats binding cracked tiles back into something watertight. From above, the town would read as a series of dense, dark-green humps, the rooflines lost. The walls are threaded through with ivy so established that the stems nearest the ground have thickened to the diameter of a wrist, gone woody and pale-barked, and in some cases have genuinely fused with the mortar, drawn calcium out of it, incorporated the mineral into tissue. Pull the ivy and you might take the wall.

Window frames have been colonized by ferns. Not the aggressive, enthusiastic ferns of disturbed ground, but the slow, settled ferns of deep shade and patience — hart's tongue mostly, its leaves long and undivided, a strange glossy dark green like oiled leather. They occupy the corners of frames where condensation pools, where a little grit has accumulated over years, and they seem entirely unbothered by the glass. The glass remains. Both things persist.

The streets themselves are paved, or were. The pavement is still there under a continuous skin of low plants — mosses, liverworts, the occasional self-heal working its way up through a crack — but it holds. You walk on it. Your foot finds something solid beneath the softness, and the softness is only an inch or two deep, and the sound of walking is muffled, a little damp, like stepping on felt.

---

## Scale and Space

The town is small. A few hundred meters in any direction gets you to an edge — not a sharp edge, not a wall or a fence, but a fading. Buildings become ruins become foundation-ghosts become empty ground, and then you're on the valley floor proper, in the sedge and the low scrub. The town knows its own size. It doesn't sprawl.

Within that size, though, it is sparse. This is the unexpected thing about the density of growth here — the plants have filled the *surfaces* completely, every wall and roof and pavement, but they have left the air alone. The streets are open. You can see clearly. The spaces between buildings are genuine spaces. What vegetation has done is thicken the boundaries of things rather than the middles of things. The town is a collection of well-upholstered presences arranged around ordinary empty air.

---

## Atmosphere and Light

The light on a typical day is what you might call inhabited. Not dim — there are no trees tall enough here to create a canopy, and the valley opens to open sky — but softened in a particular way. The green surfaces absorb and scatter rather than reflect, so the quality of the ambient light is slightly warmer than the sky would suggest, slightly more yellow-green at the edges of shadows. On overcast days this effect is stronger: the cloud-light comes in flat and even and is then modified by the chlorophyll-heavy surfaces until the town glows faintly, a biological luminescence that isn't really luminescence at all, just the physics of photosynthetic tissue in diffuse light.

Temperature: cool and even. The walls breathe moisture. On the warmest afternoons — and they are not very warm — the air in the streets is noticeably cooler than the air on the surrounding slopes. On cold nights, conversely, the microbial activity in those thick roof-mats generates a tiny, real warmth. The temperature range here is compressed. The season here is always slightly behind the season out there.

Condensation is a constant companion. By morning, every surface runs. Not heavily — no drips, no streams — but the coating of moisture is thorough and general. It beads on the moss. It makes the liverworts translucent. It fills the sandstone channels and keeps them going through the dry stretches, which are frequent. The rainfall in this valley is not reliable. The sky can be clear for two weeks and the town remains damp because it holds what it receives, passes it slowly through the roots and soils of its surfaces, releases it into the air and collects it back again as dew. The water cycle here is close and small and almost closed.

## Industry and Infrastructure

The machinery is tucked away, but it is present. There are engines somewhere in this town. You hear them when the streets are quiet — a low, regular vibration, more felt than heard, transmitted through the paving. It is the sound of something turning, something hydraulic, something that has found its rhythm and maintained it.

The infrastructure serves the plants as much as the people. The original drainage system was modified, at some point in the town's history, to *circulate* rather than merely drain. Water collected from roofs and channels and surface runoff is gathered into a central cistern — stone-lined, deep, nearly full at this hour — and from there pumped through a network of clay pipes to the buildings that need supplemental moisture most. The pump is electric, powered by a small generator that runs on something the residents don't discuss much, or perhaps simply don't find remarkable enough to discuss. It cycles on every few hours. That's the vibration.

The electrification is partial and practical. Lights in the evening, in the workshops, strung along a few of the main streets in simple glass-bulb fixtures that have gone green with algae on their inner surfaces and cast a slightly organic light. Machines in the two or three small production spaces: a textile operation that has been here long enough that its looms have grown their own particular mildew patterns, a grain processing setup of modest scale, a foundry or something like a foundry — you can smell the metal and the heat from three streets away, a strange combination with the pervasive green smell of the rest of the town.

---

## Sound

Quiet, but not empty quiet.

The drainage: always. That thin, persistent trickle below the streets, patient and purposeful.

The generator: a felt presence, cycling. On, steady, off, silence, on.

Wind through the ferns: occasional, brief, a sound like something being shuffled.

The textile looms: a wooden clatter, muffled by distance and walls and moss, rhythmic and slow, like the town thinking.

Somewhere, water dripping from a high surface to a lower one, a slow irregular percussion that has made a small hollow in the stone over years.

Birds: occasional. A single call, repeated at long intervals, from something you cannot see.

There are almost no people sounds. Not because the town is empty — there are people here, going about whatever they go about — but because people here seem to have absorbed the town's preference for moderate volume. Conversation carries, when it happens, but it happens infrequently in public space. This is not an unfriendly silence. It is simply the silence of a place that has been at itself for a long time and has nothing to prove.

---

## The Living Record

The most unusual thing about this place — the thing that emerges slowly, the longer you spend here — is the record the plants keep.

Because the growth on every surface is continuous and long-established, it has become a kind of archive. The mosses on the older walls show growth rings of a sort, not annual rings in the tree sense, but patterns of density and composition that correspond to past conditions. A wet decade shows up as a layer of particular liverworts, now buried under later growth. A period of atmospheric change — perhaps when the foundry was running harder, when the air carried more of something — shows up as a dark band in the lichen crust on the northern walls. The buildings are not just buildings. They are stratigraphic columns. Someone who knew what to look for could read the history of the town in the walls without consulting any written record.

The walls remember everything that has happened to them.

---

## Animals

Rare. The ecosystem here does not encourage much. The cool, damp, heavily vegetated environment produces an abundance of invertebrates — isopods in every crack, springtails in the moss, beetles of various slow kinds working through the accumulated organic matter on the rooftops — but these go mostly unseen. The food web doesn't build. There are not enough insects to support many birds, not enough birds to support anything above them. The foundry smell perhaps discourages. The lack of flowering plants — almost all the vegetation here is non-flowering, mosses and ferns and liverworts — means almost no nectar, no bees, no pollinators.

What animals there are have found their niches and stay in them quietly. The bird you occasionally hear. Mice, possibly, in the grain store — evidence of them rather than the mice themselves. Once, at the edge of town where the buildings give way to the valley scrub, something moving in the low vegetation: too quick, too small, gone before you can bring it into focus.

---

## Dynamic

And yet the town moves. This is crucial.

Not dramatically. Not eventfully. But continuously.

The moss grows. Millimeter by millimeter, the surfaces change. The drainage channels carry water through their slow courses. The generator cycles. The looms work. The condensation forms and runs and evaporates and forms. The water rises in the cistern over days of damp, falls over days of dry, rises again. The ferns in the window frames push new fronds out in spring, each one emerging tightly coiled and pale and then unwinding into the characteristic long blade. The lichen on the north walls is, right now, in the process of etching the stone it grows on: a process so slow it reads as stillness, but isn't.

Somewhere in the walls, moss is colonizing a surface it has not yet reached. Somewhere in the drainage, the water is carrying a small load of dissolved minerals from the foundation stone, slowly, year after year, altering the chemistry of whatever lies downstream. The foundry produces its heat and smell and product. The textiles accumulate.

The town is a set of processes that have found a stable relationship with each other and are content to continue. Not going anywhere. Not done. Actively, quietly, perpetually becoming whatever it has been slowly becoming since before anyone now living arrived.

That's the feeling it gives. Patient industry. Settled motion. The deep hum of a place that has worked out how to persist.
