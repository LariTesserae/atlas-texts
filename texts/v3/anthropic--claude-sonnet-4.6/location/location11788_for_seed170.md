# The Forge Steppe

---

Begin with the heat.

It arrives before anything else resolves — before the eye can sort shape from shimmer, before the mind can ask what it is looking at. The heat is not weather. It is not the consequence of a sun being close or a sky being thin, though both of those things are also true here. The heat is *geological*. It rises from below in the way that conviction rises from the body: not from any one point but from the whole field of being, sourceless, total, irrefutable. The ground gives it. The air holds it. The light — ordinary light, the light of a sun that looks approximately right in an approximately blue-white sky — the light does not produce it so much as *agree* with it, the way a voice agrees with what a room already knows.

Stand here and your first impression is: this place has been this way for a very long time.

---

The steppe extends.

That is the primary fact of it after the heat, and it takes longer to absorb because the eye keeps expecting an edge, a feature, a tree line, anything that would say: *here the thing ends and another thing begins*. No such boundary comes. The terrain rolls in long low swells, each one a kilometer or more crest-to-crest, and from any high point you can see twelve, fifteen of these swells stacked against the horizon, each one slightly paler and more violet in the haze, until the farthest ones become indistinguishable from the sky itself not because they're small but because the air here is faintly mineral, faintly opaque, carrying particulate that was once rock and is now simply atmosphere. The steppe does not end. The steppe becomes sky, and perhaps sky becomes steppe somewhere past the visible range, in the logic of this place, the distinction may not hold.

The elevation is real. You feel it in the lungs — thinner air, not dangerously thin, but thin enough that breathing is a thing you notice. Thin enough that sound carries differently: voices and impacts cross distance with a strange clarity and then die abruptly, as if something is absorbing them at the edge of their range. The horizon is curved very slightly — not the curvature of a planet seen from above but the curvature you perceive when your eyes can reach far enough, when there is nothing to interrupt the line of sight. Here, you can actually see the world bending.

---

The surface.

The ground underfoot is basalt. Old basalt — not sharp, not new-flow, not the kind that cuts through boot leather. This basalt has been here long enough that the distinction between individual flows has become academic. It has been welded by its own history into something like a single substance, a continental skin, layer upon layer of former eruptions pressed and annealed by time into a matt that is now simply: the ground. Its color is the color of charcoal that has been charcoal for so long it has forgotten fire, a near-black with undertones of red that become visible when the sun is low, and the surface is not flat but fractured into plates ranging from palm-sized to building-sized, each one fitting against its neighbors with the intimacy of long settlement. Rain does not fall here often enough to wear the joints smooth. Wind has worked them in its own way — a fine grit collects in every seam, the mineral corpse of whatever was softer and got abraded over the millennia, and this grit is the color of old brick, almost orange, and it drifts in thin windrows between the plates.

Scattered across this surface: lava tubes, collapsed. Their roofs have come down — slowly, probably, one piece at a time over thousands of years — and left elongated craters five to fifteen meters across and two to four meters deep, their interiors the same basalt but somehow darker, more compressed-looking, as if the inside of the earth is more serious than its surface. Some of these craters are connected. An underground system of former gas channels extends beneath the steppe, most of it inaccessible now, collapsed into rubble, but occasionally a crack between two surface plates exhales a breath of hotter air from below — not sulfurous, not chemically offensive, just *hotter*, as if something is breathing down there in the dark, something enormous and very slow.

The surface has been here long enough to have settled into itself. You do not feel that you are standing on something active. You feel that you are standing on the conclusion of something active: the permanent record, the final form, the thing that remains after all the argument is done.

---

And then the fauna.

This is where the ground rules of the place begin to unmake themselves.

They are everywhere. At first you mistake them for features of the rock — they are the right color, mostly, or rather they have achieved coloration that makes the question of their being separate from the rock feel naïve. But they move. Not quickly — nothing here moves quickly — but they move, and once you see the movement you cannot stop seeing it and the entire steppe becomes something different: not empty terrain with scattered animals but a continuous living surface, so densely populated that the spaces between bodies are the anomaly, and the basalt itself becomes merely the substrate, the excuse, the skeleton around which the real material of this place has organized itself.

What are they?

The question assumes a category system that this place has outgrown.

There are things the size of tortoises, armored not in bone-derived shell but in something that grew from the outside in — mineralized integument, accreted over decades, so that the oldest individuals carry on their backs what amounts to a personal geology, a layered record of their own passage through time, ridged and cratered and faintly striated in ways that mirror the basalt beneath them. They move at a speed measurable only by watching for minutes at a time. Their mouths — if they are mouths — work at the surface, processing the mineral film that accumulates on exposed rock, extracting whatever chemical energy inheres in the differential between sun-warmed oxidized surface and the slightly different chemistry of the protected joint beneath. There are thousands of them. Tens of thousands visible from any single vantage point.

Above them, and denser still: smaller things, centimeter-scale, moving in ways that challenge interpretation. They cluster on vertical crack faces, on the undersides of overhanging plate edges, in the rubble floors of collapsed tubes, and their locomotion is not quite crawling and not quite flowing but something between — a propagation of form, a movement of *pattern* through substance, so that you cannot be entirely certain whether an individual is moving or whether you are watching a wave move through a population. They leave traces: thin bright lines on dark rock, almost metallic, that fade over hours back to the basalt's native matte. The lines branch. They do not form obvious networks, but they do not seem random either. They form — something. Something that is the scale of network without the legibility of network.

Then the large things.

You become aware of them slowly. They are at the edge of perception for some time — large enough that you first read them as topography, as the next swell in the terrain. They are not the next swell. They are moving. Very slowly, but moving: each step is a long deliberate thing, each footfall — if it is a footfall — preceded by what looks like a period of calculation, a pause, a processing, as if the creature is performing a geological survey before committing its weight. They are eight, ten meters at the shoulder. Their bodies are irregular in the way that accumulated things are irregular: not the clean irregularity of design but the complex irregularity of accretion, of having persisted long enough to gather mass and modification from the world through which they have moved. Their surfaces are colonized by the smaller organisms. They carry the small ones the way old ships carry barnacles, the way old forests carry orchids — the large creatures are not unaware of this, or seem not to be unaware, but they do not address it. They continue their slow traverse of the steppe, heading — it becomes apparent after watching for some time — all in roughly the same direction. Not a stampede. Not a migration in any urgent sense. A drift, geological in patience, a population-level movement that is probably measurable only across seasons.

There are many of the large ones. More than should be possible, given how little appears to sustain them.

---

The sound is continuous and enormous.

It takes time to parse. The foreground contains: the dry clicking of the small-scale organisms on rock, which is individually subtle but in aggregate becomes a textured white noise, a biological static that fills the air the way the heat fills the air. Beneath this: the deep resonant *contact* of the large creatures when they step — not a thud exactly, more of a transmission, felt in the feet as much as heard, as if the whole steppe is a drum and the large ones are drumming it, slowly, in not-quite-random rhythm. And beneath that: something else. A sound from below the surface. From the tubes and channels. A sound that is not gas escaping and not rock settling but something in between, something that has the quality of language without the quality of communication — repetitive, varied, deep, carrying through basalt better than through air, so that it arrives at the feet before it arrives at the ears and by the time the ears register it the body has already been processing it for seconds.

The wind adds its part. There is always wind at this elevation, and it moves across the steppe with nothing to interrupt it for long distances, and the basalt plates it passes over and between function as reeds, as gaps in a structure, and the wind gives a low harmonic, a chord without a root, which underpins everything else the way the heat underpins everything else: simply there, part of the definition of the place, what you would notice only in its absence.

The combined sound of this steppe is loud in the way that only patient things are loud — not the loudness of an event but the loudness of a condition.

---

The weirdness is structural.

It has been here long enough to become physics.

Here is the first thing that does not parse: the large creatures cast shadows that do not quite match their bodies. The shadows are the right size and the right general shape but they are off — displaced slightly, or angled wrong for the sun's position, or occasionally they extend in a direction that has no sun to cast them. The creatures do not acknowledge this. The smaller organisms on their backs do not respond to it. The shadows move as the creatures move, maintaining their wrongness with perfect consistency, as if something decided, a very long time ago, that shadows in this place would work this way, and the decision has since become simply how it is.

Here is the second thing: objects left in the same place for long enough begin to be in two places. Not copied, not replicated — present in two places simultaneously in the way that a note is present in both its point of origin and your ear simultaneously, as if the steppe has developed its own principle of resonance and objects that remain long enough begin to resonate against the landscape and appear elsewhere in it. The basalt plates themselves are affected. The old ones — and they are all old — have a quality of existing slightly more than once. You learn to look at things from the corner of your vision when you want to be certain of their position.

Here is the third thing, which is harder to describe: the fauna know something. Not in a way that suggests intelligence in any recognizable form. In a way that suggests they have been the primary cognitive presence on this steppe for so long that cognition has distributed itself into them the way heat distributes into rock — gradually, evenly, until it is not a feature of any individual unit but a property of the material itself. When the large creatures pause in their slow drift and seem to consider before stepping, they are not doing what a large herbivore does when it considers where to put its foot. They are doing something else. Something that has to do with the doubled positions of the rocks and the misdirected shadows and the sound from below. Something that has to do with a steppe that has been this way long enough to develop its own position on the question of what a place is allowed to be.

The smallest organisms leave their metallic traces. The traces, seen from above — seen from the lip of a collapsed tube, looking down at the colony working the interior surface — form arrangements that become more organized the longer you look at them. Not organized toward a shape you recognize. Organized toward something that shape is perhaps an early version of.

---

The tech is real and it is absent.

This is the central paradox that takes longest to arrive at.

There are no structures. No built things. No artifacts, no instruments, no surfaces that were shaped by intention — none that were shaped by *human* or *human-equivalent* intention. But the steppe runs. It *processes*. The organisms function with a coordination and an efficiency that exceeds what biology alone accounts for. The energy cycling of this place — the way heat from below is captured by the mineralized tortoise-things and diffused into the smaller organisms and into the substrate traces and into the large ones and back into the ground via their long footfalls — is too efficient. It is more efficient than it should be. It is efficient the way a well-designed system is efficient, not because anyone designed it but because it has had so long to find its optimal configuration that design and emergence have become the same thing.

The steppe is a machine. It was not built. It became one.

The engines are organisms. The factory floor is basalt. The production is — unclear. The output of this system is not product in any sense that maps to familiar categories. The output seems to be the maintenance of a particular set of conditions: this heat, this density of life, this organization of presence against emptiness, this specific wrongness of the shadows, this sound from below that travels through rock better than through air.

The steppe is producing itself. With industrial thoroughness. At geological speed.

---

What you feel, standing in it, is not quite solitude — the presence of the fauna is too overwhelming for solitude — but something adjacent to solitude, which is the feeling of being present at something that is not about you, that does not require you, that predates you so completely that your presence here registers as neither welcome nor intrusion but simply as *data*, one more weight on the surface, one more thing for the shadows to get slightly wrong, one more set of footfalls for the underground to register and add to its long record.

The heat continues. The organisms continue. The large ones take another step in the slow drift toward wherever they are all going.

The steppe settles into itself another fraction of a millimeter.

Out at the horizon — fifteen ridges out, where rock becomes haze becomes sky — something is moving that is larger than the large ones, or the horizon itself is moving, or the distinction between those two possibilities is one that this place decided against long ago.

You keep watching.

The heat agrees with the light.

The light agrees with everything.
