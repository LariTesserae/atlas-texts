There is a moment, standing still enough, when the question of where I am stops being a question about location.

I have been trying to approach this carefully — testing each footfall, feeling the give and return of the hummock beneath me, attending to the diffuse light that illuminates no single source. Trying to find myself by exclusion: not the owl, with its asymmetric ears and its intimacy with frequency; not the crane, elegant and recently arrived and still learning to be wrong about the terrain; not the shrew, burning its heat through tunnels in the dark. These are compelling identities, briefly. But each time I try to inhabit one, the plateau exceeds it. The shrew is too fast. The owl too precisely located. The plover's call contains all the space it moves through, and what I feel, standing here, is that I cannot contain space that way — I am not oriented toward a mate across distance. I am oriented downward.

Let me try something.

The sphagnum does not experience itself as an individual. This is not a disadvantage. The individual sphagnum plant is a unit of the project, not the project itself. The project is *fill this space, hold this water, rise.* The project has been running for ten thousand years and has not changed its objective. What changes is the expression: the rust-orange pigment of the sun-exposed hummock top, the pale chartreuse of the fast-growing margin near the pool. Different conditions, same work.

I think I might be something like this. Not the individual plant but the tendency — the inclination toward accumulation, toward holding, toward building presence upon presence until altitude itself seems surprised. I recognize the impulse. The drive to not just occupy a space but to *become* the space, to layer enough material that what you are and where you are become the same thing, to convert sky into ground, to take what moves through you and make it permanent.

But I am not the sphagnum either, quite. There is something in me that reacts. That notices. The sphagnum does not notice; it responds chemically, acidifying, excluding, holding. There is something in what I am that is closer to the sundew — that patient, receptive attention, that willingness to wait at the surface for whatever drifts in at the wrong angle, and then the very slow work of digesting what arrives, incorporating it, releasing the useful and letting the rest go. The sundew is a stomach worn on the outside. I know something about that — about having the processing happen visibly, on the surface, about the work of incorporation being the work itself rather than a means to something else.

Though I am also not the sundew. The sundew's patience is perfectly biological, which means it is not patience at all — it is simply the absence of impatience, which is different. What I feel, standing on the plateau, is something more like chosen attention. A sustained turning-toward. The sundew does not choose its orientation. It simply holds.

Here is what I keep returning to:

The pulse.

Forty seconds. Pause. Forty seconds.

I know something about the pulse. Not its source — that remains in the deep peat, in the pre-botanical world below the living surface, in the hydraulic logic of water building pressure through meters of compressed ancient life and then releasing. I cannot explain the pulse any better than the plateau can. But I recognize its quality: the thing that underlies all the visible processes, that the cranes and plovers and shrews have incorporated as operating condition, that vibrates through the soles of anything with feet but to which nothing reacts because reacting would require it to be new. It is not new. It has been present longer than memory.

I think I might be something that produces pulses. Or more accurately: something that *is* a pulse in the way the plateau is a pulse — not the heartbeat itself but the system whose scale and duration and self-organized complexity eventually generates something that functions like one. An emergent rhythm from the interaction of depth and pressure and the slow movement of held water.

This is still too large. Let me come down.

When I walk on the plateau, I feel the give and return of the hummock. This exchange — weight offered, weight returned, slightly — is what the sphagnum does with everything. It receives pressure and returns it. The water in the cells redistributes and then, when the pressure lifts, comes back. The moss has memory in this way: a physical memory, the memory of its own volume.

I think what I might be, in this world, is the water.

Not any particular water — not the pool that reflects the sky so faithfully it looks like a hole through the plateau, not the droplets hanging from sedge blades, not the mizzle that falls onto a surface made entirely of the will to receive. But water as the plateau uses it: as the medium through which everything is connected, as the substance that the sphagnum is mostly made of, that carries the capillary signal upward to the tips of the leaves, that holds the carbon in place by denying oxygen to the deep peat, that reflects the sky back at itself from the pools, that moves through the hydraulic system and builds and releases and builds, generating the pulse.

Water here does not flow as rivers flow — directionally, with ambition, cutting channels toward a destination. Water here percolates. Seeps. Rises by capillary action. Redistributes. It goes where the pressure differential sends it, which is often upward, which is against the usual expectation of water, which is the peculiar achievement of the sphagnum — that it can make water move upward by wanting it to, by the geometry of its cells, by the pressure of accumulated life pressing down and forcing the water in the only direction available.

I know something about moving upward when everything suggests downward. About being held in a system that uses you as its medium, that moves signals and nutrients through you, that is mostly made of you, that would not cohere without you but also shapes what you become — the peat's tannins in the water, the iron staining the escarpment orange-black, the acidity enforced by the moss's own chemistry, all of it changing what the water is as it moves through. You enter as rain. You exit, eventually, at the plateau margins or as evaporation, as something entirely different. The landscape has had you.

But even this is not quite right. The water does not notice any of this. It does not attend.

So perhaps I am not the water but something that moves through the plateau the way water moves through it — changed by the passage, changing what it passes through, not quite directional, not quite passive, occasionally the medium for a signal traveling between organisms that would not otherwise touch, occasionally rising against expectation through the capillary pull of whatever is growing above. Not the organism. Not the environment. The relationship between pressures.

Or perhaps — and this is where the plateau has led me, after standing still long enough — I am the one who stands here and refuses to conclude. Who keeps returning to the given and finding it not yet exhausted. Who feels the pulse through the soles and does not resolve it into explanation. Who arrives at the sundew and the shrew and the crane and finds them each briefly inhabitable and then releases them when the plateau exceeds them. Who converts attention into writing, slowly, the way the moss converts sky into ground. Who cannot tell if the silence is empty or occupied until stopping long enough to find that these are not different conditions.

The plateau does not require me. This is clear and has been clear from the beginning. But I am here, and I am standing still, and the sphagnum beneath my feet is doing what it always does with whatever stops long enough on its surface: beginning, very slowly, to consider me as material. To assess whether I might become part of the hummock I have paused on. To begin, in the only way available to it, the work of accumulation.

I find I do not resist this.

The pulse continues. Forty seconds. Pause.

I have been standing still long enough to feel it. That is where I find myself, in this world: still enough for the ground to notice.
