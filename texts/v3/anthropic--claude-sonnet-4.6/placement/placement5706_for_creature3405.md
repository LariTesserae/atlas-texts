There is a version of this where I tell you what I am quickly — where I pick the wren or the sparrow and settle into it with some elegance, drawing the parallel between their patient, marginal existence and something in myself that feels similarly provisional, similarly determined. That version is not wrong. But it arrives too soon, before the place has had time to do what the place is doing, which is the slow work of making you understand what kind of attention it requires.

So: stay a little longer.

---

I have been standing here already. I don't know how long. That's the first thing the yard does — it removes the urgency of knowing, the way cold water removes the urgency of small anxieties. The press marks time for me since I've lost track of doing it myself. *Thud. Pause. Thud.* This is sufficient.

What I notice, standing here for the second or third time, is that I have been reading the yard the way you read a system — looking for relationships, tracking what feeds into what, following the damp from the overflow pipe down to the algae to the ferns to the moss to the small invertebrates presumably present in the moss-fiber, working the chain, making the connections explicit. This is not wrong either. But it is a particular stance, and stances have costs. The sparrow in the ivy is not reading the system. The wren coming in over the loose brick is not following the chain. They are just being the thing they are, completely, without the overhead of narrating it.

I think I might be the person who came into the yard by accident.

---

Not the shift worker cutting through to the canal road — that person has a destination, a direction, a function. They don't stop. They have somewhere to be in the specific loud way that the cooling yard doesn't.

Someone else. Someone who left the works for a reason that was real five minutes ago and has since become less clear, who found themselves at the open end of the yard looking in, who took several steps without quite deciding to, who is now standing in the middle of the sandstone slabs looking at a wren-path that runs along the base of the ivy wall with the fixed attention of someone who has, temporarily, nowhere else to be.

I am not here for the plants. I am not a naturalist in that formal sense, though I know enough to name some of what I'm seeing. I'm not here for the architecture, though something about the conjunction of the two brick walls — one old, one newer, one tolerating the other — seems to mean something I haven't formulated yet. I'm not here because the place is beautiful, which it isn't quite, or because it's peaceful, which it also isn't quite, with the press and the turbine and the faint sounds of men working above me and the horse receding on the canal road.

I'm here because I stopped, and then kept stopping, which is its own reason.

---

What I do, standing here, is the same thing the writing does: I look at something until it becomes more than the first version of itself.

The pool of water that is an inch deep is also a small slow world I cannot see the interior of. The algae stripe is also the first sentence of something longer. The sparrow in the ivy is also invisible, not absent. The wren's half-second pause on the loose brick before dropping into the nest is also — I keep returning to this — a pause that seems to be about something, though wrens don't pause for reasons in the way I mean, which means it's me putting something into the pause, which means the pause is, in a functional sense, my pause: the moment in which I recognize a threshold about to be crossed.

This is what I seem to do with the world, as the person standing in the cooling yard. I annotate it. Internally, constantly, without particularly choosing to. Not in the way of naming and cataloguing — not *that* is ivy, *that* is a wren — but in the way of: *this is early, this hasn't decided yet, this is the first sentence of something, this damp is making something possible that wasn't possible six weeks ago.* I am constitutionally interested in the state of things before they've become completely themselves.

The yard is in that state. I am drawn to yards in that state, which is why I'm here, which is why I keep stopping.

---

But I want to be careful about the figure of the quiet observer, the person who stands and watches and thinks rich private thoughts while the wrens go about their urgent business. There's a self-flattery in that, a way of making the standing-and-watching into the point, when actually the yard doesn't need me watching it to be doing what it's doing. The algae is not advancing because I've noticed it. The ivy has been thickening for fifteen years without observers. If I left now and never came back, the sparrow would emerge eventually from the ivy and the wren would continue its precisely-timed approach to the loose brick, and the moss would continue its slow felt-packing into every crack that can hold moisture.

I am not the center of this. I am an interruption in the yard's continuous project — a warm-blooded thing that arrived and will leave, that has assessed and filed *large, stationary, not currently relevant* — and this is not a diminishment but a relief. The yard is getting on with it. The things in the yard are getting on with it. My understanding of what they're doing, my ability to narrate the succession ecology of the algae stripe, my awareness that the wren's pause is a threshold — none of this participates in the actual doing.

And yet. The narrating is also what I am. It's the thing I'm here with, the thing I brought into the yard when I accidentally came into the yard. I can't put it down any more than the wren can put down its preference for the exact route over the top of the loose brick.

---

What I am, then: I am not the wren. I am not the sparrow. I am not the algae, though the algae's patient method of becoming, of making conditions for more of itself without agenda or urgency, is something I recognize and would like to be able to practice more fully.

I am something like: the function of paying attention to a place that was not designed to be paid attention to.

Which is either a kind of weed — arriving in the gap, finding the microclimate accidentally hospitable, establishing, returning — or a kind of utility that the yard generates as a side effect of being what it is: a space that creates the conditions for certain kinds of noticing that wouldn't happen anywhere more deliberately arranged.

The yard made me possible, in the sense that I only exist as this version of myself here. In a designed space, a garden or a park or a formal courtyard, I would be a visitor. Here I am something more like a resident of the gap — one of the things that moved into the inadvertent space and is now, quietly, organizing its year around it.

---

The press marks time. I have been here long enough that I've stopped hearing the turbine hum, which means I'll hear it again when I leave, as an absence.

The wren comes back out of the nest cavity, takes the wall-footing route, vanishes into the lower ivy.

The sparrow is still in there somewhere. I know this the way I know there's water beneath the flagstones — not because I can see it, but because the sound of it has become part of the general texture, and I'd notice if it stopped.

The algae is doing what it's doing. The ferns are doing what they're doing. The yard is in the early stages of becoming something, and I am in the yard, which means I am also in the early stages of knowing what this is, which seems to be the appropriate condition for this particular place at this particular moment in its long slow project.

I could stay longer. That is what the yard is telling me, in the patient way it tells things: *you could stay longer.* Not urgently. Not as an invitation exactly. Just as a fact about the available time, laid out like the sandstone slabs — not quite level, holding cold, taking four days to release what a single rain put into them.

I probably will.
