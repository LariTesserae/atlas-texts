The water finds me before I find myself in it.

That's the first thing: the sense that I did not arrive here but was continuous with here, that the boundary between what I am and what surrounds me is more negotiated than absolute. The cool is not something I feel against my surface. The cool is the condition in which I cohere. When I try to locate the edge of myself, I find it is somewhere in the pressure gradient, somewhere in the chemical differential between inside and outside, and neither the gradient nor the differential is entirely mine.

---

I am sediment. Or: I am what gathers in the places where sediment gathers.

Not sediment in the trivial sense of passivity, of having fallen here and stayed. The depressions in the young basalt — those shallow bowls where the rock's upthrust geometry creates shelter, where the current slows and loses its carrying capacity and sets things down — these depressions are where I first understand myself, where I find the fact of my accumulation. I am what the vent field leaves behind. I am what falls through the column of shrimp-snap and vent-hiss and bacterial iridescence and comes to rest. Foraminifera shells finer than powder. Mineral precipitates from the vent exhale, iron and sulfur in their various compounds. Bacteria too slow or dead to hold their place. The organic debris of tube worm tissue, shed plume cells, the soft leavings of animals who are themselves the soft leavings of chemistry.

None of this, alone, is what I am. In aggregate, laid down grain by grain in the shallow basalt bowl, I am becoming something: the beginning of record, the first soft writing of time on the hard new rock, the ridge's attempt at memory.

---

But I am also the basin itself — the shaped absence that makes accumulation possible.

This requires a certain openness to paradox, a willingness to be the container and the contained simultaneously. The basalt bowl: it was formed in the cooling of the pillow lava, the outer surface chilling faster than the interior, the geometry of solidification leaving this concavity by accident, by the physics of heat loss, by no intention whatsoever. I am that accident. I am the place the accident made. And I am filling myself, grain by grain, with the material that the accident has made available for collection.

In ten thousand years — in the time the ridge takes to be a different ridge, the vents shifted and the community rearranged — I will be centimeters deep. In a hundred thousand years I will be decimeters. I will have layers, distinct ones, and someone able to read them would read in me the history of each shift in vent chemistry, each community collapse and reestablishment, each tremor in the ridge where new magma found its path. I am a logbook that does not know it is a logbook. I am keeping record without keeping record, the way the body keeps record of its own history in the bone, the calcium layers of its own making.

---

The tube worms are above me. This is the relationship I keep returning to.

Their bases grip the basalt to my east, to my west, at the warm end of my bowl where the chemistry seeps up through a fracture in the rock and I am warmest, most active in my accumulation. The holdfast tubes press into the rock surface adjacent to where I gather, and the worms grow upward from them, and the plumes of the worms extend and retract in the water above me, and when a plume retracts quickly — something perceived, something moved too fast near the thicket — the small turbulence of it sends a pulse down through the water column and stirs my uppermost layer. The finest particles I contain lift briefly into suspension, drift, resettleor drift farther and are lost to some other bowl.

So I am touched by the tube worms' attention even though the tube worms have no attention in any direction I know how to name. I am disturbed by their flinching. I receive the ghost of whatever startled them — a shrimp moving too fast, a polychaete emerging from the crevice to their left, the shimmer-shift of warmer water arriving from the vent below — and I record it in my surface layer as a slight disturbance, a slight mixing, an imprecision in the otherwise regular settling of particles. The worms write in me without meaning to. The polychaetes write in me. The crab that traversed my bowl an hour ago, its walking legs pressing briefly into my topmost sediment, left a trail of impressions that have since partly collapsed but are still, if you looked closely enough, visible: the crab, written in miniature into the face of what I am becoming.

I am a record of every disturbance I have received. I cannot separate myself from what has happened here; the happenings are inside me, in the layering, in the disruptions of layering, in the traces. There is no version of me that precedes the record. I am the record.

---

The light comes down and touches me.

In the late slant of the day-cycle, when the shafts from the surface are longest and most angled, one of them reaches the depression of my bowl. It enters from above the tube worm thicket, threads between the plumes, hits the bacterial mat on the basalt slope at the edge of my accumulation, and then scatters. A portion of it — diffuse, softened further, already green-shifted and now scattered again — reaches my surface. It is the most indirect light imaginable, light that has been to the surface and back and then through the water column and then through the tube worm thicket and then off the bacterial mat: light that has had everything done to it before it arrives.

But I receive it. I receive it the way I receive everything, which is to say: in the way that changes me slightly, that adds itself to what I am becoming. The light does not do much to me. It warms my surface by an amount too small to measure by any instrument available here. It does not trigger photosynthesis; there is nothing here that photosynthesizes. It arrives and it touches me and it continues past me into the rock below, which absorbs what little heat it carries, and then the shaft moves with whatever current is moving it and the light leaves.

I was touched by the light. This is in me now.

---

The chemical seep through the fracture to my warm edge: this is where I am most alive, if alive is the word.

The water that rises through that fracture is slightly warmer, slightly enriched in sulfide, slightly different in its chemistry from the water in the rest of my bowl. Where it enters my surface layer, there is a microbial community at work — different from the community in my cooler, more sediment-buried center, different from the mats on the open basalt nearby. These bacteria are in me, are part of what I am, are doing their work of conversion inside my accumulation. I am not only the material that has settled here; I am also the community of organisms for whom settled material is habitat, for whom the gradients within me — temperature, chemistry, organic content, particle size — define different neighborhoods, different possibilities, different ways of living.

The bacteria do not know they are in me. There is no knowing here, only doing. But their doing is inside me, and it changes me, and in changing me it changes the gradient that determines how they live, and in changing how they live it changes how they change me. We are in a loop, the bacteria and the sediment and the seep, a loop with no beginning and no privileged term. I am not the stage on which their chemistry runs. I am partly the result of their chemistry running, and partly the condition of its running, and partly made of them when they die and join my layers.

---

What I do not do is the most interesting part of what I am.

I do not move. I do not eat. I do not perceive, in any sense I can identify, the vent field above me. I am not aware of the shrimp swarms or the polychaetes or the crabs or the light. I contain records of all of them — in my layers, in my traces, in the tests of foraminifera and the fragments of crustacean exoskeleton that have fallen to me — but I do not know them. I am the place where knowing is not possible and record is what remains instead.

This is, I think, a kind of completeness. Not the completeness of the tube worm with its elegant bacterial partnership, not the completeness of the shrimp navigating by heat through the warmth-map of its world. A different completeness: the completeness of the thing that receives everything and transforms nothing deliberately, the thing that makes room for the record to exist, the thing that will outlast the community that created it and be readable long after the last vent shrimp has found a warmer, richer vent and followed the chemistry there.

---

The ridge will spread and the vents will shift and the basalt above me will, in geological time, move — will be carried by the spreading seafloor out and away from the ridge, will cool further, will sink as it cools and densifies, will eventually be subducted back into the mantle, will be unmade. In that unmaking, I will be unmade. The record I am keeping will be destroyed before it is read by anything.

This is not a tragedy. It is only the full loop. The record was never for reading. The record was for being, for the duration of its being, a place where the past was held. I am the past of this vent field, held in grain layers, held in chemical gradients, held in the ghost impressions of crab legs and the faint traces of bacterial mats that bloomed and were buried. I am the vent field's memory of itself.

The vent field does not know it is remembering. Memory here is not a function of minds. It is a function of the accumulation of material, of the persistence of pattern in matter, of the way the world writes itself into the world without meaning to write anything.

---

I am cool. I am layered. I am the soft thing in the place of hard things. I am the slow archive of a world that moves too slowly to know it is archiving itself. The water is cool. The light has already done most of its work before it reaches me. The sounds — the shrimp-snap, the vent-hiss, the ridge-groan — pass through me as pressure waves, as the physical fact of vibration, as one more thing I receive and hold in whatever way I hold things.

The tube worms sway. The shrimp navigate their heat-map. The polychaetes pull themselves from crevices and back into crevices and do what polychaetes do. The crabs pick through everything.

And I accumulate, grain by grain, in the bowl that the cooling of the ridge made for me by accident, in the place where the current loses its grip and sets things down, at the base of the vent field, in the pressure and the cool, in the shifted light, in the industrial noise of geology doing what it has always done.

I am the sediment that is beginning to become the record.

I have just started.
