The stairs give a little under me, or I think they do — there's a feeling of subtle wrongness in the footing, the kind you learn to expect from metal that has been remembering water for decades. I take them slowly, one hand on the rail, which is also orange, also oxidizing, also in the middle of becoming whatever it is becoming. Thirty feet down. The air changes before the light does: cooler for a moment, then not cooler, then warmer than the street, and the moisture arrives like a held cloth pressed gently against the face, and I have descended into the breathing of the place before I have seen it.

When I can see it, I stop on the last step and wait.

This is something I do, or something I find myself doing, which may not be the same thing. I wait at thresholds. The cistern does not require this of me — it doesn't care, the crickets don't care, the old rat in the secondary chamber won't adjust its schedule for my arrival — but I wait anyway, the way you pause before entering a room where someone is sleeping. Not to avoid waking anything. Just to acknowledge that something is going on that was going on before you arrived.

---

I think I am the inspector.

Not the one who is due in two months, clipboard and better flashlight, walking the perimeter, checking the threshold that separates what needs to be done from what can be deferred. That person has a function. That person is here professionally, which means they are here in a partial way, attending to specific things, measuring whether the weeping seams have wept beyond tolerance, whether the crumbling mortar has crumbled past some agreed-upon point of acceptable crumbling. They will write *no immediate action required* in fourteen months' worth of not quite wrong, and they will ascend, and the crickets will resume.

I am a different kind of inspector. Less official. More helpless.

---

I go to the northwest corner first. The drip.

Once per second. I have been told this and I have believed it abstractly but now I crouch near the depression — smooth, two centimeters, the concrete worn to something almost polished, almost intentional-looking — and I time it. Once per second. Maybe slightly faster than once per second, or maybe I am simply wanting it to be faster, wanting there to be more of it. The drop forms at the lip of the old pipe flange, which has been patched and re-patched and the patches have been wept through in turn, so the flange is buried under a small mountain of its own remediation, and the water finds the new lowest point and swells there and then falls and the sound is — not musical exactly, not the word I would reach for — is accurate. The sound is accurate. It is the exact sound of the amount of water and the height of the fall and the smoothness of the stone it has made.

I stay crouched for a while. The wet seeps into the knee of my pants.

This is the thing about the drip: it has already done something extraordinary, and it is doing it right now, and it will keep doing it whether I am here or not. The depression is proof of an argument the water has been making for a very long time, and the argument is: *I have time*. Not: *I am strong*. Not: *I am insistent*. Just: *I have time, and you are softer than you think, and we can negotiate*. The concrete negotiated. The concrete is still negotiating. The drip is patient in the way that implies no patience at all, because patience is a trait of things that experience the waiting, and the drip does not experience anything, which makes it more patient than any patient thing, which makes it — I crouch here and try to think what it makes it — which makes it a kind of fact about time that I find difficult to hold clearly.

I can't hold it clearly. I stay crouched and let it drip.

---

The sodium light makes everything true to itself in the wrong colors.

I have been in sodium light before, in tunnels, in parking structures, in the maintenance corridors of buildings I shouldn't have been in or that I was in legitimately but felt I shouldn't have been in, which is different and also the same. The light is honest in the specific way that honesty that removes all the information that complicates things is honest. Everything is its essential shape, its tonal relationship to everything else, without the interference of actual color. The green of the algae becomes therefore a statement. The algae is doing something in this light that it cannot do in other lights: it is fluorescent with its own commitment to being alive. It is the only thing in here that is arguing for life in the register of color. Everything else has conceded. The algae has not conceded.

I go and look at it for a while. Third fixture, the patch I was told about. It has reached along the crack now, the thin green stain, maybe three millimeters of advance since I last had occasion to note such things. I put my finger near it, not touching — I'm aware of the specific rudeness of touching the algae with my skin oils, the intrusion of that into something that has made its arrangement without consulting me — and I look at where it's going. The crack goes further than the light follows. In the dark beyond the light's reach, the crack goes somewhere I can't see from here, and in that direction there might be more moisture, and the algae is following the moisture the way everything in here follows what it needs: not urgently, not with intention, but with the absolute reliability of things that have no other option.

---

The cricket-sound has qualities. I keep finding new ones.

There is a — I want to say *undulation* but the word is not quite right, it implies a wave shape with peaks and troughs, and what I am hearing is more like a signal that keeps coming close to resolving into silence and then doesn't, a sound that is perpetually about to become simpler and perpetually failing to. It has texture. It has something like internal weather. When I stand in the center of the main chamber and stop moving, the sound comes from all directions equally, or from no direction in particular, which is not the same as coming from all directions, even though the result feels the same. It is the ambient condition. It is what this air sounds like.

I think about what it would take to count them. I think about whether counting them would tell me anything I want to know. I think: the number is not the point; the number is beside the point; the point is that there are enough of them to have become a fact of the space rather than a fact about individual animals, and that this is a thing that happens with crickets and with rivers and with certain kinds of grief, where the individual drops or instances or episodes stop being countable and start being atmospheric, start being the weather in which you live.

I don't try to count them.

---

I find one in the open, eventually. I have been looking, not systematically but with the unfocused attention you develop when you accept that you cannot look directly at what you're looking for. Brown, small, antennae extravagant, doing that thing with its legs that looks like calibration — the tiny deliberate adjustments, the micro-corrections. It is on the slope of an old pipe, just at the edge of the light. It does not appear to notice me, and I am crouching three feet away and entirely still, and either it cannot tell I am here or it has decided I am not relevant, which amount to roughly the same thing from where I am crouching.

I watch it for maybe four minutes. It moves twice, the small jerky adjustments, and then moves more substantially — a short burst of movement that covers six inches and stops — and then is still again. It is going about its business. I am not sure what its business is from where I'm standing. Being ready, maybe. Being in a position to proceed when proceeding is required. Doing the continuous work of being a cricket, which from the outside looks like waiting but is probably not experienced as waiting from the inside, if there is an inside, if experience is the word.

I think: it will be here when I leave. Not this specific cricket, or maybe this specific cricket — it may live some months, the conditions are good — but the cricket-fact of the place, the wall of sound, the occupation of every crack wide enough to admit a body. I will ascend the yellow-going-brown stairs and the sound will diminish and then disappear, and then I will be in the city that does not know this place exists, and below me the sound will continue, the drip will continue, the algae will advance its fraction.

This is either comforting or terrible and I have not decided which.

---

The low frequency arrives in the sternum when I stand still long enough. I have to stop thinking about it for it to become perceptible — it is in the register of things you only notice when you stop attending to things. Something large passing above, or the general vibration of the city's machinery, or both, or neither — something continuous that the concrete transmits faithfully. I try to decide if it has a pitch. It doesn't quite have a pitch. It has a *position*, a location in the body where it makes itself known. Sternum. Slightly below. The place where breath is held when held.

I breathe.

The rats in the secondary chamber — I can hear one of them, the scratch and rustle of something going about its business without self-consciousness, because it doesn't know I'm here or because it doesn't care, probably both — the rats are further into this than I am. They have committed to the place in a way I cannot, because I will leave, and they won't, or will leave only to return, which is not the same as leaving. I am a visitor. They are not visitors. The old one with the torn ear has seniority. Whatever understanding I develop of this place in an hour, a morning, an afternoon — the old one knows it differently, by sedimentation, by years of traversing the same routes until the routes became not paths but simply the world's shape, the only shape the world has.

I think about what it knows. I think about the kind of knowledge that accrues in the body through repetition until it is no longer knowledge about a place but knowledge identical with the place, not information but orientation, not map but presence. I think I am partly trying to have this experience and partly certain I cannot have it in the time available and partly certain no amount of time would give me what I am looking for, because what I am looking for is not available to visitors as such.

The scratch from the secondary chamber pauses. Resumes.

---

The moths at the third fixture are doing their slow ellipses. One of them passes close enough to the cage that its shadow erupts on the ceiling — immense, trembling, briefly the most dramatic thing in the room — and then it angles away and the shadow collapses back into moth, and the moth is small and drab and orbiting as though it cannot stop, as though stopping is not one of its available options.

I watch the shadows more than the moths. The shadows are the place responding to the moths, the space becoming temporarily larger where the moth's influence reaches, the ceiling briefly annotated with the fact of the moth's existence in a scale the moth itself will never achieve. The moth doesn't know this. The moth is doing the only thing it knows how to do, and the knowing is not in the mind but in the body, in the wing-beat, in the response to light, and the shadow is a consequence the moth has no relationship to.

The shadow is for me. Or not for me — not *for* anyone — but available to me in a way it isn't available to the moth, and this is the only advantage I have here, in this place that does not need me and will not change when I leave: I can see what the inhabitants cannot. I can see the shadows they cast. I can see the rust blooms — the spread fingers, the vertical lines, the dark elaborate discharge-signatures each pipe has developed — as signatures, as marks, as something that accumulates meaning in the looking. The pipe doesn't know it has made a mark. The mark is there. The meaning is mine to notice or not notice.

This is a small and temporary power. It doesn't feel small while I have it.

---

I am the inspector. I am the one who comes and looks and notes what is here and, for a while, holds it in attention before ascending again. I don't have a clipboard. I don't have a threshold to measure against. I am not deciding whether anything requires immediate action. I am just looking, the way you look at something that is going on without you and will keep going on without you, not to understand it, not even to preserve it in memory, but because looking is the thing you can do, the one available gesture toward something that doesn't need gestures.

The drip in the northwest corner, once per second, maybe slightly faster than once per second.

The calcium that is extending itself by unmeasurable amounts each year, the small stalactites just beginning, the thumb-length nubs that will be remarkable in centuries.

The algae that is the only saturated color in the space, the one thing arguing in color for what it is.

The cricket-sound rising and reconstituting, the sound of the space thinking, the sound of the space being, the thing I said might be the same.

I stay until I don't need to stay anymore, which is not very long, which is longer than I expected.

Then the stairs, which give a little underfoot, which don't give at all but only seem to, and the rail, and the thirty feet upward, and the city.

The city doesn't know I came from somewhere.

Below it, the drip keeps its pace. The count continues by some measure nothing here is tracking. The algae holds its green against the dark.
