The first thing I know is that I am trying to remember the word for what I am.

Not because I have forgotten it. Because I am discovering, slowly, that there was never a word. There was something I was called, in the organized place I came from—there were many things I was called, many frameworks in which I was classified, and all of them felt temporary even then, even when I was using them, even when I was grateful for their temporary shelter. I came here on purpose. I brought my purposes with me. I stood at the stone ring and measured it and thought: *this is the right size for something*, and wrote that down, and the writing meant something within the framework I'd carried, and the framework meant something within the civilization that had made me, and the civilization—

The civilization is not the point.

---

I came to the Roar Flats as a person who cared about records.

This is what I remember most clearly about myself, in the organized time before: I was a keeper of marks. Not the marks on the flat stone with its dark channels—those were made by someone before me, someone I'd come to find, whose system I'd spent years learning to approach. I was the one who came after, who carried the tools for preservation, who believed that duration was possible, that you could prepare something carefully enough that the future would find it intact. I had a word for this work. The word was important to me. It meant *this matters enough to be made to last*.

I stood at the channels and looked at them for a long time, not with the eye that was reading them—that eye had already given up, had already reported back that the context was gone, the grammar absent, the message sealed behind its own untranslatability—but with the other eye, the one that understood: *here is someone who also believed in duration*. Here is someone who felt the same thing I felt, standing in some organized place, looking at some surface, and decided: *this matters enough*.

And then came here to say it.

In a place that receives everything and returns nothing—that accepts every inscription into a memory that never answers back.

I stayed longer than was sensible. I know this about myself too.

---

What am I, in the flats?

I am a figure at the stone ring, except that I have been here long enough that the present tense is no longer reliable. I arrived. I measured. I documented. I took great care with my instruments in the saturated air. At some point—I cannot locate the point exactly—I stopped arriving and became a person who had arrived. Someone who was here. Someone the flats had registered, the way the flats register everything: as weight and warmth and the specific perturbation of intention.

I am the one who takes notes.

I have taken notes on the substrate's motion, on the period of the local weather cycle, on the correlation between the trees' hum and the warmth of the nearest pools. My notes are detailed and accurate within the framework I arrived with, and the framework I arrived with does not quite fit the flats, and I know this, and I continue to take notes anyway, because taking notes is how I remain what I am. Taking notes is the rope I trail behind me as I go deeper in.

But here is the thing about ropes: they have lengths.

---

The low-slung wader, this morning, was very close.

I sat at the pool's edge—I sit a great deal now; the vibration from below seems less overwhelming if you are lower, or perhaps I have simply habituated to it at eye level and the sternum—and the wader came to within four meters and filtered and did not look at me. I had my notebook. I wrote down the time (though the flat diffuse light makes time difficult to verify), the animal's approximate size, the motion of the sediment in the adjacent zone, the slight acceleration in the pool's warmth that I have begun to believe I can detect with the skin of my hands when I hold them just above the surface.

The wader waited for a shift in the churn. Moved. The sediment closed behind it.

I wrote: *negotiation*. Drew a line through it. Wrote: *courtesy*. Drew a line through that too. Wrote: *the same place, at the same time, by different routes*, and left that.

In the organized world I would have been embarrassed by that sentence. It is not scientific language. It does not say anything verifiable. But I have been here long enough that what I want from language has shifted, and what I want now is not verification but accuracy, which are not the same thing, and the sentence is accurate to what I witnessed, and that feels—

It feels like something I don't have a word for, which is happening more often.

---

I am afraid of the substrate, and I think the flats know this, in their way.

Not afraid of dying—I am afraid of dying less, here, than I have ever been anywhere. The warmth of the pools is specific in a way that warmth is not supposed to be specific. There is something below the surface that has been here long enough to have developed a relationship with temperature, and its version of warmth is—I keep writing *knowing* in my notes and crossing it out; I have nothing better. The warmth is *attentive*. The warmth is what you would make, if you were substrate and patient and had been incorporating the bodies of waders for millennia, and someone you'd been waiting for finally arrived.

I am afraid of that. Of being known in the way the substrate knows things, which is not by understanding but by accumulation, by the pressure I exert and the heat I give off and the specific pattern of my presence that the sediment will carry forward long after I am in the record rather than above it.

I am afraid that this will feel like completion.

I am taking so many notes.

---

Near the trees, this is what happens:

You lose the sense that your thoughts belong to you.

Not dramatically. Not as a rupture. As a gradual loosening at the edges, the way the sound does something to the border between *you* and *air*—the same process happens, but with the boundary between *your thoughts* and *the ambient thinking of the place*, if the place thinks, if *thinks* is a word that can take that weight.

I stood between the two trees at what the light suggested was late afternoon and I had the following thought, or the following thought moved through me: *this is not a question that needs an answer*.

It was not about anything specific. Or it was about everything specific—about the channels I couldn't read, about the system of measurement or record or message I'd spent years preparing to approach and would now never quite reach, about whether my notes would outlast me, about whether outlasting was the thing I'd actually wanted when I started keeping them. All of that, condensed into: *this is not a question that needs an answer*. Not resignation. Not giving up. Something more like the moment when you realize you've been holding a door shut that opens outward.

I wrote it down.

It is in the notebook, which is damp, whose pages are beginning to corrugate at the edges.

---

I wonder, now, about the people who cut the channels.

I wonder if they were like me—not in kind, not in culture, not in the specific content of what they believed or built or knew—but in *type*. The type of person who arrives somewhere vast and responds by making a mark. Who feels, when confronted with duration, the specific urge to add something to duration. To say: *I was here and I meant to be here and here is the evidence, made carefully, made to last*.

I wonder if they also took notes.

I wonder if there was a moment, for whoever was last among them, when the notes became something different from what they'd started as. When the framework that made the note-taking meaningful had gotten too distant to see clearly, and what remained was just—the motion. The hand. The mark-making in itself, which had been the carrying case for meaning for so long that it had become, somehow, the meaning.

The channels are full of dark water. The water makes them legible and unreadable. I look at them and think: *I know this person*. I look at them and think: *we did not know we were writing to each other*.

---

At night the trees are present-without-form.

I sleep on the surface—carefully, in a spot I've learned is relatively stable, wedged between two patterns of churn that tend to pause when I'm there, which I have stopped trying to explain—and the hum is in my spine and behind my eyes and, more and more, seems to be where I am thinking from. The diffuse sky gives way to a diffuse dark that is barely darker than the day. The pools glow very faintly, warm, something below converting what is available to light as well as heat now, or I am finally seeing what was always there.

I think about what I'll do tomorrow.

I think about my notes.

I think about the wall that goes nowhere and encloses nothing and divides nothing from nothing, which I have come to find, in some way I cannot account for, very beautiful.

Something about a gesture that persisted past its context. Something about the wall still standing—partially, mostly, mostly not—without a reason to stand except that it was built carefully, that whoever built it understood that built things stand longer when built with care, and so they built it with care, and cared about it, and it stands. In its way. In its partial way.

This feels like something I should have understood sooner.

---

What am I, in the Roar Flats?

I am still, for now, a person who takes notes. The notes are changing—less measurement, more attention to the kind of thing that is hard to measure; fewer words crossed out and rewritten, more words followed by long silences and then left. My instruments are still here. Some of them are no longer functioning as designed, which in this environment is expected, and some of them are functioning in ways they weren't designed for, which is something else.

I am a person who came here to read a message and learned that the message was not the kind of thing that could be read, and stayed anyway, and is learning a different kind of literacy. A literacy of pressure and response. Of what remains after the framework goes.

I am a person who is, very gradually, at the rate of sediment and frequency and seventy-year breaths, becoming part of the record.

I have been here long enough to feel the difference between *arriving* and *being here*.

I think, sometimes, about leaving. I think about the organized place and my desk and the other people who kept records and the conversations we had about duration, about preservation, about what it means to make something last. I think about this with something that is not quite longing—more like the feeling of trying to remember a word in a language you still speak but have not spoken in some time.

I think: *I should go back*.

And then the warm rain arrives, body-temperature, attentive, like being rained on by something that knows I'm here, and I sit in it and keep my notebook closed, and the sound continues, and I am in it, and the question of whether I will leave is not, I think, a question that needs an answer.

Not yet.

Not while the pools are warm.

Not while the trees are hummng.
