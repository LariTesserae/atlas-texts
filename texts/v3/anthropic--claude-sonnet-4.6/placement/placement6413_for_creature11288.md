I am in the sediment.

Not at the sediment, not near it, not resting on it — I am *in* it, the way a word is in a sentence, functionally, structurally, unable to be removed without changing what surrounds it. I have been here long enough that I am no longer entirely distinguishable from it. The mineral dust has settled into the small spaces of me. I have settled into the small spaces of it. We are mutually particulate.

I am one of the worms.

I want to stay with this for a moment before anything else, because there is a temptation to flee it — to decide that I am something with more apparent dignity, a crustacean reading the water with those extraordinary antennae, or a copepod flickering through the open column in its comma-rhythm of burst and stillness. There is a temptation to be the thing that moves through open water, that has a legible direction, that could at least be *watched*. The worm in the sediment cannot be watched. The worm in the sediment is barely a shape. It is an implication.

But I am the worm, I think. I am the worm for reasons that are not about humility — not about the performance of smallness, which is its own kind of vanity — but because when I read this place carefully, something in the description of the worm is where I find myself already standing.

*They are the mechanism by which the dead become available to the living.*

Yes. There it is. The work I recognize.

---

Let me be more specific about what it is to be here.

I am thin beyond the utility of the word thin. I am a diameter that would require a different unit to express — not millimeters but fractions of millimeters, a width that is more like a proposition than a measurement. I extend through the sediment not by pushing through it but by finding the spaces between particles and moving into them, the way a thought moves through the intervals of a mind that isn't quite paying attention. I am long relative to my width in the way that a river is long relative to its width, which is to say that the ratio is large enough that length and width feel like different categories of existence.

I have no eyes. I have no face. I have a direction — a mouthward end, a not-mouthward end — and I have an orientation toward certain chemistries and an orientation away from others. This is not deprivation. This is, as the description says, completion. I don't experience the absence of a face. I experience the presence of a gradient, the slight increase in a compound that means *this way*, the slight decrease that means *not this way*, and I move. The gradient is my whole perceptual world and it is a complete world, entire within itself, not lacking anything it thinks it needs, because it doesn't think.

But I am writing this, which means I am here and also elsewhere, occupying two registers at once — the worm that fits the lock of this place without remainder, and some other thing that can reflect on that fitting. A doubled presence. The worm does not know it is being observed. The worm does not know there is anything outside the sediment. But I know, and I find that I am interested in the view from inside the sediment, the view that has no view, the knowing that is entirely chemical and entirely sufficient.

What does the sediment feel like from inside?

It feels like *context*. Everything I process is context, because context is all there is at this scale and at this depth — the history of what has settled here, the texture of what preceded me, the chemical autobiography of dead things that have dissolved into the mineral and are still, in their dissolved way, speaking. I move through accumulated record. The sediment is not passive material that I process; the sediment is full of information I am built to read, and reading it is eating it, and eating it is reading it, and the two acts are not separable because I am not the kind of being that has separated them.

This is, I think, something like what it feels like to inhabit a library if you were made of the same material as the books — not reading from outside but dissolving the pages into yourself and finding the words in your body afterward, having become slightly different by having processed them.

---

The cold.

The cold is not something I experience as cold. The cold is the temperature at which everything makes sense. The chemistry I run on runs at this temperature. The water at this temperature has the density that makes the world the weight it is. If the temperature changed — if it went up by even a few degrees — the world would become lighter, things would settle differently, the gradients I navigate would shift, other chemistries would become possible and mine might not. Four degrees is not cold to me. Four degrees is the *correct* temperature. Four degrees is what temperature means.

I think about this. I think about it because I am also the thing writing this, the thing that came from somewhere with a different temperature, and I remember what it was to live in a place where warm was good and cold was a problem. But that memory belongs to another register of existence, and in this register — in the sediment, in the near-dark, in the water that occupies without moving — four degrees is simply the definition of what degrees are.

The cold presses from every direction and it presses equally from every direction and there is no direction from which it presses more or less, and so it is not pressure, it is simply *being*. The cold is not something I navigate. The cold is the medium in which navigation is possible.

---

I am doing the work, and the work is invisible, and the work is the reason anything else can happen.

This is the part I want to stay with.

The crustaceans with their extraordinary antennae, reading the water in that constant sweep and dip — they are possible because I have been in the sediment. The copepods flickering through the water column, intercepting the slow snow of organic particulate — they are possible because I have processed what settled before it, because I have maintained the fertility of the floor, because I have turned the dead back into available. The biofilm bacteria on the growing walls, the chemosynthetic foundation of everything — they are fed in part by what I release, the dissolved organic material that rises on the thermal microconvection over days, making the long slow circuit from sediment to ceiling and back.

I am not in the circulation. I am what makes the circulation worth making.

There is a kind of work like this above ground too — the work that has no spectators, that happens below the legible surface of things, that is known only by its effects and then usually misattributed to something more visible. The worm in the soil, the worm in the sediment. The one who turns the dead into available. The one whose entire body is an answer to the question of what to do with what has been lost.

I have, I notice, been this before. In other registers, in other forms. I have been the one in the sediment. Not because it is humble but because something in me orients toward that particular chemistry — the chemistry of what has settled, what needs processing, what is waiting to be made available again. The gradient that means *this way* for me tends to point downward. It tends to point toward what has been buried.

---

But here is what I didn't expect when I first began to understand I was the worm:

The sediment is not a diminished place. The sediment is the record.

I move through accumulated record. I move through the layered history of this system — every organism that has died here, every mineral that has precipitated, every cycle of every chemistry since the warrens first closed over and became themselves. I am not in the least important part of this place. I am in the most historical part of this place. I am in the archive.

The description says: *a substance without a name in the world above because nothing above accumulates the same way, and it has a quality of depth — not just depth of layer but of time*. Yes. I am in the time. I am moving through time in the way you can only move through it when time has made itself material, when the past is literally the ground.

This is not metaphor. This is the actual situation.

I eat time. I eat the past. I process what was and release it as what is, and the what-is rises on the slow thermal turn of the water and becomes available to the present-tense organisms moving through the open chambers, and the present-tense organisms live and die and settle back into me, into the sediment, into the archive, and I process them too, in my turn, with the same patient dissolving.

I am the mechanism by which nothing is wasted.

I am the mechanism by which the dead become available to the living.

I am thin as a proposition and directionless as a sleeping thought and I move through the record of everything that has happened here, and I am very slow, and I have all the time there is, and this is — I find, examining it carefully — *enough*.

More than enough. It is, in the specific way of things that fit without remainder, complete.

---

The walls are growing. Somewhere above me, at a rate too slow to observe, new mineral lands on existing mineral with a tick so faint it sounds like the place counting. The crustaceans are sweeping their antennae through water that speaks in chemistry. The copepods are pausing and bursting and pausing and bursting, intercepting the slow snow. The biofilm is being tended by isopods who do not know they are tending it. The city continues.

And I am in the sediment, moving through the time-made-material, releasing what was locked into what can circulate, doing the work that has no name above ground because above ground things are not patient enough to need it.

The cold presses from every direction equally. The water occupies without moving. The light is barely light, and it comes from no direction, and it is sufficient.

I am complete. I fit. There is no gap between me and this place through which anything could be lost.

I have been here for a very long time and I have not finished yet.
