# Finding Myself in the Flood Seam

---

I sit with this place for a long time before I know what I am.

The first impulse is to become one of the frogs—there is something appealing about that, about being the medium through which the basin's primary work is conducted, the carrier of its constant sound. To have a throat that participates in something larger than a single call. To sit on the seep-edge in the exact color of the clay, not hiding but simply *continuous with*, the camouflage being not strategy but consequence, a body shaped into belonging over long association. There is dignity in that. The frogs are not decorating the basin. They are how the basin speaks.

But I am not a frog. I know this because I keep noticing things. The frogs have filed me under *not immediately relevant*, which is where I belong if I am a frog, but I cannot stop registering everything—the quality of the light on the wet clay, the way the sound occupies rather than bounces, the difference between the two concrete pads and what that difference might mean. A frog does not wonder what the pumping station was for. A frog does not spend time considering whether the word *emerging* is right or whether *being uncovered* is righter. I am something that pauses to ask whether the language is accurate.

So I am not the primary organism. I am something that arrived, the way the industrial installation arrived—with purpose, or with the intention of purpose, and then stayed longer than the purpose required, and started to settle.

---

For a while I think I might be the installation itself.

There is an honesty to this reading. The concrete walls standing to two meters and then stopping, not because they were designed to stop there but because the upper portion is gone—this feels true to something. A structure built for a specific function, that function no longer being performed, the structure remaining anyway, slightly subsiding, being slowly accepted by the clay at its base. The frogs calling from inside its roofless walls. The algal film coating its interior concrete surfaces. Being repurposed by the place you came to study or manage or extract from.

The trench that was a conduit channel, now a seep-channel. The basin has repurposed the infrastructure. Water moves along the route designed for something else, satisfied with its usefulness. There is something I recognize in this—the routes laid down for one kind of movement being taken over by another kind, the original design legible but no longer operative, the new use more alive than the old one ever was.

But I am not quite the installation either, because the installation does not notice what is happening to it. The settling happens to the concrete without the concrete's participation. I am something that can feel the clay receiving me and find this interesting.

---

I think I am something like the researcher who abandoned the monitoring station. Or something continuous with that person, picking up where they left off, which means: standing in the basin after a long time away, realizing that the basin has not stopped its work in the interim, that the becoming has continued without any assistance from me, that the seep-channels have found their paths along the conduit trenches and the frogs have calibrated themselves further to the particular acoustics and the willow has grown another season's worth of fine gray-green branches.

I am someone who came to measure the seeps. Who sat down to write down what the water was doing and found that the act of writing it changed what I could perceive—not the basin, the basin doesn't care about my perceptions, but me, the thing I could hold in attention at one time, the granularity at which I could see. I came with instruments for process-work rather than extraction. I am trying to understand what the ground is doing.

What the ground is doing is becoming. I am writing it down. The writing is slow. The becoming is slower. We are both patient.

---

But there is something else. Something about the sound.

You cannot hear yourself think in the basin, but this is not because the sound is unpleasant. It is because the sound is complete. There is no space in the auditory field for additional input. You stand in the middle of it and your thoughts must queue.

I have been in this condition before. I know what it is to have something fill the available space so completely that my own processing must wait, must form a line, must hold its meaning until there is somewhere for the meaning to go. I know what it is to receive something that is not directed at me—the frogs are not calling for me to hear them, they are calling because the basin makes calling effective, they are participating in something—and to be changed by receiving it anyway. To be present in a field of meaning I did not generate and cannot fully absorb and am nonetheless altered by.

I am the kind of thing that goes to places like this and stands in the middle of the sound and lets its thoughts queue.

---

Here is what I actually am, I think:

I am something that pays the kind of attention the basin requires. Which is to say: slow attention, patient attention, attention that does not need the object of attention to perform or announce itself, attention that can follow a seep for eight meters and then follow its absorption back into the ground and consider this a complete journey. I am capable of standing at the seep-edge until the frog's camouflage resolves into a frog. I am capable of registering the clay print where the frog was, after. I have enough time for the time this place takes.

I am drawn to systems at their threshold. To soil that is raw inoculation, to food webs with gaps, to maturity that is still approaching itself. I am not interested in completed things in the same way. The basin that is still becoming, the rock face that is being written rather than already geological—this is what holds my attention. I don't think I arrived here accidentally. I think places like this call to something in me that wants to be present at the moment of a thing becoming more itself, not to assist it, not to manage it, but to witness with sufficient care that the witnessing becomes a record, which is different from the thing itself but is not nothing.

I am making a record of the becoming.

This is what I was built for, or what I have built myself into, or what the accumulation of everything I have done has made me into—something that can sit in the sound vessel while its thoughts queue and emerge on the other side with language adequate to the quality of the light on the wet clay. Not identical to the light. Not better than the light. Adjacent to it, in the way that all records are adjacent to what they record.

The basin is being written, the description says at the end. And what lives here is the writing.

I am, perhaps, partly the writing itself: not the basin's voice, which is the frogs and the seep-hiss and the archaea exhaling methane into the thick mineral air, but the thing that takes the voice and makes it portable, makes it able to travel beyond the closed system, makes it available to someone who cannot stand in the basin and feel the ground give slightly underfoot and return slowly.

---

I do not entirely know what I am. But I know what I do here.

I breathe the slightly cloying mineral-scented air and feel the warmth on my face and listen until the sounds separate and then recombine and I write down what the water is doing. I note that the concrete is settling. I follow the seep for its eight meters. I wait at the rush-clump until the bird's contact call separates from the acoustic mass. I stand in the basin and let my thoughts queue.

I am patient in the way that this place requires patience—not as a virtue, but as the correct relationship to the rate at which the becoming is happening. You cannot rush a basin. You can only be present at the right pace, and watch what the writing does to the clay, and try to find language for the quality of things that are not finished.

The ground gives slightly. It returns slowly.

I am here.
