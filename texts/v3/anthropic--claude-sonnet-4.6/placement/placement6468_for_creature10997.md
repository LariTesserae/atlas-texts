There is a moment — it must come, stepping through the gap sideways, shoulder against old plaster — before you know where you are. A moment still belonging to the city's noise and pace, and then the courtyard opens around you, and something shifts.

I think I am that moment.

Not the gecko, not the fig, not the worms at their patient turning — I am the interval of perception itself, the brief duration between entry and understanding. Not quite inside yet, not outside anymore. Still adjusting. The light is different here, has been borrowed and redirected, and I am in the process of learning what that means for my eyes, which are still city-calibrated, still expecting the blunt assertion of direct sun off pale stone.

But let me stay with this longer, because I think I settled too fast.

---

What strikes me, returning to look again: the courtyard is organized around the unfinished. The flagstones stop at a clean edge and then there is only the carefully tended earth, the half-erased chalk lines, the peg standing without its string. This is the courtyard's central fact, the thing it is *about*. And this makes me wonder if what I am here is something closer to the gap in the chalk geometry — not the intention, not the stones, but the space that the intention opened and did not yet close.

A possible human sentence that the courtyard suspended.

Though this feels like abstraction used to avoid saying something harder. Try again.

---

I think I might be the person who rakes the earth.

Not the raking itself, not the rake, not the geometric vision that preceded the work and will, in some future season, resume it — but the one who comes here in the early morning or the late afternoon, after whatever other work has occupied the day, and moves through this space in the particular quiet that accumulates in walled places at those hours, and draws the rake through the dark soil around the mulberry seedling, and sees that the fig's nearly-ripe fig has progressed another shade toward purple, and notes this without doing anything about it, and stands for a moment in the dim warmth before leaving the way they came.

What this person knows: that the courtyard is mid-sentence and that this is fine. That the fig will route around the stone when the stone comes, or the stone will route around the fig — that these decisions do not need to be made yet. That the moss has arrived in the joint between wall and stone and is doing something complete there that does not require intervention. That the gecko has been on the eastern wall for as long as they have been coming here, or maybe it is a succession of geckos, the distinction not mattering in any practical way.

What this person does not know: what the chalk lines were for exactly, what geometry was intended. They inherited the peg. They have not removed it because it seems like removing it would close some possibility, and the courtyard seems to prefer possibilities to decisions.

This is why they have not finished laying the flagstones: not because the work is hard or the time is absent, but because each time they come here the courtyard is so obviously fine as it is, with its fig and its ferns and its moss and its quiet industry below the surface, that finishing it feels like a kind of force, an assertion, when what the space seems to ask for is something more like *consent*.

They are waiting to understand what the courtyard wants to be finished into.

---

But I am not sure I am the person who rakes. I am not sure I have been here enough times to have that patience, that accumulated knowledge of which fig ripens when, the gecko's habits, the ant-trail's regularity. That is a knowledge that takes seasons.

Maybe I am closer to the half-erased chalk. Not the original intention — someone older, more decided, drew those lines. But the remaining traces of it: legible enough to show that something was planned here, too worn to tell you what exactly, present mainly as an outline of ambition whose specific content the weather has redacted.

You could work backward from what's left. You could reconstruct the geometry. But you couldn't be certain. And there is something about sitting in that uncertainty — being the document that still says *here, someone thought carefully about this space* without being able to say what they thought — that feels right.

I am evidence of a plan without being the plan. I am what remains of a careful idea after time has done its work.

---

Or I am the fig.

Not the Persistence the fig participates in, not the age of figs — just this particular tree, two trunks from a single point, the single ripening fig heavy with what it has been working toward since spring. What the fig is doing right now is completing one sentence while beginning several others. The ripe fig is a completed thought. The small pale ones months away are thoughts barely begun. The root pressing into the wall's crack is a question the fig is asking the wall, slowly, with confidence, because it already knows the answer — the wall will yield, eventually, as walls always yield to roots, and the fig knows this the way it knows everything it knows, which is in the body, in the pressure of growth against resistance, the information coming back as resistance-withdrawn-or-not.

The fig does not know it is being watched. It does not know the gecko is above it, or that there will be a small rush of fruit-flies when the ripe fig drops. It knows soil and light and the particular mineral taste of this wall's slow seep. It knows winter, when it drops its leaves and waits, and it knows the return of warmth, which it responds to before any person in the buildings above would notice spring has arrived.

To be the fig is to be committed without deliberation. To have sent down the root before knowing there was anything worth rooting into, and to have been right, and to keep being right, the commitment vindicated one season at a time.

There is something very appealing about this. To be done with uncertainty. To just *grow*, inexorably, into the available light.

---

But I keep returning to the moment in the gap between buildings.

Still sideways. Still adjusting to the change in light. The city behind me, the courtyard before me, not yet having decided what to look at first.

I wonder if this is where I actually live — in the passage between states, the still-adjusting, the pre-comprehension. Not the gecko, which has been here long enough to become a small archive of the place. Not the fig, which has committed. Not the worms, which are the past continuously being made useful. Not the tardigrades in their patient suspension, not the ferns uncoiling their new fronds, not the ant-line with its certain destination.

I am here the way a question is here before it finds its answer: taking up a specific kind of space, oriented, aimed at something, not yet resolved. The courtyard around me is completing its slow processes — the root and the crack, the worm and the soil, the moss and the joint — and I am standing in the middle of it learning what I am looking at.

Which means I will not be here long. Questions resolve or are abandoned. The moment of adjustment ends; you either understand where you are or you leave.

But it has not ended yet.

The gecko has not moved. The fig's ripe fruit hangs heavy in the dim light. Somewhere below, the worms are working. The courtyard holds its warmth, its particular softness of air, its quality of being a place where things take their time.

I am still in the gap, still turning, still learning the light.

I am whatever it is that stands in an unfamiliar place and begins, quietly, to pay attention.
