There is a moment, arriving on the shelf for the first time, when I do not understand where I am in the order of things.

I had come up from somewhere below. The valley, probably, or one of the transitional places between valley and shelf that have their own rules, their own arguments with elevation. I had come up carrying something — not a load, exactly, but a reason, the kind of reason that moves a person vertically through a landscape that is mostly trying to discourage vertical movement. And then the shelf was there, suddenly, the way ledges present themselves when you have been climbing a face: as an horizon that was always there but invisible from below, becoming real only as you arrive at it.

I stood at the shelf's edge, still catching breath that had less in it than breath should, and did not know what I was.

---

The uncertainty lasted.

I am trying to be honest about this rather than resolving it too quickly into a story with a shape. I did not arrive knowing. I arrived and the shelf immediately presented itself as a place with its own intense specificity, a place that had already decided what it was, and I was a question mark standing in it. The pale new stone. The vessels everywhere, their still water reflecting a sky I had just climbed through. The buildings with their deep-set openings, almost blind, turned inward. The wind finding the pots and making the choir of them, the warning, the welcome, both.

I stood there and felt the particular embarrassment of arriving somewhere that is complete without you.

---

But here is what happened over time, and this is the part I am trying to tell.

I became permeable.

Not immediately. Not through effort, exactly. Through the process the shelf requires of anything that stays on it long enough: the slow recalibration. My hearing changed first, or I noticed my hearing changing, which may not be the same thing. I began to distinguish the wind that was doing what wind does from the wind that was carrying something, bringing weather from above the scarp or news from the valley in its freight of pressure and smell. I began to hear the ticking of the stone, the small reports of rock separating from rock along lines of old decision. I began to sit up before I knew I had heard anything and then listen and understand that the upper scarp had just spoken.

This frightened me, the first times. I want to be accurate about this. It felt like losing a boundary rather than gaining a sense. The shelf was getting into me through some route I had not consciously opened.

I mentioned this to the person who managed the vessels — she was older than she looked, or looked her age in ways I was not practiced at reading — and she made the near-pause, the grammatical silence, that I had begun to understand meant *that is correct, continue thinking this way.* Then she said, in her quiet precise way, something that translated approximately as: yes, the boundary is always a fiction at this elevation. The air is thinner and so is everything else.

I have been thinking about this for as long as I have been here.

---

What I am, I think, is this:

I am the person who arrived carrying a reason and forgot it gradually in the way you forget the lower elevations' concerns — not in the way of forgetting something you need, but in the way of an eye adjusting to new light, the old details receding not because they weren't real but because there is a different resolution available here and the eye takes it up.

I am the person who learned to make the near-silences. Not perfectly. With an accent, still. But I learned them, and the people here will sometimes listen to my silences as though they are receiving something real, something I meant, and occasionally I find that they have understood something I did not know I was saying. The silence knew before I did. The shelf does something to the gap between intention and speech.

I am the person who sits in the hour before full dark and watches the light on the new stone of the upper scarp and still, despite everything, finds it worth watching. This is not nothing. This is, perhaps, the qualification that matters most here: that you can look at the same pale bright stone in the same failing light for the tenth year or the twentieth and still find it offering something, still feel the looking as an event rather than a habit. The people born here can do this. I came to it later. But I came to it.

---

There is a specific thing I do that I did not expect to do.

I sit with the vessels when someone has died.

Not as a formal role. Not assigned. It is simply that when a person's vessels are moved to the cliff face and filled with the deep cold water, I find myself returning to them in the blue-silver hour, the social hour, when the passages are full of voices and the people I have been living alongside are in the doorways with their hands doing their independent work. I return to the cliff face and I sit with the vessels and I do what they taught me here to call attending — the full presence, the complete receiving, the practice of being in the place you are in without editing it.

I am not sure why I do this rather than someone else. Or rather: I suspect I know, and it is something about having arrived from outside, about still carrying, faintly, the sense of the verticalness of this place, the shelf as something provisional in the face of the mountain. People born here have the shelf in their body; it does not astonish them. I came up from below and it astonished me and it continues to, in the way that things that astonished you when they were new continue to carry that quality even decades later, a faint luminosity, a sense that you might at any moment reencounter the feeling of arriving.

The vessels of the dead hold that quality too. They were here. They are not. The water in them is cold and old and has been through the stone and is now in the air for the first time. Looking into a vessel and finding the sky — my face beside the sky, my face and the sky together in still water — feels like the right thing to do with whatever it is I am. The attending that the shelf requires of all of us, but done here, at the edge, where the seep-water comes through and the moss is darkest and the sound of water in rock is most continuous.

I sit there until I understand something I cannot say. Then I go back to the passages and someone hands me work to do with my hands and I do it, and the work is the shelf, and the shelf continues.

---

I am also, I should say, bad at several things they are good at.

I am slow at reading the fractures. I will not be good at this before I die; I know this now. There is knowledge that requires a childhood of listening, a particular early calibration that I did not receive, and the fractures speak to me in an accent I can roughly follow but will never lose the effort of following. This is fine. This is a thing I accepted when I accepted the shelf, the way you accept that a language learned late will always carry a slight interior foreignness even when it has become your primary tongue.

I am also — and this amuses the people here, who do not display their amusement but whose near-pauses when I do this have a quality I have learned to read as something warm — I am also still sometimes loud. The voice goes up. I have been here long enough to have mostly learned the quiet, to understand that loud speech belongs to everyone in range and quiet speech belongs only to those who chose to listen. But occasionally, unexpectedly, the valley comes back into my throat and I say something at a volume that crosses three buildings and two passages, and somewhere in the city the heads turn slightly, and the person I am talking to makes the near-pause that means *noted, go on,* and I lower my voice and go on.

The shelf has taught me most of what I know. The valley is still in me. Between these two things I am whoever I am here, attending, permeable, watching the light on new stone and finding it sufficient, my hands doing their work, the water in the stone going on, the mountain still arriving.
