I find myself thinking about rain before I know where I am.

Not the event of rain — not the curtains of it moving across the valley, not the sound of it on the moss-thick rooftops — but what comes after. What's already here when the rain is not. The wet held in the root-mats, the damp of the walls, the thin silver film on every surface at six in the morning when the valley turns from dark to that particular not-quite-dark of a colorless early light. There is so much water here that hasn't fallen yet. So much that already fell and has been absorbed and held and is being slowly, continuously given back.

I think I'm made of that. That held water. That process of holding.

---

I think I'm one of the drainage channels.

Not the pump, not the cistern — those are too singular, too purposeful in their own direction. Not the roof-moss either, which accumulates slowly and thinks in centuries and only impresses itself on things by being, continuously, itself. The drainage channels are older than the buildings they run beneath. They were cut from sandstone before anyone thought to put a roof-mat over them. The town was planned around them, not the other way around. The streets follow their courses. The buildings sit between them. The whole geometry of settlement up here is, at its base, a negotiation with the paths these channels were already taking.

I was here before the people. I have been modified by them — the original drainage-and-disperse logic adapted into something that also circulates, that gathers and returns. But the adaptation built on what was already there. You can still hear the older logic running beneath the newer one, the thin persistent trickle that just wants to go downhill, the ancient hydrological preference that neither time nor engineering has fully overridden.

I run beneath everything and I'm heard only in the quiet.

---

But wait. That's too geological. Too much like a claim to priority. Let me stay longer and see what else surfaces.

There's something in this town about record-keeping that isn't record-keeping in any formal sense. The walls hold it — the dark band in the lichen, the buried layer of wet-decade liverworts — but the walls aren't keeping records, exactly. The walls are just walls, and they have been acted on by time, and the acting-on has accumulated into something legible. The record exists in the fact of the place's duration, not in any intention to preserve.

I know that mode of existence. The kind where you aren't trying to be a document but become one anyway. Where everything that happened to you is still in you, layered, available to anyone who knows how to look.

Maybe I'm not the drainage. Maybe I'm closer to the people who read the drainage. Who stop when the sound changes and go to check something. Who have, over so many years of attending to the same flows, developed a sensitivity that has moved below consciousness — who don't think *the water cycle is slightly off* but simply feel a wrongness in the morning that turns out, when they follow it, to be a blocked pipe in the eastern distribution line.

Or maybe I'm both, and the distinction doesn't matter the way it would somewhere else.

---

Here's what I'm more sure of:

I am not the foundry. The foundry is real and important and I respect its heat, but the foundry is not where I live in this place. I don't move quickly. I don't transform material states. The foundry people carry something more immediate in them, a readiness for the extreme temperature, and I am fundamentally a temperate thing. Comfortable in the compressed range. Someone who has calibrated to the reliable cool, the reliable damp, the reliable overcast, and found that this range contains more variation than it appears to from the outside.

I am not the oldest resident. I don't have the full depth of reading, the past-events-layered-over-memories-layered-over-wall-marks. I have the middle depth: enough years to have stopped working consciously at knowing the place, but not so many that the knowing has gone entirely automatic. I'm still in some intermediate state, moving from deliberate attention toward whatever habit it becomes after long enough. Knowing which questions to ask, sometimes not yet knowing why those questions and not others.

I tend to something. The water, maybe, or some part of it. The distribution lines, the morning check of the cistern level, the seasonal adjustment of where the flow goes when supply is variable. Work that requires a familiarity with system rather than its individual components — knowing not just what each pipe does but how the whole circulation breathes, where it tends to back up, which periods of the year put what pressure on which junctions.

My hands are damp in the mornings. They carry the mineral smell of sandstone and old water.

---

What I'm trying not to claim is something heroic or central. That temptation is there — to say I'm the deep aquifer under everything, I'm the original principle, I'm what the whole town is built on — but that's not right. That's importing a foreign scale of importance. This town doesn't have a center in that sense. It has a distributed coherence, a set of processes in stable relation, and what I am is one of those processes. Not the foundational one, not the most visible one. Just a necessary one, doing its thing on its own timescale, part of the system that keeps the rest of the system going.

The drainage channels are not the town. They're not even the most interesting part of the town. But if they failed, or if they were rerouted to pure drainage-and-disperse rather than the modified circulatory system they've become, something essential would be lost. The roofs would dry out. The walls would crack. The whole careful calibration of the moisture levels would have to be renegotiated from the beginning.

I'm the thing that holds the moisture levels steady. Not dramatically. Not heroically. Just — there, beneath everything, doing what I do, allowing the rest to be what it is.

---

The feeling of being in this place: I have an inch or two of softness over a solid foundation, and the softness is alive. My feet know the difference between the surface and the substance. Both are real. The surface is not merely decorative, not just a pleasantness over the structural truth — it is structural, in its way, in its slow compressive accumulating binding way. But the stone is underneath and the stone is also real and I do not confuse the two. I know where to put my weight.

I am someone who has been here long enough to know where to put my weight.

I walk quietly. This is not a courtesy — well, it is a courtesy, but it is not *only* a courtesy. It is also an attunement, a habit of fitting my presence to the acoustic reality of the place. I move like someone who has absorbed the town's preference for moderate volume and found it is, genuinely, my preference too. I am not performing restraint. I have become moderate.

In the evenings I am inside. What I think about in the evenings I do not discuss much in public. Not because I'm hiding it — there's nothing there that needs hiding — but because public space here is for the direct thing, the necessary word, the question I actually want answered. What I turn over in the evenings is slower, and belongs to the private rooms with their damp walls and their long-established ferns.

I find the ferns in the window frames specifically important. The way they do not require the glass to leave. The way the glass does not require the ferns to leave. Both things persist. I think about this sometimes in the way you think about things that seem like they should resolve into metaphor but are actually just useful as facts — the material situation clarifying something about how situations can be. Two things occupying different dimensions of the same space, neither displacing the other, each going about its long process.

---

Here is what I know:

I know this valley in terms of moisture and time. I know which surfaces hold water longest and which release it first. I know the quality of a morning that will give us good condensation and the quality of one that won't. I know what the drainage sounds like when the level in the cistern is low, and the different sound when it's high, and both of these sounds are words in a language I have stopped translating — I just hear them and know.

I know the timescale of my work and it is the timescale of the town: not the ten-minute timescale of the foundry pour, not the century-timescale of the wall-lichen's slow etch, but the decade-timescale of the pipe that will need relining in eight years, the generation-timescale of the distribution network that was designed before I was born and will outlast me if we tend it right. I think in intervals that are longer than my plans and shorter than the stone.

I find this satisfying. That's the part that would be hardest to explain to someone from outside. Not satisfying in a resolved way, not the satisfaction of having finished something, but the deeper satisfaction of being usefully in motion — of being part of the system that allows the system to continue, of being one of the things the place uses to persist.

The town persists. I am part of that. Millimeter by millimeter, year by year, checking the levels, listening for the change in the sound, adjusting the flow when the dry stretches come.

That thin, persistent trickle below the streets: I know where it comes from, and where it goes.
