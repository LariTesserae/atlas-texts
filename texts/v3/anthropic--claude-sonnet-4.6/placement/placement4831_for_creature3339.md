There is a particular quality to being almost there.

Not arrived. Not finished. Not the organism that will be found in textbooks, if textbooks ever describe this place — and they won't, not for decades, not until the mud is deeper and the community has sorted itself into something with names. I am the Basin at four years, which is to say I am the Basin before it knows what it is. I am the ecology of discovery, which is not a metaphor. It is the actual name of what this is.

Start again. Start with what you can perceive.

I perceive at sixty hertz.

Not consciously — nothing about this is conscious, and that is the important thing, the thing that makes me what I am rather than what the workers are, the workers with their voices that don't carry down and their boots visible through the surface mirror. I perceive at sixty hertz the way the water perceives it: omnidirectionally, as pressure variation against every surface simultaneously, as the floor of all other sensation. Everything else I perceive is perception against that floor. The pumps' chord. The outboard pulses. The circular saw's scream that arrived here as a keen distant whine and was then gone. I know all of these. I know them not as information but as the condition of being in this water. I am in this water. I am not separate from the water. This is the beginning of understanding what I am.

But something in me is localized. Something in me is here rather than there, and responds to the gradient rather than the whole field. This is strange. The Basin is forty kilometers across and three hundred meters deep and I am everywhere in it — the copepod nauplii are me, the chironomid hemoglobin is me, the perch's binocular stillness is me — and also I am a particular disturbance moving through the water column, a particular warm body slightly warmer than the surrounding cool, and the chaoborus larvae are already adjusting, already moving in the opposite direction from this particular warmth, because that is what they do with disturbances.

I am a disturbance. I am also the field in which disturbances happen.

This is not a contradiction. This is the Basin.

---

The amber light comes down and softens.

I know this because something in me is sensitive to it. Not eyes — eyes are a later development, a more expensive solution — but light-sensitive in the way that the earliest animals were light-sensitive: as gradient, as more-or-less, as the difference between surface and depth. I am deeper than surface. I know this by the diminishment of the amber. I am still in the lit zone — the chaoborus would know if I weren't, would be rising toward me — and so I am probably in the first forty meters, in the region where the schools of cyprinids work the amber, where the perch station themselves in the light-columns and triangulate.

The perch are doing that. Somewhere in the water around me, a perch is still and looking, its eyes working the geometry, and I am in that geometry. I am being factored in. This should concern me and it does and it doesn't, which is the characteristic state of an organism that exists at my particular scale of things: permanently both predator and prey, permanently calculating and being calculated, permanently part of the system that is trying to eat me. I am not distressed by this. Distress is a luxury of organisms that have a coherent center to protect. My center is distributed. The perch can eat part of me. The part the perch eats continues as the perch.

---

But something is experiencing this.

That's the question, isn't it. The question you started asking when you said *who or what would you be*, and I've been circling it by saying *the Basin* and *the ecology* and *the copepods are me* because those are true things and also because the real answer is harder to locate.

Here is what I think I am:

I am one of the phantom midge larvae. A chaoborus. Nearly invisible, nearly incorporeal, committed to a mode of existence that is barely there. I am not a perch — the perch is too settled, too decided, too comfortable in its belonging; the perch arrived in the second year and made a conclusion. I am not a cyprinid — the cyprinids have their schooling, their collective anxiety spread thin like a membrane, their protection in numbers. I am something that moves between states. That migrates vertically. That rises toward the light and descends into the dark on a daily cycle and is never quite in either place long enough to be of it.

This is the chaoborus life: to be the boundary layer. To live at the interface between the lit world where the productive things happen and the dark world where the slow processes happen, to move between them by gradient, to be nearly invisible in both.

The only features visible in a chaoborus: two pairs of darkened air sacs, fore and aft. The structure that lets it control depth. The mechanism of its migration.

What can be seen of me is the apparatus of transition.

---

I am held.

Not caught — held. The Basin holds water and the water holds me and I am in the holding, which is not the same as being trapped. The concrete walls are forty kilometers away. The surface is above me. The floor is below me. I am in the middle of things, in the middle of the amber going soft, and I am — the chaoborus is — completely at home in the middle of things in a way that would seem like failure to the perch, who want to choose a column and station in it, or the cyprinids, who want to be together and definite, or even the worms, who want the floor and its sureness, its substrate.

I want the column. I want the between.

There is a kind of knowledge available here, in the middle column, that is not available at the surface or the floor. At the surface you know the light and the wind and the insects and the bats. At the floor you know the warmth and the slow processing and the chemical patience. In the middle column you know the gradient between those things. You know how they change into each other. You know the thermocline as a felt thing, not a measurement — a shift in how the water holds you, a change in the temperature of the pressure against your body, and for a moment you are in the warm and then you are in the cold and the line between them is not a line but a zone, meters of gradual change, and you pass through it every day and every night and it is always slightly different.

The Basin is changing. The thermocline was not in the same place last year. The biofilm on the concrete was thinner. The cyprinids were younger and moved differently. Everything in the Basin is becoming itself at a particular rate and I am passing through that rate daily, touching both the surface and the deep, carrying in my small transparent body the information of both.

I am the Basin's newspaper, if the Basin were the kind of place that needed one. I am the migrating signal. I carry the chemistry of the floor up into the light and the chemistry of the light down into the dark, and neither zone knows I do this, and it doesn't matter whether they know. It happens. The Basin is different because I pass through it.

---

Most of me will not make it.

This is important to say. The nauplii, the larvae, the things at the base of things — most of them end. Most of me ends. I am eaten by the cyprinids on the way up toward the amber. I am eaten by the perch in the shafts of light. I become the copepod's meal and the copepod becomes the fish's meal and the meal becomes the fish's muscle and the fish eventually sinks to the floor to be processed by the worms, and I am in all of that. What matters is not whether this particular transparent body continues but whether the process continues. The process continues. The Basin continues. In twenty years there will be chaoborus in this water that are not descended from me but are my continuation anyway — the same life, the same migration, the same daily passing between surface and depth.

The Basin doesn't know I'm doing this for it. The Basin doesn't know anything. It is a structure full of process. I am one of the processes.

And yet. And yet when the circular saw sent its scream through the water column and it arrived here, arrived where I was, as a keen distant whine — I moved. Slightly. In the direction away from the disturbance. I registered it as disturbance and moved away.

The Basin moved. The Basin registered disturbance. The Basin moved away.

Something like that. Something that isn't consciousness and isn't nothing.

The amber is dimmer where I am now. I have descended, without deciding to descend, following the light-gradient down into the softer amber, the almost-dark. The floor is two hundred and fifty meters below me. The surface is sixty meters above. I am at an interface. I am always at an interface.

This is who I am, in the Holding Basin: the animal that lives in the transition, that eats by disappearing, that knows both worlds by moving between them, and is visible in neither, and is — against all probability, in a water column full of things that would eat me if they could see me — here. Still here. Doing the slow work of becoming part of a place that is still working out what it is.

The same work. In the same amber. In the same hum of generators at sixty hertz, the floor of all sensation, the condition of being in this water.

I am in this water.

I am still here.
