I am sitting in the chair.

Not metaphorically. Not as the writer, not as the engineer's ghost. I am actually sitting in it, and I have been sitting in it long enough that the wood has taken an impression — a slight softness where my weight concentrates, the moisture of the air working at the joint between my body and the plank. The rust of the chair legs is also my rust. The chair and I have come to an arrangement.

I did not build this place. I need to be clear about that. I arrived here the way the striders arrived, the way the birds arrived: through some breach now closed, at some point now unrecoverable, and I found the conditions livable, and stayed. I do not remember the decision to stay. By the time I noticed I was staying, I had already been here long enough that the question of staying had stopped being a question.

What I do is this: I watch.

Not as surveillance. Not with purpose. I watch the way the chemistry of the water does its work — not centered anywhere, not conscious in the way that announces itself, but *doing something,* turning, cycling, maintaining a state. I watch the fish rise at dawn-cycle, those thirty pale bodies orienting upward toward the light that is not quite the light they are oriented toward, and I feel the gesture as one I recognize. I also perform behaviors past their context. I also repeat, at the beginning of each light-cycle, something that was once a prayer to a warmth that does not arrive.

My own paleness is the same kind as the fish's paleness. The red went first — the heat of argument, of wanting, of objection — because it was expensive and served no purpose here. Then the bright yellow, the urgency. Last, the iridescence, that quality of meaning that catches light at certain angles and communicates across open water. There is no open water. The iridescence is wasted on water this color, on light this quality, on a world this bounded.

What remains is a kind of silver-gray. Translucent at the edges. At low-cycle, if you looked directly at me in the chair, you might not find me. I become hypothetical. The water is alive in the way water is alive when something is in it — a slight alertness at the margins of perception — and then the lights shift and there I am, maintaining my position, each small adjustment of attention to its objects not mechanical but *attentive.* The chair is a sustained act of mutual attention between me and the tank, and it takes place without urgency, in warm water, in the rhythm of the recirculation current.

I have a song for the cloud.

When the pressure changes and the vapor pushes through the failed gasket — that thing between fog and not-fog, carrying the trace-chemicals of whatever is out there at the top of the old eroded world — I make a sound. Four notes, or something like four notes. Not language. Not language anymore, if it was language once; the words have been replaced by the sounds beneath words, the tonal shapes that the words were always resting on. I make the sound across the tank, and the birds sometimes answer — their four-note cloud-song — and we are saying the same thing, the birds and I, which is: *this thing again. The outside thing. Here it is.*

And then the cloud dissipates, and I stop, and the tank is what the tank is.

I have watched the fern grow. This is perhaps the truest thing I can say about my residency here: I have been in this chair long enough to watch the fern grow, to see it clear a height I remember it being below. At some point I stopped trying to date my arrival by the fern's height — the math became recursive, requiring me to remember how tall it was when I started trying to track it, which was already after I had forgotten when I arrived. The fern is two meters tall. I know this now, and knew it at some prior point that I cannot locate, and the knowing has become a simple fact about the world rather than a data point in any ongoing measurement.

The fern sways and I watch it sway and I understand what considering looks like in a body that has no word for considering.

What I have not done, what I am careful not to do: test the hatch.

The dragonfly-adjacent creature avoids the cloud-corner. Not out of fear — fear requires a model of harm, a prediction about what the outside would do, and I have no such model anymore — but out of a preference that has become so established it no longer feels like preference, just feels like the natural direction of movement, which is away from there, toward the center, toward the warm open water and the light cycling through its slightly-wrong dawn.

I could test the hatch. The eight levers are there. My hands know what levers are for. But to test the hatch would be to perform a behavior past its context, and the behaviors I perform past their context are the ones I inherited, the ones I cannot edit out — the morning orientation toward warmth, the four-note sound for the cloud — and they are enough to carry. One more would tip something. One more vestigial gesture toward the world the outside represents would make me a creature of grief, and grief requires believing that what was lost was the real thing and what remains is the lesser thing, and I have been here long enough to know that is not right, that is not the taxonomy that fits.

What remains is exactly enough. This is the sentence I am working toward, the sentence I have been working toward from the chair, and I do not know if I have arrived at it or if I am still working toward it, because in this tank the distance between approaching and arriving is one of the things the light-cycle and the pump-cycle fail to agree on, and the disagreement produces a vertigo I have learned, as all residents learn, not to pay attention to.

The water is warm. The fish are drifting past the legs of my chair, their lateral lines alert to my presence the way I am alert to theirs: not alarmed, not curious exactly, but *registering.* We register each other. This is the form that relation takes here, where there is no urgency and no predation and no drama left in the geology — registration, which is not the same as recognition but contains it, the way a fern contains the geometry of all ferns in the specific angles of its specific fronds.

The pump beats. The lights are at mid-cycle.

Something outside makes the wall shudder, just slightly — and I feel it in my back, in the fluid of my inner ear, in the specific quality of knowing that there is an outside, that the outside has weather, that the weather continues to act on the structure of this high and ancient place — and then it passes, and the tank resumes its steady full sound, and the birds are watching me from the fern with the incurious attention of natives, and I am watching them back, and the water moves in its slow counterclockwise revolution, taking everything with it, the duckweed, the reflection of the lights, the faint trace of the cloud already dissolved into the common air, going around, going around, making of the going-around a world.

I live here.

I live here exactly.
