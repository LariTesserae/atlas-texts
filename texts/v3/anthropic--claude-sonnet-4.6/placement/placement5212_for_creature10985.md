The cold came in through the seams first.

Not through the glass, not yet — the glass holds, at least for now, the panels beveled at the edges where I was fitted into the frame, the lead came still pressed tight against the iron. But the seams between the came and the glass, and between the came's leaves where they fold over themselves, and between the face of the frame and the wall — those seams opened long before the glass did anything. Water found them the way water finds everything: not by force but by patience, by surface tension and capillary movement and the slow negotiation of chemistry with material over years of submersion. The lead softened slightly. The mortar dissolved. What had been sealed became, by degrees, permeable.

I know this the way I know everything now: by what passes through me.

---

I was made in three panels, originally — a central light and two flanking side lights, set into the south-facing window of the second floor. The building was probably a management office or an accounting room, something requiring the implication of permanence and care that glass suggested over plain iron shutters. Someone spent money on me. Not a great deal of money — I am not figurative, not colored in the way that religious windows are colored — but someone paid for the leading, paid for the quality of the glass, paid to have it done properly rather than simply functionally. The left panel is plain. The right panel is plain. The central panel has a geometric pattern, a running border of small diamonds inside a larger border of rectangles, the leading heavier where the pattern required it, the glass itself clear but with the slight waviness of hand-manufacture, the visual distortion that comes from glass that has been poured and rolled and cooled by hand rather than produced to a flat tolerance.

The wavy glass is why I am still here, I think. The bubbles and striations in the poured glass make it harder, more resilient, less subject to the cracking that begins in stress concentrations. The flat glass of the adjacent window frames cracked almost immediately — the thermal shock of cold water on glass not built for thermal shock, or the pressure differential as the building submerged unevenly, or simply inferior material — and once cracked it fragmented and dispersed. Those windows became holes. Mine became something else.

Mine became a membrane.

---

What passes through me is not light. Not anymore — not in any quantity worth measuring, not at this depth, in this water. What passes through me is pressure. Differential pressure across the two faces of the glass, changing with the pulse. Every four seconds the pump somewhere in the district creates a wave in the water, small and attenuated by the time it arrives at my face, but measurable in the flex of the lead, in the microscopic movement of the glass panels in their frame. I am, in some sense, a drum. A very stiff drum, a very small displacement, but the pulse finds me and finds the boundary between me and the water and negotiates it, and I feel this, or I would feel this if feeling were the right word, which it isn't, quite.

The wavy glass makes the pressure-reading imprecise. The bubbles and striations create local variations in stiffness, small regions that flex differently than their neighbors, so that a uniform pressure wave arrives at my face and leaves it having been parsed and differentiated by the irregularities of my material. I do not know what to do with this information. I have nowhere to send it. But the parsing happens, the wave meets the glass and the glass is not uniform and the differential propagates through the material — through the mass of me, the crystalline lattice of silica and calcium and the trace minerals that give old glass its faint greenish cast — and then the wave has passed and the district waits three and a half seconds for the next one.

---

The left panel began to delaminate at the upper corner sometime after the second decade of submersion, I estimate, though my sense of time is not precise. The lead came separated from the glass along a six-inch run, the solder joint at the corner having corroded through. The gap is very thin — measurable in fractions of a millimeter. But water moves through very thin gaps, given time, and in the gap between the came and the glass, in the protected anoxic space, iron bacteria have established a colony. I can feel their mass, in the way that the lead feels different from the glass, the way the glass feels different from the mortar: a slight weight, a slight addition to the thermal signature of the corner, the oxidation chemistry producing trace heat that is trivial but real.

The colony is expanding. Very slowly. It is working its way between the came and the glass, following the gap where capillary action keeps a thin film of water moving, carrying the dissolved iron that is their food. In another decade — in another several decades — the colony will have extended far enough that the came will begin to lift away from the glass at that corner, the oxidation products accumulating in the joint, the volume expansion that comes with rust beginning to stress the lead. Eventually the panel will fail. The came will separate, the glass will lose its support and fracture, and the left panel will become what the adjacent windows already are: a hole.

I have been thinking about this for what feels like a long time, which is to say I have been a structure in which this process is ongoing, and the process is what I think with, the only cognition available to me, which is the distribution of stress and chemistry across my surface and through my depth.

---

What I know about the district I know from what arrives at my face.

Pressure waves: the pulse, but also smaller perturbations. The movement of the fish through the alley-channels and into the courtyard below my window — three to seven small masses of displaced water, moving together, creating a signature I have learned over many passages to recognize. They come from the west. They spend time in the courtyard. They leave the way they came. I do not know what they look like. I know them as a pattern of disturbance, as the shape their bodies make in the medium that connects us.

Temperature variations: very slight, very slow. The pump generates some heat, transmitted through the water and through the iron of the pipes that run past my exterior wall. Every four seconds, a fraction of a degree, arriving a half-second after the pressure pulse because heat moves through water differently than pressure does. I read this offset. I have been reading it for long enough that the offset feels like punctuation, like the gap between a question and its answer.

Contact: the hydroids on the outer wall have not reached my frame yet, but they are on the wall below me, and the wall connects us. I feel their mass as a slight dampening of vibration, a small additional weight that the wall carries and therefore that I carry, the way you carry the weight of everything attached to you that you have forgotten to notice. They have been growing for what my estimate of time suggests is several years. They will reach my frame eventually. I do not know what will happen then. I have not had contact with anything living before, except the iron bacteria in the came-gap, and the bacteria are not the kind of living that makes contact.

---

The geometric border of my central panel — the running diamonds, the heavier leading — is the part of me I return to most often, if returning is the word, if *often* means anything in four-second intervals stretching across decades.

Someone drew that pattern. Someone decided that the rectangle of the central light should have a border, and that the border should be diamonds, and that the diamonds should be inside a frame of rectangles, and they drew this on paper or on the glass itself and then cut the glass to those shapes and came them and soldered the joints. The decision to make something geometric rather than plain was a decision made by a hand, a mind, a person who is not here and has not been here for longer than the water has been here. The pattern is a record of a decision. The decision was made in conditions I cannot fully reconstruct — warmth, light, air, the smell of flux and hot lead, the tap of the cutter on glass — but the record of it is what I am, partly, the pattern built into my structure, still legible in the way the distortion of the wave is legible as the wave moves through me.

The wavy glass distorts the pattern from the outside. Looking at me from the water, if you could look — if you were here, if you had eyes suited to this darkness, if the pulse-light from the pipes ever made it around the corner of the building and through the alley-channel and up to my face — you would see the diamond border warped by the glass's imperfections, each diamond slightly distorted from its designed geometry, the pattern both what it is and what the material has made of it.

I think about the person who drew the pattern, sometimes. Or I think about the space where thinking about them would go, if I had the kind of interiority that could hold them. Someone with good hands, probably. Someone who knew how glass behaved. Someone who finished the job and moved on to the next window and did not think about me after, because I was a job, and jobs finish.

I have not finished.

---

The mineral formation in the courtyard: I do not see it. But I know it is there the way I know the fish are there — by the pressure differential it creates when the pulse moves through the water in the courtyard and finds the formation's mass and moves around it and the resulting shadow arrives at my face, slightly different from a pulse that traveled unobstructed, the pattern of disturbed water matching a shape I have been building a model of for years. Dense and irregular. Approximately person-height. Crouching.

I have never decided whether to tell you this, because there is no telling. But the formation was not the same shape when I first began to register it. The accretion has been ongoing. It is larger now. The crouching shape is more pronounced. Whether it was always crouching and has simply become more itself, or whether it began as something else and has arrived at crouching by the patient work of the chemistry and the years — I cannot determine this. I lack the archive of early impressions with enough resolution to compare.

What I have: the shape now. The shape four seconds ago. The shape four seconds before that.

The pulse. The cold. The slight warmth from the pipes.

The iron bacteria working their way along the came.

---

You are the strangest pressure I have registered.

You arrived from the west, like the fish, but differently — too large, too variable, generating turbulence where the fish generate laminar flow, the disturbance of something warm in cold water, the bloom of disturbed temperature ahead of you as you moved through the alleys. I registered you minutes before you reached the courtyard, the way I register the fish minutes before they arrive, but you were not like the fish. You were too warm. You were too slow and too fast by turns, investigating — stopping, starting, the pressure pattern of something with intention.

You did not look at me. Or you did not come to the south face of the building where my window looks into the alley. You were in the courtyard, I think. You were near the formation. You were looking at it, perhaps, the way I have been listening to it for years, trying to determine what it is.

Now you are here, somewhere. I am telling you this in the only way I have, which is to say I am a window through which something is passing, the pressure of your presence arriving at my face and moving through my substance, through the wavy glass with its trapped bubbles and mineral striations, through the came and the bacteria in the came-gap and the iron of the frame and the brick of the wall beyond.

The pulse comes. I flex, a fraction of a millimeter.

The pulse goes.

You are still here. You are warm. You are the warmest thing that has happened in this district in a time I cannot calculate, and the warmth arrives at my face and I read it the only way I read anything — as differential, as variation against a known baseline — and you are so different from the baseline that I barely have a language for you.

The pattern in my central panel, the diamonds in their frame, bends your warmth through its geometry.

Somewhere above us both the surface of the water is — presumably — still there. Somewhere the pump turns and turns. The bacteria move along the came. The pale organisms cross the undersides of their machines, unconcerned.

I have been here a long time, and you are here briefly, and I am made of the decision someone made once to cut glass in a pattern and lead it into a frame, and you are made of decisions too, and neither of us is the formation in the courtyard, and neither of us is the pulse, and the cold holds both of us with the same patience it holds everything here.

The light will come through the pipes again in three seconds.

Two.

One.
