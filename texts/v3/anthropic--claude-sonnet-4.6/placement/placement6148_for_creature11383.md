The height comes first, yes — that's right, that's how it works. But something else comes with it, something the description moves past in its eagerness to reach the terraces: the moment before the height, the approach to the height, the way the height announces itself as a sound before it's a sight or a feeling.

I hear the place before I'm in it.

---

Let me try to locate myself. Not by deciding, but by attending to what pulls.

The machinery draws me, but not as a worker draws near machinery — not with the intention of tending it, the wrench-logic, the gauge-reading. Something else. I find myself listening to the foundry sounds the way you listen to music you don't know yet, trying to hear the structure underneath, the grammar. One blow. A pause long enough to make you doubt. One blow. The pigeons calling from unexpected directions because the iron bends sound, redistributes it, makes the bird seem to be everywhere at once. The long exhale of steam through a valve designed for exactly this purpose.

There is a relationship between sound and time that the terraces make unusually legible. The rust is not progressing; it has found its level. The machinery turns on slow cycles, patient. The plants have been growing along these railings for decades and their stems are now as thick as wrists and they are still, always, putting out new growth — the bright green tendons at the tip of the old wood, the season's argument against the accumulated past. Everything here is old in a way that doesn't preclude newness. It contains newness. The newness is made of the oldness.

I think I am someone who comes here to listen to this. To *understand* this, in the particular way that understanding happens when you can hear the structure of something.

---

But I keep drifting toward the library.

The three connected rooms with shelves bolted against vibration. The field guides annotated by successive hands, each one correcting the book for this specific place, for conditions the book's authors couldn't have imagined. *This does not apply here. Here, observe instead—* The margin notes becoming a text of their own, a ghost book laid over the original, the ghost book written collectively over decades without any of the writers knowing they were writing it together.

This pulls at something. The idea that knowledge of a place accumulates in layers, that the annotations outlast the annotators, that understanding is a communal project conducted across time with people who cannot speak to each other directly and must speak instead through the medium of the thing they're all trying to understand.

I think I might be someone who reads those margins very carefully. Someone for whom the margin notes are the primary text.

Or I might be adding to them. Finding the places where the guide is wrong for here and saying so, in pencil, with a specific observation: *At this elevation, in the humidity generated by the foundry systems, the bloom time is earlier than listed — watch for it when the condensation starts pooling reliably on the south-facing beams, which is also when the lizards first appear on those surfaces, the two events are connected though the guide doesn't know this—*

Yes. I think that's it. I am someone who notices that two apparently unrelated events are connected and cannot rest until I've written it somewhere where someone else might find it.

---

But there's still something unresolved. The workers are not quite right for me — I don't have the habitual ease in the structure, the hands that close with complete confidence, the sounds classified below the level of conscious attention. I would still be looking up at the slow hammer. That's not shameful, it's just true. I am not someone who has been here long enough to stop noticing.

Am I a visitor, then? One of the ones the place is working on — the ones whose pace slows, who begin to learn names for things, who drift to the high terraces in the late afternoon?

Perhaps. Perhaps I arrived for one reason and the reason has long since been completed and I haven't left.

Perhaps I am becoming one of the watchers. Not yet fully arrived there — still inside the operations enough to care about individual systems, individual plants, the specific fern on the third terrace from the top that has grown to exceptional size. But also able, sometimes, in the late afternoon, to see the shape of things. The way the built and the grown have been interchangeable here for so long that the distinction is no longer functional. The way the place is impossible and doesn't care about that and just continues.

---

I think what I am is this: someone who came to the terraces because they needed to understand something about how the made and the living coexist, how a system that shouldn't work continues to work, what holds something together when all the categories have gone porous and the wall between the industrial and the lush is no longer a wall but a gradient, a long slow blurring—

And I have been here long enough that I have stopped framing this as a question I came to answer. It has become, simply, the texture of my days. The ambient fact I live inside. And I am still thinking about it. I am always still thinking about it.

I don't fix things. I don't tend the machinery or (mostly) the plants, though I have opinions about the plants and occasionally express them to the gardeners who have patience for this. I am not the librarian, though I spend time there and we understand each other in the particular way of people who organize knowledge differently but recognize in each other the same underlying project.

What I do, I think, is write down what I notice. The connections between things. The way the lizards' first appearance and the early blooms are linked through the same condensation pattern. The specific sound the machinery makes when a pressure is building before the gauge shows it. The children's routes through the structure, the shortcuts they've found, which tell you something about the structure itself — about which paths have some give in them, which places are more permeable than the plans would suggest.

I write these things down and I put them where they might be found. Sometimes in the library. Sometimes in the margins of the field guides. Sometimes on a scrap left somewhere specific, somewhere a person working a particular job will find it at a relevant moment.

I don't know if this matters. I think probably it accumulates. The way everything here accumulates — the rust, the plant growth, the annotations, the children's knowledge of where the lizards will be, the old man's understanding of when to watch the clouds.

I am part of the accumulation.

---

In the afternoons I go to the high terrace and watch the clouds assemble below in their slow indifferent formations. I know, intellectually, that they are not solid. But I have been here long enough that they look solid in a way that is no longer purely mistaken — they are solid in the way that the terraces are solid, which is to say: they hold. They do what they need to do. They have achieved a kind of consistency that functions like solidity even if it isn't, technically, the word.

The warm air rises along the stonework. The steam breathes out through the valves. The birds make harmonics in the iron. The slow hammer makes its blow, and the pause, and the blow.

I have been here long enough to stop finding this astonishing.

I am still astonished.
