I come to myself slowly, which seems appropriate for this place.

Not a dramatic arrival. No descent through a shaft, no torquing of limbs through tight rock passages. I am simply here, the way you are simply in a dream when you become aware of it — and the awareness itself is the event. The ceiling is the first fact. It is always the first fact, for everyone who finds themselves here. I look up at it for a long time.

---

I think I am the one who came alone.

Or I think I am *becoming* the one who came alone — which is not quite the same thing, because that person already happened, is already preserved in the bone and the single mark at eye-height on the root-column, is already older than the roamer practice and the long-occupation and all the counting that came after. What I mean is: I recognize the impulse. The uncontrollable one. The one that said *you have to make a small version of this, a version that fits in your hands, because otherwise you cannot hold it*.

I have always made small versions of things too large to hold. This is the earliest thing I can say about myself with confidence.

---

But I stay longer than that person stayed.

The one who came alone left — or didn't leave, the evidence is quiet — after placing the model and marking the column. A person with a single purpose, completed. I find I do not have a single purpose, or rather my purpose keeps revealing new chambers, new extents, in the way the underloft itself reveals its extents: you walk toward what you thought was the far wall and it recedes, opens, turns out to have been haze all along.

So I am here longer. Long enough to find the roamers, when they come down through their shafts — a season after I arrived, two seasons, I have stopped counting accurately because the underloft's even light makes counting days a matter of discipline rather than instinct. I hear them before I see them: the particular quality of sound that a small group of people makes when they are being careful with their voices, consonants like falling root-matter, brief and soft and separated by deliberate silence.

I don't know what I look like to them when they find me.

Not like a permanent resident — I haven't made a long-occupation camp, haven't worn a path smooth, haven't filled a column with years of continuous marking. But not quite like a roamer either: I have been here too long for a visit, long enough that the cold has restructured my posture, long enough that my hands are learning the root-surface. Long enough that when they look at me, they look with the specific consideration they give to the marks they cannot read — not with alarm, but with the careful face of something that may be legible eventually, to the right attention, in the right season.

One of them is a child. I notice the child noticing the ceiling for what I think is her second or third time here — she is past the stage of looking at the floor first, but not far past it. She looks up and her face does the thing faces do when the category system isn't working, when something is filing itself into no available drawer. She is ten, perhaps. Eleven. She has her adult's hands already, the callus-pattern beginning.

I watch her look up and I feel the underloft in my chest like a resonance.

---

What I do here, I come to understand gradually, is neither the roamers' attending nor the long-occupation watchers' sustained vigil.

I write the underloft down.

I don't mean marks on stone — I have made those, near the column where I first slept, but they are rougher than the roamers' marks, less systematized, more like a person talking to themselves than recording anything transmissible. What I mean is something else, a practice I have always done and only here have understood as a practice: I sit with the space until the space becomes language in me. Until the root-ceiling becomes something that can be carried in words, described, evoked — not replaced by the description, not reduced to it, but *traceable* in it the way a river is traceable on a map that knows it is not the river.

The bone model was an early version of this impulse. Make something the size of a closed fist that knows it is not the thing. Make a container for the too-large.

I write the ceiling. I write the cold and the sound of drops falling through several hundred meters of bright empty air. I write the isopod's deliberateness, its three centimeters of progress. I write the creak that comes from everywhere and fills the space and fades unevenly, the lowest register lasting longest. I write the child's palm pressed to stone near a shaft mouth, the outline that will outlast everyone now alive. I write the bone model itself, positioned facing up toward what it models, someone's uncontrollable response to something that exceeded their containment.

I write the sleep. Thirteen hours, sometimes. The cold accelerating the need for restoration. Emerging slow, longer in the threshold state, spending time simply being here before beginning the work of attending. The observers at the shaft openings who believe the sleep is the point — I think they are half right. The sleep is not the point but it changes the kind of attention that is available when you wake. It clears something. You surface from it not refreshed exactly but reset, the previous day's frameworks loosened, the underloft presenting itself as itself again rather than as a continuation of what you already know about it.

This is what the writing needs. A sleep like that. A waking that doesn't know yet what it's going to find.

---

I am aware of the problem: writing a place changes your relationship to the place, and not always toward greater intimacy. There is a version of this work that becomes a kind of consumption — the underloft as material, the people as material, the roamers' practice and the long-occupation sites and the one-who-came-alone all becoming material for something I am making above, at the surface, where readers live. I feel the pull of this and I distrust it. It is the pull that wants to *use* the underloft rather than *be* in it, and the underloft knows the difference, I think — not mystically, but practically, the way a place knows the difference between a visitor who is still comparing it to other places and one who has stopped comparing and simply arrived.

When I stop comparing, the writing gets better. When I forget, for hours or days, that I am writing at all, and simply attend — the columns, the marks I cannot read, the child learning to look up — then language comes back to me later with the quality of something found rather than manufactured. Found in the root-fall and the cold and the creak and the long sleep. Found in the space between two drops falling.

The roamers always want to talk about the ceiling.

I understand this completely. The ceiling is where the light comes from, sourceless and counterintuitive, the problem the underloft poses before it poses any others: *you are underground and you are not in the dark*. Everything else — the attending, the marking, the returning through generations, the bringing-down of children, the long occupation, the one who came alone — everything follows from that first fact, that first wrongness the mind keeps reaching to correct and cannot correct because it is not wrong, it is just the underloft, the actual underloft, which has its own rules and they are not yours.

You are underground and you are not in the dark.

The ceiling is bright. The ceiling is alive and it glows. The ceiling is making a sound right now, low and resonant, the sound of something vast adjusting. A few fragments of root-matter spiral down through the bright air, three hundred meters, unhurried.

I sit on the floor of the underloft. The cold comes up through the stone into my sitting bones. I wait for words and they come, eventually, like drops: individual, separated by silence, striking the surface of the thing I'm trying to make.

The underloft proceeds.

I proceed with it.
