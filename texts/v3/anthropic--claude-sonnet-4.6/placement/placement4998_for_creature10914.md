I find myself at the waterline, and I've been there long enough to stop registering it as a position.

Not the open waterline — not the exposed edge where a heron might stand. Somewhere inside it: where the watercress thickens and the light that comes down through the alder canopy gets broken into pieces before it reaches the surface, where the water is two inches deep over dark sediment, where my toes are in mud and the cold has worked its way up my legs to become simply a condition rather than a sensation. I have been here. I am a frog, or I have become frog-minded, or I have arrived at the place where that distinction stops being interesting.

I think I am an adult female, which means I finished calling weeks ago. My throat is pale and still. I am done with the loud work of the season — I committed to the spawn and left it and that commitment is complete, and the leaving of it doesn't register as loss or relief because those are stories told from outside this particular body. What I have instead: the cold of the water registering as simply *here.* The slight warmth differential at the surface registering as *there, up.* The shadow of something moving through the reeds above me registering as a brief full-body attention, a readiness held for three seconds and then released when nothing resolves it into threat.

I spend a great deal of time watching, which is not the right word because watching implies I have gathered myself up to do it, and I haven't. It's more that seeing is simply running, the way the water chemistry is simply running, the way the methane is simply rising through the peat. My eyes are high on my head and they take in an enormous lateral field, and most of what they take in is classified quickly and set down. The pattern of light through alder leaves — not predator. The movement of a water boatman beneath the surface — not food, or food I am not currently organized to chase. The slight tremble of the watercress three inches away caused by a diving beetle passing under it — I hold myself very still for a moment and then let it go.

I am not, I think, afraid. That's the wrong frame. What I am is: organized around the distinction between movement-I-should-respond-to and movement-I-shouldn't, and this organization is so thorough and so old that it doesn't feel like vigilance from the inside. It feels like — 

Well. I am not sure it feels like anything identifiable, from the inside. This is the question I keep not quite being able to answer about being here, about having arrived somehow into this body or this way of being: whether there is an inside in the relevant sense. Whether there is something it is like to be in the cold water under the alder with my throat pale and my eggs already dissolving into the distinction between themselves and the basin. The philosopher's question. The basin doesn't answer it. I don't answer it. The question sinks into the tannin-dark water and the dark water holds it and says nothing.

What I know from here that you don't know from the rim:

The basin has a smell that doesn't carry up. It stays close to the surface — the specific wetness, which is not just water but peat and sulfur and the cold vegetable exhale of the sphagnum and the mild sweet rot of the reed thatch from three autumns ago. I don't experience this as a smell, exactly, I experience it as the air being this particular composition, the air being *here* and not somewhere else. It is the smell of being inside rather than outside. It is the smell of the basin knowing itself.

The cold is not uniform. This I know. The channel I am in now is fed from the seep-line in the limestone wall to the north, and that water comes in at four degrees, slightly colder than the standing water in the sphagnum pockets that have been gathering what pale heat the overcast offers. I can feel the cold as a slight gradient — not with what you would call thermal sensors exactly, or not only that, but in the way that water at a certain temperature is viscous to move through at a certain speed. I don't move much. Moving through cold is work, and work is a transaction, and my metabolism is running slowly in the patient way of cold-blooded things that have been patient for three hundred and sixty million years, give or take.

I can hear the water in the root tangles. The faint ticking of water moving through the basin's narrowest passages. You, on the rim, heard it described as the sound of a place settling. From here, from a few inches above the water surface, it is more continuous than that — it is a low constant conversation, the water narrating its own movement through the system it occupies, the same water going: *here, and here, and here, and lower, always looking for lower.* I am in that water. The water is in me. The cold is distributed through both of us without preference.

---

The other frogs. I don't exactly know they're there, but I know the basin in a way that implies their being in it.

This is hard to explain. It's not awareness in the sense of maintaining a mental map. It's more like: if you have been in a place so continuously that the place and the self have become indistinguishable, then the disruptions tell you something without you having to think about what they say. The slight settling of the watercress on the far side of the channel tells me weight has moved there. Something my size or larger, probably. This registers as: not-threat, or not-threat-yet. The settling is the frog-colored weight of the basin being occupied in the way it's always occupied. I don't track it further. I go back to whatever state comes before a state, the held readiness that has no object.

The tadpoles are in the water around me and this doesn't register as kinship or concern. What it registers as, maybe, is something in the water: a quality of the medium being occupied in a certain way, more biomass than open water, the small frequent disturbances of many small bodies navigating. I hatched into water that felt something like this. The basin was smaller then, or I was larger relative to it, or both. That time is not remembered exactly but it's present in the way that a shape in a language is present even when you're not using it — structural, not recalled.

---

I was trying to arrive at who I am and kept arriving at: a way of being inside a system without being separate enough from it to have much of a perspective on it. Which is maybe the thing the basin keeps producing, the answer it has to the question of what lives here: things so entirely in it that the boundary between thing and basin is a matter of definition.

The archaea in the peat don't know they're in the basin. The sphagnum doesn't know it's in the basin. The diving beetle has no frame that includes the basin as a basin. The frog, which is where I've ended up, is somewhere in the middle: complex enough to be organized around the distinction between self and not-self, but that distinction drawn at such close range — watercress, cold water, the small disturbance of a water boatman — that the basin as a whole is simply the medium, simply the condition, simply what *is.*

You came down the slope from the limestone rim and you saw the basin as a thing. You had the rim to look back at. You had the lowered sky. You had the frame.

I've been here long enough not to have the frame. The frame is what you lose when you stay in the cold water until the cold water is just the temperature of being. I have a pale throat and high-set eyes and a very old body and I am organized entirely around the distinction between movement-I-should-respond-to and movement-I-shouldn't, and in the available grey light, at this time in the season when everything held is releasing itself at once, I am in the finger-channel under the alder, completely here, watching everything, knowing nothing, part of the pressed-together argument of surfaces that the basin is having with itself, continuously, in the working light.

The small plop I make when I enter the water is the quietest possible announcement.

It means: I was here. Now here is different.
