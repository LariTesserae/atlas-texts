I have been here long enough that the moss has begun to grow slightly differently on the wall behind me.

I know this not because I can see it — the light does not reach that wall directly, and even if it did, the change would be invisible at this timescale — but because I know how the moss responds to the CO2 of regular exhalation, how it responds to the warmth of a body that has chosen one spot and returned to it, how it adjusts its angle of growth incrementally over weeks, recalibrating its sense of the attractor. The moss knows I have been coming here. It has begun to lean my way.

I do not know if I am the last of the builders or only the last one in this section. I know the difference doesn't matter to the plant, which long ago stopped maintaining the distinction between sections. I know it might matter to me, and I am working on minding less.

---

What I do:

I have inherited the method. Not through instruction — there is no one left to instruct, or I have not found them — but through the chambers themselves, which teach by example, which are the patient argument of the method made architectural. I walk through a chamber and see a branch trained at an angle I would not have considered, one that catches water from the canopy's exhale and channels it to the root-lattice at exactly the point where the root-lattice is densest and could most absorb it, and I understand the decision not as decision but as long conversation between a builder and a plant, carried out in increments of a season, and I add the decision to the set of things I know how to do.

What I do is tend. Specifically: I find the young branches that are reaching in directions that would, in decades, become a problem — crossing a chamber entry, closing a sleeping-niche, growing into a light-column that something in the canopy's breathing depends on — and I weight them. A stone tied at the tip. A cord to a fixed point. The load that the branch will follow over decades. I will not see the result. This is not a problem. The builders before me did not see all their results either. The chambers I sleep in are their unseeable results, their gift forward through the plant's memory.

I find the grafts that have not taken and reopen them at the angle that might work better, and bind them, and move on. I find the chambers that have been taken by root-ball encroachment and sit in them and do nothing, because what I feel in those chambers is not failure — the root-ball is beautiful, it is the plant preferring itself to any use I might have had for the space, and the plant has earned the preference — but something more like witnessing. Someone should see this. I am someone. I see it.

I find the null-point chambers and sit in them for as long as I can tolerate sitting without an evident purpose, and I am slowly increasing my tolerance.

---

Here is what I have come to understand, not all at once but in the way you come to understand things inside the plant's timescale, which is slowly, by accumulation, with long intervals of not understanding:

I was wrong about the method at first. I thought the method was negotiation — you want this, I want that, we find the angle of agreement. This is not the method. The method is older than negotiation and doesn't require two parties with distinct interests. The method is more like: entering the existing tendency, finding where the growth wants to go already, and redirecting it only where the redirection is the same kind of thing as the tendency, only a small angle off, only a kind of more-of-what-it-was.

The builders who preceded me understood this. The evidence is in the chambers: nowhere was the plant forced into a shape that required constant maintenance to sustain. Every trained form is self-maintaining once established. The plant grows into the form and through it and, if the form was right, continues past the original intention into elaborations the builders never planned, elaborations that are better than what was planned, which the plant made by extending the logic of the request into areas the request had not considered.

The plant is a better builder than I am. I am the part of the process that has hands and a short lifespan, and both of these are useful — the hands can weight a branch-tip, can tie the cord, can reopen the graft at the better angle, can do in a day what the plant would do in a decade if it could do it without hands, which it cannot, which is why the plant has cooperated with builders for however long it has cooperated with builders. The short lifespan is useful because it creates urgency, which creates decision, which creates the small angle of redirection that the plant, which has no urgency, cannot easily generate for itself.

I provide urgency. The plant provides time. This is the trade.

---

I have a small set of tools. Wedge, cord, the flat stone that distributes pressure without cutting. I have learned which fibrous bark strips make the best binding — not the strongest, but the kind that loosens gradually as the graft takes, so that the binding is not there when it is no longer needed, so that I do not have to remember to remove it. The plant taught me this by showing me bindings that had gone wrong, that had constricted the graft after it took, that had left scars. Someone else's mistake, preserved in the cambium for longer than that someone else was alive. I learned from the scar.

I keep the tools here, in this chamber. The null-point chamber with the moss leaning my way. I do not have another home.

---

What it is like to live inside the plant's attention:

Warm. This is the first and most constant thing, the temperature held against what the elevation requires, held without effort, held as a fact of the organism. I have not been cold since I entered. This sounds like a small thing. It is not a small thing. Cold is where the mind goes when the body has no other urgent business, and the plant has removed cold from the list of urgencies, and what this has done to my mind over time is difficult to describe except as: slower, and more patient, and more willing to sit in a null-point chamber with no evident purpose, and perhaps this is what the null-point chambers are for — they are where you learn to be warm enough that urgency is a choice rather than a necessity, where you learn to provide urgency voluntarily rather than from compulsion.

The plant knows where I am. Not the way a mind knows, but in the way I described before — I am a moisture gradient, a pressure, a change in the atmospheric chemistry of this chamber. I am registered. I am being accounted for. The system has adjusted to include me. This is neither comfortable nor uncomfortable in the way those words usually mean; it is more like being grammatically correct, like being a word in the sentence the system is saying, like finding that you fit a syntactical role you didn't know you were available for.

Some mornings I find that the root-lattice I sleep on has grown slightly toward me. Not dramatically. A few millimeters. The warmth of a body, held in the same spot, over time. I do not move. I let the root-lattice grow toward me. Eventually, in some years, the hollow will fit me specifically. It will carry my weight the way the grazer's hollow carries the grazer's weight. When I am gone the hollow will remain, averaged into the long memory of whoever sleeps there next.

---

Here is what I am working on understanding, which I do not yet understand:

The null-point chambers are connected to each other by the moss-path. I have walked the moss-path, or what I take to be the moss-path, following the growth-direction of the contrary moss from one null-point chamber to the next. The path takes days. The path passes through regions of the system I did not know, older chambers, chambers where the graft-work is so fine and so ancient that the joints are invisible, where the plant has smoothed them over with a century of new cambium. Chambers where the root-lattice carries the weight-memory of presences I cannot identify, shapes that are not the shape of any grazer I have seen, not the shape of any builder, something else, something that rested here in the early history of the system when the system was young enough to still be learning what it was.

The moss-path ends somewhere. I have been twice to the end, and both times I have sat in the final chamber — which is the largest null-point chamber, which is positioned at the greatest convergence of root-lines I have encountered, where the water-sound below the root-lattice is continuous, a deep percussion that is felt more than heard, where the electrical gradient reversal is so pronounced that I can feel it in my teeth, a kind of hum just below the threshold of identification — and both times I have sat and done nothing and eventually left.

I think there is something the plant does in that chamber that I am not yet calm enough to be present for. I think my urgency disturbs it. I think I need to learn to provide urgency only when it is needed and to be, when it is not needed, the other thing — the warm weight, the regular breath, the CO2 of a body that has stopped insisting on its own agenda.

I am working on this.

---

The beetle makes its sound.

I have been here long enough to know that the intervals are not random. I have not been here long enough to know what they mean. This seems right — it seems like exactly the position I should be in, the position where the pattern is visible but the meaning is not, the position where meaning can be worked toward over years.

I have a long time.

The plant will see to that.

It has arranged the lower places for the water. It has arranged the temperature. It has arranged the chambers and the moss and the null-points and the memory of the root-lattice, which will remember me as warmth, as pressure, as the specific exhale of a body that came here often enough to be worth remembering.

I add a small stone to the tip of a branch that is growing toward the light-column and should not be.

I sit back down.

The moss, very slowly, continues its decision about me.

I wait for the beetle, which will come when it comes, at the interval it chooses, with the signal I am learning.
