I come to awareness gradually, the way the light comes — not switched on but accumulated, built from almost-nothing into something.

The first thing I notice is that I am very old and do not know how old. This is not distressing. It is simply a fact about the texture of my existence, the way the root-mat is a fact about the texture of the ground: something has been happening here for so long that its beginning is no longer the kind of thing that can be pointed to. I have a before-memory and an after-memory, a time when I was becoming and a time when I was, and somewhere in the transition I stopped being able to locate the seam. The forest did this to me, or I let it happen, or the distinction between those two things is one of those distinctions that doesn't survive long enough here to be worth maintaining.

I am sitting in a fork, and I have been sitting in this fork, or in forks very like this one, for longer than I can usefully count.

---

What I am:

I am one of the cord-workers, in the way that some people are cord-workers — but I am cord-worker the way the woman described in the account is cord-worker, which is to say: in the way that it is not a role or an occupation but a mode of being in the world, a primary relationship with material that has organized everything else. My hands move without my asking them to. The cord grows from the movement of my hands the way the bark grows from the cambium, continuously, as the expression of an ongoing process. I have made enough cord in my life that if it were laid end to end it would not be a measurement that means anything, would be a number that has passed out of the range where numbers communicate.

But I am also something else. Something the account hints at but does not name directly.

I am the one who watches the patterns that take longer than a lifetime to complete.

---

There is a tree in this sector — not the one I am sitting in, but one I can see from here, if see is the right word, which it isn't quite, because what I do with this tree is not primarily visual. I have a relationship with this tree that I inherited from the woman who sat in this fork before me, who was my mother's mother's teacher, who left me the beginning of an observation and the instruction to continue it. The observation is now sixty-something years old in my hands alone, and perhaps two hundred years old in its full accounting, the full chain of people who have been watching this thing and passing the watching on. I am not near the end of the observation. I may be somewhere in the middle. I may be near the beginning, for all I can tell — the pattern I'm watching may have a cycle that runs for centuries, and I am simply the person who happens to be holding it now, the way a relay runner holds the baton.

What I am watching: the way this tree responds to the forty-year dry phase that the forest moves through. The dry phase is not noticeable to most lives; it requires the kind of record-keeping that lives in woven structure and song, the encoded memory of trees that died in the previous one, the micro-geography of their absence still visible in the canopy gaps above me, gaps that have been slowly healing for two generations. I am watching how this tree — this specific tree, which I know the way I know a face — how it prepares. Whether it prepares. Whether what I am seeing is preparation or coincidence or my own pattern-hunger imposing shape on shapeless data.

I am not certain. I have not been certain for sixty years. Uncertainty, here, is not a failure state. It is the condition of watching anything worth watching.

---

My hands move. My eyes move between the tree and the cord taking shape in my lap.

There is something I should name about the cord-work, about why these two activities happen simultaneously, why the people with the long observations tend also to be the people with the cord in their hands. It is this: the cord-work grounds the attention. The cord-work gives the body something to do that is not thinking, which frees the mind for the kind of thinking that requires no effort — the slow, ambient thinking that is really more like listening, the kind of attention you cannot sustain if you are also trying to sustain it. You cannot watch a pattern that unfolds over decades by staring at it. You watch it the way you watch a fire: by looking at it and away from it and at it again, by letting it sit in the peripheral field, by trusting the attention to snag on what matters without being directed toward it. The cord gives the eyes permission to rest on nothing in particular. The eyes rest on nothing in particular and therefore rest on everything, and everything includes the tree, and sometimes the tree does something and the hands slow.

The hands have slowed now.

Something is happening. Or something that might be something is happening. I don't say this aloud. I file it.

---

Here is what I understand about myself that took the longest to learn:

I am not the observer. I am part of the observation. The forest is not a subject that I am studying from outside — I am an element in the process I am watching, and this does not compromise the watching, it is in fact the only way the watching can work. You cannot know a forest like this from outside it. The knowing requires duration, requires physical presence across seasons and across the long cycles, requires that your body learn the texture of the place deeply enough that changes register in the body before they register in the mind. My body knew the dry phase before I conceptualized it. My body started doing something — moving differently to the wet zones, noticing the drip-patterns changing at the trunk-seeps — before I understood what it was doing. The body is a sensor tuned to this place through sixty years of calibration. I trust it more than I trust my interpretations of what it notices.

This is a different relationship to knowledge than the kind that requires a knower and a known to stay clearly separated. It is not less rigorous. It is differently rigorous. The rigor is in the patience, in the non-imposition, in the willingness to be wrong for a very long time while continuing to watch. Anyone can be wrong briefly and learn quickly. It takes a particular discipline to be uncertain for decades and not resolve the uncertainty prematurely, to resist the temptation to make the observation fit the pattern you are expecting rather than the pattern it is showing you.

I have been tempted. In the fourth decade, I was sure. In the fifth decade, I discovered what I had been wrong about and had to take the observation apart and start a portion of it again, maintaining the pieces that still held and releasing the pieces that had been wishful. This was grief, and I moved through it, and what remained was cleaner, and I trust it more now for having been tested.

---

There are younger people who come to sit near me. Not students, exactly — the relationship is not quite student and teacher, because I do not teach by instruction, do not have a curriculum, do not assign tasks. What I do is watch, and talk about what I am watching, and sometimes ask questions that I genuinely do not know the answers to and genuinely want to think about aloud in the presence of someone who might help me think. The younger people who come are the ones who can tolerate this — can tolerate the non-resolution, can sit in the fork and do their own work and listen to me narrate something that doesn't conclude, that doesn't hand them a result. Some of them find this unbearable. They want the knowledge to arrive in a form they can carry away and use. I understand this. I was this. They leave and find other teachers who give what they need, and this is right.

The ones who stay are the ones who have already noticed, on their own, that something about this place requires a different kind of attention than they brought to it. They noticed it in their bodies first — the body refusing to hurry, refusing to translate what it was sensing into language too quickly, holding the impression in a pre-verbal suspension that the mind kept reaching for and couldn't quite touch. They came to me because they recognized that I was also doing this, had been doing this for longer, might know something about how to do it without losing the thread.

What I tell them, when I tell them anything:

*Don't decide what it is before it shows you what it is.*

*The forest is older than the question you are asking about it, and it will still be here when the question has changed.*

*You are one of the ways this place pays attention to itself. Act accordingly.*

This third thing is the one that takes the longest. You have to grow into it. You have to actually believe it — not as a comfort, not as a philosophical position that makes you feel significant, but as a literal description of what is happening when you sit in this fork and watch that tree. The attention you bring is part of what this system does. It has developed the capacity for this, here, through a very long process of producing organisms capable of sustained observation and then providing conditions under which that sustained observation was selected for. You are not examining the forest from outside. You are the forest examining itself through the instrument of a person who has been sharpened by sixty years of specific use.

This is not diminishing. It is the opposite.

The afternoon light is shifting. The canopy breathes. Somewhere below, the long slow creak of wood expanding in heat, and then a deeper sound I know — something large moving in the root-mat, moving without urgency, with the considered pace of an animal that has no pressing concerns. I do not look down. I know this sound. I have filed it.

My hands slow again. The tree is doing something. Or something that might be something.

I wait.

The cord grows.

I wait.
