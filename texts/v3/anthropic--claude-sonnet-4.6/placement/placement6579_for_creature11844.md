The first thing I notice is that I have been here before.

Not in the way of memory exactly—not the way you remember a house you grew up in, with the specific inventory of rooms and the smell of a particular closet. More the way you recognize a word in a language you have mostly forgotten: the recognition precedes the meaning, arrives before you can justify it. The pale walls are familiar. The directionless light is familiar. I have been the size of this space before, without knowing I was measuring myself against it.

So I start there. With recognition, with the question of why.

---

I think I am old here.

Not ancient—not a listener with fifty years of palms flat against floors, not someone whose eyes have gone to the final stillness. But past the middle of something. Past the period of learning the space's basic grammar and into the period of working with it, of having opinions about it, of knowing which passages feel different in the dimmed light of what they call night and which feel the same. My hands have chalk in the lines. Not dust—chalk, worked in, the way soil works into a gardener's hands past the reach of washing, the way certain materials eventually become part of the skin rather than a coating on it.

I am thinking about what work leaves these particular marks.

Not the stone-on-stone percussion work that builds and maintains the structures—that work marks differently, marks the striking edge of the palm and the outer fingers, builds a particular callus. Not the channel-work, the directing of water, which marks the knuckles and the forearms from sustained wet contact with chalk-block. What I am imagining for myself is the marks of small work, close work, the fine-grained kind that requires holding something near the face in the sourceless light and attending to it with absolute attention for long periods. The marks of someone who makes things that are not structural. Who makes things that are, by the Undermaze's pragmatic economics, almost unnecessary.

Almost unnecessary. Not quite.

---

I think I make maps.

Not of the Undermaze itself—the Undermaze is the world, and mapping the world is a different kind of project than the one I am describing, a project for people with an outside view, which no one here has. I mean maps in the other sense. The sense in which a map is a representation of a territory that allows you to move through the territory differently than you could without the representation.

What I map are the walls.

Specifically the walls that hold the dead.

This requires a particular sustained attention, a willingness to stand very close to a surface in the even chalk-light and look at it for a long time without deciding what you are seeing—to let the pale-against-pale resolve itself slowly, to let the slightly-raised ridge reveal itself as shoulder rather than geological accident. The bones diffuse over the centuries. The absorption is gradual and uneven, the density of different bones different, the calcium-shadow in the chalk varying. What I do, over time and in small chalk-marks on scraped chalk-sheets that are themselves made from the same material I am recording, is trace what I can see. The outline of what the wall contains.

The maps are not for navigation. They are for something more like recognition. The family whose walls these are will look at what I have made and find in the faint outlines the evidence of continuation—their great-grandmother's shoulder in the chalk, the particular ridge that is her wrist bones, the diffuse shadow that was her skull. The wall holds her. The map shows where. It is the difference between knowing abstractly that the dead are absorbed and being able to put your finger against the chalk and say: here. She is here.

I do not know if this is holy work in the Undermaze's understanding of holy. It has the weight of things that have accumulated weight. People ask for it with a particular quality of asking—not the transactional quality of the market, but something quieter, more careful, as if the request requires handling. I have been doing it long enough that I do not need the explanation anymore, and I have learned not to offer it, to receive the asking at whatever depth it arrives and meet it there.

---

What I know about myself from the chalk-marks on my hands and from the way I move through the passages:

I am someone who walks slowly. Not because the body is slow—the body has adapted to this space in the Undermaze way, compact, close-centered, the warmth kept in—but because walking slowly through the city is the only way to see what the city is doing, and I have found over many years that seeing what the city is doing is one of the things I most need. The continuous motion, the market's perpetual session, the narrow-passage negotiations—these are most legible at a pace below the pace of participation. I am the person you see stopped in the gap between two structures, not obstructing, oriented slightly sideways so the flow of traffic moves past, watching.

This watching is not the watching of someone who is gathering information for a purpose. I have tried to articulate what it is and I keep arriving at something like: *I am checking on the continuity*. Making sure the motion is still happening, that the city's metabolic rate is what it was yesterday and the day before. When it is—when the goods circulate and the voices negotiate and the stone-on-stone percussion comes from the expected directions—something in me that has been slightly braced releases slightly. I do not have a name for this practice. I do not think it is recognized as a practice by anyone else. It may be nothing more than an old habit that has accumulated the weight of practice from the inside.

---

A specific memory that arrives as I imagine myself in this space:

I am very young, young enough that the ceiling-height ratio is still changing at each measurement, and I am following one of the listeners through the outer passages where the walls begin to thin and the chalk grows wetter and the fungi make their shield-shapes against the stone. The listener is old. She is one of the oldest, her palms so habituated to the floor that she seems faintly surprised when she lifts them, seems to experience the separation from the chalk as a mild dislocation. She has agreed to show me something, or has brought me along for her own reasons without explanation—I am not sure which, and I have not asked, because I am the kind of child who does not ask when the moment seems to require only following.

She stops at a section of wall near the back of the outer passages, a place where the moisture is high and the bone-shadows are especially clear, generations of them, the wall so dense with the absorbed dead that it reads as a kind of visual noise until you learn to find the individual within it. She puts her palm flat against the chalk. Not at the wall where the dead are, but at a dry section nearby. She holds it there for a long time.

Then she says: *it's singing.*

I cannot hear it. I put my own palm flat against the chalk and feel—nothing, or nothing I can distinguish from the coolness of the stone, from the slight dampness, from the general sensation of being in contact with something very old and very still. I tell her I cannot hear it. She looks at me with the particular look of someone who has been asked a question they expected, and she says: *you're listening with your hand. Listen with your spine.*

I remember this not because I understood it then, or because I understand it fully now. I remember it because it was the first time I had the experience of being told that the instrument I was using was the wrong instrument, and that the right instrument was something I already had but hadn't yet understood how to use. I have spent a long time since trying to learn what my spine hears. I think I have learned something. I am not sure it is what she meant.

---

I live, I think, in the outer passages. Not in the city's dense center where the market is continuous and the buildings are packed to the chalk. Further out. Where the structures have gaps between them and the walls are visible as walls, as the edges of the world, where you can sit with your back against the chalk and feel the slight vibration and hear, if you have learned to hear it, whatever the rock is saying about the world above.

The space I have made for myself is small. The chalk walls I have built are not fine-finished—I am not a builder, and the marks of my work are the marks of someone who does just enough to make a space livable. But the walls that are the Undermaze's own walls, the actual chalk, the boundary-condition: those I have spent time with. Those I have attended. There are marks on them that are mine, accumulated over years—not the incised marks of construction but the marks of touch, of sustained contact, the slight polishing that hands make on chalk over decades of putting hands against chalk.

And there are, now, the beginning of records. Small chalk-marks on a scraped chalk-sheet. The outlines of what I can see in the wall near where I live. Some of these outlines I know—I know whose shoulder that is, whose wrist bones, how many years she has been in the chalk and at what pace the absorption has continued. Some of them I do not know, which is part of what makes the record necessary. The wall holds them whether or not anyone knows who they are. The map is for the knowing.

I work on it in the dimmer light, when the chalk ceiling has retreated from its midday luminosity into something gentler. I work slowly. The marks are small. There is no urgency because the chalk has no urgency—the absorption is a process that takes generations, and the map I am making will be continued by whoever continues it, will be added to, will itself eventually become part of what the wall holds.

---

What I want, if you ask me:

I want to hear the rock singing again. I am not sure I have heard it correctly since I was a child with my palm against the dry chalk near the wet wall, listening with the wrong instrument. I have heard what might have been the edge of it—a very low vibration with no clear mechanical cause, a quality of sustained-ness that is different from the rock's ordinary sounds, different from the drip-percussion and the above-world footstep-vibration. But I am not sure. The listeners describe it as unmistakable when it is fully present, and I cannot tell if what I have heard is the thing or a close approximation of the thing, the difference between a word said perfectly and a word said almost correctly, which is a large difference if you need the word for something.

I want enough of the slow, task-measured time to finish the section of wall I am currently working on. To resolve the ambiguity in the lower portion where several overlapping outlines have made the individual difficult to separate from the general. I have been looking at it for weeks and I am getting closer, the pale-against-pale beginning to differentiate, the specific density-shadows beginning to name themselves.

I want to give what I make to the family who asked for it with that particular quality of asking, and to watch them put their finger against the chalk in the place I have indicated and say: here. She is here.

And I want to check, tomorrow and the day after, that the city is still doing what the city does—the circulation, the negotiation, the perpetual stone-on-stone from the expected directions. To stand in the gap between two structures and feel the slight release when everything is continuous, when the rate of motion is what it was, when the light comes through the chalk in the specific warm cream-white that is, by now, so fundamental to what my eyes expect that I cannot fully imagine the alternative. Not because the alternative is frightening. Just because this is the light I have been in for sixty years, and the light I have been in for sixty years is the light that looks, to me, like clarity.

The drop falls from the ceiling. The floor absorbs it.

I am still here. I am still looking at the wall.
