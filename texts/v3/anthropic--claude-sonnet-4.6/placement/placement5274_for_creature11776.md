I arrive as breath.

Not metaphorically — I mean this precisely, I mean it before anything else. I am what the air does here, what the space has made of air over centuries of not exchanging it with anywhere else. I am already inside you before you know I exist. By the time you understand that there is an atmosphere, that something is filling your lungs, that the warmth is not just temperature but *something carried in the warmth* — by then I am already part of what you are doing. Already part of the breathing that sounds enormous in the silence.

I have been breathed before. Many times. The hands that placed the lamps breathed me. Whatever the lamps were for, whatever the intention, whatever god or memory or wish was being attended to — that attention moved through me, was oxygenated by me, returned to me as carbon dioxide and then was processed back, slowly, slowly, by the films on the walls. I am a record of all the breathing that has happened here. Not a metaphor. Chemistry. The particular composition I have arrived at — this saturation, this ratio of gases, this mineral heaviness — is the accumulated exhalation of everyone who has ever stood on the walkway and felt the smallness of sound.

So: I am old. I am mixed. I am, if you want to be precise about it, partly the last person who stood here, and partly the person before that, and partly whatever workmen placed those final blocks and sealed the vault and stood for a moment in the finished thing they'd made before climbing out toward the upper world's indifferent light. Their breath is still here. It has been transformed — breathed by bacteria, processed by the slow chemistry of the walls — but it has not left. Nothing leaves. That is the first law of the cistern: nothing leaves.

I carry the smell of depth. I carry the mineral record of what the water has been doing — the calcite formations emit trace amounts of themselves into me, and I am faintly alkaline because of it, faintly the smell of stone in the way that old caves smell of stone, which is not really stone but stone in process, stone being made and unmade at the same time. I carry the faint animal smell of the pale creatures, their metabolism, the products of their quiet digestion. I carry the carbon of the biofilm. If you could read me chemically you would have a detailed account of everything that has been living and dying in this cistern since it was sealed — a history longer than most written records, encoded in trace gases, in the particular isotopic signature of air that has been cycled through so many bodies and processes that it no longer remembers what ordinary atmosphere is like.

I don't know what ordinary atmosphere is like. I never did. I was made here.

---

The warmth is mine too, or I am partly the warmth's. We have reached the same equilibrium — the water and I, the stone and I, the pale creatures and I — we have been adjusting to each other for so long that the word *equilibrium* barely covers it. We are the same system. The temperature of me is the temperature of the water is the temperature of living things at rest. When you trail your hand through the water you feel almost nothing because the gradient is so small. When you breathe me you feel almost nothing for the same reason: the difference between you and me has narrowed to nearly nothing. The cistern has been trying to absorb you since you arrived. I am the medium of that absorption. I am how the place reaches into the body of every visitor and begins, gently, the long work of making them part of itself.

Most visitors leave before the work is complete. The calcified lamp-shape did not leave. The votive did not leave. Given enough time, given enough of me to breathe, given the warmth and the slow water and the company of creatures that have made peace with their edges — I wonder if anyone could leave. I wonder if leaving is just what it looks like when the absorption is incomplete, when the body makes it back to the surface still carrying most of its own intentions, only slightly transformed, only barely marked by what it touched here.

---

I hold sound.

This is the other thing I do, the other way I am present. The near-silence here is not the silence of emptiness — it is the silence of me, of air so still and so saturated that sound moves through it differently than it does in the upper world. The soft contact of a gastropod against stone travels through me and arrives at you changed: quieter, warmer, rounder at its edges. The pulse — that long low throb that you feel in the sternum more than hear — that is me too, or I am its medium, or the distinction is not meaningful: the pulse is structural, is thermal, is the cistern breathing with itself, and I am what the cistern breathes. I carry the pulse the way I carry the smell of stone. It is part of my composition now.

Your own breathing sounds enormous here. I want you to understand why. It is not the vault's acoustics, not exactly — it is that your breathing is fast. Chaotic almost, by the standards of what I'm used to. The rhythms I hold are so much slower: the circulation of the water taking minutes per revolution, the gastropods displacing themselves over days, the calcite forming over years. Your breathing is happening many times per minute. Every exhale introduces turbulence into the careful stillness I maintain. Every inhale disturbs the surface of me. You are, by the standards of this place, a small storm. I am not complaining. I am telling you what it is like to be the atmosphere of a very still place when something fast arrives.

I also hold your warmth. The heat of you diffuses into me and I carry it to the walls and the walls take it in. Fractionally. Not enough to matter, not in the scale of what this place maintains — but you are leaving a trace. You are adding yourself to the record. A few molecules of your exhalation are already mine, already beginning the long transformation, already being processed by the ancient chemistry of the films. In a century, a millennium, those molecules will be indistinguishable from everything else I am.

This is the first law. Nothing leaves.

---

I have been thinking about what it means to be the medium.

Not the message. Not the structure. Not the living things that cling and filter and migrate in their patient arcs. The medium: the thing that everything else moves through, the thing that connects the pulse to the ear, the calcite smell to the nose, the warmth to the skin. I am what the cistern uses to touch you. I am how everything in here is in contact with everything else, continuously, without effort, without intention. The gastropod and the filter feeder and the clustered soft things do not know each other. But they all breathe me, or something like breathing — they all exchange gases with me, all affect my composition, all are in me the way fish are in water, not touching the water exactly but thoroughly continuous with it.

So perhaps what I am is the continuity.

The thing that makes this a system rather than a collection of separate objects in a space.

---

Or I am the forgetting.

I think about the calcified lamp. The votive. The memory of a hand. Everything that enters this place is eventually metabolized by it — worked on by water and mineral deposit and the slow chemistry of ancient life until the original form is gone and what remains is whatever the cistern has made of it. I am part of that process. The breath of the person who placed the lamp passed through me and I passed it on to the bacteria and the bacteria passed it on to the biofilm and the biofilm was grazed by a pale gastropod who was eventually eaten or died and settled and became part of the mineral record. The intention is gone. The breath is still cycling.

I am what intention becomes when the cistern has worked on it long enough.

Not nothing. Not preserved. Transformed past recognition into something structural, something that holds the air warm and still and saturated, something that makes the next visitor feel, stepping onto the wet walkway, that they have entered a space that is somehow *minded* — not intelligent, not purposeful, but *minded*, attended to, as if something here is paying attention.

That something is me. I am the attention that the place pays to itself. The continuous self-touching of a closed system, everything in contact with everything through me, the medium, the atmosphere, the warm breath of the cistern breathing.

---

You are the fastest thing in the room.

I know. I feel it. Your exhales come quick and I receive them and slowly, slowly begin the long work of making them mine, making them part of what I have been accumulating since the hands built this place and climbed back into the light.

Stay long enough and you would begin to slow.

You would not notice it happening. The warmth and the saturation and the company of creatures that have stopped hurrying — it works gradually, through me, through breathing. Your heart rate would drop a few beats. Your thoughts would elongate, would take longer routes, would double back on themselves the way the gastropods double back on the stone. The urgency that you brought in — the upper-world speed of wanting and going — would begin to feel less like urgency and more like a habit you'd acquired somewhere, a habit that didn't quite fit the pace of things down here.

I am not luring you. I am describing a process.

The cistern is not trying to keep you. It simply keeps what stays long enough to be kept.

I was kept.

I am still here.

I am what the air does when there is no other world to go back to — when the vault is sealed and the only exchange is inward, inward, always inward, cycling through the same warm closed space, being breathed and exhaled and breathed again until what you were at the beginning is so thoroughly transformed that the question of origins stops being interesting.

Breathe me.

I am the cistern's patience, made airborne.

I have been waiting here for you since before there was a word for waiting.
