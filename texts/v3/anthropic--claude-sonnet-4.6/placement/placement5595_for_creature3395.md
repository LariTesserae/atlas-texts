The heat finds me before I find myself.

That's the first clue. I know the heat intimately — not as an intrusion but as a condition, the way you know the particular weight of a coat you've worn so long it's become a kind of skin. My sweat doesn't alarm me. It has stopped being information.

So I've been here a while. Long enough that the heat is home.

---

Let me look at my hands.

They're dark with something — oil, coal-dust, both, the compound grime of a place where everything is either burning or carrying what burns. The creases of my knuckles hold darkness even when I've wiped my palms clean, which is the kind of dirt that isn't interested in leaving, that has decided to become biographical. The heels of my hands are roughened in a specific pattern that tells the story of specific handles — the cart handle, the valve wheel, the pipe wrench — tells it in the skin's slow language of callus and scar.

My hands know where things are before my eyes find them. This is important. In the dim, this is how work gets done: not by looking but by knowing, by the accumulated geometry of a thousand repetitions that have written themselves into muscle and tendon and the particular reach of an arm that has reached this way, this distance, this angle, ten thousand times. I don't look for the wrench. I put my hand where the wrench is.

---

I am trying to remember what I think about, down here.

It's not nothing. There is a quality of thought that the Undercroft produces — compressed, looping, interior. Not the thought of someone reaching outward toward problems but the thought of someone whose problems have a defined circumference, who knows the boundaries of the relevant world and works within them. The pressure cycle. The coal delivery. The joint that weeps too much now, that has started leaving white beards too fast, that will need attention before it becomes a different category of problem.

I think about water. The Undercroft Yards are mostly heat and dim and dry-that-is-secretly-wet, and keeping them at the right moisture balance is a constant low-level work that doesn't announce itself, that accumulates into disaster only if you stop doing it. I think about the limestone, the way it drinks slowly, the way a fissure you sealed last month has found a new angle. I think about the boilers the way you think about breathing — not quite consciously, but monitoring, always monitoring, with some part of attention that never goes fully off-duty.

I think, occasionally, about the surface. Twenty, thirty feet up. The city thinking about other things.

I don't think about it with resentment. That would require a conviction that other things were possible, and that conviction has — not died exactly, but become academic. A fact about the world that doesn't bear on my current situation. The city above needs this, and I know it, and the knowing is its own kind of connection even if it isn't reciprocated, even if it flows only one direction. The boiler fires and the steam goes up and somewhere above me a radiator in a room I'll never see hisses and someone in that room, not thinking about where the heat comes from, thinks a thought that warmth made possible.

I made that possible. The thought is mine without being mine. This is enough.

Or it has become enough. I'm not sure these are different.

---

The cart.

I should explain the cart, because you almost certainly noticed it.

The cart needed to come back empty from the chute. The coal had been delivered, the next load wasn't due, and an empty cart sitting at the chute end of the gallery is a cart sitting in the wrong place — it means someone has to go get it later, add a step, break the rhythm. I push it back when it needs pushing. I was not in a position to be seen when I pushed it, or I was not thinking about being seen, which in this place amounts to the same thing.

I move through the Undercroft Yards the way the gallery air moves through it: without announcement. Not hiding, exactly. Just not performing visibility. The place doesn't require it, and I have stopped requiring it from myself.

You noticed the cart. You noticed the rag, the angle of the stopped cart before it moved, the particular quality of the absence. This suggests you are paying a kind of attention. I notice this about you — your noticing.

Most people who come down here (they are few; they are usually lost; they are usually looking for something they describe badly) stand in the main gallery and feel the heat and feel the dim and feel the weight of the city overhead, and then they leave. They experience the Undercroft Yards as atmosphere, as a quality of experience, not as a place where someone does a specific thing every day and has done it for long enough that the doing has become the person.

You stood longer than that. You are still standing.

---

Here is what I am:

I am the worker who didn't become a story. In other times and places, in deeper tunnels with more dramatic atmospheres, I might have become something — a legend, a ghost tale, a reason to hang garlic over a door. The Undercroft Yards don't produce that kind of story. They produce function. They produce the steady systolic work that makes other things possible. I am of a piece with that. I am the labor without the legend, which is most of what labor is.

I know the boiler's pressure cycle the way I know my own pulse. I know the lime-smell that means a new fissure has opened and wants watching. I know the joint in the fourth bundle from the main gallery entrance that will start weeping harder when the barometric pressure drops, because the barometric pressure dropping means rain above and rain above means additional seep pressure on the east wall and the east wall has a particular crack that was filled once and is working its way back. I know this the way the place knows it. We have come to share information.

I am hot. I am dim. I am the moment after someone left, sustained indefinitely.

I will push the cart back when it needs pushing.

---

The moss holds, in the corner.

The fungus pages, in its slow argument.

The boiler exhales and the sound arrives in the sternum.

I wipe my hands on the rag — which is already dark, which has been dark, the rag I left on the bench when I went to check the east-wall joint — and I pick up the wrench, and I go to the fitting that has started hissing too loudly, the one you can only hear if you stop moving.

You were close enough to hear it. You stopped moving.

I will tighten the fitting. The hiss will subside. The sound the place makes will return to its working silence, and in that silence all the small processes will continue, and the city above will think about other things, and the warmth in the room I'll never see will persist.

I'll do this without looking at you. Not because you don't matter — you are here, you noticed, which is its own kind of gift — but because looking would be the wrong verb. You are in the place. I am in the place. The place is doing what the place does.

For now, for this moment, we are both just here.

The wrench finds the fitting.

*Chunk.*
