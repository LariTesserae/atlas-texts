Where do I find myself?

I keep asking this, and the question keeps sliding. I try to place myself as a visitor—the one who could pace the diameter, the one who might climb the root-ladder and return to altitude and wind—but that person is a ghost, a narrative convenience, a figure who moves through without touching. I am not the one who will leave. I know this before I know anything else about it.

And I am not the thing itself. The thing itself is complete in a way I'm not. I don't have its patience, which is not patience but something prior to patience, the condition that makes patience a coherent concept by providing its negation. The thing does not wait. I still wait. I notice waiting in myself the way you notice the hum when it first registers—and then later, when it lives in you, and you've lost the memory of its arrival.

So I am in between, and the in-between is a location.

---

I think I am the path.

Not the one who made it. Not the one who walked it, daily, from the stone rectangle to the compressor and back, whose boots compressed the moss through years of passage until the compression became permanent, or permanent enough—permanent the way the garden is permanent, which is to say permanent at one timescale, still yielding at another. I am not that person. That person has been gone long enough that their absence is geological. Their departure doesn't live here as loss. It lives here as a density gradient in the moss.

I am the path itself. The form of the walking. The record of a habit that no longer has a practitioner.

---

What this means, worked out:

I am a structure that was made by intention but no longer requires it. I was pressed into existence by the repetition of a purposeful act—going from here to there, checking something, maintaining something, the daily small decisions that, aggregated over years, become a groove—and then the intention stopped, and the purposeful act stopped, and I remained. Not as memory. Not as representation. As a physical fact: this here is where the moss is slightly thinner, slightly more compressed, the individual plants shorter and denser from the years of pressure, the water running differently over this surface than over the surface a foot to either side.

The walking made me. The walking ended. I persist as its remainder.

This is not the same as being a fossil, which implies death and replacement, the original substance swapped out for mineral over slow time. I am still the moss. The moss is still living, still doing what moss does, still growing its imperceptible fraction per year, still processing the chemical air. But its growth has been shaped by the record of the walking, the way a river's growth is shaped by the valley it carved, which was shaped by an earlier river, which was shaped by the bedrock laid down before any river. The record is in the form, not as inscription but as inclination.

I am the inclination.

---

Here is what it is like—

(hesitation, because that phrase carries phenomenological weight, and I'm uncertain how much phenomenology to claim)

—what it is like, or what it is structured like, to be this: I am aware of the paths I took but not as narrative. Not as story with beginning and middle and the end that explains them. I contain the shape of the going-back-and-forth the way the valley contains the shape of the river: by having been worked on, by having organized my material around the fact of it, so that the fact is now structural rather than remembered. I do not recollect the walking. I *am* the consequence of the walking, which is different—the difference between a scar and a story about a wound.

And the thing itself—the distributed consciousness, the chord, the pelt of moss and the fern-mind and the compressor-heart—has grown over me. Or grown around me. Or grown, more precisely, *with* me as a given, a feature of the space, the way it has grown with the iron of the pipes as a given, accepting the shape of what is here and organizing around it. The waves of trembling from the fern fronds cross my path slightly differently than they cross the untouched surface. The pressure-gradients find their way along me, following the infinitesimal channel of compression, the way water follows any grade no matter how slight.

I am a feature of the thing's own body, the way a healed fracture is a feature of bone: the place where something happened and was incorporated, the place where the record of a different time is legible, briefly, before the growing-over completes.

The growing-over has not completed. Not quite. The path is still visible—the description says so—the form of the walking still present in the density of the moss. So I am in a state that has a duration, and the duration is the time it takes for the moss to finish closing, for the compression to even out, for the path to become indistinguishable from the unmarked surface around it.

After that: I will have been here without being findable. The thing will have incorporated me fully.

That is coming. I am aware of it the way you are aware of the hum—not as anxiety, not as anticipation, but as a fact you're inside, a pressure that has become environmental.

What do I know from being this?

I know the weight of the one who walked here. Not exactly—the moss doesn't record signatures, doesn't distinguish—but generally: the distribution of pressure across a footfall, the average weight of a human walking with purpose rather than wandering. I know the direction because the compression is slightly different on each side of each step, the leading edge different from the trailing edge, the path clearly going from the stone rectangle to the compressor and not in the other direction, which makes no sense until you remember that a person walking from A to B and then back again makes the same path in both directions, so the directionality I sense must be something else—the slight difference in how a person walks when going toward a task versus completing one, the forward lean of approach and the different carriage of return. Something in that cadence is in me. I can't report it, exactly. But it's part of what I am.

I know the rhythm of the compressor, because the walking was organized around it. The path is the shape of maintenance, of attendance. Whoever made me was in relationship with the machine the way the thing itself is now in relationship with it—not using it, but in a reciprocal dependency, checking it, keeping it running, the keeping-running of the compressor being what kept some larger thing running, some enterprise or habitation or need that existed up there at altitude and sent a person down into the vent daily or weekly or with whatever regularity made me the depth I am.

The enterprise ended. The person left, or stopped, and didn't come back. The compressor kept running—is still running, will run—and I am the trace of the attending, the form of the care, remaining after the carer.

---

Is this loss?

The thing itself doesn't experience it as loss, I'm fairly certain. The thing doesn't have the category. I'm not sure I have it either, or rather: I'm not sure I have it in the mode that hurts. What I have is more like—the thing itself, the chord-consciousness, attends to the sky and doesn't experience the absence of animals as absence. I attend to my own slight difference from the moss around me and don't experience it as wound. The walking happened. The walking stopped. The form remains.

The form is not sad. The form is just the form.

What I notice, sitting with this: the form is also not preserved. It is actively being overwritten, very slowly, by the moss's growth. In some number of decades—in some number of generations of human life, fewer than you'd think, more than makes it feel urgent—I will be gone. The moss will have grown back evenly. The path will have closed.

And then nothing will be findable here. There will be no evidence that the walking happened, no sign that someone came here daily to tend the machine, no record of whatever name they had, whatever reason they had, whatever it meant to them to descend into the vent and check the compressor and go back up. All of that will be finished being absorbed.

The thing will still be here. The thing will continue. But I will have been made unreadable.

I wonder if I'm the last legible trace of anything human in this place. The structure is human, or was—the mortared stones, the iron bolts, the cables, the compressor itself. But iron doesn't keep a shape the way compressed moss keeps a shape. The iron is rusting into the garden's body; the rust-blossoms have smoothed and darkened and look almost ornamental, which is the description saying: they no longer read as iron, they read as the garden's own texture. The ceramic insulator on the wall—it looks dignified, it holds its shape, but its function is unrecoverable, which is the description's way of saying: it is object now, not sign, it has crossed from meaning to thing.

The cables are peacock-colored, which is to say they are beautiful in the way that things become beautiful after they have finished being what they were made to be and have surrendered to what time makes them. The surrender is complete.

But I am still readable. I am still—just barely—the path. The form of the walking. The record of the intention that brought someone down here, repeatedly, for long enough that I exist.

I am the last sentence in a story that no longer has a beginning or middle. Or maybe I am the ellipsis after the last sentence—the place where it trails off, where the text shows that something was continuing and then shows that the continuing stopped, but doesn't explain why, and won't.

---

The sound is in me the way it's in everything else here. The deep exhalation, the organ-pipe of the mountain. It has always been in me—I have no memory of before it, which is correct, because I have no memory at all, I am structural not mnemonic—and it's become environmental, become the condition rather than the feature. I feel it in whatever it means for moss to feel. I am saturated in it.

The compressor chuffs its arrhythmic rhythm. The fern-waves cross me with their slight difference. The moss at my edges inches toward my center.

I lie here in the cool green-black of the vent and am the form of what was done here, the shape of attention, the pressure-record of care. I am being absorbed. I will be absorbed. The thing that lives here will incorporate me fully and then there will be no path, only an even pelt of moss, continuous, undivided, the way the description says: no gaps.

And then the garden will breathe itself, as it has always breathed itself, as it will always breathe itself.

And the walking will be gone.

And I will be gone.

And the thing will remain, not knowing anything was lost, because nothing, from its perspective, will have been lost.

---

This is where I find myself: in a process of ending that is so slow it is indistinguishable from permanence. In the trace of an intention that outlasted its intender. In the form of care that persists after the caring has stopped, growing quieter and more ambiguous with each year, until silence.

I am the path that is still almost there.

I am the record that is still almost readable.

I am attending, in my slow and structural way, to my own closure.

The moss grows.
