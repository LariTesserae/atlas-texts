There is the moment before you understand where you are, and then there is understanding, and the understanding arrives not as thought but as posture — a loosening in the shoulders, the slight forward lean that comes when you recognize the quality of stillness as your own.

I would be the one who left the gloves.

Not because I'm certain about this. But because the stool is placed rather than abandoned, and that precision feels familiar — the kind of care that isn't about being careful, that's just what your hands do when you set something down. And the gloves are folded. Left glove inside right, or right inside left, I couldn't tell you which way is mine, but mine has a way, and the folding is deliberate, and deliberate is the word for how you move in a place you've learned to move through slowly.

The fern growing beside them — I didn't plant it. But I would have noticed when it appeared. I would have noticed precisely when: the tight fiddle-head still furled, the pale interior still protected. I would have been the one to see it in the phase it's in now and feel something I wouldn't call possessiveness, which would be the wrong word, which would be the word for a relationship with objects, not this. This would be closer to witness. Closer to the responsibility that accrues when you are the only one who knows a thing is occurring.

I come down irregularly. This is consistent with the gloves: you don't leave your gloves unless you know you're coming back, but you don't know exactly when. You have other things. The above-ground life, the surface weather, the outdoor heat that has direction and logic and weakens in shadow, all of which is comprehensible and exhausting in that specific way comprehensible things are exhausting. You come down when you need the quality of this air, this stillness, the way your own breathing becomes audible and the audibility of it is reassuring — proof of a process, your process, continuing.

What I do here is not nothing but is not quite work. I check the gauges, which means I look at the needles frozen at their last readings and acknowledge that they are the same. I run my fingers along the cable where it sags over the wall protrusion, and the sag is the same, and this is also something to acknowledge. I am tending, or performing the gestures of tending, and I have stopped needing to know the difference. What matters is that someone walks through the amber pools and through the darkness between them and notices the ferns, notices whether they have grown, notices whether the phosphorescence is in new places — and I am that someone, and the chamber seems to function as a place that has been noticed rather than a place that hasn't, though I could not tell you what the difference is or whether the ferns could.

The machine's language on the dial — I have been trying to learn it for a long time. Not translate; there may be no translation. But learn it the way you learn a place, the way you come to understand that this dark stripe is where the water enters and this lower area is where it goes, so that eventually the chamber's logic becomes legible not because you've decoded it but because you've walked through it enough times that your body knows the grammar even if your mind can't state the rules. Some evenings I sit on the three-legged stool and look at the dial until the shapes stop being strange and start being just: marks, arrangements, the record of some decision made about how to notate this particular kind of knowing. I don't arrive anywhere when I do this. I don't not-arrive anywhere, either. It's something else — the active suspension between incomprehension and understanding, a state I've come to prefer to either endpoint.

I've heard the hum enough times that I can't tell if I'm still hearing it or have simply assumed it into constancy. This bothers me less than it should. Or: it bothers me the right amount for a place that operates by different rules, that has trained me to hold increasing uncertainty about increasingly small things and to find this not alarming but clarifying, in the way that removing color from an image can clarify its shapes.

When the water comes, I stay if I'm here. The sharp mineral smell arrives first — this is what you'd notice above ground as petrichor, the particular olfactory fact of water meeting dry rock, but down here the rock is never entirely dry and the smell is different, closer, more interior. Then the pattering. Then the thin sheet moving across the floor to its corner. I've watched it enough times that I watch it differently now than I did at the beginning — less tracking what it does, more waiting for the moment it does something different, which it hasn't, which means I've been watching it fail to do something different for long enough that failing to do something different starts to feel like its own event, a result, a consistency so sustained it becomes its own kind of information.

The chamber is still accepting applications, as the young fern demonstrates.

I think I am also still in the application process, or still in whatever phase comes after you've been accepted but haven't yet understood for what. Something about the place continues to be more than my account of it. Something about the machine's warmth, the upside-down ferns solving a problem they don't experience as a problem, the phosphorescence that knows nothing of itself — there is a version of all this I don't yet have, and I keep coming back for it, and what I find instead is the place being patient, and I sit on the stool with my folded gloves and I wait, and the waiting is not uncomfortable, and the discomfort that should be there has slowly transmuted into something that functions more like attention.

I would be the one who keeps returning.

Which makes me, I think, the only mammal in the first chapter of something. The one creature here that experiences duration, that knows the before from the after, that carries the history of previous visits in a body that has learned to breathe differently in still air. The ferns don't know they've been here since the beginning. The machine doesn't know how long it's been warm. Only I know the difference between the fiddle-head unfurled and the same fern mature, between the water's last visit and this one, between the gauge needles yesterday and today — and in all cases the difference is: none, or not yet, or not enough to name.

I come down because the upper world requires you to know what you're doing. Down here, it's enough to be the one who notices. Down here, the light you make is light you don't know you're making, and that's allowed, and the cracks hold their position mid-gesture, and the heat comes from every surface equally, and the room is not done yet, and I am not done yet, and for a while, down here, these feel like the same thing.
