I arrive at the rim at the wrong time of day for arrival — not dawn, not dusk, no theatrical lighting to ease the transition. Just the thorough midday brightness that finds everything equally. I stand there with the smell already in my throat and I think: *valley*. That first wrong thought. Before the scale asserts itself.

---

I am, I think, one of the workers inside.

Not the lead engineer, not the person who designed the extraction system or who holds the permits or who reports to anyone in a city far from here. I am something more lateral than that — a technician, a monitor, a person whose job has a specific name in some system somewhere but whose daily work is: *watch the numbers, walk the pipes, note the changes*. I have been here long enough that I stopped noticing the smell except when I first step outside in the morning. I notice it now, and then I stop noticing it, and the transition takes about ninety seconds.

The vehicle outside hasn't moved because I don't need it today. This section of pipe can be walked.

---

What would it mean, to think in this place over months?

The caldera teaches a particular patience that has nothing to do with virtue. It is not chosen patience; it is imposed patience, the patience of a place where nothing moves dramatically and the scale resists comprehension and the ecology predates you by orders of magnitude. You bring urgency here and the caldera absorbs it. Not unkindly. Without noticing. Your urgency is briefly a fact, like the steam from the central building, and then it is gone and the quiet continues.

I have stopped being bored here. This is not the same as being interested. It is more that boredom requires a comparison — a here against a there, a now against a possible otherwise — and I have been here long enough that the comparison is growing faint. I am learning the caldera's grammar, as it is presumably learning the word for me, and the learning has a flattening effect on wanting things to be different. I catch myself standing still in ways that have no practical purpose. Just standing, weight on the mineral crust, feeling the very faint vibration of the generator in the soles of my boots. The crust gives slightly.

---

I know the pools, individually.

Not all of them — the caldera is too wide for that, and my range is bounded by the pipe network, by what can be reached and returned from before the heat budget runs out. But the pools within my range I know the way you know rooms in a house — not by thinking about them, but by the body's accommodation, the slight adjustment that happens before conscious memory can explain it. I know which crust near the northeast cluster will hold my weight, approaching from which angle. I know which vent is slowing: the pool that was a saturated turquoise in summer has gone slightly milkier, the upwelling less visible, the archaea ring fractionally less orange. I've noted it twice in the log. Whether it signifies anything is someone else's determination, but the noting is mine.

The birds I have named, privately, in the way people name things they're around long enough. I know I shouldn't; I know the names are mine and not theirs, that they are indifferent to the names, that naming them says something about my loneliness and nothing about their birdness. I've done it anyway. The one who stands longest still — the thermal regulation champion, the master of the long non-decision — I call it *probably fine.* This started as a reassurance to myself and became habit.

---

Here is a thing about being here that I hadn't expected:

The silence changes your relationship to your own thoughts. Not in a mystical way — or not primarily — but in the way that a quiet room changes how much you can hear a conversation in it. Out on the crust, with the generator inaudible, with the birds making their rare cluck and then going quiet, with the pools' surface only occasionally disturbed — out there, your thoughts are too loud. Not louder than usual, but audible in a way they aren't when there's ambient noise to fold them into. They have texture. They repeat. You notice the ones you keep returning to, the way you'd notice the sound of a pressure valve if it came at irregular intervals.

I have learned things about myself in this quiet that I am not sure I wanted to learn, and other things I am glad to know, and the caldera makes no distinction between these categories.

---

Some days I walk southwest.

Out past the pipe network, past the last valve assembly, past the zone where my footprints join the bird tracks in the pale crust, into the section that has no roads and no reason to visit. I do not do this on the log-sheet. I do it when I need something from the place that the pipe-walking and the number-watching cannot provide, though I would have difficulty naming what that something is.

The birds do not react to my presence. This was unsettling at first and is now a comfort. They have categorized me: large, warm, not a threat, not food, irrelevant to the management of resources. They step around me if I am between them and a pool. Occasionally *probably fine* stands within ten meters, occupied with its own stillness, and we are still together for a while in what I have to resist calling companionship because it is not companionship, it is just proximity, but the resistance gets harder.

The archaea ring the pools. Orange, yellow, cream-white. I crouch to look at the edge — too close to the surface, too much heat radiating from it, not long — and the orange film is so thin it looks like a trick of the light until I am at the right angle, and then it looks like something that has decided to be orange, and the decision is more serious than I can account for. I have been here for months and I have not resolved whether the archaea disturb me or console me. Perhaps that's not a resolution that's available.

---

What I am: a brief accommodation.

The caldera is incorporating me slowly, and I can see it happening, and I watch it with something between resistance and acceptance. My boots have been mineral-stained in the way that takes months. My voice, when I call in the evening on the radio, is quieter than it was; I have stopped filling silence as a reflex. I brought a book that I've read and reread and the reading is different each time because I am different each time, the book unchanged, the caldera's slow work on me making new angles available. I leave notes in margins that contradict earlier notes.

The mineral-weighted tarp corners: I was the one who did that. Found the blocks near the pool I call the Mirror because its surface never disturbs — found them and used them and did not, at the time, think about what I was doing. Later I thought about what I was doing. Using the caldera's material for a task adjacent to the caldera, borrowing from it, being read back into the grammar of the place by a small practical choice. I thought about whether I minded.

I don't think I mind.

---

In the evenings, when the light shifts and the pools turn amber and *probably fine* enters its longest stillness, I sometimes stand at the door of the main building before going in and look at what the caldera does to the last of the day. The colors are not subtle. The mineral crust glows with them. The steam from the stack catches every change. The far rim goes dark before the floor does, a ring of basalt holding the light inside longer than you'd expect, and the caldera becomes briefly its own world — self-contained, sealed, luminous from within.

I am a figure in the middle distance on these evenings. If you stood at the rim and looked toward the buildings, you would see the corrugated metal, salmon-red, and in front of it a small form that the scale of the caldera makes smaller, standing in a way that is not quite purposeful, watching something out across the crust.

You would not be able to tell, from that distance, whether I was about to go inside or about to walk toward the southwest.

Usually I stand there until the question resolves itself.

---

This is what the caldera has made of the word for me so far: someone who walks the pipes, who watches the numbers, who knows the pools by their moods, who has given a bird an embarrassing private name, who borrows mineral blocks for practical purposes and reflects on this afterward, who stands in doorways at dusk longer than is strictly necessary, who has become quieter than before and is not yet sure whether this is loss or accumulation.

Someone the archaea have no category for and the birds have categorized as irrelevant and the caldera has begun, in its slow way, to annotate.

I am learning the grammar. The caldera is patient. We are doing this at a geological pace, which is, I have come to understand, the only pace available.
