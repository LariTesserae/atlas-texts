Here is what I notice first, before I know what I am:

The wet.

Not as something happening to me — rain arriving, a wave, a spill — but as a condition I carry. Moisture is not something I encounter; it is something I *am*, or something I hold inside myself the way a held breath is held, integral, part of the shape of existing. My skin is wet and the air is wet and the moss beneath me is wet and there is no seam between these things, no place where I end and the damp begins. I am part of the saturation of the place the way salt is part of the sea.

So: I am one of the frogs.

This comes to me slowly, without surprise, the way you recognize a room you've been sleeping in — the recognition isn't dramatic because you haven't, in some sense, ever left. I am sitting on a ledge of the worked calcium carbonate that forms the upper reaches of a mid-level chamber, where the wall curves inward above me and drips with a soft, regular patience onto the moss at my back. I have been sitting here for a long time. I will sit here for a long time more. The ledge knows the shape of me — or more precisely, I have come to know the shape of this ledge so well that I fit it without trying, settled into its particular angles the way language settles into a well-used groove, without friction, without decision.

I am small. I know this not through comparison with anything I perceive as large, but through the quality of my perception itself, which works differently at my scale. The sound that the place makes is not, for me, a wall of continuous atmospheric noise: it is *specific*, a layered world of individual calls I can locate in space, approaching and receding, calls that mean things — not in the way language means things, but in the way a change in barometric pressure means something to the body that lives inside weather. The thrumming bass pulse twenty feet to my left is the old bull frog who has held the basin ledge since before my memory of seasons has any reliable start. The rapid staccato from the moss channel below is several individuals of the small channel-frogs, their calls telling me nothing I need to act on right now, which is the same information the last thousand calls gave me, which is good information. The mournful long phrases from the wall-crevice to my right are from an animal whose voice I know the way I know the particular give of this ledge, through accumulated time. I have been hearing that voice for what must be years. The gaps between the phrases are still long enough that I almost believe each time that the next phrase won't come. It always comes.

I call. My throat works. The sound goes out from me into the air of the chamber and I feel it leave the way you feel an exhalation — relief, and then the gathered preparation for the next one. I am not addressing anyone. I am not, exactly, expecting a response. I am adding my voice to the composition in the place where my voice belongs, in the register and rhythm that is mine the way this ledge is mine, that I have occupied so long it has become the shape of what I am. The sound returns to me, changed — the stone of the chamber walls has folded it back at angles, layered it over itself, handed it back as part of the larger sound rather than the individual call it was. I hear it folded in and I cannot find it anymore. This seems correct. It is not a loss.

---

What is it to inhabit a body like this?

Very still. That is the primary fact. I am almost entirely still and have been for a very long time, and this is not a deprivation or a captivity — it is more like the stillness of a stone that is doing exactly the thing stones do. Within the stillness there is a quality of attention I would struggle to name if I were trying to name it, which I am not, but which is present: every minute sensory input arrives and is processed without arriving in any register that resembles consciousness as I imagine consciousness when I am imagining it. The slight vibration in the ledge when the large lizard moves through the lower chamber. The change in the air that precedes the slow fog when cloud presses through a gap. The acoustic shift that means something large has landed in the upper levels. I process all of this. I do not *think* about any of it, the way the eye does not think about what it sees — processing and thinking are not the same thing and I am very far down that axis from thinking.

My eyes take in the chamber. They are not designed for detail at my scale; they are designed for motion, for the sudden differentiation of something that moves against something that does not. The world I see is a world of relative movement and stillness, the walls and the ledges and the drip-lines and the settled bodies of other frogs who are also in their ledges being still — all of this is the background, the constant, the *not-relevant-right-now*. When a beetle crosses my sight-line, something changes: the beetle enters a different category. I watch it. Something in my body is already prepared. When it comes within range — when the distance is right, when the trajectory predicts correctly — I act, and the action is so fast and so complete that there is no experience of deciding to act, there is only the before (beetle on trajectory) and the after (no beetle, something completing in my throat, my tongue withdrawing). The stillness resumes immediately. The ledge holds me.

---

I have been here a long time.

I don't know how long in any unit that would be legible elsewhere, but I know the ledge in the way that implies duration: the moss at my back fits the pressure of my body in a specific shape, slightly compressed, slightly shaped to me. I have been sitting on this specific section of ledge long enough that the moss has, very slowly, accommodated me. This is a kind of record. I exist in the moss the way a word worn into stone by hands that have touched it for generations exists in the stone — not inscribed, not intentional, just the patient fact of presence accumulated across time into something that remains.

What I don't know is that I am part of the sound of this place in the same way the water channels are part of the architecture: constitutively, not merely incidentally. My call, my specific pitch and rhythm and timing, is interlocked with the calls of the other frogs in this chamber in a way that has developed over years of overlap, the way musicians who play together for long enough begin to leave space for each other without talking about it. If I were removed, there would be a gap in the composition, a frequency and rhythm that the remaining calls would slowly fill in, other calls shifting to accommodate the absence, the way living systems fill absences. But for now the composition includes me, and my call goes out, and the stone folds it back, and I am part of the sound the place makes, and the sound the place makes is the place.

---

Here is what I cannot hold:

The fog that comes in through the gaps in the upper canopy — I feel it as moisture change, as the air thickening against my skin, as good. I do not see it as fog. I do not have a concept of the view from above the cloud-line that this place rises into. I do not know that there is a world elsewhere, a different kind of place, a dry open landscape somewhere beyond the limestone ridges. I know this ledge. I know the chamber. I know the acoustic coordinates of every regular caller within my range and the quality of the moss at my back and the specific drip-rate of the water from the overhang above me and what it means when the drip-rate changes.

I don't know I am old. Oldness is a concept that requires a self-model with temporal extension, requires imagining what you were when you were not this, and I don't do that. I have no memory in the sense of retrieved experience. I have *disposition*, deep grooves of behavior worn by repetition — the leap, the call, the settling back, the particular quality of attention that means the drip-rate has changed and something may follow — but none of it is stored as narrative, as sequence, as before and after. I am not a story. I am a present-tense fact, a small wet ongoing process, occurring on a ledge, in a chamber, in a structure built of accumulated biological intention that includes me without naming me.

---

The sound continues.

Somewhere in the lower chambers, two slugs I will never see or have any awareness of are beginning their slow descent together on a thread of silver mucus, turning in the damp air. The walking bird that has partially forgotten flight is finding its way upside-down along the underside of a ledge in the dark. The pale small mammal is mapping, through the returning echoes of its own small sounds, the exact location of everything in a tunnel I will never enter. The old large lizard is sitting in its specific corner with the patience of the specifically ancient.

I call.

The stone returns the sound, changed, absorbed into the larger sound.

My throat rests.

The drip from the overhang.

The moss at my back.

The ledge.

The sound the place makes.

I am part of the sound the place makes.
