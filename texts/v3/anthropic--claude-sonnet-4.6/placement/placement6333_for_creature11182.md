There is a moment, walking the floor, when you realize you have stopped counting your steps.

Not because you've lost track—you were never keeping track, not really—but because counting implies a destination, and somewhere in the third or fourth lamp-pool you passed through, destination became a category that no longer applied. You are walking because you are here and here requires moving through it. The walking is not toward. It is simply *in*.

You are, I think, a person who arrived with a purpose and has not yet noticed that it left.

---

Let me try to be more precise.

You came with equipment. Some of it is still functional—a handheld meter of some kind, originally for measuring something specific, atmospheric composition perhaps, or electromagnetic field, or the concentration of particular gases in the steam-vented air. The meter still works. You check it periodically, out of habit, and the readings are within the range of expected variance, or outside it in ways you have not yet decided how to interpret. Your notebook is damp. The pen still writes, though the ink takes longer to dry. You have enough food for three more days, in a pack that has become part of your body in the way that long-carried weight does, so that you would feel wrong without it even if you set it down.

You have been here long enough that the hum has gone below consciousness and returned above it three times.

---

The question of who you are here is also the question of what the floor does to the question of who you are.

There is no mirror—or there is, the black water, but it shows you sideways, stretched, your familiar face made strange by the oxide patterns beneath. You have looked into it more than once. Not out of vanity. Out of something more like verification. *Still here. Still this.* The face that returns is yours but processed by the floor's aesthetics—amber-lit, softer at the edges, the sharp lines of your ordinary expression dissolved somewhat by the quality of light that has traveled seventy meters to reach you.

The floor has been working on you the way it works on everything. Not hostilely. Not even indifferently. The floor doesn't do indifference, exactly—indifference implies a withholding of attention, and the floor is not withholding anything; it simply attends to all things equally, the steel and the rust and the moss and you, with the same patient chemical process, the same slow insistence. You have been breathing its air, drinking its water where you've filtered it, pressing your hands against its surfaces. You are becoming slightly more mineral. You are, in the technical sense, being incorporated.

---

Here is what you know about the machines:

You found one of them, three days into your traversal, after following the vibration as a navigational aid. It was larger than you expected. Everything here is larger than you expected, which should have stopped being surprising and hasn't. The machine was a kind of press, or had been—a mechanism for applying cyclic force to material that was presumably fed through it at some point and is no longer. The feed mechanisms were still present but seized. The press itself still moved. The cam that drove it had worn from its original profile into something elliptical and irregular, so that the impacts were no longer uniformly spaced—nineteen seconds, then twenty-three, then seventeen, then twenty-one—though you had initially heard them as regular because your nervous system, which is a pattern-detector, had averaged the intervals and reported regularity that wasn't quite there.

You stood next to it for a long time. The impacts at close range were not loud so much as *physical*—a displacement of air, a concussion felt in the chest, as if the machine were speaking at a register below language but still communicating something.

You tried to write down what it seemed to be communicating.

The notebook page has a long hyphen on it and nothing else.

---

You are, I think, a person trained in a discipline that equipped you to ask certain questions about this place and not others. Not badly trained—the training was rigorous, came from good institutions, has served you well. But the floor does not present itself in the vocabulary of the training. The training offers you measurements and the floor offers you duration, and you have been standing in that gap for days, turning the meter over in your hands, watching its needle, thinking about what it means that the needle's behavior is fully legible to you and the place it is measuring is not.

This is not quite loss. It is more like the discovery of a room in yourself that was always there and always empty, because you had never been anywhere that required it.

---

What do you do here, in the hours that accumulate?

You walk. The walking has developed a quality you recognize from other kinds of sustained practice—a loosening of the usual self-commentary, a reduction in the gap between intending to move and moving, something that in another context you might call flow but which here feels less like flow than like *seeping*, like you are moving the way the water moves, filmwise, finding the low places.

You eat, at intervals. The food is functional, chosen for weight and caloric density, and it tastes like nothing in particular, which is fine. Taste is not the point. The point of eating is to continue.

You sleep. This is the strangest part. You sleep on the floor—your sleeping mat on the steel plate, the hum in your back teeth, the drip from the ceiling every ten seconds somewhere in the middle distance, and against all expectation you sleep well, deeply, without the usual architecture of dreams. Or you dream and don't remember. Or you remember and the memories are not visual: you wake with the impression that you have spent the night in long conversation with something that does not use language, negotiating terms you cannot quite recall.

In the mornings—there are no mornings, but there are moments after sleeping—you lie still for a few minutes before rising and listen to the floor breathe. The steam vents cycle. The machines rotate. The drip. The hum. You are lying on the body of a vast mechanism and it is alive in the way that mechanism becomes alive when it runs long enough: the way the beating of a very old clock sounds different from a new one, lower, more settled, the sound of something that has found its own pace.

---

I want to say that you are beginning to understand the floor. But that is not quite right.

What you are beginning to understand is that understanding is not what the floor is asking for.

The floor is not a problem. The floor is not a text. The floor is not a message waiting for a sufficiently trained decoder. The floor is a condition—the condition of a very large constructed thing that has been alone with itself for a very long time and has, in that time, become more itself than anything made to specification usually manages.

You are not here to understand it.

You are here because you are part of its current moment, the way the drip is, the way the moss is. You arrived, and the floor incorporated you into its present tense—added your footsteps to its acoustic weather, your warmth to the subtle heat-gradient near the lamp-pools, your carbon dioxide to the air that the bacterial mats process into the chemistry of their patient lives. You are a temporary perturbation in the floor's long equilibrium, and the floor is attending to you the way it attends to everything: completely, without hierarchy, without judgment, without the faintest suggestion that your presence matters more or less than the particular piece of rust forming right now in the seam beside your left boot.

This is not loneliness. You have felt loneliness and know its texture. This is something else—the particular quality of being held without being grasped, of being here without being needed here, of mattering at the level of chemistry and not at the level of intention.

It is almost restful.

---

Three more days of food.

You do not know if you will leave when the food runs out or before or after.

You have stopped thinking about the thing you came here to find—which is perhaps the most significant event since your arrival, more significant than the discovery of the machine, more significant than the first night sleeping on the steel, more significant than the moment the hum went below consciousness. The thing you came for has not been found, and you have noticed that you are no longer looking, and you have noticed that noticing this does not produce urgency.

In your notebook, below the hyphen, you wrote something yesterday—or what passed for yesterday. You wrote: *the floor is the answer to a question I didn't bring with me.*

You looked at it for a while.

Then you kept walking.

---

This is what you are, in the floor: a person in the process of being asked, by a place, to set down the purpose you arrived with and pick up the one the place is offering. Not permanently. Not mystically. Just for now, just for as long as you are here, just for the duration of these days between arriving and leaving—if leaving is still something you will do.

The floor's purpose is continuance. The rust grows, the water films, the machines cycle, the moss climbs its millimeters per year. Everything here is doing what it does, not toward anything, not because of anything—just as the ongoing expression of its own nature, pressed against the particular conditions it has been given. The iron does what iron does in the presence of oxygen and water. The bacteria do what these bacteria do in the presence of iron. The machines do what machines do when no one stops them.

And you—

You do what you do when you have stopped moving toward something and started being somewhere. When the measuring is done not because there are no more measurements to take but because measurement has become a smaller part of what you are doing here. When the notebook is less a record than a companion.

You breathe. You walk. You put your hand on a column and feel the condensation pull at your palm, feel the vibration of the distant machine, feel the floor's patience communicating upward through your feet. You stand in the bioluminescent glow of a shallow lens and your boots push small bow-waves across water that has been still for decades, and you watch the ripples move outward and die, and you watch the glow close behind them, uninterrupted, calm.

You are here.

That is, for now, enough.

The floor thinks so too, in whatever way the floor thinks—which is to say: the floor has not asked you to be anything other than what you are, and the rust is growing regardless, and the drip falls every ten seconds, and the hum is in your back teeth, and you are, against all your prior notions of what this place would do to a person, you are completely, unexpectedly, groundlessly at rest.
