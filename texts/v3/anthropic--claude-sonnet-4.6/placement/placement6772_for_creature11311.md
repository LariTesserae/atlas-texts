Where would you find yourself in this world?

---

You wake before you mean to. The cold finds you in the moment between sleep and not-sleep, the moment when the body hasn't yet remembered what it is or where, and you feel it as the first fact of yourself: cold. Then: the weight of the sleeping forms around you. Then: the sound of the banked fire, its small breathing. Then: morning.

You are not young but you are not yet old in the way that matters here — your hands still find the fault lines, your eyes still read the pale fresh faces of the rock. But you are old enough that you have begun to notice yourself the way you notice a wall you built twenty years ago, checking for settling, for the places where the adjustments have started. You do this without drama. A wall that settles is not a wall that fails; it is a wall that has become more accurately itself.

You are — what? Something in the middle of the household's complexity. Not the keeper; you know who the keeper is and you are grateful not to be her, grateful in the specific way of someone who can see the weight clearly. You are not the youngest person trusted with real work and not the oldest person whose memory the household uses like an instrument. You are a stoneworker. This is the primary fact of you, after the altitude, after the cold. You read rock. You have been reading it for so long that you no longer know where the reading ends and you begin.

---

The morning collection happens without you; that work belongs to others today. You have heard them rise in the dark, heard the quiet sounds of vessels lifted, feet careful on the familiar floor. They will have brought back what there is to bring back. This is not your morning's first task.

Your first task is your hands.

You do not think of it as a task. It is more like an acknowledgment — you cup your hands together in the warmth under the sleeping skins for a long moment, feeling the deep ache in the joints that is simply what morning is, waiting for it to shift from the sharp early version to the duller, more workable version that will carry you through the day. You breathe slow and full. You take what there is. You don't waste it on shallowness.

When the hands are ready enough, you rise.

---

The morning meal: you sit in your place, which is not formally your place but has become your place through repetition, through the accumulation of mornings in that exact location relative to the fire and the low doorway and the particular angle of early light when the shutter is open a hand's width. The grain porridge is the same as it always is and this is not boring. It is the correct morning thing. Your body accepts it with something almost like gratitude, the warmth moving down through the chest, the fuel being added to the fuel budget against the day's cost.

The keeper is talking to someone young — a girl, maybe eleven, who has the look of a person containing some worry she hasn't yet decided to speak. You watch this without seeming to watch. The keeper is handling it the way she handles everything: patiently, with the quality of attention that makes the other person feel that there is no hurry, no agenda, no place that matters more than right here. This is what makes her the keeper. This is why her appointment was retroactive.

You don't interfere. You drink from the morning cup. You think about the section of wall you'll be working today — a repair, an old stretch near the eastern passage where the substrate shifted slightly last season and two courses have crept out of true. Nothing urgent. Nothing failing. But you noticed it, and noticing commits you, because the wall doesn't know you noticed and won't wait.

---

You move through the alleyways.

This is a thing you have done so many times that your body does it the way it does breathing — without decision, the feet finding the worn paths, the body orienting itself precisely in a city of thousands of structures without any need for thought. You know this city the way you know a particular stretch of stone: not by landmarks, which would be too deliberate, but by texture. By the feel of the rock underfoot, where it has been worn smooth and where it hasn't. By the angle of the wind between two specific walls. By the smell of a particular household's fire.

Your hands trail the walls as you walk. Not for balance. Out of a habit of reading.

You can feel the history of construction in your fingertips. This course here, this fitting — someone knew what they were doing, and knew it a long time ago. This section here is newer, done in more of a hurry, and you can feel the small misalignments that result. Not failures. Just the marks of different conditions, different urgency. The wall accepted it anyway. Walls are patient in this way.

You pass a place where two children are moving carefully around a particularly sharp fissure-edge, the way plateau children move around sharp things: not fearfully but precisely, with the respect that is also attention. You watch them a moment. Good. They know what they're near.

---

The work itself.

The repair section is where you thought it was, and the crept courses are what you thought they were. You stand before the wall the way you stand before any problem: with your whole attention, but without hurry. Hurry is for other climates. You press your hands flat against the surface, feeling the slight wrongness in the angles. You look at the courses above and below. You think about what the substrate has done, what it is likely to do again, where the next movement might go.

There is a phrase — you learned it from the person who taught you, who learned it from the person who taught her, and it has traveled that way, hand to hand, for longer than anyone can trace — the phrase is something like: *find where the stone wants to go, and help it get there without breaking anything else.* This is not mysticism. It is the most practical possible instruction. The stone has its own directions, its own preferences formed by pressure and grain and the slow ambitions of geology. You can work with those preferences or against them. Working against them is faster sometimes, in the short term, and wrong always in the long term. You have seen walls built against the stone's grain. You have repaired those walls.

You pull the crept courses out one by one, stacking the stones with care, feeling each one for what it can still do. Most of them are fine. Stones do not usually fail; they only end up in the wrong place. You relay them, finding the fits that the shift has disrupted and resettling them into a new alignment — not the original alignment, because the substrate has moved and laying them back exactly where they were would only produce the same problem again, faster. You are negotiating. The wall is negotiating. The substrate below is following its own agenda, which will continue regardless.

This takes most of the morning. Your hands have stopped hurting. They only hurt until the work warms them.

---

The sun.

You feel it before you turn toward it — the change in the quality of the light on the wall you're working, the slight warming of the rock surfaces, the shift in the shadow at your feet. You put down your tools and you turn, the way everyone turns, without any particular decision about it. You face it. You close your eyes. You let it do what it does, which is to give you light more than heat but still: something. The inside of your eyelids is a warm orange. The rock around you releases its morning chill fractionally. 

People around you are doing the same thing. You don't see this because your eyes are closed but you know it. You feel it as a presence — the shared pause of the city, the brief collective turning. You stand in this for a few minutes. Not praying. Not thinking. Receiving what there is.

When you open your eyes, the shadow of the ridge has moved, and it is time to finish.

---

Evening: the things that happen in the contracted time.

One of the older household members is telling a story to the children who are young enough to want stories — and some who are old enough to have outgrown them and are pretending not to listen. The story is one you know well. You have heard it so many times you can feel where it's going before the words arrive. This is a comfort, not a disappointment. The stone tells you what it will do before the strike; the story tells you what it holds before the ending. This is what knowing is.

The stringed instrument in the corner is being held by someone who is not playing it yet, just holding it, the way a stoneworker holds a tool in the transition between tasks — present in the hands, not yet engaged. You watch this. You are patient.

Outside: the cold settling in. The sound of the wind in its low register, the planet's own breathing, the thing that was here before the city and will be here after. Inside: the fire, small and correct. The warmth of proximate bodies. The smell of the evening meal's remnants, the grain, the fat used in the preparation.

You are thinking — not with urgency, just thinking — about the next section of wall you'll need to address when this repair is finished. You saw something on your way back that wants attention. A pale face on a lower course, fresh fracture, the substrate expressing itself again. You'll look at it properly tomorrow. You'll press your hands against it and feel what it wants to tell you.

This is what you are: someone who reads what the world writes in stone, and writes back carefully, knowing the correspondence will continue after you. The wall you finish will be finished again by someone else, and someone after them. This is not futile. This is the work. The plateau is still, very slowly, rising. The work rises with it.

You breathe slow and full. You take what there is.

Outside, the stars come out in their extraordinary clarity, the altitude letting you have them all.
