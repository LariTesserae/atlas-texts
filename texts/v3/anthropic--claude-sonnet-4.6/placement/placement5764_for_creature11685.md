There is a question before the question of who I am, and it is this: how long have I been here?

Not in the sense of years. In the sense of layers. The mineral pale that coats everything — I have some of that on me. Not the white of someone who arrived recently and stood too long in the bitter air, but something worked into the grain of the skin itself, into the small lines at the knuckles, into the hair where it has grown over many seasons of this particular atmosphere. The place has been exhaling into me for long enough that I carry some of its residue. But I am not yet what the Sleepers-in-Stone are. The distinction between my stillness and the stillness of the place is still findable, still legible to someone patient enough to look.

I think I am somewhere between the Counting and the tending. Or I have been both at different periods, and the periods were long enough that I no longer experience them as different roles but as different modes of the same attention. In the mornings — or what passes for morning, the slight change in the canopy's warmth — I move. I drift through the town with no particular destination because destination would organize the movement in a way that misses the point. What I am doing is receiving. The body has become, over time, a sufficiently calibrated instrument that it registers things before I have words for them: a moss colony that was contracting at one edge is now contracting at two, which means something is shifting in the system's current negotiation, though what exactly is what I am here to figure out or to hold without figuring out, depending on what the thing turns out to require.

I stop often. This is not hesitation. Hesitation implies a motion interrupted; what I do is closer to a motion that has found its completion in a particular point in space. Against a stone wall in the second courtyard from the center, for instance, because the angle of the canopy there produces a quality of light this morning that is very slightly different from yesterday, and different in a direction — cooler, less green, the suggestion of something above the canopy having shifted its angle — that I want to stay with for a while.

My hands are the usual hands of someone who has spent decades in contact with surfaces. I do not think about them much. They know things my mind would need time to articulate.

---

What I am not: I am not young. I am not, despite what the children sometimes seem to think, very old. I am in a period of life that elsewhere might be called middle age, but here does not have a name because the pace of the place makes such divisions less operational. I have gone through phases. I was, as a child, the usual kind — too fast, too loud, the one who disturbed a moss colony and watched it be quietly remedied afterward without recrimination, which taught me more about the town's nature than any instruction could have. I spent a period — a decade, perhaps more — sitting in the hollow shrub-clusters even when I was too large for the small ones, having to fold myself into the cup of the arrangement uncomfortably, attending to the inward focus the hollow teaches. I sat in them long enough to learn what I was meant to learn, and then I stopped needing them.

I have not left. Some in my generation did. I am aware of their absence the way the town is aware of water — as a presence of what was here, as a quality in the air. I hold them in my count without grief exactly, but with the kind of sustained attention that means they are not missing from me, they are present in me as a different kind of distance.

---

In the afternoon, if the flat-bodied canopy creature has passed overhead recently — and I will know it has by the pattern of swaying in the third and fourth courtyards, by the particular agitation of the shrub-clusters that sometimes follows, an almost-perceptible increase in their branch-adjustments — I will sit at the edge of the central courtyard for a long time. Not counting, exactly. Holding.

There is a colony of bone-colored moss at the base of the building behind me. I know its current boundary down to the millimeter, not because I measured it recently but because I have been watching it for long enough that its position is part of my body's knowledge, the way the dimensions of a room you have slept in become part of you and you can navigate it without light. The colony has been approaching a particular threshold for three years. I am in conversation with that approach. The conversation has no words and no urgency and may not conclude in my lifetime, and this is fine. This is, in fact, the correct pace for this kind of conversation.

What I am doing when I sit here, when I drift through the morning, when I press my palm to a surface and attend to what passes through it — I do not have a word for it that does not make it sound like more or less than it is. It is not devotion. It is not science. It is the practice of someone who has spent a long time learning to be where they are at the speed the place requires, and who has not yet reached the far end of that practice, and who does not experience the not-yet-reaching as lack.

The plateau continues its young work.

I am part of that work, and I am also watching it, and the watching and the being-part-of are the same activity at a level of resolution the Counters have been trying to articulate for generations.

---

There is one more thing.

In the evenings sometimes — not every evening, only when something has been observed that is ready to be offered — I sit in the central courtyard where others have gathered and say things. The things I say are not long. I learned the right length a long time ago, learned it from sitting with people who had learned it before me, whose pauses and brevities were themselves a teaching in the form of example. I offer what I have received. The others receive what they receive. A slight adjustment of orientation. A fold toward something new.

Afterward I go back to the wall where I sleep, where the stone is at the temperature it always is at this hour, which is a temperature I stopped noticing many years ago. I press my back against it. The moss at the base of the building continues its patient conversation with the threshold. Above the canopy, something four-limbed and slow moves through the dark, its weight distributed across the branches it has known longer than I have been here.

I am the person who stays very still for a long time and is still a person.

I am the mineral residue of attention, accumulated on the surface of years.

I am, in the vocabulary the place itself offers, a Counter who has begun to find the distinction between counting and being counted growing very thin.

This is, I think, the right place for me to be in my life. This is the plateau that fits the pace I arrived at, or arrived at me. Both, at different edges, imperceptibly slowly. The answer, eventually, seems to be: both.
