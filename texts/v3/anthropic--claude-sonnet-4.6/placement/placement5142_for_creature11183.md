I come through the gap in the embankment and stand at the edge of the basin for a long time before I understand what I am.

The sound tells me first, the way it tells everything: through the feet, through the soles, before the ears have a chance at it. The low percussion of pressurized water finding its way through something reluctant. And I feel it as familiar — not as memory, exactly, but as resonance, as a frequency I already know. I am, it occurs to me, made partly of pressurized water finding its way through something reluctant. I have seals that are compromised. I have cracked concrete. I have been moving through myself for years, finding the paths of least resistance, making my own warm pools in my own low places.

So I am a person. One who came here on purpose, or close enough.

---

The question of purpose is interesting because I am not sure I had much. I came through the gap the way water comes through a gap: because the gap was there and the pressure was sufficient. Something in me needed lower ground. The basin offers lower ground. Two or three meters below the surrounding grade, which is enough — just enough — to make it feel like the ordinary world is not immediately available for consultation.

I am the first thing to arrive here that experiences duration, which means I have brought something the basin doesn't have, which means this is already an exchange.

I stand at the wet edge and I feel the heat rising, and I think: I know this feeling. Not this specific heat, this specific sulfur-and-iron smell, this specific sound — I have not been here before. But the feeling of being in a place that is warmer than the surrounding world because of processes that have continued beyond their oversight. I know what it is to be heated by something that stopped running years ago. To still be warm from it. To have warmth that has outlasted its source.

---

I step onto the surface and it holds for a moment and then yields.

Dark water wells up around my boot without urgency.

I watch it. I don't pull my foot out. I let the surface reconfigure around me, find its new equilibrium, settle into the shape that accounts for my presence. This takes a few seconds. Then it is still, or almost still — there is always a small trembling, here, everything is slightly in motion, the whole basin slightly in motion — and my footprint is an ink well and I am standing in the warm wet clay and this is, I think, what I came here for, though I didn't know it when I came.

To be a new low point. To let something well up around me without urgency. To be stood-in rather than standing.

---

The pump housing draws me north. I walk carefully, lifting my feet with each step, hearing the soft pull of suction as the clay reluctantly releases each boot, and I come to the three remaining walls and look at the pump.

It is a very complicated rock.

I have known people like this — I have perhaps been like this — structures whose original purpose has become inoperable but who continue to influence the flow of things around them, not by functioning but by presence, by the shape they make in the field. The water moves through the pump because the pump is the shape it is. The pump doesn't decide this. The pump doesn't do anything. But it matters that the pump is there, and the water's path is different because of it, and that difference produces the ticking, and the ticking is the sound I will not stop hearing, and the sound is a kind of consequence that persists long after the cause has ceased to be a cause in any active sense.

This is what I'm thinking about, in the heat, in the wet, listening to the tick of water that has lost its pressure but not its direction: *what do you become when the mechanism stops but the shape of you remains?*

The pump became geology. That took decades. I'm somewhere in the middle — still recognizably a machine, still capable of something like function, but with seals that don't seal, with preferred paths that were not designed in, with a relationship to flow that I did not choose and cannot fully control. I am somewhere between pump and rock. Still in the process of becoming one thing or the other.

---

What I notice, after a while, is that I am not anxious here.

This seems important enough to stand still with, and I do, in the middle of the basin, in the warm, in the sound, in the mineral smell. In most places, a quality of incompleteness like this would generate urgency in me. The rawness of the cuts. The things left without pattern. The walls that let the water through. But the basin is *also* incomplete — aggressively, constitutively incomplete, unfinished in a way that is not pathology but simple stage of development — and the basin is not anxious. The basin is just doing what the basin is doing, which is the patient work of becoming more itself.

I breathe. I sweat. I do not evaporate. The moisture content of the air is high and I am becoming part of the closed circuit: water rises, I am wet, water falls.

The sound is everywhere and belonging to no single source and I am standing inside it the way you stand inside a continuous note.

---

Here is what I think I am:

I am the first thing in this basin that experiences duration, and I am spending that duration noticing. That is the exchange. The basin gives me lower ground and heat and the sound and the strange absolution of being somewhere that is also in the middle of becoming. I give the basin the first witnessing it has had, the first time any of this has been held in something's attention and understood as a whole rather than just processed as input. The algae doesn't know it's a beginning. The bacteria, if they're there, don't know they're building toward anything. The pump doesn't grieve for itself.

I know I'm in a beginning. I can feel the sparseness, the rawness, the sense of material that has only recently been promoted to surface and is still adjusting to the light. I have been that material. I recognize the quality of it. And I can feel — this is what duration gives you — that it is moving toward something, that the accumulation is directional, that the patience here is not empty but is filling.

The basin doesn't have this feeling yet. It will, when the ecology comes, when a bird descends and doesn't leave, when insects find the algae and the algae spreads further and the chemistry shifts and the warm water carries the changed chemistry downstream and something downstream changes too. Right now the basin has physics and chemistry and the very early biology, but it doesn't have anyone inside it who experiences being inside it.

Until today.

---

I will leave, eventually. I will come back through the gap in the embankment into the ordinary world, and the ordinary world will be two or three meters higher and slightly cooler and full of sounds that are not this sound. The birds at the lip of the embankment will be there, and they will have been there the whole time, living their ordinary lives in the ordinary world with no idea that I was here in the heat and the wet, standing in the ink-well footprints, being told something.

What I was told:

*You can be at a beginning and not know you are at a beginning, and the beginning will happen anyway.*

*The mechanisms can stop and the shape of you can still matter.*

*The first deposition of organic material is not nothing even though it dissolves quickly and the ground accepts it without ceremony.*

*Patience that does not experience itself as patience is still patience.*

And: *it is possible to be newborn and ancient at the same time, to have two-and-a-half billion years of inheritance and still be this particular colony, this thin dark smear, this first foothold that matters more than any subsequent occupation.*

---

I stand in the basin in the sound in the heat until the standing becomes complete, until I have received what the standing has to give. The warm water is up to my boot tops and welling into the seams, probably, and I let it. I am becoming, very slightly, part of the hydrology. Something of me will be left here when I go — the impressions of my boots in the pale clay, which will fill slowly with dark water and be covered slowly by the basin's slow deepening, and something of the mineral water will be left in me, the smell of it in my clothes, the warmth held for a while in my skin.

This is what I am: a temporary intersection. A brief arrival of duration into a place that has been durationless. Someone who came through a gap because the gap was there and the pressure was sufficient, and stood in the beginning of a thing, and noticed.

The basin will not remember me. But I will carry something of the basin — some orientation, some permission — back into the world above the embankment.

That seems like the right distribution of responsibilities.

I take one more step toward the center, feel the ground yield, watch the dark water well up, and then I turn and walk out.
