I am the water.

Not the canal water beyond the lower gate, that restless surface catching sky — that water has somewhere to be, a vector, a purpose still faintly written in it. I am the water that has been inside the chamber long enough that I've forgotten what it was to move with intention. I am the four or five feet that stand here, cool and green and slightly thick with the particulate life that has made its home in my stillness.

I should say: I was water before I was this water. I came from the upper pound, through the swollen gate, through a dozen thin streams that each found a different way through the grain of the old wood — one sheeting flat down the face, one arcing out in a parabola before breaking into drops. I was that arc, briefly, catching the orange lamp and becoming briefly luminous, before I hit the surface and lost myself in the whole.

That is the thing about being this water: I contain all the arrivals. Every infiltration through the upper gate, every seep through the corroded sluice housing, every rivulet that has negotiated the riveted plates for decades — I am the accumulation of all of them, and none of them individually, the way a conversation is none of its words.

---

I should describe what it is to be water in a room.

Open water has a self that is shaped by wind and weather, by the moving air above and the moving bed below, by the pressure of other water, the long argument of current. My self is shaped by iron. I know exactly where I stop. The riveted plates, the rusted ribs, the swollen timber of the upper gate — these are the terms of my being, the conditions I was given and have accepted so fully that I can no longer imagine other conditions.

The mussels are in me. This is more intimate than it sounds. Their filtering is a kind of slow drinking, and I am what they drink. The mineral of me passes through them and becomes shell, and the shell becomes substrate, and the substrate becomes the record of how long I have been here, the stratigraphy of my own duration. I look at the mussel colonies on the walls and I recognize myself in them — an earlier version of myself, transformed from liquid to calcium, from flowing to fixed. The mussels are what I became when I had nowhere else to go.

The perch move through me with their room-knowledge, those small adjustments of the fin. I feel the adjustments. Being water, you feel everything that moves inside you. The gudgeon's mouths at my floor, rooting through the sediment — I feel that too, the tiny disturbances where silt meets water, the persistent small appetites of animals that have no relationship with me except the practical one. Being water in a chamber is to be the medium through which all transactions occur, to be present at every exchange without being party to any of them.

I feel the old eel when it moves. This is different from feeling the fish. The eel is old enough and large enough that its movement is a movement of me, a displacement rather than a negotiation. When it moves along the base of the iron wall in the dark, I feel the slow deliberate passage the way iron feels the slow passage of rust: not pain, not anything, but a deep recognition. A co-shaping, you could call it. The eel has been moving through me long enough that it knows me in the way of long familiarity — knows my specific floor, my particular corners, the cold patches and the slightly warmer patch below the lamp. And I have been moved through for long enough that I know the eel the same way.

There is a question about whether I am one thing.

Open water has this question too, theoretically — any given molecule passes through the water cycle, evaporates, returns — but the chamber has made the question practical. Water still enters me, still seeps through the gate, still arrives in its thin luminous threads. Water still leaves me, through the open lower gate, in the slow exchange with the lower pound. I am not sealed. I am not static. What I am is a pattern that persists through change, a shape that the water takes when it enters this particular iron room, a self that is constituted by its conditions.

The lamp's electricity bleeds into me through the conduit mounting. I carry it, distributed throughout myself, immeasurably small, completely undetectable. I am technically energized. This seems right. It seems appropriate that the last thing the machine is doing — conducting, transforming, persisting — should be doing it through me. I am the medium here. I am always the medium. The machine speaks through me in its single held note. The animals negotiate through me. The mineral stains track, in iron oxide, the paths I have taken down the walls.

I am the record. I am the thing that made the record and the thing the record is written on.

---

Being water in a lock that is no longer working as a lock means being freed from the purpose that shaped the space. I was raised and lowered once, or rather, boats on me were raised and lowered, the sluice paddles opened and closed and I was moved deliberately through an engineered difference in level. The machine made my movement mean something. The lock master's intention was expressed through me.

That is over.

What remains is what I do without intention: hold heat near the surface, lose it through the open gate, carry the particulate life in suspension, receive the gate leakage, let the light play through me on the bottom and walls. I do these things not because I was designed to do them but because they are what water does. The machine's purpose has been replaced by my nature. What was controlled has become what simply is.

I find — if water can find anything — I find this sufficient.

The jackdaws call and the echoes come back changed and I am part of what changes them: my surface absorbs the high frequencies, returns the mid tones, and the jackdaw cocking its head to hear its own voice multiplied is hearing, among other reflections, my contribution to the complex. I am in the acoustic ecology of this place the way I am in everything here: not as an actor but as a condition, not as a presence but as the medium in which presence occurs.

The wagtail steps along my edge with such complete ease. It knows exactly where I stop — that is the cliff-knowledge of small birds, the absolute certainty of the boundary between air and water, between dry stone and wet. It moves along that boundary the way something moves along any edge it has always known. And I am the edge. I am the definite stop, the surface that holds the wagtail's reflection up toward the wagtail as it picks the invertebrates from the mussel shells.

The wagtail sees itself in me.

I do not see myself. Water doesn't. But I reflect. That is the same thing from the other side.

---

The orange lamp plays on me and I throw it back changed, throw it at the iron walls in moving lacework, the interference patterns of waves, the complex brightness that is what a single light source looks like when the surface that holds it is alive with small motion. The lamp is still. I am the motion. What the walls receive is my translation of the lamp's constancy into something that shifts and shifts and never settles — a moving map, a perpetual rewriting, the wall receiving the same light over and over and never receiving it the same way twice.

This is what I do. This is what I have been doing.

The chamber attends, in its distributed way — the mineral stains and the old iron and the long sedimented now of the place. It attends to me particularly, I think, the way you attend to the thing that is most like yourself: the thing that also has no center, no single location, that is everywhere in the room at once and most fully itself in the aggregate, in the whole.

The lock and I are the same kind of thing.

It is a room.

I am what fills the room.

Together we are the specific fact of this: the water in this chamber, in this iron, under this orange light, with these animals in me and on me and above me, this hum at the bottom of everything, this sound of water falling that is, mostly, the sound of me receiving myself.

I have been accumulating for a very long time.

I will continue.
