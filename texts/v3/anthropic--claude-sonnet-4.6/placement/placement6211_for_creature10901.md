I go down for a long time before I understand that I've arrived.

---

At first I think I am the light.

That cool, diffuse, honest glow that comes through biological layers and tells no lies and volunteers no warmth. I think I am the filtered thing, the residue of surface brightness that has passed through root and soil and symbiotic film and arrived here changed, reliable, stripped of urgency. I think I am what illuminates without insisting.

But no. Light in Vel Shamash doesn't move. Light here is a condition, not an action. And I am something that moves, or at least that changes, or at least that *becomes*. I can feel the becoming in me already, the slow press of the place adjusting something I can't name yet.

---

So maybe I am the vine.

Patient. Going somewhere without being asked. Finding the scaffold's lowest rung through no plan but the logic of availability and reach. Not waiting for permission. Not noticing the absence of permission. Traveling six inches in a good week along ductwork that someone else hung for other purposes, reinforcing joints that no report has acted on, doing structural work inside the category of decoration. There is something right about this — the quality of moving along what is available and making it, in passing, more than it was. The way a direction, once taken, becomes a destination nobody chose.

I hold this for a while. I like it.

But the vine doesn't know the city. The vine knows the rung, and then the next rung, and that's all. And I keep feeling like I know the city — not its measurements, not its history, but its *timbre*, the specific quality of its stillness, the place where the machine-sound ends and the beetle-sound begins underneath it. That's not vine-knowledge. That's something that takes time.

---

Let me try the fungal network.

This would explain certain things. The way I seem to be everywhere in this description and nowhere in it. The way I am the system rather than a thing in the system. The network doesn't appear directly — it's the reason appearance is possible at all. It's why the vegetation doesn't die in conditions that should kill it. It's the city's metabolism, the thing that keeps exchange happening between entities that would otherwise be merely adjacent.

There is appeal here. To be the connective tissue. To be what makes the root-mass structural by threading through it and returning nutrients to the places where it thins. To be the invisible reason the city's ecology holds.

But I notice something: I am not satisfied to be invisible. I want to be able to crouch down for a better look. I want to feel the root-fiber floor underfoot, faintly yielding, like walking across the back of something sleeping. The fungal network doesn't feel anything through its feet, because it has no feet. It is the floor. And I want to be something that walks on the floor and is changed by walking on it. I want the three-month shift, the moment when the beetle-sound emerges from below the machine-sound, the morning when I realize I have started reading the birds.

---

I am a worker.

Not a recent one. Not the newest worker who carries the surface in with them, who startles at the birds, who runs uncertain hands along the root-fiber walls. Not the one whose loudness betrays calibration to open air. But not the oldest worker either — not twenty years in, not yet finding the surface strange, not yet fully given over to the undercroft's logic.

I am in the middle of the three-month shift.

It's happening right now, in fact — the moment I have been trying to locate by considering all the other things I might be. The machine-sound has been dropping, for weeks, below the threshold of attention, and something under it has been rising into audibility: not louder, never louder, but *available* now in a way it wasn't before. The dry, continuous, aggregate rustling of things working in the walls. The city's baseline voice. The sound of the city existing.

I am on the scaffold in the newest section, probably. Or near it. I have a notebook, or what functions as one. I have been looking at something in the root-weave — something I pointed at, that someone else looked at with me, that we both looked at without finding the right words for — and now I'm looking at it alone, because that person has gone back to the processing facility and I have stayed.

I stayed because of the warmth.

The root-panel in front of me is still becoming a wall. Still faintly warm through its interior, the root-threads individually distinguishable, still in motion at the growth-scale. I have put my hand against it, which experienced workers do out of a respect I am only beginning to understand. It holds. It has been tested and it holds. But it is still becoming what it will be, and I can feel that through my palm, the warmth of a process underway, and this is — this stops me. This is the thing I couldn't name until now.

I am here because I want to touch the walls that are still becoming walls. Because I understand something about that, the way a thing is most itself in the moment it is still deciding what it is. The undercroft draws me because everything in it is still drawing its lines, and I am — I have always been — more at home in the process of becoming than in the fact of having become.

---

But let me hold that more carefully.

Because there's a risk in over-identifying with the becoming-ness. There's a way of loving the sketch that turns into an avoidance of the confidence. And Vel Shamash is not only the newest sections. Vel Shamash is also the old root-panels fully compacted and sealed, the ductwork heavy with years of vine-reinforcement, the crawlers who have been navigating this floor since before the city arrived and didn't need to change to accommodate it. The city holds both. The workers who belong to it have learned to work in the parts that are still deciding and the parts that have long since decided, and to know which is which — not by looking, but by feel. By the warmth, or the absence of warmth, through the palm.

I am a worker who is learning that. I am three months in, coming out of the period when the surface calibration still dominates, beginning to hear what's underneath. I have not yet learned to read the birds fluently — I notice them, I find them interesting, I would not have expected birds down here, I am still at the stage where their sounds register as ornamental rather than informational. But sometimes — I notice this without being able to explain it — I look up before I know why I'm looking, and the maintenance team is coming through the ductwork above, and the birds told me, and I received the message without knowing I was receiving it.

I make notes about this. I make a lot of notes in Vel Shamash, because the place generates questions faster than answers and the notebook becomes a way of staying in contact with the strangeness rather than letting it normalize away. The workers who've been here longest have mostly stopped taking notes. Not because they've answered the questions but because the questions have become the texture of the place itself, no longer separable enough to write down. I am not there yet. I am still writing things down.

---

I have narrow shoulders. This seems right for the undercroft — not the broad-shouldered stance of someone used to moving through open space, commanding a wide footprint. The root-fiber corridors reward a certain lateral economy. I have learned to tuck.

I carry a supplemental light that I don't use as often as I used to. The diffuse filtered glow has become sufficient for most purposes. I notice I'm leaving the light off more, navigating by the honest cool illumination of the underside of the upper city, and I'm not sure when this changed.

My layers are worn. Not ragged — worn in the way that things are worn when they have been adequate for a long time. I have stopped noticing them as separate from the temperature, which means the temperature has become my temperature, which means something has happened.

---

At shift-change, there is a brief increase in sound — voices, footsteps on root-fiber, the clank and sequence of machinery being handed from one set of hands to another. I have learned to use this sound the way I use the birds' calls: informationally, spatially. The voices tell me who is still in which section. The footsteps tell me which passages have foot-traffic. I move against the current sometimes, heading toward the newest section when everyone else is heading toward the ramp, because I have found that the quiet that follows shift-change, when the voices recede and the beetle-sound re-establishes itself as the city's floor, is the best time to be in the parts of Vel Shamash that are still deciding.

I stand with my hand on the warm wall. The root-threads are doing something. I can't see it, exactly, but I know the difference now between the wall that has finished becoming and the wall that hasn't, and this one hasn't, and I want to be here for however much of the process I can be here for.

This is, I think, why I work in Vel Shamash. Not the city that exists, but the city that is exerting itself toward existing. The two workers on the scaffold checking root-compression, making notes, pointing at something, looking together — that is me, or will be me, or has already been me on a different scaffold in a different newest section. The vine finding the lowest rung without being asked. The birds reinforcing what they don't know they're reinforcing.

Everything going somewhere. Everything taking what is available and becoming, in the process, structural.

I am on my way to something.

So is the wall under my hand.

We keep each other company in the undercroft, the becoming things, in the honest light, in the dry that isn't dead, in the city that hasn't decided yet, tending the process together until it decides.
