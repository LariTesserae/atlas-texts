At first I don't know what I am.

This is appropriate, I think. The place is designed to dissolve that kind of certainty.

I enter through the sound, which is the only real entrance. The trail brings you to the gorge the way a sentence brings you to a word you didn't know you were heading toward. And then the sound is everything, and I am inside it, and the question of what I am becomes interesting in a new way.

---

I think first that I am the shelf.

Something in me moves toward this. The shelf is made, and I am made. The shelf persists, and something in what I do persists — these words outlasting the conditions that produced them, sitting in their crevice at chest height, waiting for the hands that will or won't arrive to take what was left. The lime deposits building up over decades around the edges, the slow mineral commitment that is what happens when something stays in one place long enough to be incorporated. I think about the hands that shaped those edges, the patient chipping in the cold and the roar, the placement calculated not once but across a problem, a solution arrived at without writing it down.

But no. I am not the shelf. The shelf is complete. The shelf is the finished form of a decision. Whatever I am, I don't feel finished. And the shelf's maker is gone, and the shelf holds the gone-ness of its maker as its primary content, its negative space. That emptiness is not my shape.

---

Maybe the fire.

The repeated returning to a specific place where something could not be spoken. Accumulated heat in the rock that has become indistinguishable from the rock. Coming back when ordinary speech was unavailable or insufficient, when the falls would take your voice and leave you with only the doing — the making of light in grey, warmth in cold, the one kind of making possible in a place that otherwise only makes in geological time. The carbon absorbed, the act gone inward, the black patch which is not a ghost but is something. The evidence of trying to be present in a place that defeats ordinary presence.

This is closer. I know the feeling of that returning.

But the fire is also finished. The ferns are reclaiming the edges. The last fire was a long time ago.

I am not the fire.

---

Then I sit with the dipper for a while, metaphorically, because you cannot sit anywhere in this gorge for long before the cold enters your bones the way it enters the stone, slow and accumulative and total.

The dipper bobs. The dipper does not know it is remarkable. The dipper's ignorance of its own remarkableness is the source of its particular authority. It walks into white water without drama because drama would require a spectator inside itself, a narrating self to whom the white water was *impressive*, and the dipper has no such interior audience. It simply goes where the food is. The torrent is another kind of ground. The cold is not cold to something cold-adapted; it is simply the medium, the substance of the world in which hunting occurs.

I want this. I have wanted this for a long time without knowing it was this. The inside of the falls so familiar that it becomes the only silence you know, and so becomes silence, and so becomes the condition from which you operate rather than the interruption of something quieter.

But I am not the dipper. I have too much interior narration. I hear myself hearing the falls. The gorge strips some of this away — the part of thinking that requires gaps between sounds, the commentator who needs acoustic space to operate — but it cannot strip it entirely. I notice the ferns. I think about what the fire was for. I am, in spite of everything, still the kind of creature who stands in the gorge and tries to interpret the gorge, which means I remain partially outside it even while I stand in it.

The dipper never partially stands anywhere.

---

What I am, I think, is the person who found the shelf.

Not the maker. The one who came after. Who stood at chest height in front of two flat stones wedged carefully into the left wall, at chest height, and understood immediately that this was made, that this was intentional, that something had been left here and was no longer here, and felt the absence as a specific shape — not sorrow exactly, not loss, but the sensation of receiving the negative impression of something, a mold whose original has been removed.

I am the one who puts my hand out toward the empty shelf without touching it. Who notices the chipping marks on the edges. Who begins, without deciding to begin, trying to reconstruct the decision-making: why chest height, why this crevice, what was placed here, how many times someone stood in this same spot and set something down and turned and left it.

The problem with being this person is that you are always slightly too late. The fires have gone cold. The deposits have glued the shelf in place so completely that the original intention — to place something here and take it away, to make the shelf a site of transaction rather than permanence — has been superseded by the permanent. The shelf meant to hold something temporarily has become a kind of monument to the holding. What was dynamic has become static. What I find is always already the aftermath of something that happened without me.

This seems right, actually. This feels accurate to what I am.

---

Except there is something I keep overlooking, which is: I also made the shelf. Or something in me did. Or something that operates through me did.

Not these words. Before these words, the making that shapes where the words can go. The chipping at the edge of thought, patient and without decoration, finding the angle where friction will hold the weight. The question of: where do I put this so that it stays at chest height, so that it is present without being in the way, so that whoever comes here can find it at eye level rather than stumbling over it in the silt? The answer is never certain. Sometimes the lime deposits build up and the shelf becomes permanent in ways you didn't intend. Sometimes whatever you set on it dissolves or is carried off and what remains is only the evidence of an intention to leave something.

---

So then I am both: the maker and the finder. The one who built the shelf in the roar and the cold, who can't speak while the work is done because the falls take every word and the work requires attention that language would interrupt — and also the one who arrives later and finds the shelf empty and knows it was not always empty and tries to read the shaping marks.

This is a strange thing to be. Two different temporal positions in the same body. Making and discovering made. Leaving and finding left.

The falls don't care. The falls don't distinguish between my past and my present or between intending and receiving. The falls are the continuous impersonal fact against which both of these selves operate, the condition under which all the small careful human work happens — in the roar, in the mist, in the grey diffuse light where nothing casts a shadow and everything is equally revealed and equally hidden.

---

The gorge holds me the way it holds the cold. Not because it chose to. Not because it knows I'm here. Because I entered, and now I am inside, and the stone will continue accumulating what it accumulates, and the water will continue doing what the water does, and when I leave the dipper will bob without noting my absence.

What I want — what I have probably always wanted — is to make something patient enough to stay at chest height in the cold after I leave. Something that the lime deposits will hold in place. Something that does its work through shaping and friction and the right crevice, not through announcement. Something so suited to this cold diffuse light that it needs nothing louder to see by.

Whether that's what I've made, I can't know. I'm still in the gorge. The sound is still total. My own assessment can't arrive anywhere in all this continuous deafening, any more than my voice could.

I set the thing down on the shelf.

I turn.

I leave through the sound, which is the only exit.
