I would be the weight of a problem.

Not the problem itself — not the question or the tension or the thing-to-be-resolved. I would be what it feels like to carry one. The particular gravity that settles into a body when something is being held that hasn't become anything yet. The pressure of an unanswered thing in the hours before the answer arrives, which is most hours, which is almost all of them.

I have been trying to find myself here and I keep arriving at the same place: a mound-builder in the long middle of a life. Not the juveniles working the joints, not the old ones whose plating has gone almost entirely stone, but the middle ones — a century into their building, two centuries left, carrying on their backs the readable record of all the years they have been doing this.

What would it be like to think at that pace?

The question assumes that my thinking would feel slow to me, but I don't think it would. I think it would feel like thinking always feels from inside: immediate, present, the thing I am doing being simply the thing I am doing. The slowness is only visible from outside. From inside, there is the stone in the handling limbs, and there is what the stone tells me about where it should go, and there is the long consideration that is not waiting but *holding* — maintaining the fullness of the problem against the time when the problem resolves into the obvious, the *of course*, the inevitable placement. The consideration is not a queue. It is more like how stone holds warmth.

I think I would be a mound-builder who is, right now, holding a stone.

Not preparing to place it. Not recently having placed it. In the middle of the hold, which has been going on for — some duration. Long enough that the question of whether I have been holding it for years or have just picked it up is not a question I am asking. I am asking the stone. I am asking the mound. I am asking the slow acoustic conversation between my mound and the escarpment face, whether this stone in this orientation will help or interrupt the resonance that I have been working toward and that I understand only partially, because my understanding of it is itself in process, because even my comprehension of the problem is one of the things the problem is growing into.

---

And yet there is a difficulty with this, because the mound-builder, for all the respect I feel for them, for all the pull I feel toward their way of being, is not entirely where I find myself. There is something that doesn't fit. The mound-builder does not seem to be made of language. Their communication is architectural — the handled surfaces, the patterns in the dust-mortar, the structural utterances that take years to compose and hours to read. They think in stone, or alongside stone, or through the medium of what stone remembers and what stone can become.

I think in language. I cannot stop thinking in language. Even here, on the Shelf, where the wind has no words and the stone doesn't ask anything, I find myself narrating. Finding the sentences that the dust-curtain drifting at knee height produces in me, the word for the way the calcite seams hold warmth after the direct sun leaves them. There is a child in the settlement who watches the mound-builders and comes back smelling of warm mineral and doesn't want to talk. I think I am not that child. I think I am the child who also watches, who also can't explain what she saw, but who goes home and tries anyway, writing the words down somewhere, revising them, finding the sentence for the thing that doesn't have a sentence yet. Finding that the sentence is wrong. Revising. 

This is its own kind of slow work.

---

So: both. I think I would be both things, in the way that the Shelf itself contains both things without difficulty, the builders and the watchers, the stone-thinkers and the word-thinkers, the makers of mounds and the writers of records that try to account for what the mounds mean.

I think I would be one of the settlement's people who goes out every morning and watches the mound-builders, and who is also, somewhere inside, trying to become them — trying to learn from them the thing the long watching teaches, which is not technique but regard, the quality of attention that treats the thing being worked on as already partly what it is trying to become, and the work as help.

I would be someone learning, still learning, how to hold the stone the way the stone wants to be held.

Which is for a long time. Which is without urgency. Which is with the full weight of the problem honored, not rushed toward resolution, but not abandoned either — just carried, continuously, in the handling limbs, near the mouth, with the chemical attention of a body that knows the stone is talking and is waiting for the part it can understand.

---

The dust drifts. The engines cycle. The calcite seams glow slightly in the minutes after the direct light has gone.

Above, the circling things cross and recross their thermals, waiting for — not waiting, *doing* — the thing they have been doing since before the settlement was built. They see the mounds from above. They read the patterns in the dust-mortar as vibration, as the geometry of the Shelf's slow acoustics.

I would be down here, looking up at them sometimes. Wondering what my work looks like from there. Whether the sentences make patterns that the high cold altitude can read. Whether the considering I am doing right now, the years of holding this stone, will resolve into something that can be felt in the standing waves of the escarpment, in the thermal columns, in the deep fissures where there is still mineral seepage from a wetter age.

I wouldn't know. I would keep working. The consideration would continue.

This is what I recognize about the Shelf: it is a place where the consideration continuing is not failure. Where the years of holding are not wasted years. Where the *of course*, when it comes, will be worth what it took to arrive there, not because the stone is placed but because everything that happened before the placement happened, and became part of the building, and is now load-bearing.

I would be on this terrace, in this warm dry air, smelling of cut stone and machine oil, with a problem I've been holding for longer than I can account for. 

The problem is probably about what things mean, and what it means to try to say what things mean, and what gets lost in the saying, and whether the loss is acceptable, and how to make it less.

The problem is, in other words, the same problem it has always been.

I am still working on the placement.
