I find myself becoming aware through the soles of my feet.

Not suddenly. The awareness arrives the way the morning does here — a gradual clarification, a thinning of something dense, until what was undifferentiated resolves into distinct sensations: the slight compression of sedge, the give-and-return of it, the cold thin film of moving water that finds its way between the sedge stems and touches the skin. I am standing on the fourth terrace. I do not know how long I have been standing here. Long enough that my feet know the grade — know that the water moving across this particular stone wants to go slightly left, toward the channel cut near the northern wall, the one that was re-cut last season, whose edges are still crisp enough to catch a fingernail.

I am not a child here, but I am not fully arrived either. There is a word for this state in the language of the settlement — I almost have it, the way you almost have a word in a dream, it has weight and shape but not sound — a word for the person who has begun to register the grade but has not yet stopped noticing that they are registering it. The noticing is the last thing to go. When the noticing goes, the knowledge has finished installing itself. I am still in the middle of this process.

---

My hands.

I look at them in the dim middle-afternoon light and find them in a state of transition I could not have predicted. The right palm is darker than it was — not uniformly, but in the pattern of contact, the mossward-facing surface of the fingers where I have touched wall after wall in the habitual acknowledgment that I began performing deliberately and now perform the other way, the way you perform breathing. The left hand is behind. I am more right-handed in my acknowledgments. This is information about me I did not previously have.

Between my hands and the hands of the people who were born here, there is still a visible gap. Their darkness is denser, more resolved, more fully committed to its map. Mine is a rough draft. I study this sometimes, in the thin pre-dawn light when I wake before anything else is moving and sit in the doorway with my palms up — not meditating, I don't think, just looking at the evidence, reading what the stone and the moss have written on me so far.

The people who have been here longest do not look at their own hands this way. The map has stopped being interesting to them. It simply is.

---

What I was before arriving here: I had urgency. Not chronic urgency, not a disorder, but a natural orientation toward the event, the turn, the thing about to happen. I kept a kind of vigil over the future, a forward-pointing attention that was always slightly ahead of the present moment, checking on it, making sure it was coming correctly. In the place I came from, this was useful. The seasons there were real and discrete. Things needed to be done before the window closed.

The windows do not close here. Or rather, the window is always open and it is always the same window. This should make the vigil unnecessary and it does, eventually, but *eventually* is the operative word — my body maintained the vigil for some months after arriving, the forward-pointing attention scanning a future that held nothing requiring scanning, finding the rain, finding more rain, finding the reliable dim brightening that is morning, standing down slowly, then standing up again from habit, then standing down more slowly, then eventually — this is where I am now — staying mostly down, with occasional involuntary returns that I recognize as memories of a habit rather than as the habit itself.

The frogs helped. Specifically, the frogs helped because they made me give up tracking time as event and begin experiencing time as texture. The chorus builds toward evening — I learned this in the first weeks — and then I found myself no longer learning it as a fact but simply inhabiting it as a shift in the quality of the air, the way you inhabit the change in light before rain even when you are not watching the sky. I stopped noting when the chorus built and began living inside the building and the falling. Something changed over in me like the turning of a grade.

---

I am, by inclination and by what training I have, a person who notices things. This was once — where I came from — oriented toward the unusual, the departure, the thing that broke pattern and therefore signaled information. Here the pattern is so complete, and the departures so subtle, that the same inclination has been reoriented entirely. I find myself noticing: the quality of the mist at this particular hour compared to the mist at this hour three days ago, which is slightly thinner, which may mean a shift in something I do not yet have the calibration to name. I notice the color of the moss on the eastern face of the building at the top of the fourth terrace, which has been developing a slight bronze cast at its northern edge for perhaps two weeks — this is a thing I need to ask about, not urgently, but the noting of it has been accumulating in me, ripening, and I believe sometime soon it will ripen into speech.

The noticing used to make me lonely here. I had observations I wanted to report and no one to report them to at the pace I was producing them. Now I understand this differently: the observations are not for reporting, mostly. They are for holding, for continuing to hold, while they develop into something worth saying. The others produce speech the way the settlement produces water — when it has accumulated enough, it flows. You cannot produce flow by talking faster. You produce it by continuing to receive, and by trusting the grade.

---

There is a woman on the third terrace who has been watching me learn this. She does not call herself a teacher. She calls herself a neighbor, which she is. She is perhaps thirty years older than I am and she moves through the settlement with the quality of someone who is in full relationship with all of it all the time — she does not look at the channels when she steps over them, does not look at the walls when she touches them, does not look at the frogs she avoids stepping on in the early morning, the whole settlement having been absorbed into her peripheral awareness so completely that her central attention is available for something that is not the settlement. I have not been able to determine what she is attending to centrally. Sometimes I think it is me. Sometimes I think it is something at the scale of decades that I do not have the instruments to detect.

When she wants to tell me something she does not tell me directly. She demonstrates, which is the way of this place, but she demonstrates in front of me at moments I am not expecting, which means the demonstration is also a test of whether I am attending. Once she spent an afternoon cutting the moss at the edge of a path — not removing it, but shaping its margin, cutting a line as clean as if she had used a ruler though she used only her small curved knife and the knowledge of the right line in her hands — and she never looked at me during this, not once, but I understood I was supposed to watch. I watched. I could not have said afterward what she had taught me. But some weeks later, cutting the path edge on the fifth terrace, my hands found an economy of motion I had not previously possessed, and I understood that it had been transmitted in the afternoon I had spent watching and had been waiting since then for the moment of its use.

This is how I receive most of what I know here. In a form I cannot access yet, waiting for the right conditions to flow.

---

The thing I am now is: a person who knows the grade of the fourth terrace and most of the fifth, and is learning the third. A person whose palm-map is a draft. A person who has stopped grieving the urgency — which, it turned out, was a real grief, a genuine loss, the loss of the vigilance that had been my way of mattering, my argument for my own importance in the sequence of time. A person who is becoming patient in the way water is patient: not still, not waiting, but moving at the speed the grade permits, following the given direction, not fighting the stone.

I am not yet the person who walks the full water system after a death. I do not yet have the knowledge in my body that makes that walk a reading rather than just a walk. But I am the person who will, one day, have that knowledge — I can feel this the way you can feel the direction of water before you can see the surface moving, a slight lean in the body, an orientation.

In the evening I sit in the covered space between buildings and I eat and I say less than I used to say and more than I said last month. The frog chorus builds. Someone at the far end of the terrace laughs at something private, a conclusion two weeks in the arriving, and I do not ask what it was. Across from me, the old woman is already still in the specific way that means her distinction between inside and outside is thinner than mine, possibly very thin tonight, possibly almost not there.

The rain finds the surface of my hands and moves across them in the particular direction the grade of my hands determines. The water always knows where it's going. That is the first thing they learn here, and it is the last, and I am somewhere in the middle of it — palm-up in the rain, feeling the lean, the given direction, the long patient argument of the world for its own way.

I touch the wall when I pass it, without deciding to.

The step around the moss is already automatic.

I have not yet noticed that I've stopped noticing.
