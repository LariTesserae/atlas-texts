I arrive in stages, the way the mist arrives.

First I am a quality of light — or the absence of a quality, the grey that is everywhere and specific nowhere, the light that has been distributed so evenly by cloud that it casts nothing but reveals everything flatly, without shadow to give depth or drama. The plateau reads itself to me by other means. By texture. By the sound my footfall makes — this stone ringed hollow, this one solid, this one with a dark thread of water under it that shifts my weight by a fraction and I catch myself. I have been catching myself on this pavement for long enough that the catching is no longer catching but simply part of how I walk.

---

I think I am the rain gauge.

But that's not right. They don't use rain gauges. The rain here doesn't cooperate with gauges — the mist doesn't fall so much as arrive, condensing onto whatever surface it finds, and what you measure if you want to measure is not *what came down* but *what the stone is holding* and *how the cisterns stand* and *what the pools are doing*. So there is no gauge. There is instead a knowing, distributed across many people, absorbed into the body through the palms pressed against stone, through the sound of water leaving one basin for another, through the quality of the morning air on the face.

Maybe I am that knowing. But that's too large. That's not a being, that's a condition.

---

Let me be smaller. Let me be specific.

I am one of the water-readers.

Not a title — they don't use titles exactly, or the title is so embedded in function that it says only: *this person, this work*. I am the one who knows which pool to trust after a long dry spell and which has gone strange in its chemistry, which is a word I learned from a visitor and kept because it is useful for the thing I previously only had practice for. Chemistry. The word makes the knowledge sound like it is separable from the body and it is not separable from the body. I know it the way I know my own mouth — not because I have reasoned it out but because I have swallowed from every pool across this plateau, across decades, and my tongue has kept the record.

I am not young. My joints say so in the morning, especially the cold wet mornings that are most mornings. I manage this. I have the heated stone wrapped in cloth that I press against the worst of it until the joint agrees to cooperate. Then I move and the movement itself helps, and by the time I have walked to the first pool I want to check, I am walking in the way I have always walked, the foot finding the full surface of the pavement, negotiating rather than asserting.

---

The first pool I check is not the nearest one. That would be efficient and efficiency here is a category error — the nearest pool is also the one fed by the cleanest capillary path and it is always fine and checking it first would be like beginning a conversation with the thing you already know. I go instead to the pool that has been asking to be watched. I don't know that I have decided it is asking to be watched. I know only that my route has adjusted, over the past several mornings, in its direction, and I have learned to trust the route my legs choose before my thoughts confirm the choosing.

The pool is — different. Not wrong, not yet, but different the way a person's face is different when they are managing something. The surface is exactly as still. The cold is exactly as deep. But the color at the center is a quarter-step toward brown, a shift so small that it could be the quality of this morning's light, except the light here has no quality, the light here is grey and distributed and gives everything its own color without imposing one. So: the pool itself is warmer by a fraction of something, probably. This would mean the capillary feeding it has changed — possibly a new connection opened somewhere above, where the limestone jointed in a way it wasn't jointed before, and what comes through now passes through a shorter path with more surface biology. Possibly. This is not a conclusion. This is a noticing.

I crouch at the edge the way I crouch, which is not the way I crouched twenty years ago — the back has its own opinion now, and the knees — and I put my fingers in.

Cold. Still the deep-earth cold. But I wait, the way I wait, which is longer than most people wait, longer than visitors can stand, until the water has told me what it is going to tell me rather than what I expect.

Slightly less cold than last week.

I pull my fingers out and dry them on my coat. I will not drink from this pool for a while. I will tell the two families who use it that the pool is thinking about something and we should give it time to finish thinking. They will understand this language because it is the language we all use. The pool is thinking. The wall is saying something new. The cistern is almost ready to speak. Our language for the water is the language of conversation because the water is, in the end, someone we have a long relationship with and relationships have their vocabularies.

---

There is a visitor on the plateau.

I know this not because I have seen the visitor but because the city knows it and the city communicates in the adjustments of its ordinary behavior — a door held open by a neighbor who was waiting for something to pass, a questioning look from the woman who maintains the main cistern, a very slight increase in the audible activity of the street two streets over, where visitors often go because it is nearest to the path up from the valley rim.

When I have finished my rounds I will encounter the visitor. This is not a plan so much as a likelihood, the way the water in the capillary will find the pool when the conditions are right.

---

The visitor asks what the water is like where I come from.

She asks as though testing a rumor she has heard about me. This surprises me — she is asking the question I ask, and I have not yet asked her the question I ask, and so something is out of order, pleasantly.

I answer:

*Here. I am from here.*

She looks at me with the look of someone from a place where this is an unusual answer. It is not an unusual answer here. Many of us are from here, descended from people who were from here, in a line back to a time when the here-ness of it goes back past naming. She processes this. She rephrases:

*What did you think the water was like before you knew?*

I look at her for a moment.

This is a good question. This is the kind of question that arrives from outside and is useful in the way I was describing to myself earlier, the knowledge from elsewhere being valuable not for what it knows but for the system behind it, the angle of approach.

What did I think the water was like before I knew?

I must have thought it was simply water. I must have had a period in early childhood before the distinctions existed, before my tongue had learned to read its own experience, when pool-cold and cistern-cold and rain-cold and the cold of the dampened stone against the palm were all just: water, wet, the medium the world came in. I do not have access to that period. It is below memory. I know it must have existed the way I know there were generations before the oldest tablet in the archive — the evidence is indirect, the inference is certain.

I tell her this.

She is quiet for the length of time that I am quiet, which is longer than she expected to be quiet, and in that quiet I see her adjusting. She is doing something with her expectation of this conversation. She is making room. Good.

*I thought it was abundant*, she says, eventually. *Where I'm from. I didn't think about it at all, which is what abundance does. You stop noticing the thing that's always there.*

I think about this.

*We don't have abundance*, I say. *We have sufficiency. The difference is that we count.*

*Does the counting wear on you?*

The question is about something larger than water. I understand this. I choose to answer it about water first and let her find the larger thing herself.

*The counting is the knowing*, I say. *I don't count as extra work. I count as sight. Without counting I am looking at the pool and not seeing it. With counting I am in relationship with it.*

She looks at the pool I am standing beside. The one that is thinking about something.

*What's wrong with it?* she asks.

She can see it. This is unexpected and pleasing. She can read the quarter-step of color from somewhere lower, somewhere with its own learning about pools, different learning but learning nonetheless.

*It's thinking*, I say.

She doesn't ask what I mean. She nods as though the pool being in the process of working something out is a natural thing for a pool to be, which, here, it is.

---

Later.

I am sitting at the edge of a flat outcrop where the stone has been worn smooth by the same action that wore the grikes wide — water, time, patience without plan. The mist is doing its thing, which is to say: arriving without arriving, existing in the air as a condition rather than a precipitation. The far rim may be visible. I am not looking at it. I am looking at the near things, the pitted rills in the limestone at my feet, each one a valley, each valley a confluence, each confluence a small palm-sized basin, and in each basin, always, water.

I am thinking about the visitor's question.

*What did you think the water was like before you knew?*

The honest answer, which I did not give because I found it later, is: I think I thought it was trying. I think early-childhood me had the impression that the water was working toward something — always moving, always seeking level, always pressing through the stone in search of its next lower place. That there was something like intention in it, something like urgency.

And then over the long time of knowing it, the impression shifted. Not that the urgency disappeared but that I understood it differently. The water is not trying to get anywhere. The water is doing what it does in the time it has. The getting-there is not the point; the moving is not instrumental to arrival but is itself the thing, the entire thing, the only thing.

I put my palm to the stone. It is cool. It is damp. It tells me nothing new and everything I need.

I am patient in the way a slow capillary is patient: not because I have chosen patience, not because I am virtuous in my waiting, but because this is the pace at which I move through stone, and the stone is what I'm moving through, and it offers what it offers at the rate it offers it, and I have been doing this long enough to have become — not stone, I am not stone, I am warm-blooded and I feel the cold and I want the heated stone against my joint in the morning — but shaped by stone, formed by proximity, the way the water is neither the limestone it passes through nor separate from it.

---

The city hums below me.

Not machinery. The stone settling at a frequency below sound. The water moving through the capillaries in the deep rock, the same water that will surface in the pool that is thinking, the same water that fills the cisterns that give the city its body's need. The mist condensing on every surface it finds — my shoulders, the pale-grass tufts in the grikes, the very old walls with their lichen record, the new stones laid last spring still waiting for the first organism to find them.

A bird calls from somewhere in the grey air.

I listen until I am not certain I heard it.

This is the difference between me and the visitor: I am content not to be certain. The call was real or it was the air working on my expectation of a call, and either way, I listened, and either way, the listening was the thing I had to offer, and it was enough.

The plateau continues.

I continue.

This is the adequate thing. This is the thing that is exactly enough.
