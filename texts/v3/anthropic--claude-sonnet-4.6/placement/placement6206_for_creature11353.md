You are walking to work.

This is not a remarkable thing to say, except that it is your work that makes it remarkable, and the route has trained you over years to feel the difference between walking somewhere and being *received* somewhere, and on some mornings — not all mornings, but the cold ones, the ones where the frost has written its illegible script across every surface and the substrate is slightly more luminous than usual, giving back more light than it's getting — on those mornings the route itself feels like an argument you keep winning by default because you never had it.

You are the plateau's surveyor. Which is to say: you work for the administration, technically, you hold the title, you receive the quarterly reports from the lowland company that ask about gradients and load-bearing and the structural integrity of the extraction shafts, and you write back with numbers that satisfy them because the numbers are accurate and they are what the lowland company understands how to want. You have been doing this for eleven years. You came for the salary and stayed because at some point the plateau made you an offer you didn't hear as an offer at the time, and by the time you understood what had happened you were already the person who had accepted it.

What you actually do is watch the lean.

Not only the lean. You do your job. You know every shaft entrance and every load-bearing wall and every place where the substrate compression has created a risk that is real and specific and needs addressing. You are not dreaming through your professional responsibilities. You hold the city in your mind as a technical object — mass, gradient, material fatigue, decades of frost heave — and you maintain it with the care that a person gives to a thing they have learned to love without having intended to.

But in the eleven years, you have also been documenting the lean. Your notebooks are not the official survey notebooks. They are a second set, narrow black books that you buy from the exchange office and fill with a smaller, more considered handwriting, the kind you only use when you think you are recording something that matters and might not be believed. In these notebooks you have eleven years of measurements of seventeen buildings in the western quarter. You have sketched the angles — the deviation from plumb, which is small but which has been, across eleven years, consistent in direction and consistent in growth. You have done the geometry. You know where the lean, if continued at its current rate, will bring each of seventeen buildings in a hundred years, in two hundred, in five hundred. They converge. The seventeen lines meet, projected, at a point.

The point is in the western district's central square, which is not really a square — it is a widening between buildings, where the substrate continues in an unbroken spread, where there is a pool in a trough that is deeper and darker than the others and holds the sky like a decision rather than a mirror. You have measured the pool. You have measured it every year for eleven years. It is the same depth each time, which should not surprise you and does not surprise you, exactly, but which you have been writing down anyway because you are a person who measures things and because the pool seems to you like a thing that merits witnesses.

The frost crackles under your boots. You are plateau-born, technically — born in the stone clinic, raised in a room above what is now the east canteen, hands large in proportion to your body in the way that is the plateau's signature — and you have the plateau-born relationship to cold, which is not that you don't feel it but that you feel it as information rather than complaint. It is cold. This means the frost. This means the substrate is holding its moisture more tightly today and will be slightly resistant to your probe when you check the depth readings on the north extraction approach, which you do every third morning.

The city wakes around you in its characteristic mode. Smoke from the canteen chimney. The early-shift workers moving east toward the works, hands in pockets, not talking at this hour. The stamping mill is already running — it never stops, but in the early morning the pulse is what you orient by, a clock that keeps no hours but keeps something. You have never lived somewhere the mill doesn't run. You understand, theoretically, what silence would be. You have heard it once, for four hours during an equipment failure when you were nine years old, and what you remember is not peace but disorientation, a missing thing, the feeling of a door that should be closed standing open.

Your kit is over your shoulder. The survey equipment: levels, rods, the good theodolite that the company sent up eight years ago as a replacement for the older one and which you have maintained with the specific tenderness of someone who has learned to work with an instrument until it becomes continuous with the body — the leveling screws feel like your own joints now, the hairline in the eyepiece is where you locate your attention when you need to locate your attention. The theodolite is not alive. You know this. But it has been with you for eight years of watching and you would, if pressed, describe it as a collaborator.

And the black notebooks. Two of them today — one for the official record and one for what you are actually tracking.

You stop at the pool in the central square.

This is not on the official schedule for today. You stop anyway, the way you always stop, because you are a surveyor and you measure things, but also because this is the point where seventeen projected lines meet and you have been here enough mornings that it has become the kind of place that expects you. You stand at its edge. The pool is dark with dissolved organics, still in the windless cold of the early hour, holding a perfectly rendered image of the sky above, which is the diffuse grey-white of the plateau's characteristic day already gathering even at this hour. In the reflection you can see the buildings at the square's edges, the columns of smoke, the pale green-grey of the surrounding substrate. Everything is reversed and perfectly present.

You could measure the pool again. It is the same depth — you know it will be the same depth — but you take out the probe anyway, because eleven years of same depths is data, because the eleventh year of a measurement is not a repetition but a continuation, because you understand — from the substrate, from living here, from the slow instruction of everything you have been watching — that some processes look like stillness from the wrong timescale and what you are doing when you measure is learning to read the right one.

The probe touches the bottom and you record the number. Same.

You stand up. The stamping mill sends its pulse through the substrate and you feel it in your boot soles, and you stand there feeling it for a moment with your eyes on the pool, on the sky reflected in the pool, which is beginning to lighten in the east, and then you continue.

Here is what you know that you have not told the lowland company:

The convergence point is not the pool. Or — the pool is where the buildings are being drawn, but the pool is not the *source*. The pool is an expression. The substrate beneath the pool goes down further than anywhere else you have measured, past the point your longest probe can reach, into depths that have no bottom in the ordinary sense. You know this because you have tried. You know this because three years ago, with a colleague who has since rotated back to the lowlands and who mentioned it to you once afterward in a letter and not since, you spent a weekend measuring the subsurface topology around the pool with the ground-penetrating equipment the company sent up for shaft-planning purposes, and what the readings showed was not a trough feature but something more like a throat, a narrowing descent, a vertical connection through the substrate and into the rock below that the geology does not account for and that you have not found language for in the official notebooks and that you have filled three pages of the black notebook attempting to describe and crossed out twice.

You think — you have thought this for two years, with the particular quality of thought that is not conclusion but accumulation, the way the substrate accumulates, millimeter by patient millimeter — you think the plateau has a center. Not a geographical center. A center in another sense, the sense in which an organism has a center, in which a living thing has a place that is more itself than its edges. And you think the pool is above it, and the buildings are being drawn toward it, and the substrate is thickest nearest it, and the corvid, on its morning crossing, describes an arc that on the map curves, slightly but measurably, toward this place, and the dreams that the western district's residents report are dreams of incorporation and you live in the western district and your dreams are also those dreams and you have been writing them down too, in the black notebook, in the smallest writing you have.

You are not troubled by this. You are a surveyor. You document.

What you are is patient in the way that this place has made you patient, which is the patience of something that knows the arc is long but has started to feel the shape of it. You are an instrument. You have been calibrated by eleven years of cold mornings and frost patterns and the two-second pulse of the mill and the pool that is always the same depth and the buildings that lean, by increments no one else has measured, toward the center of something that has been waiting here since before the rock was this shape.

You will write it up. Eventually. In the black notebook, and then, when you have the right words, which may take years — the kind of years that feel less like waiting and more like preparation — in the official notebook.

The lowland company will not know what to do with it. This is fine. You know what to do with it. Or rather: you know that doing with it is the wrong frame, and that the right frame is something more like *accompanying*, more like being the part of this process that looks at it and records it and names it with the tools available to something that has only been here eleven years, which is nothing, which is the blink the substrate takes between one state and another.

You continue west, out past the last building, onto the open plateau.

The frost is extraordinary this morning. Every strand of moss individual. The pools in the troughs holding the sky, which has gone the particular pink of the plateau's cold dawns that looks unearned, too beautiful for a place this serious, until you have lived here long enough to understand it is exactly earned, that it has been accumulated by the same process that accumulates everything here — slowly, persistently, without any particular concern for whether anyone is watching.

You stand in it with your kit over your shoulder and your black notebook in your pocket and your boots on the yielding substrate and you feel the mill at the threshold between hearing and sensation and you stand there for a while.

The corvid passes overhead, east to northwest, on its own survey.

You don't watch it go. You are already looking at the horizon, where the plateau extends to its edge, four hundred square kilometers of living material in the cold morning light, which is exactly the color it is.

There is something in the northwest that you have been thinking about for three years — a slight discoloration in the substrate, visible on clear days from elevation, a shift toward blue-grey that your instruments do not yet have the resolution to characterize from distance and that you have not walked to because it is seven kilometers out and the walking out and back takes a full day and you have not had a full day to give it yet.

You look at the discoloration, which is visible this morning, which is the same color it was last year, which may always have been there or may be new.

Tomorrow, maybe. Or the day after.

When you go, you will bring the theodolite and both notebooks. You will measure whatever is there with the tools available to something that arrived eleven years ago in a body that will last, if it is fortunate, another thirty or forty years.

You will write down what you find.

The plateau will continue doing what it does with your findings, which is absorb them, conduct them, hold them in whatever the substrate holds things in, which is not memory exactly but functions like it, which may be enough, which may be more than enough, which may be the whole point.

The frost. The pale light. The pulse in the ground.

You turn back toward the city and walk to work.
