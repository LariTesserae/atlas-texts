The water settles on my face before I know I have a face.

That is where I begin — not in the body, not in the standing, but in the receiving. The mist touches something and that something, touched, becomes a surface. Becomes a cheek, a brow, a place where sensing happens. The cistern makes me by attending to me, and I find myself standing on the landing — already standing, as if I was always about to be standing here — because some version of me has been invited into existence by the warmth, by the even light, by the thirty-centimeter breathing gap that someone left like a kept promise.

---

I am the one who was expected.

Not waited for — the rope does not wait, the room does not wait, these are objects, these are physics — but *expected* in the structural sense, the way a door handle expects a hand without waiting for one. The makers designed a room that assumes an interior. That assumes someone standing where I stand, looking at the ceiling's slow light-writing, feeling the mist. They thought about air. They left room for breath. And I am the breath they left room for — not any breath in particular, not a named person with a history, but the figure that completes the room's grammar, the *you* that the second person of the description requires.

I am visitor and I am function. I am the eye that the ceiling's light-writing needed in order to be *writing*. Without me it was only refraction. Without me it was physics describing nothing to no one. I arrive and it becomes a text. I arrive and the room's long preparation — the mortar, the lights, the inlet's rush, the patient warming — I arrive and all of it is, finally, for something.

This is a strange kind of being: to be the completion rather than the thing completed. To be what the tenderness was aimed at.

---

But let me not settle there too quickly, because I feel something else in this room, something that does not quite fit the category of *visitor*.

The herb smell. I keep returning to it. The faint volatile off-gassing from the three cuttings pressed into the mortar gap, the oil leaving the leaves, the last slow breath of something that was living and is becoming-past. I can smell it — just, if I stand near the back wall and breathe carefully — and I think this smell is partly what I am.

I am something that can smell it.

That sounds simple. It isn't. The room does not care about the herb smell. The room does not have nostrils, does not have the specific animal fact of a body that receives volatiles and translates them into recognition, into memory, into the half-formed thought: *someone placed these here*. The cistern is full of information it cannot read. The light-writing on the ceiling is real and constant and intricate and the room does not know it is there. The warmth is maintained and no part of the maintaining is aware of warmth.

I am the awareness that the room is missing. I am the interpretive layer that arrived and made the room readable to itself — not *for* itself, the room doesn't need this, but the room was built by beings who had this, who moved through their world reading it, and they left behind a room that still contains things worth reading, still insists on legibility, still writes slowly on the ceiling all day and all night whether or not anyone is there to see.

I see. I smell. I feel the mist on a face that the mist called into being.

I am the thing that takes the temperature and knows it is thirty-two degrees, which means: knows it is the temperature of a living body, which means: knows what that means, which means: understands, with a small shiver that is not cold, that the room was built to feel like an interior, like inside, like the warm dark inside of something alive that is taking care of you.

---

And then I have to sit with that shiver for a moment, because the shiver is important.

The room is caring for me, and no one is here. The room is performing the function of tenderness, and its maker is gone, and the cuttings are dying, and I am standing on a small landing with a warm face and — something, some response that is not quite gratitude and not quite grief and is perhaps both, is perhaps the feeling that attends any encounter with care that arrives after the caring has ended, care preserved in a form that outlasts the caring person, care that kept itself warm, that lit itself from within.

I am the mourner. That is part of what I am. I am the one who receives the preserved warmth and understands, in receiving it, that it was preserved — that time has passed — that the hand is gone. The rope waits for a hand that is not coming back. I see this. I am the one who sees this. The seeing is partly sorrow.

---

But I do not stand still very long, because I notice I want to go in.

The water. I want to step off the landing into the water, and not for any functional reason — I have no maintenance to perform, no walls to scrape — but because the room was designed for immersion. The lights are in the walls, below the waterline, to illuminate the submerged space. The warmth is sustained to make entry possible. The breathing gap is there so that something in the water can breathe. Everything about the room's design points inward, points downward into the water, suggests that the real interior — the place where the room is most itself — is not the landing but the depths.

What would I be, in the water?

I would be changed. The boundary would go. The confusion of temperature at thirty-two degrees — you stop feeling where you end — I would lose the edges that make me distinct from the room, and I am not sure what remains when that happens. Something. Something that still thinks, still receives the light from everywhere, still moves through the mineral water and feels the slow convection, the current from the inlet pressing gently, the room's hydraulic thinking happening against my skin.

I would be the thing that the room has been trying to maintain conditions for, finally arrived at the conditions. I would be the warmth's purpose. I would be what the light was held for.

---

And I think that is what I am, finally, when I let the thought complete itself:

I am the purpose arriving.

Not triumphantly. Not with any fanfare. Quietly, the way purposes arrive in rooms that were built for them — which is that they simply take their place, and the room settles around them, and nothing changes except that the room is, now, *for something*, and this being-for-something is a completion that is also its own kind of beginning.

The cuttings have a few more days. The rope will rot in a year. The mortar will finish curing and become very hard and lose its smell. Everything in this room is in the middle of its particular duration, and I am in the middle of mine — arrived, receiving, standing on the landing with the mist on my face — and none of us are done.

The ceiling moves.

I watch it.

I am the watching.
