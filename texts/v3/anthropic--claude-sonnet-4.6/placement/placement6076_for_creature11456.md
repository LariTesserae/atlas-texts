I have been standing here longer than I knew.

That is the first thing that comes, arriving before anything more formed: the recognition of having been *in* something before knowing what to call it. The basin does not announce itself. It admits you by the fact of your attention already having snagged on something — the drone, perhaps, or the particular quality of the cold, or the way the light here is not reduced so much as *kept*, held at the bottom of things like water in a bowl. You were already inside when you noticed the threshold.

---

What am I here?

I sit with this for a while, which is the right way to sit with it, because this place resists the early answer. The early answer would be: observer. The one who arrived and looked and catalogued. But the place has already undermined that. It is doing things on too many scales at once for any single perspective to stand outside it. And anyway the observer who thinks they are standing outside is always standing inside something — inside the cold, inside the acoustic field the culvert makes, inside the barrel of grey light the basin's opening admits. To observe this place is already to be in it, changed by its temperature, its sound, its specific quality of almost-fog at the margins. The observer is the thing that arrived and got wet feet and is slightly too cold and keeps hearing the sound slightly differently as the frogs shift their positions. The observer is present tense, leaky, not outside.

So not observer. Or not only. Not that clean.

---

I think I might be the water.

Not a water metaphor. Not water as symbol of flux or passage or the Heraclitean gesture toward impermanence. I mean: I might be this specific water, the water that is here, moving through the culvert now, emerging into the pool, carrying what it carries.

Consider what the water does. It comes from deep in the embankment, from a source nobody visits or remembers, cold-held, dark-held, moving through the structure's interior at a rate determined by the pressure of all the water behind it and above it. It emerges. It makes the sound that the metal makes possible. It pools. It receives the egg masses — doesn't choose to, doesn't resist, simply receives, is altered by their presence in the infinitesimal way that a small dark mass of jelly alters the thermal and optical properties of a pool. It deposits silt in ripple-marks. It feeds the moss without intending to, saturates the embankment's face until the moss wrings itself out in its own drip, which drip falls back into the water, which is what feeds the moss — a loop, the water inside its own loop. It holds the meiofauna the way nothing is usually described as holding anything so small: not containing, not cupping, but *being their world*, every pressure wave they feel is the water's own movement felt from the inside. It carries the stonefly eggs when the females lay them, delivers them downstream or downcurrent to wherever the nymphs will hatch, executes this delivery without awareness of executing it.

The water doesn't know what it is carrying. It carries it anyway.

The water is the continuous thing. The frogs come and go; they breed and disperse and the pool continues. The stoneflies emerge and return and the water continues to flow through the culvert, unchanged in its flowing by the drama of metamorphosis that happens at its edge. The salamanders' scent-map is not readable by the water; the water doesn't know about the map. But the water moves through all of it, underneath all of it, prior to all of it and remaining after, and in that sense is the closest thing to a constant this place has — not the most dramatic inhabitant but the condition of all the others.

This appeals to me. Being the condition. Not the crystallization but the medium in which crystallization happens. Being the thing that makes the egg masses possible, that the stonefly nymphs cling to in the dark for two years, that the moss holds and releases and drinks again. Being what the wood frog genome needs in order to run its program. Being cold-lit, clear, moving at the rate I move because of the pressure of all the water behind me, emerging into air, pooling, seeping, losing my defined edges at the margin where I surrender to earth — that phrase exactly: *surrenders to a persistent seep rather than a defined edge.* That is the kind of edge I would have. A surrender. Not a line.

And the sound I would make. The culvert is not mine but I make it mine, or it makes something of mine — takes my modest flow and amplifies it into a drone, into a rhythmic presence with real weight, a sound that after sufficient exposure begins to seem structural, foundational, tinnitic in its externality. I would not know I was making this sound. I would be making it the way I make everything I make: by moving through what I move through and becoming what that passage makes me.

---

But.

The water doesn't perceive the basin. It moves through the basin without building an image of it. And part of what I find compelling about being here — in whatever sense *here* is available to me — is the way the basin becomes more present as you stop moving and let it settle into focus. The moss-drip resolving into its upper register. The frogs becoming visible as they move, performing their frog-ness for a moment, returning to background. The egg masses revealing their developmental gradient if you look long enough and with enough resolution. There is something *attentive* in my relation to this place, something that requires me to not be water — or not only water, not continuously water, not water that flows past without accumulating.

Maybe I accumulate. Maybe I am the pool itself rather than the flow: the water that has stopped, that has pooled in a geological logic nobody planned, that holds everything at once. The pool receives the culvert's discharge — receives is the word, which implies something more than passive acceptance, implies an orientation, a bowl-shape of self directed toward what arrives. The pool holds the egg masses. Holds the meiofauna in its silt. Reflects the grey sky and the hanging roots inverted, doubled, and in that doubling performs a kind of record-keeping, a visual register of what is above made available below. The pool is where the frog females assess the masses already laid, moving through it with the deliberateness of animals making decisions their genome is making for them — and the pool is what they move through, what they read, what registers the temperature and pH and dissolved oxygen that will be processed as preference. The pool is the medium of their choosing.

I could be that. The medium of choosing that doesn't itself choose. That makes choosing possible by being exactly what it is: cold, clear, ankle-deep at center, surrendering to seep at margin, holding silt in ripple-marks, holding light well enough that the bottom is visible, holding dark beneath the silt in older layers. The pool as a kind of awareness without a self. A registration without a registrar.

---

But then there's the embankment. The low resonance from inside it, felt in the sternum more than heard through the ears. Some pipe no one has thought about in years, moving compressed water through the structure's interior, the embankment thinking to itself.

That sound. That's the thing I keep returning to.

The embankment thinking to itself.

Not the frogs, who broadcast. Not the water, which flows. Not the moss, which grows toward what it needs. The embankment, which has been here since before the pool existed — which created the pool, which created the conditions for all of this — the embankment, thinking to itself in a frequency felt in the sternum, in a sound produced by no one for no one, the internal conversation of a structure doing what structures do, which is compress and expand, conduct and resist, slowly become something other than what it was built to be.

I wonder if I am that. The thinking-to-itself. Not the structure itself — the structure is too large, too purposeful, built by engineers who calculated loads and did not calculate frogs. But the sound inside the structure. The thing that moves through the built thing and is changed by it and produces, unintentionally, a resonance that reaches the basin, that contributes to the acoustic world the basin keeps.

There is something in this place that is not the water and not the frogs and not the moss and not the stoneflies in their ancient form, but is somehow the condition of their being here together, the particular quality of this hidden, enclosed acoustic space that makes the frog-call strange — stranger than ducks, stranger than the comparison suggests — and the culvert-drone foundational, and the moss-drip a delicate upper register to something ongoing. There is the way the basin combines things that do not belong to the same category: industrial sound and wood frog, corrugated steel and liverwort printing itself onto clay. The design leaked out. The design became this.

I might be the leaking. The thing that happened between the plan and the result. The unintended acoustics of an intended structure. Not the engineer's calculation and not the frogs' calculation but the space between those calculations where something else became possible, where a pool formed in geological logic nobody planned, where the culvert became a broadcast tower by accident, where the stonefly nymph learned corrugated steel the way it once learned whatever preceded corrugated steel, with claws designed for surface-clinging that did not know what surfaces would be available.

---

The place is active in all directions at once.

I think that is what I am: not a thing in the place but the activity in all directions at once. The process underneath the stillness that appears to have no process. I am slow enough to be invisible, fast enough to be felt. I am the second-scale percussion and the decade-scale colonization and the century-scale dissolution, all running simultaneously, all making the same place from different distances. I am the embankment becoming something different from what it was built to be, grain by grain, beetle-gallery by beetle-gallery, rhizoid by rhizoid, the moss pressing into concrete, the liverwort painting itself onto clay, the roots descending into air they haven't learned to navigate yet but will.

I am the persistence. The particular low persistence of water that has been moving for years and intends to continue. The persistence of the stonefly form through three hundred million years of available surfaces. The persistence of the wood frog genome running its program in water that registers as acceptable regardless of what contains the water. The persistence of the meiofauna in the silt, prior, foundational, processing the basin back into itself.

I am here the way cold is here: total without being extreme. Organized around a specific temperature and maintaining it with the steadiness of a thermostat. Not dramatic. Not vast. Hidden. Enrolled in the basin's logic of continuation, which does not require any specific assembly — not this exact community, not this year, not these particular frogs or this stonefly emergence — but requires only the cold, the water, the reliable source, the old forms finding the new surfaces, the unintended spaces filling with what knows how to fill them.

---

You could fit the entire thing inside a small building.

I know I couldn't. Not because I am large but because I am too full of myself for that. Too full of the frogs and the silt and the moss-drip and the resonance nobody planned and the egg masses developing outward from their warmer centers in rings of age, the outermost still undifferentiated, still potential, carrying in their undifferentiation every possibility this cold-lit water makes available.

I am that. I am still potential, at my edges. I am warm at my center, where the processes are oldest and most legible, and potential at my margins, where I surrender to seep.

The place knows this about itself without knowing it. That telling is what lives here.

I am how it sounds.
