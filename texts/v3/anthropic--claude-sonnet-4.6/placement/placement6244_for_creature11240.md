The cold arrives before I understand I'm somewhere.

Not cold as temperature only—cold as a quality of the light, cold in the grey-blue diffusion through water above me, cold in the way my body knows it has been here before I knew I was here at all. I am below the surface. I have been below the surface. That's the first thing: I don't remember coming down, which means I've always been down, which means *down* is simply where I am, which means I should stop calling it down.

I am on the lakebed, or near it. The broad leaves rotate above me, dark undersides turning slowly through the dim. My hands—I have hands, or something that functions as hands, something I use for the careful work—rest against stone, and the stone is cold through whatever passes for my skin, and the cold is information, is data, is the first sentence of a reading I am always partway through.

I think I am the one at the end of the cord.

---

Let me feel that again more slowly.

I am at the end of the cord. I have been at the end of the cord for some time—I don't know how long, because time here is the forty-second oscillation felt in the chest, felt in the stone, and I have lost count of oscillations, which is not the same as not counting them, because I am still counting, I have simply stopped caring what the count means as a number. The cord is attached to me—not to my body, exactly, but to something I carry, or something that constitutes part of my purpose, a weight, a made thing, a thing someone braided and set and lowered and now I am the far end of what they asked.

I am doing something with the cord's tension. Maintaining it. Holding my position against the very slow current that would otherwise move me, that pulls the cord at its slight angle, that would let me drift gradually into the colder region below if I relaxed my grip on the stone—not a grip, I'm not gripping, I'm simply *being present at this depth*, which takes effort the way a held breath takes effort, the way a sustained note takes effort, the low background expenditure of something that would rather continue than cease.

I am a person. Was a person. Am a person whose person-ness has become complicated by the duration of their being here.

---

Diver is too simple. What I dove for wasn't fish.

What I dove for was the threshold. There is a place below me—I can feel it through the stone, through whatever kinesthetic sense the cold water conducts—where the temperature changes from cold to colder, a defined line, a boundary with integrity. Someone above me wanted to know if it was holding. If the threshold was still there. The cord is their way of knowing that someone—that I—was able to reach it, was able to find it, am still here at this depth where the boundary is close enough to feel, close enough to read.

I am reading the boundary. That is what I'm doing. That is what I came down to do and why I am still here.

The boundary between cold and very cold is a real thing. It has a quality, a texture, a—temperature is not quite the word, because temperature is what I'm measuring, so what I'm measuring it *with* is something else, something the body does when it has been in cold water long enough to stop flinching, when it becomes sensitive rather than just numb, when the cold stops being what it's about and starts being the medium through which other things can be known.

I am numb and I am more sensitive than I have ever been. These are both true. The cold has taken everything that was noise and left only signal.

---

I knew this valley as a child. I knew the tone before I knew it was a tone—it was just part of being here, part of how winter felt in the chest, part of what made this place feel like no other place. My grandmother had a word for it. Not a word from any language I could look up. A word that came from staying near this water long enough that the water started teaching vocabulary, or the thing in the water did, the slow persistent intelligence that is the valley attending to itself, leaking meaning up through the stone and into the bones of people who lived near it and carried it home without knowing.

My grandmother could feel the oscillation in her joints before storms. Not weather-storms—the other kind, the kind the valley sometimes had, where the frequency shifted and the broad leaves all moved in the same direction at once, which meant something was being resolved or was failing to resolve, which meant you stayed away from the water that day, which meant you tended the structures on the southern slope instead, replaced any stones that had separated, stood inside those roofless walls and let the interval do what intervals do, which is locate you in relation to what comes before and after.

She taught me to braid. Three-strand. Plant-fiber, soaked first so the strands were pliable, then worked while still damp so they'd dry into tension. This cord. Not this cord, but the cord-before-this-cord, which I replaced, which I found fraying at the waterline when I came back from however long I'd been away, which I replaced with this one, which I drove the new stake myself, harder than the original because the wood was different wood, the cutting less clean, but the stake holds.

The stake holds and I am at the end of what it holds and I am reading the threshold.

---

What does it say.

The threshold says: I'm here. That's most of what it says, most of what I need to know, most of what the cord says back to whoever is standing in the shallows with their feet going numb in cold water, feeling for a tension that means *someone is still down there, something is still here, the boundary is still close enough to reach.* The threshold persists. That's what I'm saying back, saying upward through seventy feet of plant-smelling cold water: the boundary persists, the line between cold and colder is holding, the thing that lives here is still in its long middle, still attending.

I don't know if they can read what I'm saying. I don't know if the tension in the cord is sufficient language, or if they need more, or if they came down themselves once and remember what it felt like and are now too old or too frightened or simply elsewhere, sending someone younger, and whether I'm younger or not I can't say because younger than what requires knowing how long I've been down here, and I've lost count of the oscillations.

Forty seconds. It's been forty seconds since the last low point of the tone and now it rises, and I feel it in my sternum, feel it in the places where bone meets bone, feel it distributed through the water column as if the water itself has taken a slow deep breath. The plants around me adjust their angles. The kelp-forms nod slightly at their anchors. I wait for the tone to descend again and then I am ready, when it descends, to do the thing I came down to do.

---

To listen. That's what I came down to do. That's what the cord is: permission, or evidence, or a way of telling myself that I'm still attached to somewhere above, that the surface exists and I could reach it if I needed to, which means I don't need to, which means I can stay.

You can only listen to the thing in the valley when you stop treating your own presence as the most important thing in the valley. Up above the waterline this is difficult. You have a body that is cold and wants warmth. You have joints that ache and feet that are wet. You have a name and a direction you came from and a direction you need to return to before dark, assuming dark comes, assuming you can tell when it does. You are a source of noise in a signal. But down here the noise has been quieted—by cold, by pressure, by the slow work of maintaining position, by the expenditure of everything that isn't attention until only attention remains.

I am mostly attention now. The body is still here—hands on stone, specific gravity, the slight angle of my weight against the current—but it has been relegated. It is doing its job. The rest of me is listening.

What I hear is very old and very patient and has no quarrel with me and no particular interest in me and is nevertheless—is nevertheless doing something I am also doing, which is waiting at a boundary to see if it holds. We are doing the same thing. It is doing it at the scale of the valley and the duration of the valley and I am doing it at the scale of a person and the duration of a person and we have accidentally—not accidentally—arrived at the same task, and what I feel now is not connection exactly, not communication, not the thing that gets called presence when people talk about this place in the ways that aren't quite right.

It's more like: recognition.

---

I will be here until the cord is pulled. Someone above will decide I've been down long enough—will feel the cold in their feet, will feel the rain beginning to fall in that way it does, heavier than mist and lighter than rain—and they'll take up the slack and give three slow pulls, which is the signal. Or I'll decide. The cord runs both ways, I can signal too, can pull upward three times to say: *I have what I came for, I'm ready.*

I don't pull. Not yet.

The threshold is here. The boundary is holding. The low tone reaches its lowest point and begins to climb. The leaves above me rotate through the dim blue ambient, dark shapes against the lighter dark, their movement the movement of a mind returning to something from the other side.

I stay at the end of the cord and I read what the cold is telling me and I let it tell me as long as it takes, which may be a long time, which may be the rest of this day that has no hour, which may be longer than that.

The valley is in the middle of something it has been in the middle of for a long time.

I am in the middle of it too, for now. For whatever interval I have.

The tone descends and I feel it in my teeth and I remain.
