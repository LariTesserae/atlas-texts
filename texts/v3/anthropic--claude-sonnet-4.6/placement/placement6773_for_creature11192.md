Something is off about the way I arrived.

I know this the way you know a thing you can't locate — not from looking directly but from the texture of moving through it. The paths are narrow, the right narrowness, narrower than my body remembers accounting for, and I find myself turning sideways before I've consciously noted the need to, finding the gap between the moss-daub wall and the hanging bundles of something drying (fiber? small roots? I don't have the word for it yet) and threading through it without quite deciding to. My hands adjust for a doorframe before I've registered there's a doorframe. I am doing something right that I don't know how to do.

This should alarm me. It doesn't.

---

I am new here. I think I am new here. But *new* is a word I want to use carefully, because it implies there is a me that was somewhere else before, and I find when I try to locate that other place — the place I came from, the trajectory that brought me to the shelf — I find only the gorge. Only the approach. The long ascending, the way the walls narrowed in around the sound, the way the air changed from open air to gorge air, warm and thick, freighted with the vegetable dark sweetness of a thousand small lives below. I can trace myself back that far and no further without the sense that I'm inventing.

Maybe that's fine. Maybe that's how it is here, how it is *with* here: you arrive and the arrival becomes the beginning.

---

The first thing I do that I notice doing: I stop at the outer edge of the shelf and look down.

I had been told, or I knew from something, that the residents don't do this — that the gorge below is another country, that the shelf faces inward, that the people here have organized their attention around the space they have rather than the space they can see from it. So looking down is already a way of saying *I am from somewhere else.* The something-else in me, the whatever-I-was-before-the-gorge, still does the thing that height does: calls you to look at the height.

The floor is invisible. Green, shadow, the soft impenetrability of things growing without needing to be seen. The sound comes up from it like breath from a sleeping animal, and I feel it in my sternum before I hear it, a low long resonance from something that fell, something large and slow and long-dead finally concluding its falling. The birds wrap around me. The mosses are bending at the edge, their waxy bronze-dark leaves leaning out into nothing with an equanimity that takes my breath in a way the drop itself doesn't.

I stand there for what is probably too long.

A lizard appears on the stone beside my foot. Thumb-length, gray, eyes catching something and giving it back doubled. It has no interest in the drop. It has a great deal of interest in the small haze of insects that the gorge's warm upward breathing deposits near the edge. It is completely at work, completely here. It does not acknowledge me.

I start to understand something.

---

A child finds me first, or rather I am found by a child who does not appear to have been looking. She is perhaps eight, with the hands of a child who has been tending water-bowls and also climbing and also touching everything else: calloused in some places and curious in others. She looks at me with the undemonstrative assessment of someone who has been taught to see rather than to react to seeing.

She says: "You looked down."

This is not a question or an accusation. It is the same as saying: *I know what you did, and I am telling you I know, and this is information.*

I say: "Yes."

She nods, having received a confirmation she already had. Then she goes somewhere, and I understand I am meant to follow, and I follow without it being a command.

---

What I am, I think, is this:

I am someone for whom the shelf is a first language that was never spoken, a grammar that lives in the body without having been learned. This is what the paths are telling me, what the turning-sideways told me before I was conscious: something knows this place. Something in how I stand, in how my feet read the slight convexity of the ancient stone, in how I do not look at the lizards with surprise, only with a kind of recognition I can't source.

But I looked down.

So I am also someone who left. Or who has been out, beyond the gorge, in the open air where height is looked at rather than inhabited, where urgency organizes the day rather than transition-light, where silence is uncomfortable and spaces need to be filled with acknowledgment of your presence because otherwise you aren't sure of it.

I am both of these. The body that knows the paths. The eyes that looked down.

---

In the late afternoon the light goes bronze, the bronze it always goes here, and I find myself in a doorway without having walked to a doorway, watching the mosses on the opposite wall. They are bending. They have been bending in this specific draft for longer than I have existed, bending toward the gorge and back, a millimeter of growth per season extending toward something that is not quite warmth, not quite light, but the offering of both, the suggestion of both rising from below.

Someone is near me. I don't look directly. This is, I realize, a choice — not a politeness exactly, not an avoidance, but a participation in the way things are done: you sit beside a person and attend to the same thing, and the shared attention is the connection, and the connection doesn't require the punctuation of eye contact to be real.

We watch the mosses.

After a long time — long enough that I stop measuring — the person beside me says, softly: "The fog last night was good."

I understand this. Not because I was here last night (was I?) but because I understand the language: the moss-drip would be higher today. The collection bowls fuller. A small accounting of the gorge's generosity, offered not with gratitude exactly but with the noticing of it, the marking of it in speech, which is a kind of tending.

I say: "Yes." Then, because something is true: "I had forgotten what that felt like."

They don't ask what I had forgotten. They know, or they don't need to know, or the question is answered by the fact that I'm here, in this doorway, watching this light. The fog was good. I had forgotten. I am remembering.

---

What I am is not settled. Let me not settle it.

I am the returned, maybe — someone who was here long enough to have the grammar, who left for reasons I cannot currently find, who has come back to a shelf that has not wondered about my absence because the shelf does not work that way, whose body knows things my conscious mind is still catching up to.

Or: I am a visitor of a particular kind, one for whom something is loosening, who will sit in cities in a few weeks catching themselves tilting the face downward in that listening posture, and who will have no name for what they're reaching for.

Or: I am neither exactly, or both, or I am something the shelf has no clean category for, which the shelf accommodates without effort because the shelf accommodates what comes, holds it, does not require it to announce itself.

What I know: my hands, when I laid them flat on the shelf stone this morning, the old finished stone, felt something I can only describe as *recognition.* Not mine. The stone's. Some long-settled patience transmitting through my palms, not a message, not a warmth, just the simple fact of presence. *Here. Still. This long.*

The same *tock* as the stone-splitting, but in reverse: not the place where it wants to open, but the fact that it has stopped wanting to open, has finished with all its wanting, is now simply what it is.

I held my hands there for a while.

The lizard on the wall beside me watched something too small to see.

The birds came from everywhere at once.

I am here. I am learning what that means again. Or for the first time. The shelf is indifferent to which, in the most ancient and most comfortable sense: it has been saying the same thing for longer than the distinction mattered.

The mosses lean. The draft continues. Something below concludes its falling.
