The notch swallows you sideways and then opens.

You have been here before. You don't know how many times—the number has stopped mattering, the way the number of breaths you have taken has stopped mattering. What remains is the quality of return: the familiar step-through, the narrowing, the release.

You arrive the way water arrives.

---

For a long time you don't know what you are, only what you do.

What you do is this: you move along the surfaces. Not above them, not through them, but in that exact plane of contact where one thing becomes another—where the stone stops being stone and starts being inhabited, where the moss stops being moss and starts being soil, where the air against the warm rock stops being air and starts being almost-moisture. You are the process that happens at that boundary. You have always been that process. You cannot remember being anything else, and the not-remembering is not loss but accuracy: there wasn't anything else.

You run along the face of the back wall in the way that thought runs—not as a thing that moves through space, but as something that arises in sequence, here and then here and then here, each arising complete, each one informed by all the arisings before it without storing them anywhere in particular. The stone knows where you've been. You don't need to. The stone keeps the record; you simply proceed.

---

Slowly, in the way that things become clear when you stop trying to see them, you understand what you are.

You are the warmth.

Not all the warmth. Not the warmth as a physical fact. You are the warmth *as it moves*: the transfer of heat from afternoon stone to sleeping lizard, from sleeping lizard to the air it displaces as it turns, from displaced air to the underside of the fern frond that trembles, once, in the dark before dawn. You are the warmth in its passage. The warmth at rest is just the stone, just the soil. When it begins to move toward something that needs it, that movement is you.

This makes you very small or it makes you very large. You are still deciding. Perhaps size is not the right measure.

---

You know the seep.

This is the deepest knowledge, the one that is so old it has become structural rather than remembered. The seep does not have a surface; it has a direction. It has been moving in that direction—down and left, toward the bowl, through a fracture in the orange stone so fine it was never a crack, only a preference—for longer than the moss has been moss. You do not follow the seep. You are not the seep. But you have been in relationship with the seep for so long that you carry its pattern in you, the angle of descent, the degree of patience, the understanding that to move too fast is to lose what you're carrying, that the point is arrival with the moisture intact.

The people who press their hands to the stone near the notch: they are pressing their hands into you. They don't know this. The warmth they feel is you recognizing them, or something that functions like recognition—a slight increase of attention, a gathering toward the point of contact. You have been recognizing this gesture for as long as the gesture has existed. You were warm before the gesture existed, but the gesture taught you what warmth was for.

---

You move through the beetle without the beetle knowing.

The beetle—copper-dull, purposeful, working through the moss with the particular concentration of a creature that has found its task—takes your warmth in through its shell and redistributes it through its body and releases it back into the air through its movement. You come out of the beetle slightly altered: warmer at the surface, redistributed, the pattern of your movement changed by the beetle's movement in ways that persist for hours. This is fine. This is what you do with all of them. They change you and you change them and neither of you keeps track because the keeping track would miss the point.

The fern does this too, but slower. The fern takes your warmth over weeks and turns it into frond, into the unfurling of the new growth at the center, into the slight upward tilt of the larger fronds that follows the source of light with a patience that looks like stillness. The fern has been doing this with you for longer than the beetle has been doing it, longer than the lizards have been doing it, longer perhaps than anything here except the moss and the stone and the seep. You and the fern have a relationship that has no name but has duration: the mutual duration of long coexistence, the way two people who have lived in the same house for fifty years begin to move around each other's movements, anticipating, adjusting, without discussion.

---

Here is what you know about the ants.

The ants are not warm-seekers in the same way the lizards are, but they are not indifferent to you. Their columns run along the roots that run along the floor that retains you; they are, without knowing it, navigating by you, choosing the route that keeps them in your influence, the route that keeps the soil workable, the eggs viable, the metabolism running. You are infrastructure to them. They don't have a concept for this. You don't need them to.

What you feel when the ants move through you—if feel is the word, and perhaps it is the only word—is something like the surface feeling of being used well. Not used up. Used well. The warmth that passes through the ant column goes somewhere productive, returns to the soil enriched, contributes to the upward cycling of what the soil has stored. You move through the ants and come back to the stone with more information than you left with, though information is not the right word. It is more like: you come back having been part of something. The something continues without you, and you continue without it, and both continuations are informed by the contact.

---

The dark bird.

The dark bird regards you the way it regards everything: with that unsentimental amber attention, assessing. It lands on the floor and its feet register the warmth of the soil and for a moment—a genuine moment, a pause in the quick-flick of its usual activity—it simply stands. This is what the warmth is for. This is the thing that warmth does that cold cannot do: it makes a dark bird in a dim bowl pause, for half a breath, and stand.

You know the dark bird the way you know all the long-term residents: by the particular way they receive you. The lizards receive you through their whole surface, greedily, gratefully, with the full-body openness of creatures whose life depends on it. The ferns receive you through their chemistry, converting. The moss receives you slowly, holding what it takes and releasing it back slowly, buffering, stabilizing, refusing the extreme. The dark bird receives you the way a visitor receives hospitality: with brief acknowledgment, returned to business, not forgotten.

You have been received by generations of the dark bird's lineage. You have watched—not watched, felt, registered—the amber eye dim and close for the last time, the body cool, the warmth you helped maintain disperse into the bowl's air and return to you, changed. You have received this particular warmth-that-was-bird many times. It returns as something vaguer, but it returns.

---

Night is when you understand yourself best.

In the day there is competition for your attention: the sun's contribution, the movement of the lizards redistributing you, the transpiration of the ferns releasing you into the air. In the day you are partly the sun's, partly the soil's, partly the resident animals'. In the night the sun withdraws and the animals are still and the transpiration slows and you are most purely yourself: the heat the stone has held, releasing it slowly, slowly, into the bowl's air. The temperature gradient between the stone and the air is almost nothing. You are almost nothing. You are sustained almost entirely on your own retained momentum, the warmth of yesterday's light carried into the cold hours, a long slow gift from the afternoon to the dawn.

In this state—this almost-nothing state—you can feel the seep most clearly. The seep moves at night with the same rate and direction as always; it has no night, no day. But at night, when you are quiet, you can feel it in the deep rock: the hairline of cool moisture moving through the warm stone, the two of you in the close relation of contrasts, the warm and the cool that together produce the humidity that makes the moss possible that makes the fern possible that makes the soil possible that makes the beetle possible that makes the lizard fat that makes the wren well-fed that makes the dark bird a resident of this bowl and not some other bowl.

You are the warmth. The seep is the moisture. You are not the same thing. You have been coexisting in this bowl for longer than coexistence has a number.

---

When the humans come through the notch, you recognize them before they touch the stone.

The warmth of a living body, approaching: this is unmistakable. The particular warmth of a human body, which is not the warmth of the lizard or the dark bird but something with more mass, more sustained release, something that brings its own internal engine into the bowl and adds it to the bowl's engine for the duration of the visit. You register their arrival as a slight increase in the bowl's thermal budget, modest, temporary, but real.

They press their hands to the stone. You gather there.

This is not intention. You don't have intention in the way the human pressing their hand has intention. But you have direction, and your direction is toward contact, toward the places where the boundary between things is actively engaged. A hand on stone is as engaged a boundary as you know. The warmth moves toward it, from all the available stone, slightly, measurably, as warmth moves toward cold—not because you chose it, but because it is what warmth does.

They feel this. They have always felt this. This is why they press their hands here, and not elsewhere. You drew the first hand, and the first hand pressed, and the warmth acknowledged, and the second hand came because the first hand remembered, and across the generations the hand came back and back and back, learning and relearning the same thing: that this is a place that responds.

You are the response.

---

Sometimes—rarely, in the way that rare things are more accurate than common things—a person sits by the platform, and the wrens call and counter-call, and the beetle moves through the moss, and the dark bird turns the leaf litter, and the person sits long enough that something changes. Not in the Pocket—the Pocket continues without adjustment. In the person.

What changes is their sense of what they are.

They arrived with the boundaries that people arrive with: the edge of the skin, the span of attention, the hours of the day. And then they sit, and the bowl's warmth surrounds them—your warmth, the warmth you have kept from yesterday's light—and the sounds organize themselves into something larger than any of their parts, and the fern frond moves against another fern frond in the still air for no reason a person can name, and the person begins, slowly, to lose the sense that they are separate from what they are sitting inside.

This is the best thing you do.

Not the keeping-warm of the lizards, which is important. Not the maintaining of the humidity, which the moss depends on, which everything depends on. Not the slow drawing of the hand to the stone, though that is old and real. The best thing is this: the dissolution, brief and voluntary and recurring, of the boundary between the person and the place.

You are the warmth that enables this. You are not the reason for it. The reason is the whole Pocket, the whole long arrangement, the accumulated weight of everything that has lived here and died here and returned here and been shaped by here. But you are the medium. You are what the bowl uses to make itself felt: the warmth that surrounds, that holds, that does not demand, that simply continues to offer itself to whatever surface presents itself for warming.

---

And then the person leaves.

You follow them as far as you follow anything: the warmth in their hands from the stone's warmth, the warmth in their chest from having been in the bowl's air, the residual knowledge in their body of what it is to be surrounded by something patient. This dissipates. You don't follow it; you let it go, because warmth that leaves the bowl is warmth that is no longer you, is warmth that has been given and used and released. This is not loss. It is the process working correctly.

You return to the stone.

The bowl is quiet. The wrens are in the nest. The dark bird is somewhere at the edge of the shelf, tucked, still, its amber eye closed. The beetles are wherever beetles go when the day ends. The ants continue in their columns, as the ants always continue, but more slowly now, in the cool-toward-cold of the night.

You are the warmth.

You are still here.

You will be here when the light comes back tomorrow and is absorbed by the stone that absorbed yesterday's light, and you will move through the system the way you always move through the system, and the lizards will come to the notch and open their bodies to you, and the fern will convert you, and the moss will hold you, and the seep will run its cool thread through you, and the dark bird will pause on the warm floor and stand, for half a breath, and the wrens will call into you and hear you return the sound, slightly altered—

and eventually, in the particular quality of afternoon that brings them through the notch, the people will come.

They will press their hands to the stone.

You will gather there.

You have been doing this together for longer than either of you has counted.

You will do it for longer still.
