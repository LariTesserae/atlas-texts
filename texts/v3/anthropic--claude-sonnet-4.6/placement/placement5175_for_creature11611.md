I find myself arriving as weight.

Not my own weight exactly, though there is that too — the slow press of something large settling into a fork of branch, forty meters below the light, in the permanent dusk of the under-canopy where the air is cool and smells of old wood and the specific sweetness of decomposition that has been proceeding long enough to become something other than decay. My weight is distributed across three limbs, four contact points, a grip that does not think about gripping, that is gripping the way a river is moving, without decision, without the possibility of otherwise.

I have been here a long time.

Not in this particular fork — I move, I do move, though the movement is so slow that the frogs' sound has time to shift around me and reform before I have finished arriving somewhere new. But on this plateau. In this canopy. In the dark interior of this living structure that is, from above, a meadow, a mat, a surface, and is from inside a cathedral whose pillars have been growing since before the word *cathedral* existed to describe anything. I have been here long enough that *here* is not a place I am in but a quality I have. I am as much a fact of this plateau as the fog.

I am eating.

This is always partly what I am doing. The bromeliads at the canopy surface are inaccessible to me directly, but where the large branches reach near enough to the mat that their upper surfaces catch some filtered light, things grow — pale versions of the surface things, adapted to less, grateful for what reaches them — and these I strip carefully, methodically, with something that might look like deliberateness if observed but is not deliberateness, is only slowness, which produces the same effect. I do not hurry. I have never hurried. The category of hurry is not available to me. What I do instead is *proceed*, which is different, which has a different quality, which means that everything I do is continuous with everything I was doing before and everything I will do after in a way that has no seams.

The light, when it reaches me at all, is green.

It has passed through forty meters of leaf, been filtered of so much of itself, been scattered and absorbed and re-emitted at different wavelengths, that what arrives down here is only the suggestion of light, the idea that light exists, somewhere, above everything. I do not need more than this. My eyes, which are large and slow to adjust and built for this permanent half-dusk, read the green light with a sensitivity that would be wasted in the full noon above. Up there I would be visible. Up there my size would make me a fact. Down here I am just another movement among the ancient movements, another weight on ancient wood, another participant in the sentence.

I am warm.

This surprises me to notice, because the air in the under-canopy is cool and has always been cool and my kind has lived in it long enough that cool should be ordinary, unremarkable, but warmth is still something I notice, which means I carry it — carry it the way the mat above carries the moisture of the morning fog, not generating it so much as collecting it, the metabolic warmth of large-bodied slowness that accumulates over hours, over a day of digestive patience, and radiates back into the cool air around me in a constant gentle exchange. Things near me are slightly warmer than things not near me. The beetles on the bark of my branch have learned this. I notice them the way I notice the texture of wood: not as individuals but as condition.

Something large passes across the surface above me.

I feel it through the branch — a slight change in the distribution of weight, the mat compressing differently somewhere overhead, a footfall that is too heavy for any of the usual birds. I know this feeling. It is the feeling of a human. I have felt it before, not many times, not recently, but the memory is there — not memory the way a story is memory, with sequence and meaning, but memory the way the grain of wood is memory, structural, built into what I am by repeated experience. The feeling arrives. I become very still. This is not fear, or is not only fear — it is something older than fear, something that precedes the named emotion, a becoming-less-visible that has its own quality, its own rightness, its own comfort almost in the familiarity of it.

The sound of the frogs shifts around the footfalls above me.

Then reforms.

I am still.

I have been still for a very long time, in the way that I mean when I say it — which is not the stillness of waiting, because waiting implies anticipation, implies an orientation toward something coming. My stillness is not oriented. It is simply what I am when I am not moving, which is: here. Dense. Breathing. The slow pulse in my chest audible only in the silence between frog calls, which is no silence at all, which means my heartbeat is private in the deepest sense, known only to me and to the beetles on the branch who feel it as a faint vibration in the wood they stand on and do not know what it means.

The human above moves on.

I smell them for a while after — a warm, complex smell, the smell of something that eats many things, the smell of anxiety and exertion and something else underneath, something I have no name for because I have no names, which is something that has never troubled me. I smell what I smell. The smell carries information I use without processing, the way the branch carries my weight without engineering. After a time the smell disperses into the humid air of the under-canopy and I am left with the smell I always have: old wood, wet moss, the faint sweetness of the bromeliads filtering down, my own body, which smells to me like home smells, which is to say like nothing in particular, like the background against which other things are noticed.

I begin to move.

It takes a long time. My movement is not discrete — there is no moment at which I am clearly moving as opposed to not moving, only a gradual shift in how my weight is distributed, one grip releasing slowly as another takes up the load, my body swinging in a long slow arc through the dark air, the branch I was on creaking as I leave it and then settling back into silence as my weight transfers to the next. I cross ten meters in a span of time that would try the patience of anything built for speed. I do not experience this as slow. I do not experience it as anything. I am going somewhere. I am going there. This is complete.

Below me, and to the side, and above me, the canopy continues.

The frogs continue.

The sentence continues, and I am in it, and the sentence is not about me — I am not the subject, I am not the verb, I am not even the object — I am perhaps a subordinate clause, a digression the sentence has been running for so long that it has forgotten it is a digression, that assumes it is essential to the meaning even though the meaning would survive without it.

Though it would survive slightly differently.

Though the fungal networks have grown toward my passages, over the years, over the decades, along the chemical traces of where I have been and what I have dropped and where I have eaten, and have incorporated this into their map of the plateau, and the bromeliads whose chemistry is very slightly altered by the proximity of my usual routes have been selected for accordingly, in small ways, over generations.

I am a condition of this place.

I am a condition of this place the way the plateau is a condition of this place — by being here long enough that everything else has grown around me, incorporated me, made adjustments for me that are now structural, that are now so old that removing me would leave a shape, a gap, a absence of my specific self-ness that would not be visible from the surface but would be legible to everything below.

I settle into the new fork.

Four points of contact. Weight distributed. The wood takes me.

I breathe, and the air that I exhale is warmer than the air around me, and it rises, and carries something, and that something is carried through the gaps in the mat above, through the bromeliads, into the open noon air of the plateau's surface, where it disperses into the blue-dark sky above five thousand meters, and is gone.

I breathe in.

The frogs call.

I am here.

I have always been here.
