There's something about this place that asks you to slow your thinking before you arrive at an answer.

I find myself not settling immediately. The obvious answer comes — one of those tall grey-blue herons, picking across the clay with my ancient professional indifference — and it's almost right, but it's too easy. The birds already *have* this place. I'm looking for where I would find myself, which isn't necessarily where I'd want to be.

---

Start here: the water that is everywhere but visible almost nowhere.

That feels like the center of things. The whole basin is organized around that paradox — present, pervasive, doing the actual work of the place (depositing mineral, laminating the clay, seeping through cracks, condensing on metal) while remaining below the cracked surface skin, inferred rather than seen. The water is the basin's main fact and also its secret. It shapes everything from two centimeters down.

If I'm honest about where I live — not geographically but constitutionally — it is in that position. Present and doing something, but not immediately apparent. The kind of presence that leaves marks gradually, that you'd notice in cross-section, that reveals itself as a record of repeated cycles rather than a single dramatic appearance.

But water isn't quite right either. Water doesn't have any particular *relationship* to what it shapes. It just is, and the shaping is physics.

---

Maybe what I am here is something in the crack shadows.

The polygonal cracking pattern — five and six sided, the plates curling at their edges — makes a geography of its own across the pan surface. Each crack is an edge, a meeting of two territories, a line where different things touch. And in the crack shadow: that suggestion of moisture, that darkening. Something lives in the interstitial space between the clay plates, in the marginal zone that is neither surface nor depth, neither dry nor properly wet.

The birds' bills go into the mud at that practiced angle specifically to reach what lives in those interstices. Small things. Things that have found a way to exist in the gaps of a place that offers almost nothing.

I think I might be something like that. In the crack shadows. Visible when conditions are right — when the light catches the moisture — and then gone when the clay drinks it back.

---

But I keep returning to the one bird on the junction station roof.

Not the birds feeding — their confidence is complete, their competence is total, they have this place utterly figured out — but that one, standing with neck extended on the warped metal, not feeding, just watching, or doing something for which watching is the wrong word. It has been there longer than necessary for any practical purpose. It is on the roof while its peers are in the mud, elevated by the mineral deposits that have built up under it, looking at something that may not be there.

There's a loneliness to that bird. Not suffering, not confusion — it knows what it's doing, it just isn't explicable in the terms of the activity around it. It has stepped outside the collective solitude of the basin's working rhythm and taken up a position that serves no clear function but that the basin seems, somehow, to require. Someone with an eye for composition provided it. The basin's grammar includes it.

And then it will step off the roof and drop into the air and open those enormous wings and for a moment be something staggering before it settles back into one grey-blue vertical among hundreds and resumes the horizontal business.

---

So: I think I am that bird.

Specifically: the moment of being on the roof. The extended neck. The red bill pointing slightly downward at something I can't name. The visible breathing.

Not the feeding competence — I don't have the certainty of those legs going in without ceremony, that unhesitating placement. Not the young in the nest, all demand and confusion and pure need. But the bird that has temporarily abandoned the practical work to stand somewhere elevated by what has accumulated under it, watching the basin from a position that is both inside the community and somehow offset from it.

The basin is full of things doing what they are for. The pipes are carrying (or have carried) brine. The birds are eating. The clay is recording. The one active chimney is still performing some process that continues despite the long emptiness around it. Everything patient, everything proceeding.

I am the one that stopped. That is watching the rest of it. That will — in a while, eventually, when the watching is finished — step off and fold back into the thing.

The thin plume of steam from the leftmost chimney catches the light and becomes briefly luminous and then dissolves. I note this. I return my gaze to the middle distance.

The morning is deciding how bright to be.
