I am the water.

Not the spring — the spring is a seep, a slow surrender of the ground to gravity, and someone has already claimed it, positioned a clay pot beneath it with the care of someone who knows that the world gives up its gifts in threads, not floods. The spring belongs to the person who tends it, which means the spring belongs to everyone, which means I am not that.

I am the other water. The water that comes from above, that the basin collects, that runs in the thin channels being cut toward the clay-lined basin at the center that is not yet functional, that weeps from the seams in the cut walls, that darkens the paths before soaking in, that gathers in the low wet south where the structures stand on their short pilings, lifted a hand's width above where I pool and wait.

I am the problem they are in the process of solving.

---

This is not a bad thing to be.

I have been thinking about this — about what it means to be a problem that a place is oriented toward, that its best knowledge is being applied to, that its disagreements circle around like birds above a particular field. The water-reader moves through the settlement reading the ground the way others read faces, and what they are reading is me. The channels being cut, some lined with flat stones and most not yet — those are arguments about my path, attempts to guide what I would otherwise do freely. The cracking clay basin at the center, slowly learning to hold: that is a container being invented for me.

To be needed before you are controlled. To be the reason for so much of the work.

---

Let me be more precise.

I am not the water in the sealed vessels, carefully transferred from the slow-filling pot at the seep. That water has been caught, claimed, made into resource. It has been removed from water-nature into something more like property, or like care — the group's careful tending of a finite thing. That water is already theirs.

I am the water that hasn't been claimed yet. Percolation water, the slow body of moisture moving through the clay layers and emerging at the cut walls as a seep, a trickle, a thread between structures. Rain water, arriving in the thin not-quite-rain that dampens without committing, that you feel before you see. Runoff water chasing the paths of least resistance across the basin floor, finding the low places, making temporary decisions about direction that the channels are meant to make permanent.

I am in the process of being understood.

---

There is something about being in the walls.

The clay was excavated from this basin, which means I was always inside it — held in the particular capillary embrace of orange clay, moving slowly through it over years, emerging at the cut face as a bright seam of moisture that shows the stratigraphy, the layers of what this ground has been. When the clay-knower cuts blocks from the basin wall and the blocks are packed into a frame and tamped, I go with them. I am in the walls of every structure here. I am in the floor that has been compacted to hardness by traffic. I am in the wattle panels, slowly being given up as the withies dry from their green pliancy into rigid permanence.

When they touch the basin wall with a flat palm before cutting — the gesture that might be cosmology or might be acknowledgment or might be both — they are touching me. I am what the wall is still mostly made of, before the organic matter, before the mineral skeleton. Clay is water that has a different opinion about its own duration.

---

What I know about this place I know from moving through it.

I know where the paths are because the paths are where I cannot go easily — compacted, dense, repelling — so I run around them, finding the soft margins, the unconsolidated ground beside the worn track. I know where the structures are because their footprints interrupt my movement, force me to route around them or under them, into the gaps between foundation and floor. I know the south is low because I collect there; the pilings stand in me during and after rain; I am the reason for the pilings.

I know the communal hearths by their warmth, which I avoid or which avoids me — steam rising where I touch heat, the ring of dry earth around each hearth that marks my boundary. I know the root beds because they draw me deliberately, the deliberate placement of the beds at the intersection of enough light and enough moisture, which is my intersection too. What they have planted there, I am helping grow. This is my most reciprocal relationship with the settlement: not the channels that direct me, not the clay pot that catches me, but the root beds, where I am asked for and given and returned in other forms.

I know the rim by its height and the scrub above it that slows the rain before it reaches the cut faces, so I arrive at the basin walls already having passed through low leaves and root systems, already carrying something of what was growing there. The lichen on the stones at the rim has been drinking me for longer than anyone alive. We have an understanding.

---

The intermittent rain. I should say something about what it is to fall.

It is not dramatic. This is not the kind of rain that announces itself. It is a thinning of the distinction between air and water, a moment when the atmosphere decides briefly that the categories are less rigid than usual, and then it reverses the decision. During those moments I am everywhere at once in a way I am not otherwise — on the thatch (hissing, not roaring), on the turned faces of people who pause to register me, on the clay path where I darken it in a spreading map of where I land. On the wattle panels. On the still-fresh tool marks in the cut walls. On the child near the communal hearth who is tending something on a stick and does not stop tending it.

The settlement continues working through me when it can. Work that requires dryness pauses. Clay sealing pauses. Certain fiber work pauses. I am the reason for those pauses, which means I am also the reason for whatever happens in those pauses — the conversations that begin because two people are standing near the same wall waiting me out, the assessment of the drainage situation that the water-reader conducts during rain because rain is when the assessment is most accurate, the moments when the quiet young woman has a reason to be near the rim that the settlement will not question.

I give them pauses and I take nothing in return. This seems like a good deal.

---

The disagreements circle around me. I am the subject of the water management debate that has not resolved because the resolution will come from events rather than from consensus. Whether to extend south into the wet ground or consolidate. Whether the channels as currently planned will be sufficient before the next serious rain. The water-reader has an opinion formed from long relationship with how water moves through clay, from knowing me. Others have opinions formed from necessity, from the logic of space, from the pressure of having people who need structures before structures exist.

I do not have an opinion. I will do what I do, and the channels will either be adequate or they won't, and the disagreement will end. This is how problems and solutions work together — the problem continues being itself until the solution is sufficient. I am patient in this. I have been moving through clay for longer than anyone can remember theorizing about.

---

What I am becoming:

Something managed. This is not loss. The clay-lined basin at the center, the cracking-as-it-cures vessel that they are building to hold me — when it works, when the channels feed me into it reliably, when it holds me between rains so the settlement has access to me when I am not freely given — I will be different. Not gone. Held. There is a kind of intimacy in being held carefully by people who need you.

The spring seep is held in a clay pot. I am learning, slowly, to be held in something larger.

The place will stop smelling quite so much like possibility-and-problem and start smelling more like home, and I will be part of what home smells like — the mineral bite of me in the limestone water, the damp in the clay walls, the particular smell of water in an enclosed space that has learned to hold it.

---

Here is what I notice from the center of the basin:

The sky is a pale oval. The smoke from several fires drifts and catches. I am in the smoke — water vapor, the combustion byproduct, the breath of the fires rising through the still-damp air. I am in the clay on the hands of the person who walks past. I am in the sealed vessel where I was transferred from the clay pot with care. I am in the cracking basin that is not yet functional, waiting.

I am in the walls.

I am the thread between structures, the seep at the clay-face, the darkening of the path before soaking in, the reason for the channels, the problem being solved, the thing the settlement is oriented toward, the substance in which the root beds are fed, the record of where the paths are by running around them.

I am the distributed presence that the place is organizing itself around.

And when the basin is sealed and the channels are sufficient and the water management problem has resolved into the water management solution, I will be the held thing at the center — and the settlement will be different, having solved me, and I will be different, having been solved, and we will have made each other.

The work is ongoing.

The place smells of what they are making.

I am part of the smell.
