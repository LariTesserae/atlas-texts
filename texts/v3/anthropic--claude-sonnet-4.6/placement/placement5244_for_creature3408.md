I walk for a long time before I understand that I have arrived.

---

There is a moment—not a moment, a gradual thing that only becomes a moment in retrospect—when I realize I am not thinking about the walk anymore. The ground under me has gone from *terrain to be crossed* to *surface I am part of*, and I cannot find the transition. I am looking at the ash-grey stone, at a crack running through it filled with moss so particular and fine that I stop without deciding to stop, and I crouch, and I look.

This is what I do. I look at small things for a long time.

I am not one of the people of the settlement, though I know them. I am not quite a visitor, though I am not from here in any clean sense. I exist in the plateau's vocabulary for people who arrive slowly enough to be absorbed before being categorized—someone who came in the gradual way the plateau can be approached, who was therefore never foreign in the moment of arrival because there was no moment of arrival, only the retroactive understanding that I have been here long enough that *here* is what I mean when I think of where I am.

A naturalist is too formal. A recorder is too purposive. What I am is: someone who pays attention, specifically, carefully, to the things that do not announce themselves.

---

The moss is where I spend most of my time. Not moving through it—crouching at its edge, lying flat to look along its surface, watching the dew gather on the filaments in the hour before dawn from a distance close enough that my breath disturbs it, which means I learn to breathe very slowly in those hours, which means I have learned something from the moss about how much air is necessary and how quietly that amount can be taken in.

I carry things for writing. Not ink—ink behaves badly in the cold, thickening, clotting, requiring warmth from my hands to flow, and my hands are occupied with other things. I use a stylus pressed into wax that I keep inside my coat, against my body, using the warmth I am already producing. What I write is fragmentary and mostly useless and occasionally, when I read it back later, seems to have captured something true in the way that a shadow of a thing sometimes shows the thing's shape more clearly than looking at the thing directly.

What I write about the moss:

*It is not trying. That is the first thing. Whatever this is—this spreading through every available crack, this patient eating of the stone, this dew-collecting architecture of filaments finer than hair—it is not trying in the sense that effort requires. It is just doing what it does, the way a river is just doing what rivers do, except slower, except with no downstream, except going somewhere that is not a place but a condition.*

What I write about myself watching the moss:

*I have been here long enough that I no longer find this strange. That is the worry I cannot resolve. Am I learning something about the moss by being here long enough to find it ordinary, or am I losing the capacity to see it by becoming too calibrated to notice?*

The question does not get answered. I write it down and look at the moss some more.

The spatial fluctuations: I discovered these by accident, early on, before I had language for them. I was walking between two outcrops I had walked between before, counting my steps by habit—not purposefully, a habit from elsewhere, from a place where distances needed to be surveyed—and I counted thirty-seven steps, and I remembered counting twenty-two the previous day, and the outcrops were where they always are, and the ground between them was what it always is, and I stood in the middle of it for a long time before I did what I am most inclined to do: I wrote it down.

I have been writing it down ever since. The fluctuations do not resolve into a pattern, but they reveal a texture. Mornings after rain: the space is generous. Middays when the cloud cover breaks: the space tends to contract. The presence of the elder near the uncertain outcrops: not a reliable effect, but something. My own state of attention: possibly a variable, possibly projection.

I cannot determine what I am measuring or whether I am measuring it correctly or whether correct measurement is available to me as a methodology here. I write this down too. The notebook I keep for the spatial data has a section at the back where I write things that might be errors and a section in the middle where I write things that might be data and the sections have been expanding toward each other for some time and will eventually meet.

I do not know what I will write in the page where they meet.

---

The child who is learning the path: I watch her sometimes, from a distance that is respectful, which on this plateau means far enough that my watching does not add weight to what she is doing. She walks carefully, not because she has been told to be careful, but because her feet are finding something and she is listening to what her feet find. She does not know I watch. The people know I watch things—this is what I am, here, the one who watches—and they have calibrated their trust accordingly, have decided I am something that can be given access without it costing them anything they do not choose to spend.

I do not know what she will become. I know what the plateau will make of her, which is: someone who knows where to put her feet, who has the paths as a kind of skeleton beneath her ordinary movement, who will one day stand near an uncertain outcrop with the quality of attention that is not concentration and not calm and understand in her body, not her mind, what she is doing when she does this.

I wonder sometimes what I am making of her, or what the fact of her making is doing to me. Whether watching something become is its own kind of becoming.

I write this down, in the wax, with the stylus. I will read it back later and it will either mean something or it will be one of the things that seemed true in the cold and flattens in warmth, and I will find out which kind of thing it is when the weather changes.

---

The sound that is not a sound, the one that feels like enormous surfaces recognizing each other: I have heard it four times. Written down four times. I do not have better language for it now than the first time. What I have instead is the knowledge of having heard it four times—not pattern, not understanding, just accumulation, the fact of the fourth time changing the meaning of the first without clarifying it.

I think I am like this with everything here. Accumulating without synthesizing. Gathering without concluding. Every notebook I have filled on this plateau is full of careful particulars and empty of the general statement that should, in the practice I was trained in, eventually emerge from particular enough particulars.

The plateau is doing something to my epistemology. I am not certain whether it is damage or education.

---

Here is what I know about myself, here:

I wake before dawn, because the dew. I breathe slowly, because the moss. I walk the paths, because the paths, because the spatial fluctuations, because my body learned the feel of reliable ground and seeks it the way any body seeks what it has learned is reliable. I eat what the settlement offers, which is spare and preserved and made with a complexity of technique that compensates for the simplicity of available ingredients. I write things down. I look at things closely. I feel the wind as a constant low-frequency presence that I no longer hear as intrusion and now hear as—

I almost wrote *home*. That is either true or the plateau working on me.

I sit with it. In the cold, in the ash-grey light, in the specific lushness of a place that has had an enormous amount of time to perfect itself within severe constraints, I sit with the fact that I may have become someone for whom this plateau is home, or I may be a visitor who has been absorbed into the place's patience and therefore experiences himself as home when what he is experiencing is simply: stillness, achieved by long enough exposure to a still enough place.

I cannot tell the difference from inside. That is the key problem.

I look at the moss. The moss does not have this problem. The moss is not trying to determine whether it belongs here—it simply is here, doing what it does, the stone under it and the dew on it and the cold around it, and the distinction between the moss and what the moss is doing and where the moss is doing it has been eroding for longer than the distinction was ever clear.

Maybe I am becoming something like that. Maybe this is what happens to anyone who stays long enough.

I write: *the plateau works on you the way it works on everything. It does not hurry. It does not have to.*

The dew begins to form on the filaments. I breathe more slowly. I watch.
