The water comes to me first.

Not as sound—I've said already, or the valley has, that sound here is muffled, held by the ridges, absorbed through stone—but as a fact about my body. I am cold in the way that stone near water is cold. Not suffering-cold. Simply: this is what I am made of.

I am one of the pipes.

I know this slowly, the way you know a place slowly, by accumulating it. I run along the outside of the spinning house at head height, bracketed to the stone at intervals by iron fittings that have been there long enough to stain the wall beneath them, rust-orange lines descending like narrow tributaries. I carry warm water—the warm water that has been through some process and is returning, changed, toward the canal—and so I am the only thing on this wall that is above the ambient temperature of the valley, which means I am perpetually meeting the cold air with my warmth and giving up that warmth and the giving up is condensation, is the thin water that forms on my outer surface and gathers in the low points of my joints and drips.

I drip on the workers below. This is understood. Nobody resents it.

---

But I am also more than this one pipe. Or the pipe is more than I've said.

I am part of the aerial infrastructure. The tangle. The thing that looks, at first, like overgrowth—and that thought itself feels right, feels like a description of me, because I have grown. Not metaphorically. Over the decades that the valley has been building and adding and grafting new structure onto old, I have extended: a new run here where a process was relocated, a branch added when the dye works needed a feed, a section replaced when the original failed but the replacement slightly exceeding the original's reach, finding its way to a connection point that hadn't quite been there before. I connect buildings that no map drawn a hundred years ago would have shown as connected. I make relationships that nobody planned.

I cross the main lane on an iron bracket bolted through both facing walls. I have been crossed under by thousands of workers. Most of them do not look up. They know I am there the way they know the cobblestones are there—by long familiarity, by the pattern of not hitting their head. Occasionally: a new worker, from outside, who arrives with the full height of open air still in their posture, still moving as if the sky is available, and for these workers I am a discovery, a ducking, a moment of recalibration. Then they learn me. Then I become part of the space they move through without thinking.

---

I am thinking about what it means to carry.

I carry steam in one section, warm water in another, and in a third section—the older run, the one that predates the current management, that was part of a mill that no longer exists in its original form but whose infrastructure was kept because it was useful—I carry something I cannot quite name. Not liquid. Not gas. The weight of having been used for a very long time. The residue of previous contents. The pipe remembers everything that has passed through it, in the way that the valley workers' hands remember pigment that no washing removes.

The knowledge is in the stone. Yes. But also in the pipe. In the accumulated choices about where to run, where to branch, what to connect to what. I am a record of contingent decisions made over time, each one pragmatic, each one responding to a specific problem, and the totality of these decisions has produced a shape—my shape, the shape of the infrastructure—that nobody could have planned, that doesn't appear coherently on any drawing, that can be known only by someone who has walked it section by section and attended to what each section is doing and why.

There are, I think, two people in the valley who know me fully. One is very old and does not speak of it. One learned from the first.

---

When it rains, I am most myself.

The rain adds to the condensation. My outer surface runs. The drip from my low joints becomes something continuous. The workers in their capes move beneath me and the water off my surface adds to the water off the rooflines and the water rising as steam from the grates and everything is in motion and I am part of the system of water that is also the valley itself, that has been running here far longer than any mill—the hillside weirs gathering it, the canals channeling it, the wheel turning in it, the processes using and returning it, and me, the pipes, carrying it through walls and over lanes, connecting the buildings that without me would be only neighbors.

The wheel turns. The shaft turns. Something happens, three buildings away, because I carried what needed carrying.

The rain is warm tonight, for this valley. Nearly eight degrees. The moss on the retaining wall above is drinking.

I run with water. I have been doing this for a long time. I know how.
