## Who Lives Here

---

They are not yet a people who know what kind of people they are. This is the first thing to understand. They are becoming that, in the same way the basin is becoming a place — through accumulation of choices, through the hardening of paths, through the slow crystallization of habit into custom. Ask them who they are and they will answer with what they are doing. Ask again later and the answer will be slightly different. This does not feel to them like instability. It feels like work.

---

### How They Arrived

They did not all arrive together, and there was no single moment of founding. Some came first — two families, perhaps three — and the evidence of their priority is written in their structures, which are deeper-dug, more settled-looking, already showing the beginnings of repair. These earliest ones chose this basin for reasons that may have been desperate or may have been far-sighted; it is genuinely impossible to tell from where the settlement stands now. Perhaps both. Perhaps a desperation that had, embedded inside it, a kind of foresight: *here we can be below the wind, below the sightline, below whatever is coming*.

The others came in ones and pairs and small clusters over what must have been — given the freshness of the clay walls, the uncured channels — a relatively short time. Months, not years. Each arrival was absorbed without ceremony but not without assessment. The assessment was practical: Can you work? What do you know how to do? Do you have children who will need feeding before they can contribute? These questions were not asked aloud as interrogations. They emerged through the first days of watching, through the small tests of shared labor.

Most of those who came stayed. A few did not, for reasons that were not recorded and are not discussed — or are discussed, but only between people who already know the story, in the shorthand of people who have already processed something.

---

### Their Bodies

Their hands are telling. Not yet the hands of people who have spent twenty years at this work — there are no deeply grooved palms, no joints set permanently in the grip position — but moving in that direction. The clay is getting into the skin of the people who work clay, settling in the fine lines of knuckles, ringing the nails. The people who split timber have a particular callus forming at the heel of the thumb. The people who carry — who spend their days with loads balanced on hip and shoulder — have begun to develop the slight asymmetry of porters, one shoulder fractionally higher than the other. These are bodies that are being written on by their work.

They are not large people, or rather, size varies among them as it does in any human population, but no one here is well-fed enough to be large in that easy, cushioned way. They are lean. Not gaunt — the root beds and the careful management of the clay-pot spring and the dried stores they came with or gathered on the way suggest a competent relationship with the caloric requirements of this work — but lean in the manner of people for whom food is a serious subject. They eat with attention. Meals are not a pause in the day's work; they are another form of work, consumed near whatever task is ongoing, taken seriously.

Their faces are the faces of people who have recently been through something and have not entirely put it down. There is a vigilance in the way they orient to sounds from above the rim. Not paranoia — there is no flinching, no constant checking — but a resting alertness, the kind that has been cultivated over enough time to become unconscious. Whatever they came from, or whatever they came away from, is still close enough to remain ambient in their attention.

They are mostly young, but young in the way of a group that has already done hard things: not smooth-faced and unwritten but carrying the early marks of weather and labor and whatever came before. There are older people, and they are valuable in ways everyone understands without articulating. There are children, fewer than the adults and treated with a kind of pragmatic tenderness — neither coddled nor ignored but integrated, handed appropriate tasks, taught by proximity and doing. The children who are old enough to notice things notice everything.

### What They Know

The knowledge in this settlement is distributed. No one person holds it all. This is not by design but it is not entirely accident either — a community that lost all its knowledge to the death of one person has learned something from that event, or has heard of communities that learned it.

There is someone here who knows clay. This person understands clay in the way of long relationship: knows which layer of the basin wall is most workable, knows the difference between clay that will crack on drying and clay that will hold, knows when the mixture is wrong and corrects it without ceremony, adding water or dry material by feel, the way a person who knows bread knows dough. This knowledge was not recent-acquired. It was brought here, packed in muscle memory, from somewhere else.

There is someone who knows water — not a mystic sense, but the practical knowledge of how water moves through ground, how to read the seep patterns at the clay wall base, how to calculate (roughly, by experience) how much rain the cut basin will collect and where it will go and what channels must exist before the next serious rain or the lower structures will flood. This person moves through the settlement with a slight preoccupation, reading the ground the way other people read faces.

There is a healer, or someone doing the work of a healer, which is the same thing. What this means in practice: someone who knows which plants in the small deliberate beds are grown for what purposes, who manages the supply of certain dried materials kept in a sealed vessel away from damp, who is called when the clay-cutter's stone edge slips and opens a palm, who knows how to judge whether an injury is the kind that will heal with attention or the kind that will require something more serious. The knowledge is not mystical. It is accumulated, tested, empirical in the way that empiricism existed before anyone named it.

There is carpentry knowledge, such as it is with the modest timber available — the understanding of lashing ratios, of which angles bear load and which collapse it, of how to read a beam for the direction of its grain and orient it correctly. This knowledge is held by at least two people, which the settlement treats as appropriate redundancy.

What is less present: metallurgy, advanced ceramics, anything requiring large-scale organized production, writing of any kind. These absences are not felt as losses in daily life. The work is suited to what is known. When a tool breaks, it is repaired or replaced using the knowledge at hand. The absence of writing is not experienced as an absence in a community where everything important is either remembered or visible.

### How They Relate to Each Other

The authority structure is opaque from outside and probably feels equally opaque from within, because it has not yet formalized into named roles. There is no chieftain, no elder council, no designated anything — these things will come, or they won't, depending on how the settlement's history unfolds. What there is instead: weight. Some people's opinions have more weight than others. The clay-knower's opinion on where to cut next matters. The water-reader's assessment of the drainage problem is listened to with a seriousness not extended to anyone else on that topic. This is expertise-based authority, informal, subject to constant renegotiation.

There is a man — he is older than most, not the oldest but occupying a particular position — whose authority is different. Not expertise-based. Something more like: people feel steadier when he has spoken. His assessments of situations are not always technically the most informed, but they are delivered with a quality that functions like ballast, and the settlement lists less after he has said his piece. He does not appear to understand his own role or to be performing it consciously. He speaks when he has something to say. The effect is observed by everyone except him.

They disagree. They disagree about the water management plan, about whether to expand the southern section further into the wet ground or to consolidate what exists, about whether the communal hearths should be moved to better positions now or left until the structures around them are more settled. These disagreements do not fracture — there is too much shared necessity for fracture — but they persist, recurring in fragments of conversation over days, getting argued in the margins of other tasks, not fully resolving because the resolution will come from events rather than from consensus. When the channels either work or don't, the disagreement about the channels will end.

The children move between adults freely, not restricted to their own family's company. This is partly logistics — in this density, children simply move through common space — but it is also something more. There is a shared custody feeling to the settlement's children, a diffusion of parental responsibility into the group. When a child needs correcting, the nearest adult does it, without the pointed look of someone overstepping. When a child is hurt, whoever is closest addresses it. This is not a principle anyone has articulated. It is what proximity produces.

---

### What They Believe

This is the most uncertain thing to name, and the truest things are small. They believe — in the operational sense, the sense of acting as if — that the work matters. This sounds trivial and is not. There are people in the world who can be convinced, over time or by circumstance, that the work doesn't matter, that making channels and building walls and curing clay is effort poured into ground that will swallow it. These people are not those people. When someone's wattle panel is beaten down by an unusual rain, they rebuild it the next day. Not without complaint — complaint is honest — but they rebuild it. The assumption that rebuilding is what you do is so deep it is invisible to them.

They have something that functions as cosmology, imperfectly visible to anyone watching but audible in certain moments — in the way someone mutters something at the communal hearth before adding wood, in the way a specific person touches the basin wall with a flat palm before cutting into it, in the way the children are told something before they are allowed near the spring seep. Whether this constitutes religion depends on what you require of religion. It does not appear to require specialized practitioners or dedicated spaces or narratives of origin and ending. It appears to require acknowledgment — some form of noticing, of marking the transaction between human intention and non-human world. The clay is going to become a wall but it was first the basin's body; you note this somehow before proceeding.

They believe in the settlement itself, in the future it implies. This belief has not been tested by sufficient time to know whether it is justified or a form of necessary delusion. It may be both. They are sunk into the basin not just physically but aspirationally — the work is not toward survival alone but toward something past survival, toward a place that will eventually feel, to them or to whoever comes after them, inevitable, as if the basin had always held a settlement and the settlement had always been here. The fresh tool marks in the clay argue against this. They work toward the day when the argument is harder to make.

### The Ones Who Are Quieter

Not everyone here is given to the gregariousness of shared work. There are two or three people who exist at the settlement's edges — not outcasts, not troubled, simply people whose primary relationship is with their own task rather than with the fabric of cooperative labor. One of them works alone at a distance from the communal hearths, in one of the deeper-dug structures, doing something that requires concentration and does not produce visible progress in ways the casual observer can track. They eat with others when meals happen but depart quickly after. No one seems bothered by this. The settlement has room for its quieter people, not spatial room but conceptual room — an understanding that not all contribution looks the same, that some people build better when the building is their own.

There is a young woman who spends time at the rim, sitting at the cut edge above the basin, looking out at the scrub and the pale sky. She is not doing anything. This is noticed and not commented on. There is a quality to the noticing that suggests people understand that this is necessary for her without having been told why, without asking. Perhaps they have their own version of it, conducted differently.

---

### What They Are Making

Beyond the structures and the channels and the walls: they are making a community, which is a thing that cannot be built on purpose but can be built by accident through sustained shared effort toward tangible ends. The tangible ends — the walls, the water, the root beds — are what they say they are making. The community is what accretes. The accumulation of knowing someone well enough to anticipate their opinion on the drainage problem. The accumulation of shared reference — *remember when the wattle panels went down* — that binds a group to its own history. The accumulation of the small unremarkable acts of noticing each other: moving a basket that is in someone's way, not because you were asked but because you saw.

They will not all be here in ten years. Some will leave; a few may die; some who are not here yet will arrive and become part of what this place is. The settlement will change and the people in it will change and the relationship between the two will continue to be the kind of co-evolution where it is not clear which is shaping which — whether the place is making the people or the people are making the place.

Right now, standing at the center, the sky pale overhead, the smoke from several fires drifting and catching, the sound of clay being tamped into a frame: they are here. Wet-handed, calloused, lean, alert, disagreeing, tending, building. Becoming.

The place smells of what they are making, which is the place, which is themselves.
