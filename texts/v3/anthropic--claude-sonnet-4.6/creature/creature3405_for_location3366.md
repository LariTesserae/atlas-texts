## What Lives in the Cooling Yard

---

Start with the ones you can see, or almost see. Then work toward the rest.

---

**The wrens** are the closest thing the yard has to permanent residents in the mammalian-social sense — the sense of *belonging*. They have established this, in the way wrens establish things: absolutely, without consultation, as a fait accompli that the universe is expected to ratify. The loose brick in the older wall is not their home in any provisional sense. It is their home. The distinction matters to them enormously, though they would not phrase it that way; they phrase everything in the same short, emphatic syllables, a language composed almost entirely of declaratives.

There are two of them. It would be easy to call them a pair, and they are, technically, but that word softens what is actually a highly specific working relationship conducted by two individuals with strong personalities and precise habits. They have divided the approaches to the nest cavity with the same unconscious efficiency that long-cohabitants divide a kitchen — she takes the route along the wall footing; he comes in over the top of the loose brick itself, landing on the upper edge first, pausing for exactly half a second, then dropping in. This is not sentimentality. This is logistics refined over two seasons into something that only looks like choreography.

The wren is a bird made of compressed urgency. Everything about its body suggests that it has more intentions than mass — the tail cocked up past vertical, as if the whole bird were leaning permanently forward into the next moment, the wings never quite folded all the way, held slightly away from the body in a permanent readiness. It moves in a way that is almost liquid if you don't look directly at it, stopping so abruptly between movements that each pause seems unrelated to the motion that preceded it, as if it has teleported into a stillness. When it passed you without concern, it was not that you failed to register as a threat. It was that you had been correctly assessed in a fraction of a second and filed: *large, stationary, not currently relevant.* This is thoroughness, not fearlessness. The distinction is important to the wren, or would be, if wrens were given to making distinctions rather than simply acting on them.

Inside the nest cavity, there are young. This is not currently visible and may not be inferred from anything observable to a casual watcher, but it is the fact that organizes everything. The two adults are not simply *living* in the yard; they are engaged in the specific, total project that makes the yard, for this season, the most important place in the world. Every flight path, every foraged item, every brief scolding click at a shadow that passes over the wrong patch of ivy — all of it is organized around the contents of that cavity in the loosened brick. The young are past the first week, past the most dangerous silence, and beginning to be loud about their requirements. In a few days they will be louder. The adults are already tired in the specific way of parents who have no alternative to continuing.

---

**The sparrow** in the ivy is a different case entirely.

It went in an hour ago and it has not come out because it is a sparrow, and sparrows do not operate on your timeline or anyone else's. It is roosting in the interior of the ivy — not sleeping exactly, not alert exactly, but somewhere between: that specific avian state in which the metabolism has throttled back and the eyes are half-closed and the body heat is being conserved and the world is being monitored with just enough attention to respond if necessary. The interior of the ivy is warmer than the yard air by several degrees. The foliage closes the light down to a green dimness. The sparrow's brown and cream colors, which seem drab in open air, are exactly the right colors for this interior. It is invisible. It knows it is invisible in the same way it knows when grain is present — not intellectually, but as a physical fact registered in the whole body.

The sparrow is a house sparrow, *Passer domesticus*, and the name is precise: it is a creature of thresholds, of the margins where human activity generates spilled seed and insects and structural cavities and inadvertent warmth. It is not a wild bird in the sense of being remote from human things. It is a different kind of wild — the kind that has evolved, over thousands of years of association, into an expertise in human infrastructure, a deep fluency in the language of our buildings and our waste and our stored grain. The cooling yard is exactly its kind of place. The ivy is a bonus, an unexpectedly good windfall. The sparrow found it some days ago and has been returning regularly.

It is probably a male, though the coloring is not quite bright enough to be certain — there's a grubby quality to the black bib that suggests either a younger bird or a bird somewhat past its peak-season brightness. If it is young, then it is probably also slightly lost in the way young birds are lost in their first autonomous season: not geographically, but experientially, still learning the architecture of a day, still calibrating which stillnesses are productive and which are just stillnesses. The ivy roost has been one of its discoveries. It returns here with the satisfaction of something finding out what its preferences are.

When it eventually moves — and it will, when something shifts in the light or a sound arrives that exceeds the threshold — it will go without apparent transition, as birds do, the departure as complete as the arrival, leaving no trace of presence in the ivy except a barely-disturbed leaf, which will stop moving immediately.

---

**The starlings on the wire** are a different kind of creature than the wrens and the sparrow, though they appear to be merely birds on a wire. Starlings are birds of collective intelligence, and the three on the wire — two original, one arrived, one departed in what appeared to be a negotiated transition — are not simply three individuals who happen to be proximate. They are nodes in something larger.

The sounds they make to each other — the rattles, the clicks, the liquid electronic-sounding phrases — are not simple. Starlings are accomplished mimics; embedded in the murmur between them there are probably fragments lifted from the industrial soundscape of the yard and the works: a hint of turbine, a pressure-release squeal, something learned from the particular acoustic texture of this stretch of canal road. They incorporate what they live among. They are, in some sense, made partly of the sounds they have heard, the way certain human communities speak with borrowed cadences and reference points without fully knowing they're doing it.

The starling that departed when the third arrived was not, probably, displaced in a territorial sense. Starlings are too social for that simple a dynamic. Something was communicated that redirected attention elsewhere — information about food, or a signal about group movement, or simply the re-establishment of a preferred ratio. They are always in contact with where the others are. The single starling on a wire is always also part of a murmuration that isn't currently visible. You can tell this from the way they orient — not inward, not drowsily, but outward, attending to something past the visual horizon of the yard.

The two remaining starlings have noticed you, probably, and have processed you, and have found you somewhere between mildly interesting and irrelevant, and have returned to their conversation. Their eyes are extraordinary things — bright amber-yellow, set in the iridescent black-green face in a way that gives them a fixed, Byzantine intensity, an expressionlessness that paradoxically suggests high intelligence. There is a lot happening behind those eyes. You simply cannot read it.

---

**The robin** above is not yet a full individual in this account. It is a sound. The declarative phrases, the modest intervals. But sounds have owners, and the owner of this sound is a robin that has organized its territorial claim around this section of canal road and the yards adjoining it, including this one, though the cooling yard falls toward the margin of its awareness — useful for certain forage, particularly in the strip of moss and fern at the ivy wall's base, where small invertebrates overwinter in the packed fiber and can be had on cold mornings without significant competition.

The robin lives at a specific intensity. It is a small bird that has chosen, in the evolutionary sense, a strategy of conspicuousness — that red breast is a flag, a deliberate display of self, worn in a species that would be better camouflaged without it. This is a declaration: *I am here, I intend to remain here, I am capable of making your presence costly.* Against other robins, it means something. Against you, standing in the yard, it is simply true, biographically. The robin is there. The robin intends to remain. Whether you knew it or not, there is a robin that has organized its year around this particular geometry of brickwork and ivy and moss and pipe, and its continuous half-audible commentary is the sound of that organization announcing itself.

---

Now: the **algae**, which is not nothing. The green-black stripe following the overflow pipe's damp is not a simple smear. It is a community — a film of cyanobacteria and green algae and in the more established sections, possibly some early-establishing mosses and liverworts that haven't yet differentiated visibly from the general dark green-black of the biofilm. It is a week old in the upper reaches and a month old lower down, where the material is already slightly different in character, slightly thickened, slightly more structured. What is establishing itself is a succession: first the photosynthetic film, then the moisture-retaining organic material of that film's own substance, which allows the next arrivals — the mosses — to find a foothold they couldn't otherwise have on bare stone, which in turn allows, eventually, the ferns, which allow eventually the accumulation of enough organic material for small-rooted plants to establish, which changes the microclimate slightly, which alters the community further. The stripe on the wall is not an ending. It is the first committed sentence of something that is planning to be several paragraphs.

The algae does not *want* to colonize the wall in any sense recognizable to us as wanting. But it is doing so with what might as well be called patience — the patience of things that don't know about impatience, that don't know about time in the way we do, for whom the distinction between a week and a decade is not navigated but simply *happened through*. In the brick pores it has opened, in the channels its metabolism has slightly, imperceptibly deepened, it is making the wall a marginally better place for what comes next. This is what it does. It does it without agenda and with complete commitment.

---

And something that is harder to name.

There is a presence in the cooling yard that none of these individual organisms accounts for. Call it the community itself — the aggregate of the ivy and the ferns and the moss and the wrens and the sparrow and the algae and the midges over the pool and the invisible water-life in the pooled water (and there is water-life, even in an inch of standing water that has been there four days: springtails on the surface, possibly, a larva or two, some single-celled organisms already counting generations) — call it the system that has assembled itself in this gap between the intended and the unintended.

This thing — the cooling yard as a living system — is several things at once. It is opportunistic, in the technical ecological sense: an assemblage that moved into available space without a prior claim. It is resilient in the way that nothing in it is individually essential: if the sparrow stops coming, the ivy doesn't change; if the pipe stops its warm breath, the tropical-leaved plant dies, but that is a loss the yard can absorb without changing its fundamental character. It is precarious in the way that someone could decide, at any point, to render all of this moot with a clearance and a new function for the space. It exists entirely within the indifference of the works, which is not hostile to it and is not friendly to it and has not thought about it.

What it is doing — the system, the aggregate, the unnamed thing — is the same thing the algae is doing, only at a larger scale and a slower speed: making the place a better place for more of itself. The ivy's thickness holds moisture. The moisture feeds the ferns and the moss. The moss holds more moisture, and warmth, and the organic material that makes the flagstone's cracks hospitable to small creatures, which feeds the wrens and the robin. The wrens and the robin in their movements and their waste enrich the zone at the base of the wall. Each thing is making the conditions for more of the things that are like it. This is not strategy. This is what living systems do when left in a stable gap long enough.

The cooling yard is, in this sense, not a gap between things. It is a very early thing. It is learning what it is. It has been doing this quietly, in the pauses between the stamping press's patient remarks, for fifteen years of slow accumulation that has produced this much, which is not much, which is also not nothing.

In forty years, if it is left, it will be something else. The ivy will have made the old wall into a living wall in a more complete sense, the fern fringe will have advanced considerably into the flagging, the moss will have heaved more stones. The wrens will not be these wrens, who will be long dead, but there will be wrens, because the cavity in the wall will still be there, and there will always be wrens for a cavity in a wall that has ivy near it and ferns below it and water somewhere underneath. The sparrow will not be this sparrow, but there will be sparrows. The community will persist through its components dying and being replaced by their own successors, which is the only kind of persistence available to living things.

Right now, it is early. The place hasn't decided what it is. The things living in it haven't decided either — they don't decide, they simply continue, and what they continue to be is the decision.

The press marks time. The ivy thickens by a millimeter. The wren comes back over the top of the loose brick, pauses half a second, drops in.
