# What Lives in the Flood Seam

---

## The Frogs

Start with the frogs, because you cannot not start with the frogs. They are prior to everything else you will notice. They are prior to your noticing.

There are perhaps four thousand of them in this basin at any given time during the calling season, and the calling season is long — longer than it has any right to be in a temperate climate, stretched by the basin's warmth and the reliability of the seeps, which do not dry in summer because they are not fed by surface water and do not freeze in winter because they emerge from below the frost line. The basin offers what frogs require most: permanent wet ground, still air that holds humidity against loss, and the acoustic architecture that makes calling productive. A frog calling in an open field is making a small private gesture. A frog calling in the basin is participating in something. The basin does not merely amplify the call — it retains it, recirculates it, makes it available to every other frog in the acoustic field. A call that would travel thirty meters in open country travels the whole basin here, doubled back on itself, still legible at the margins. The calling season extends because it is effective, and it is effective because the basin makes it so.

The species are several. What they share is more important than what distinguishes them.

Along the seep-channels and the emergent points at the base of the rock wall, there are small frogs no longer than a thumb-joint, their skin the precise color of the dark clay they sit on — a greenish-brown that is neither green nor brown but the exact median of those colors at the exact saturation of wet mineral soil in low light. They are not hiding. Camouflage implies a threat to hide from, and the basin has few aerial predators — the still air and enclosed topography discourage raptors, the dim light discourages herons who want to see into water before they commit to it. The frogs are the color of the clay because they have been shaped by the clay over long association, color as consequence rather than strategy, and they sit openly on the seep-edges with their throats working. Each throat-pulse moves the whole small body slightly. They are putting significant effort into the calling and they look like it. Their rear feet rest flat against the clay in a posture that is not quite sitting and not quite crouching — a readiness that is also a rest, a position that can become a leap without preparatory movement.

When you approach they do not leap immediately. They wait until you are close enough that the ground vibration of your footfall reaches them through the clay — not through the air, through the substrate, which conducts vibration the way wet material conducts everything — and then they are gone so quickly that you cannot process the moment of departure. The clay holds the print of where they were: a small oval of slightly-compressed material, the ghost of a frog's underside.

Deeper in the rush-clumps, different frogs. These are larger, heavier-bodied, the kind of frog that overwinters in the substrate and has done so here long enough that the substrate's microbial particularity has become part of their biochemistry. Their skin is mildly rough, warted in the technical sense though not grotesquely so — small raised nodes that increase surface area for the cutaneous respiration that frogs perform in winter when lung-breathing ceases. The coloration is darker here in the shaded rush-bases, nearly black on the dorsal surface, the belly a pale mineral-tan, almost the color of the rock face on a dry day. They call less frequently than the smaller frogs but more powerfully, each call a deep resonant pulse that the basin holds like a bell holds a tone. They are patient callers, spaced in time rather than volume, and the pauses between their calls are filled by everything else.

The calling happens at all hours but it is not constant at the individual level — it is constant at the aggregate level because there are always some frogs calling, the population cycling through calling and silence on individual schedules that produce continuous collective sound through the statistical impossibility of simultaneous silence. Now and then — rarely, briefly — something like a wave of quiet passes through the basin, a momentary phase synchronization that no one has organized, and you hear the seep-sound, the base layer, the earth exhaling, before the calling resumes.

They are not performing for you. They have not noticed you as a category. They have noticed you as a vibration, a warmth differential, a shadow, and filed this information under *not immediately relevant*, which is the largest category in a frog's cognitive architecture. You are present in their world as a change in local conditions. They are adjusting.

The tadpoles are in the seep-channels and in the standing-water patches, in concentrations that look like density itself — hundreds of small commas, dark-headed, actively feeding on the algal film, their tails moving in the lazy lateral oscillation that is the first motion the vertebrate body learned. They are not yet legs and lungs and calling. They are processing, growing, consuming the algal film at the margins of their pools at a rate that the algal film can sustain because the algal film grows as fast as it is consumed at this light level. The tadpoles and the film have reached an arrangement. The arrangement is invisible — it looks like the tadpoles simply eating and the film simply being there — but it is the result of population pressures calibrating themselves over many breeding seasons, the tadpole density settling at the level the food supply sustains.

When the seep-channel dries in a dry year — and it does dry, partially, in the driest years — the tadpoles in the affected stretch are lost. This happens. The frogs have metabolized this fact into their reproductive strategy: lay more eggs than the average year requires, because some years are not average. The excess hatches into an excess of tadpoles and the population absorbs the loss of a bad year from the surplus of a good year. It is not planning. It is the shape that survival takes when survival requires it.

## The Insects of the Larval Phase

The water in the seep-channels is the nursery. It is not deep or fast or well-oxygenated in the conventional sense, and so it does not support the fast-water insects — the stonefly nymphs, the trout-water creatures that require current and gravel. What it supports are the tolerant, the adapted, the species that have learned to use warm, still, sediment-rich water for the larval phase and air for the adult phase, cycling between two environments neither of which is entirely hospitable to the other half of themselves.

Midge larvae in the sediment — thousands of them, some species bright red from hemoglobin adapted to low-oxygen conditions, others the pale of undifferentiated matter. They live in the fine sediment at the seep-channel margins, processing organic material through their bodies, converting it into the nutrient form that the channel's food web can use. They are doing what decomposers do, but they are doing it while still alive and growing, which creates a productive ambiguity in their ecological role. Decomposer and consumer and eventually prey and then, in the flying phase, prey in the air and pollinator and themselves consumers of whatever the air offers.

When they emerge from the larval phase they emerge in numbers and in coordination — not planned coordination, temperature coordination, a shared developmental threshold crossed when the air warms past a certain point on a certain kind of morning, and suddenly the seep-channel surface is alive with emergence, the pupal cases splitting and floating and the winged adults rising from the water in a column of thousands, a loose helix above the channel, catching the low-angle light from the wet floor, visible as a flickering. They do not swarm in the aggressive sense. They swarm in the numerical sense. The air immediately above the channel is full of them for an hour, two hours, and then they disperse into the basin's still air and become part of the texture.

Diving beetles in the deeper portions — the ten-centimeter standing water patches that persist longest after rain, the small depressions where seeps collect before they absorb downward. These beetles are predators, oval-bodied, iridescent green-black, moving through the water with the smooth efficiency of things that have solved locomotion. They carry an air bubble under their wing-cases against the underside of their abdomen, a small breathing apparatus, and they replenish it by surfacing and pressing the bubble against the air before submerging again. They eat tadpoles. They eat other insect larvae. They are the top predator in the standing-water patches and they wear this role with the absence of drama that characterizes all apex predators in very small systems — there is no display, no competition, only the smooth competent arrival and the small violence and the departure.

At night — though the basin does not experience night dramatically, the dim becoming slightly more dim rather than becoming dark — moths come to the single willow. They are small, gray, the kind of moth that is entirely overlooked except when you look directly at it on bark and see suddenly that the bark is covered with small gray moths, each one sitting with wings flat and camouflaged, each one not-bark in a way that only becomes visible in aggregate. They are feeding on the willow's small leaves. They will lay eggs on the willow's branches. The larvae will feed on the willow's leaves. The willow persists regardless because it is four meters tall and has deep roots into the wet substrate and it can afford to lose some leaves. The relationship is old. The willow has not been stripped. The moths have not been starved. They are at a balance point that looks like stasis and is actually continuous negotiation.

## The Things in the Substrate

This is harder to describe because it requires inverting your usual relationship with the visible.

The substrate — the dark mineral paste, the half-formed soil, the wet clay that gives underfoot and returns slowly — is the most densely inhabited zone in the basin. This is counterintuitive. It looks like a place things might be moving through, might be avoiding. It looks hostile to soft bodies. It is not. It is a medium, a matrix, a world that is all interstitial space and water and organic material at the correct temperature for the metabolism of small things.

The nematodes: there are more species of nematode in this substrate than there are species of everything else in the basin combined, probably. This is not specific to this basin — it is specific to all soil, to all substrate, to the fact that nematodes are the most species-diverse animals on earth and live in a dimension of scale that makes the surface world look barren. They are between half a millimeter and a millimeter long, transparent or near-transparent, and they move through the water films between soil particles in a characteristic S-curve undulation. You cannot see them without magnification. They are present in every gram of wet substrate at densities of hundreds to thousands per gram. The basin floor, experienced at the nematode scale, is a world so densely inhabited that the frogs and the insects and the single willow are almost irrelevant perturbations — surface events on a substrate civilization that has been running since before the basin was a basin.

Among them: predatory nematodes that eat other nematodes, bacterial-feeding nematodes that eat the bacteria that are eating the organic material, fungal-feeding nematodes that eat the fungal hyphae threading through the soil matrix. The food web of the substrate is entire and complete and has no relationship to the food web of the surface except at the margins, where a frog's feet compress the clay and a tadpole's body becomes part of the organic material and a root pushes down and exudes the chemicals that change what bacteria grow near it.

Oligochaetes — small, thin worms, relatives of earthworms but adapted to saturated conditions where earthworms cannot function, where the oxygen in the soil is low and the drainage is nonexistent. They are working through the substrate like the substrate's own digestion, passing material through their bodies and depositing it slightly redistributed. The soil that forms here, slowly, mineral and biological combining into something with structure, will owe something to their passage. They are writing the soil's future in the way that all low animals write the future, by moving through the present.

The bacteria and the archaea are not animals but they are the ground condition for all of this. The archaea in particular are relevant here: this is the kind of wet, low-oxygen, warm substrate where methanogenic archaea thrive, converting organic material to methane in the anaerobic depths below the surface centimeters. The methane produces the faint sulfurous quality in the air, the slightly cloying mineral note that you register as thickness when you breathe. You are breathing the exhalation of archaea that have been doing this work, in places like this, since before multicellular life existed. The continuity is unmeasurable. The archaea predate the concept of this basin's geology. They will be doing this in some descendant place long after the basin has filled in, compacted, become something that is no longer a basin.

## The Willow

The willow is not only a plant. It is an address. Things live *at the willow* in the way that things live *at the water hole* in other ecosystems — not because they have chosen the willow specifically but because the willow is the only place in the basin with the particular conditions that certain relationships require.

In the small-country soil beneath the willow — the pocket of actual organic accumulation held together by root mass — there are earthworms, real earthworms, the kind that require soil with structure and drainage, which the willow provides in this one spot and nowhere else in the basin. They are large by the basin's standards, fat-bodied, brick-red, working through the organic layer beneath the willow's root zone. They do not range into the surrounding paste-clay. They have found the only place in the basin they can inhabit and they are inhabiting it with the focus of things that know their range is constrained.

In the willow's upper branches there is a bird's nest. Or there is what remains of a bird's nest — a cup of woven grass and rush stems, built during the breeding season, now empty and beginning to weather into its components. The bird that built it is a small, nervous species, reed-adjacent, the kind of bird that spends most of its life within two meters of the ground and moves through vegetation rather than over it, threading between rush stems and willow branches with a speed and accuracy that looks magical to anything working at human scale. It built the nest in the willow because the willow is the only elevated structure in the basin and the nest required elevation — not for any sentimental reason but because elevation above the saturated floor is the only protection available against the flooding that occurs after heavy rain, the shallow skin of water that covers the whole basin floor for hours before it descends.

The bird is here now, though not in the nest. It is moving through the rush-clumps in the low registered way, hunting the midges and small invertebrates that the wet margins produce in abundance. You can hear it occasionally — a thin, sharp note, contact call, the kind of sound a bird makes to locate itself relative to a mate without announcing itself to anything else. The note fits into the acoustic mass of the basin the way a single instrument fits into an orchestra: distinguishable if you are listening for it, absorbed if you are not.

The bird has been coming to this basin for all of its four years. It knows the basin the way an animal knows a territory: as a set of reliable associations, particular places that reliably produce food, the specific acoustic qualities that tell it, from the air, that this is the basin and not some other wet ground. It does not know it is in a basin that is still becoming. It knows it is in a place with permanent water and abundant insects and one tree for nesting and no predators it cannot hear coming, because the basin's acoustic properties also serve as an alarm system: anything larger than a weasel moving through the rush-clumps generates enough sound that every frog and every bird knows something is happening.

## The Larger Presences

There is a heron who visits but does not stay. It arrives in the early morning, when the dim is at its most even and the frogs are at their densest at the seep-margins. It is a gray heron, tall and gray and still, standing at the basin edge — at the gradient where the ground becomes less confident — with the absolute stillness that herons achieve and that is not peace but predation sustained over time. It stands and the frogs near it freeze. The frogs at a distance continue calling. The heron's head moves in the slow, isolated rotation of something that is looking with its whole skull rather than just its eyes, finding depth with a brain calibrated to find frogs in low light. It catches perhaps three or four frogs in a visit. Then it leaves, the wide wings opening with a sound like a slow exhalation, a few strong beats, the dark shape becoming the sky.

After it leaves the frogs near the seep-edges resume calling. The resumption is not gradual. It is immediate, as though a switch was thrown, as though the frog-chorus was suspended rather than ended. The acoustic mass reconstitutes itself in seconds. The heron's visit is absorbed.

There are water shrews in the seep-channels — small, with dense velvet-dark fur that traps air and keeps them dry while they move through the shallow water, hunting the aquatic invertebrates with a speed and aggression entirely out of proportion to their size. They are not rare but they are rarely seen because they are fast and they are the color of the wet substrate and they move through the vegetation at the edge of the channel rather than through open water. What you notice first, if you are patient and very still at the channel edge, is a movement that is too fast to be amphibian — a small dark shape that appears and disappears through a gap in the rush-stems. Then nothing. Then the gap again. Then nothing.

They eat enormously relative to their size — they cannot afford not to, the metabolism of a small warm-blooded animal in wet conditions demanding constant fuel. They are consuming somewhere between half and their full body weight in invertebrates every day, and the basin supports this because the basin produces invertebrates continuously and abundantly, the seep-channels churning out midges and beetles and amphipods at the rate that reliable water and organic sediment produces them. The water shrew is thin and fierce and moves through the basin like something that has no margin.

## The Thing the Basin Is Making

There is a life here that does not belong to any individual creature and cannot be located in any specific body. It is the life of the basin itself, which is not mysticism but ecology — the set of relationships and cycles and feedbacks that constitute what this place is and does.

The frogs eat the insects and produce waste that enriches the substrate; the enriched substrate feeds the bacteria; the bacteria feed the nematodes; the nematodes feed the water shrews; the water shrews die and become organic material; the organic material feeds the bacteria again, and the algal film, and the tadpoles, and the willow's roots. The seeps carry dissolved minerals from the rock face and deposit them in the clay; the clay's mineral content determines what bacteria can thrive; the bacteria determine what nematodes can thrive; the nematodes determine what plants' roots can access, whether the willow's rootzone can expand. The algal film produces oxygen into the water and into the air; the oxygen allows the larger organisms to breathe; the larger organisms' breathing cycles carbon into the substrate.

It is a system that is almost closed. Almost complete. The seeps bring input from outside and the heron brings output to outside and the bird whose nest is in the willow ranges beyond the basin to find material for the nest and returns. But these are small exchanges relative to the basin's internal cycling. This place is doing most of its own work. What enters, stays. What is consumed, returns. The basin is approaching, slowly, the condition of a fully self-sustaining system, the kind of ecological maturity that builds the soil it requires and moderates the conditions it requires and produces the food it requires, all internally, all continuously.

It is not there yet. The soil is young. The food web has gaps — there are no large predators, no wolves or foxes visiting with regularity, the top trophic level that full ecological maturity requires. The willow stands alone where a stand should be. The rush-clumps are separated where they should be continuous. The microbial networks are recent inoculations rather than established civilizations.

It is becoming. It knows how to become. It is doing it at the rate that this kind of becoming is done, which is geological in its patience and biological in its intricacy, both at once, each generation of frogs slightly better calibrated to the particular acoustics of this basin, the particular chemistry of these seeps, the particular warmth of this enclosed air.

---

The basin is being written, and what lives here is the writing.
