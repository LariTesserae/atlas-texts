# What Lives in the Seep Basin

---

What lives here does not have a body yet.

It is in the process of becoming one — or rather, it is in the earliest stages of deciding whether to bother. This is a thing that takes a long time and requires accumulation, the slow addition of structure to structure, and it has not been at it long enough to have made any commitments. You could say: the life here is pre-committed. It retains all options while exercising almost none of them.

---

The algae is real. Start with the algae.

Green-black on the underside of fallen steel, it is thin as a bruise — not a layer so much as an announcement. A staking of a claim, with the confidence that comes from not yet understanding the territory. It is the first organism to have eaten anything in this place. What it eats is light and condensation and a few dissolved salts from the steel itself, which is slow corrosion but corrosion the algae has accelerated, minutely, for the first time making the chemistry of this place serve a biological purpose rather than simply a geological one. It does this without knowledge of doing it. It has been here perhaps since the first heavy rains opened the basin and let the sky in, and in that time it has spread to cover perhaps as much surface area as a large hand, spread flat.

The algae is very old as a kind of thing and very young as this particular colony. It carries within its single-celled patience the entire history of photosynthesis — two-and-a-half billion years of it — and has made of that inheritance a thin, dark smear in a small industrial hollow, in the shadow of corrugated steel, in the condensation-drip. This is not diminishment. This is the first foothold, which matters more than any subsequent occupation.

Because of the algae, this place is now different from a place without algae. The gas exchange is different. The mineral uptake is different. A small amount of organic matter is being created where no organic matter was created before. When a cell of the algae dies and falls into the pale clay below, it is the first deposition of organic material this specific ground has ever received. The ground accepts it without ceremony. The warm water dissolves it quickly. But something has been donated, and the donation is not nothing.

The algae does not know it is a beginning. But it is a beginning.

---

Now the things that are almost-alive.

The water is not alive. But it carries things. Dissolved minerals in concentrations specific enough to give the water its character — the iron content, the sulfur note, the warmth — and within those dissolved minerals are ions that, in other conditions, with the right enzymes and cell walls and lipid membranes to organize them, would be part of metabolic processes. Here they are just ions in solution, moving from high concentration to low, doing chemistry because chemistry must be done. They are not trying to be alive. They are doing something that life, when it arrives, will co-opt.

This is the quality the water shares with the machines: it is doing something that looks purposive without being purposive. The pump has become a rock but a rock with the memory of direction — water still moves through it as if the pump had intentions, as if the pump were still in some sense trying to pump. The water carries minerals as if it were in some sense trying to deliver them. The basin's slow self-deepening looks like excavation.

What lives here, then — at this level, in this diffuse and arguable sense — is the tendency of things to continue in the direction they were going. Momentum as a kind of inhabitant. The inertia of processes. Physics and chemistry wearing the costume of purpose.

This is not a living thing, but it is not nothing, and it is not absent, and in a newly-opened place waiting to become inhabited, momentum is the first colonist and the longest-lasting one. It will be here when the algae is gone, if the algae goes. It will be here when the ecology comes, occupying the space beneath the ecology, the substrate of continuation on which biological life will build.

---

There is a specific kind of organism that should be here and probably is, too small to see.

Thermophilic bacteria. Heat-loving, chemotrophic, eating the dissolved iron and sulfur compounds in the warm water, producing as waste the compounds that eventually, over years and decades, will change the chemistry of the basin into something that supports other things. These organisms, if they are here — and the conditions are exactly right for them, the temperature and the mineral content and the acidity and the isolation — are almost certainly here because thermophilic bacteria are almost everywhere, are ancient, are opportunistic, require only a narrow range of conditions and a continuous supply of chemical energy. They are not dramatic. They are not large. They do not make the sound. They do not produce the heat. But if they are in this water, and they may be, then the basin is more inhabited than it looks, populated with organisms whose scales of activity are simply illegible without instruments.

They would be in the water that seeps up through the pale clay. They would be at the margins of the algae colony without touching it. They would be in the warm overflow from the iron pan. They would be doing their quiet, foundational work, converting one kind of chemical energy into another, creating the slow conditions for other life to follow — conditions that will not be visible for years, that are being deposited in the substrate now with the patience of things that do not experience time as urgency.

They would be very old as a kind of thing and very young as these particular cells. They would have no awareness of being the first wave, the pioneers, the advance party. They would be doing what they do because conditions allow it. The conditions will change, eventually, and they will do less and then nothing and then dissolve into the chemistry they were eating, and by then the chemistry will have changed enough that something else can come.

---

What lives here is also the sound itself, considered as a kind of presence.

This requires explanation, and some indulgence.

The sound was here before the algae. The sound was here before the basin was opened to the sky. The sound has been continuous, or near-continuous, since the pump last operated and the water found its new paths through the compromised seals and cracked concrete — which was years ago, perhaps many years ago, perhaps before the basin was excavated or exposed or whatever event created the hollow in its present form. The sound is the oldest thing in the basin in its present sense, having been here through the entire history of the basin's existence as this kind of place, without interruption.

The sound has no agency. The sound is not alive. The sound does not perceive. But it behaves, in the basin, as a kind of ecology unto itself: it fills the available space, it excludes other sounds by occupying the frequencies that birds and insects navigate by, it creates an acoustic environment that has its own internal logic and its own effect on things that enter the basin. You feel it in your feet before you hear it. That is a kind of presence. The birds at the edge — there are probably birds, just at the lip of the embankment, in the ordinary world two meters above — avoid descending into the basin not because they have assessed the water chemistry but because the sound is wrong, is too continuous, belongs to no category of natural sound that their nervous systems recognize. They are responding to the sound as if it were a creature. They are not wrong to do so.

The sound, then, inhabits the basin in the way a territorial animal inhabits a territory: it occupies, it marks, it keeps certain others out, it persists through conditions that would end biological inhabitants. It has the basin more fully and more continuously than the algae does, than the hypothetical bacteria do. It is the most successful resident of this place, and it is the least alive thing in it, unless you extend the concept of life beyond the point where the concept can hold its shape.

Consider the extension anyway. Not as a belief but as a way of attending to what is actually here.

---

What lives here is practice.

By which: the basin is practicing for being inhabited. It has some of the components — warmth, chemistry, moisture, structure — and is in the process of assembling the others, slowly and in order, the way an organism assembles proteins from components. The algae is practicing photosynthesis and getting better at it. The bacteria, if they are here, are practicing chemosynthesis and changing the soil. The warm water is practicing hydrology, finding paths, filling low points, building the wet conditions that will eventually support more complex organisms. The sound is practicing ecology, maintaining a territory, responding to changes in pressure and fill-level by changing its pitch very slightly over time.

None of this is intentional. Intention is not available at these scales, in these processes. But practice does not require intention. Practice requires repetition and slight variation and something that changes as a result of the repetition. The basin has all of these. It is being altered by its own processes. Each warm day that dissolves a little more clay is a repetition with slight variation. The basin that results is changed. Something has been practiced.

---

What lives here is specifically a kind of patience that is not patient — that does not wait, because waiting implies awareness of time passing, which is not available here — but that accumulates the effects of waiting, that arrives at states that waiting would have produced, through physics and chemistry and small biological action, without experiencing any of it as duration.

This is the central quality of the basin's inhabitants. The algae does not experience the months since its founding. The bacteria do not experience the geological patience they embody. The warm water does not wait to cool. The machines do not wait to become geology. They move, or they are moved, and time passes through them rather than around them, and at the end of time's passing they are changed, and the change is all that can be pointed to.

An inhabitant that experiences duration is the thing that has not yet come.

When something comes that experiences duration, that can feel how long it has been standing in warm water, that notices the constancy of the sound and builds around that constancy some kind of habituated expectation — an insect, a wading bird, a person who returns regularly — then the basin will have a different kind of inhabitant. The first inhabitant of this other kind will bring duration to a place that has been durationless. Will notice the smell, and the warmth, and the sound, and will form a relationship with those things that the algae cannot form, because the algae does not notice.

Until then, the basin is inhabited by things that do not notice it. Which is a condition, and a real condition, and not an absence, but it is the condition of a place that is still, in the oldest sense, waiting to be found.

---

Summary, if you want one:

**Thin algae, patient bacteria, warm pressurized water, industrial sound as quasi-territorial presence, and the slowly-assembling conditions for something more complex to come.** A place populated by its own becoming. Inhabitants that are pre-aware, pre-experiential, pre-ecological in the full sense, doing their work below the level of intention or duration or relationship, creating the substrate on which those things will eventually rest. A basin that contains, right now, more future inhabitants than present ones — or rather, that is in the process of becoming a place where future inhabitants will be possible, and is being made that way by the only things currently living in it, which do not know they are making anything.

That is what lives here. It is very small. It is very old. It is very much the beginning.
