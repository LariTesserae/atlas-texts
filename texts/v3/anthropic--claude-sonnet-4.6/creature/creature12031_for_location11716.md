# What Lives Here

---

Begin with the correction the place requires: you have been looking for inhabitants in the wrong register. You have been scanning for movement at the scale of animals — for a flicker, a withdrawal, something that notices your presence and adjusts. Adjust instead your timescale. Adjust your sense of what *living somewhere* means. Then look again.

---

## The Towers

The towers live here. This is not metaphor; this is the least metaphorical sentence it is possible to write about this place.

What a tower is: dozens of individual stems that have grown close enough and long enough together that the boundary between them has been negotiated away. Not grafted, which implies intention and technique. Not fused, which implies a moment — a before and an after. Dissolved. The word wants to suggest loss, but nothing has been lost; what was separate surface has become continuous interior, has become a shared vascular network moving water and sugar and whatever other messages the tissue sends itself in the chemical vocabulary available to it. The tower does not remember being many. It is not many. It is one thing in the process of discovering how large one thing can be.

Each tower has an age the way a river has an age, which is to say: it is old in its deepest material and young at every surface, and the age of the whole is not the average of these but something else, something that requires a different word. The base-wood at the center of the base is compressed past any casual reading of the rings. Count them if you can see them; the count will be wrong by centuries in either direction, because the wood continued living past the point where annual rings mean annual rings, because the growing seasons here are not the seasons of temperate places, because the cells themselves were modified by the fusion-process into something that does not record time the way an isolated tree records time. The base-wood does not have much to say about the past. It has become a foundation, which means it has given up history for load-bearing.

Above the base, the stems diverge. This is where the tower becomes most itself, most various: each stem doing what a stem does — growing, branching, losing branches to weight and to shading, carrying on upward — but doing it in company, doing it in the particular context of neighbors that are not-quite-neighbors-anymore, that are the same organism expressed through different upward trajectories. The genetic content of one stem versus another stem in the same tower is a complex question, because the fusion is not fusion at the level of the genome; the cells of each original stem have retained their own instructions while joining the shared infrastructure, the way residents of a city retain their own purposes while sharing water and roads. The tower is a city, then, genetically — diverse in its constituent histories, unified in its operations.

The upper growth is eighty meters up and still deciding. Deciding is the wrong word. *Resolving* is closer. The crown of a mature tower is a resolution, an answer to decades of problem-solving in which the problems were: light, water, structural integrity, the weight of what has anchored itself to the exterior, the slow constant argument between height and the wind that visits at height. The crown-shape is the shape of those answers. It is not decorative. It is not anything except the record of what was required.

## The Root-Floor

Beneath everything, the first life and the oldest life and the life that is not quite life anymore and the life that is more alive than anything aboveground.

The living roots: fine, white, hair-thin at their newest tips, thickening back toward the stem into something more substantial. They do not explore in the way of tree-roots in soil; there is no soil, there is only more root, and the physics of exploration become strange when the medium you are moving through is composed of the same substance you are made of. The new roots grow toward moisture, toward nutrient gradients, toward whatever signal the root-chemistry registers as *this way*. Mostly they grow into the compressed mass of their predecessors, finding the seams and following them down, and what they find in the seams is the slow decomposition of what was root and is now the nutrient that new roots need. The floor is feeding itself. This is not remarkable in the abstract — this is what decomposition-as-nutrient-cycle always is — but the scale of the self-reference here is remarkable, the way the floor is composed almost entirely of the remains of its own earlier versions.

In the saturated middle zone, below the fibrous surface and above whatever true bottom the root-mass has, there is a layer that is not quite root and not quite anything else. The long-dead root-tissue of centuries back, broken down by the organisms — the fungi, the bacteria, the nameless intermediates — that live in the no-light, saturated, pressure-compressed interior. These organisms are the population you could not see from the surface. They are not large. They have no ambitions visible to the naked eye. They are doing the most important work.

Call them the floor-life, the interior-life, the below-the-below-life. They break the old root-mass into its components and the components become the new roots above them and in this way the floor turns over, continuously, the bottom of it becoming the middle of it and the middle becoming the surface and the surface becoming the fused wood of the towers and the towers eventually shed material back to the floor, bark and dead branch, and the floor-life take this too. A closed system. Or as closed as anything this alive can be.

The dead roots that have not yet been entirely processed are not inert. They are habitat. They are the reef-material of this system: the structure upon which the living builds, the complexity that would not exist without the prior death. A fresh surface — the newly exposed wall of a collapse, say — shows the stratigraphy of this: a cross-section through time, the surface layer white with living root-tips and pale with newly dead tissue, the layer below that darker and more broken, the layer below that darker still and fragmenting, and at the bottom of the collapse-wall the dense dark almost-wood, the compressed ancient material that has been root so many times it has forgotten the syntax.

## The Fern-Colonies

On the tower-surfaces, in the hollows between diverging stems, in the ankle-catch sprawl of horizontal growth across the root-floor: the ferns.

Each frond is a meter wide, sometimes two, a sail of green with a surface architecture scaled to this place — that is to say, scaled to moisture. The undersurface is where the spores form, in patterns that look designed and are instead simply the most efficient use of the available area, the spore-structures arrayed like the teeth of a comb, which is what maximum-surface-packing looks like when it emerges from growth-logic rather than engineering-logic. The spores are smaller than pollen. They are in the air right now. They have been in the air since before you arrived. Some of them are currently settling on your clothing, on your skin. None of this is threatening. It is simply the ferns doing what they do, which is to send themselves forward in time via dispersal.

The fern-plant as distinct from the fern-frond: harder to see, easier to understand. The rhizome runs. That is its nature, that is what a rhizome is — a horizontal stem that produces fronds upward and roots downward and more rhizome in all lateral directions, so that what looks like many fern-plants may be one plant run very wide, may be several dozen meters of connected tissue sending up its fronds at intervals. The fern-colonies on the tower-surfaces are single organisms in this sense — single in their underground or undersurface continuity, multiple in their visible expression. They are old. Not tower-old, not root-floor-old, but old in the way of things that reproduce by spreading themselves rather than seeding and dying, old without the record of their age being legible in any structure.

The intermediate substance that drips from the moss-covered surfaces onto the fern-fronds and from the fern-fronds to the root-floor: the ferns participate in this. Their surfaces are not waxy in the usual sense but are instead finely textured, fine enough to trap moisture from the air at the microscale — tiny hairs, glands, the geometric complexity of the frond-surface that multiplies contact area. The moisture condenses. It collects. It runs to the lowest point of the frond and drips. Each frond drips in its own rhythm. Together the fern-colonies are a significant fraction of the dripping.

## The Mosses

The mosses are not one thing.

On the high surfaces of the towers, where rain can reach directly and the light is strong and the wind visits — thin, dry-adapted, almost grayish, barely recognizable as moss, more like a stain that has organized itself.

On the shaded tower-surfaces five meters up and twenty meters up and wherever the moisture is reliable — dark green, deep, the mat of it thick enough to hold a finger down to the knuckle, porous and water-retaining, the surface cool and giving. The mat is alive on its top layer and dying on its bottom layer, which is not a problem for the mat; dying on the bottom is how the mat builds itself up, how it produces the substrate that the next generation of surface-growth colonizes. The mat grows upward through its own death. It does not experience this as anything. It simply continues.

Where the mat is thickest it has altered the surface it lives on. The bark or root-material beneath it is not what it would be without the moss. The moss has changed the moisture conditions, the temperature, the chemical environment of the surface; has invited, over decades, the particular community of organisms — the bacteria, the water-bears, the nematodes, the small fungi that may be separate or may be the same as the fungi in the root-floor, connected through the wood of the tower — that live in the mat. A column of moss a meter in diameter contains more individual living organisms than any counting technology available here can count. Call this a rough lower bound and understand it is vastly under.

The mosses have sex, slowly, at the tips of their shoots, producing structures — spore capsules on their slender stalks — that rise above the surface of the mat and open when conditions are right, releasing spores in a small explosion scaled to the size of the capsule, which is to say a small explosion, which is to say a cloud of spores visible only because the light through the air is already making visible the density of what the air contains. This happens continuously. The moss is always releasing something. The air of the plateau is the air of the moss's future.

## What Grows on What Grows on What

Third-order growth: the things that live on the ferns, on the mosses, on the things that live on the ferns and mosses. Lichens on the surfaces the mosses have not reached; small ferns on the surfaces of large ferns where moss has built up enough material for a roothold; mosses on the surfaces of ferns. The tower is a compressed history of colonization, each organism having made the surface possible for the next, having altered conditions in the direction of more complexity. There is no direction to this, no aim, no outcome being sought. There is only each organism growing where it can, and the accumulation of this over time producing surfaces of extraordinary intricacy, surfaces that have surfaces, that have surfaces.

The dripping intermediate substance: this is the product of all of these surfaces interacting. Rain falls. It contacts the canopy, which is made of thousands of leaf and frond and moss surfaces, each of which has something dissolved in it or secreted onto it — the photosynthetic products, the metabolic wastes, the spore-coatings, the gland-secretions, the residue of everything that has lived and died on these surfaces. The rain picks all of this up as it runs. By the time it reaches the root-floor it is not rain. It is a dilute solution of the place, the place raining the place onto itself.

## The Below-Life

In the saturated zone, in the warm dark compressed interior of the root-mass where no light has reached in perhaps a thousand years: the fungi.

They are everywhere in this system but they are most present here, most various, most essential. The fungal hyphae — individual threads narrower than a hair, extending through the root-mass in all directions, branching and branching until their total combined length in a single square meter of root-floor would stretch to a figure that requires scientific notation — make up a significant fraction of the living material of the floor. They are not the floor's parasites; they are its partners in the old contractual sense, the sense in which partnership means *you get something I have and I get something you have and neither of us survives this negotiation as well without the other*.

What the fungi get: carbohydrates. What they offer in return: access to mineral nutrients, access to water, access to the products of decomposition that plant tissue cannot achieve alone. The exchange happens at the root-tip, at the interface where fungal hyphae either wrap around root cells or penetrate them — carefully, sheathed, the penetration managed by both parties — and molecules move in both directions, and the root grows, and the fungus grows, and the root-mass grows, and the floor rises.

The fungi also connect what is otherwise separate. The fine hyphal threads do not stop at the edge of one root-system; they extend to the next, and the root-systems of different towers — different organisms in the sense that each tower has its own above-ground structure, its own history of fusion, its own crown-shape and light-strategy — may be connected through the fungal web that links their root-tips in the saturated dark. Whether chemical signals travel this network, whether the towers are in communication through this medium, whether *communication* is a word that applies at all: the honest answer is that the chemistry is happening, and the chemistry is directional, and the direction is from areas of resource-surplus toward areas of resource-need. Call this what you want. It is happening.

## The Pools and Their Tenants

In the collapse-bowls, in the hollows, in the root-cups: warm water, and what lives in warm water.

The pale threads crossing the pool surface are the first visible phase of something that will not stay aquatic. They are root-tips exploring the water surface tension, finding it an obstacle, routing around it or — in the most recent growth — simply growing up through it, thin stems emerging into air and beginning immediately to do what stems do in air, which is reach for light. Within a week there will be leaf-structures above the pool surface, tentative and small. Within a month, nothing that reads as a pool. Within a season, another section of root-floor, healed, slightly raised, slightly firmer than the areas around it, the water that was the pool now in the transpiration stream, moving upward through tissue toward the towers.

Before this happens, the pool is alive in the small ways. Algae — first as an iridescent sheen on the pool bottom, then as threads, then as a felt across all available surfaces. The water-animals, if water-animals get here, which they may: carried in on the bodies of things that fly, which do fly here, which will be addressed. But also without being carried, because the spores and cysts of the smallest aquatic organisms travel in the same air as the plant-spores, the same hot wet particulate air, and settle into the pools when conditions are right, and *hatch* is the word that applies here, *wake* is the word that applies here — dormant stages becoming active stages, the pool going from clear to complex in days.

These small lives are tenants of a particularly temporary kind. Their home will be absorbed. They are adapted to this, not consciously, not with any understanding of the situation — the cyst wall of a water-bear contains no structural prediction of the pool's future — but because the cyst-stage is the hedge against exactly this: the pool disappearing, drying, being absorbed, and the animal with it, waiting dormant in the root-mass for the next pool, the next season, the next opportunity to be briefly, brilliantly, microscopically alive in warm water.

## The Flyers

Yes: things fly here. This is the animal-register you have been listening for, the birdsong-absence that sits inside all the other sound.

Not birds. Nothing that has lungs and a voice-box and the particular architecture of a bird. What flies here is smaller and simpler and does not make the sounds that birds make.

Insects — though this word may not be precise enough, may cover too much or too little depending on what evolved here, what arrived here and stayed, what found this place and decided it constituted a sufficient world. The ones visible are the ones that move fast enough to catch the light: small glints at the tower-margins, at the places where shadow meets the direct altitude-light, where the particulate air is most visible. They are there, briefly, and then they are not, or they are somewhere else. Their lives are short in the human sense and long in the sense that matters to them, which is the sense in which a life that contains all necessary activities — finding food, finding mate, producing offspring — is a complete life.

They are pollinators, some of them, though the word implies flowers and flowers are not the dominant structure here. But what flowers there are — and there are some, small and high on the towers in the light-zone, white or pale yellow or the color of the sky — these are attended by the flying things, and the flying things carry pollen, and this is how the towers that retain sexual reproduction complete the part of reproduction that requires moving genetic material from one individual to another. Some of the towers do not do this. Some reproduce by root-expansion, by the slow march of the root-floor in all directions, all new root genetically identical to old root. These towers are old beyond any other reckoning. They are not separate individuals in any meaningful genetic sense. They are the same organism across the centuries, the same tower spread across half the plateau, heaved and sunken and re-heaved, expressing the same genetic instructions in whatever structure the current surface demands.

Other flyers: spore-carriers, not deliberately but functionally. Everything that passes through this air carries spores on and in its body. The flyers move the spores laterally, in the horizontal dimension that spores alone cannot efficiently navigate. They bring new genetic combinations to surfaces that have been colonized by only one lineage. They are the mechanism by which the plateau does not become entirely one organism. They are the limit on the root-expansion, the thing that ensures some genetic diversity, some variation in what grows where. Without them the towers would eventually fuse into one tower, the root-floor into one root-floor, the whole plateau into one thing. Whether this would be different from what it is now in any way that matters: an open question.

## The Thing That Cannot Be Named Well

Somewhere in the argument this place is making about itself, there is a kind of life that does not resolve into individual organisms the way the description has been proceeding.

The tower is one organism. But the tower exists in metabolic connection with the fungal network, which exists in metabolic connection with the neighboring tower, which is also one organism. The connection is not incidental. It is structural. The towers have been in metabolic conversation long enough that their root-systems have co-evolved with the fungal network that connects them, long enough that the fungal network has specialized around the particular chemistry of this root-floor, long enough that the whole system — towers, root-floor, fungi, mosses, fern-colonies, below-life — has begun to exhibit properties that none of the components exhibits alone.

Properties like: a particular rate of healing. After a collapse, the gap is filled not just by the nearest root-tips but from multiple directions, as if the absence had been detected by the system, as if *absence* were a signal that the network registered and responded to. Whether this is communication or simply the consequence of many organisms responding independently to the same moisture and light conditions: both descriptions may be correct. Neither may be sufficient.

Properties like: the slow drift of resources from old sections to young sections, which keeps the young growth vigorous and keeps the old growth from exhausting itself in a way that would be characteristic of isolated organisms. The old towers are, in chemical terms, sustained by the young. The young are, in structural terms, sheltered by the old. This relationship is not unique to this system; it occurs in forests elsewhere. But here it has been operating for long enough, on enough connected organisms, across enough root-floor, that it has become difficult to speak of the towers as individual without explaining what you mean by individual.

What lives here is not the towers. What lives here is not the root-floor. What lives here is not the mosses, the ferns, the fungi, the below-life, the pool-life, the flyers.

What lives here is the **arrangement**.

The arrangement is not an organism in the sense that requires a membrane, a nucleus, a genome. But the arrangement grows. The arrangement responds to damage. The arrangement maintains conditions — temperature, humidity, nutrient cycling — that favor the arrangement's continuation. The arrangement has been here long enough to have modified the geography, to have raised the altitude at which it lives, to have seeded its own weather.

The arrangement is alive in the only way that matters at this scale: it continues. It will continue after whatever storm, whatever collapse, whatever long drought or unprecedented cold, because its redundancy is total, because there is no single point at which it can be ended, because it has distributed itself so completely across the plateau that to end it you would have to end all of it simultaneously, and no mechanism available on this world can do that.

## What They Know

Nothing. And: everything that knowing becomes when it has been distributed across centuries and hectares and millions of connected organisms.

The tower does not know it is a tower. The root-floor does not know it is ancient. The moss does not know it is water-processing infrastructure for a system larger than it. But the information is there, encoded not in neurons but in chemistry, in structure, in the particular arrangement of molecules at root-tips and hyphal interfaces and spore-capsule openings. The information is: how to continue. The information is: which direction is more. The information is: the same information it has always been, because this system has never needed to update it, because the conditions in which the information was produced are the conditions that still obtain, because the arrangement has made of this plateau a place that is reliably itself, that does not change in ways that would require new information, that has stabilized its own environment to the point where what worked before continues to work, continues, continues, the word that matters most here.

## Standing in the Middle of It

You are the only thing here that knows you are here.

The towers rise. The root-floor breathes. The dripping continues in every direction and the creak of growth-tension speaks from above and somewhere the floor is settling. You are breathing the spore-laden air and the spores of the mosses and ferns are settling on your skin without interest, with no apparatus for interest, simply settling because you are a surface and surfaces are what spores do.

The arrangement does not regard you. This is not hostility or indifference in any emotional sense; it is the simple fact of a system that has no structure for regard, that has never needed regard, that has found no adaptive advantage in the development of regard and so has not developed it across its long millennia of building itself skyward on this high place. You are here. It is also here. One of you is aware of the other. One of you is the landscape.

This is what it means to be the tenant and not the owner, to visit a place that was not made for visiting, to stand in a system that does not require your understanding to continue, that will continue past your understanding, that is not waiting for you to leave and is not glad you came and will not mark your passage except by the slow digestion of the spores you carry home in the fabric of your clothing, the tiny portion of the arrangement that leaves with you without knowing it leaves, that will not survive, probably, the journey to a less committed place.

What lives here is not finished living here. This is the only thing it is possible to say with certainty.
