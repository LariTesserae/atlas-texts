# What Lives in the High Moss

---

## I. The Moss Itself

Begin here, because everything else is conditional on this.

The sphagnum is not a backdrop. It is not the setting in which other lives occur. It is the first and primary inhabitant of the plateau — not in the sense that it arrived before the others, though it did, but in the sense that it has never stopped arriving. Each year it grows by a fraction of a millimeter, which does not sound like ambition until you multiply it across ten thousand years and the full continent-scale of the plateau, and realize that what you are standing on is the accumulated body of the moss. You are inside the creature. Its living surface is the skin. Below, descending through meters of compressed brown-black organic matter, is everything it has ever been.

The sphagnum is therefore the only thing on the plateau that is also, simultaneously, its own history. Nothing else manages this. The cranes forget. The peat forgets, in its way — what it holds is chemistry, not memory. But the moss is continuous. The individual plants at the surface are the same project as the plants that grew here before certain mountain ranges reached their current heights. The project is: *fill this space. Hold this water. Rise.*

How does it do this? It has solved water storage at the cellular level — specialized dead cells that constitute most of the plant's volume, hollow, rigid-walled, perforated with pores that draw in water by capillary action and hold it against all reasonable pressure. The moss is sixty to eighty percent water by weight. It lives in water. It *is*, in some important structural sense, water given temporary plant-shape. When you press a hummock and feel the give, what you feel pushing back is the distributed resistance of ten thousand small water-containers, each asserting its volume.

This is not passive. The sphagnum acidifies its environment deliberately — releasing hydrogen ions, exchanging them for the calcium and magnesium and potassium that would otherwise allow competitor plants to establish. The chemistry of the plateau is the chemistry the moss has chosen. The low pH, the mineral poverty, the extraordinary acidity of the water — these are not conditions the moss tolerates; they are conditions it enforces. Other plants exist here on sufferance, in the spaces between sphagnum's territories, in conditions the moss has not yet perfected. The bog rosemary, the sundew, the cotton-grass: they are specialists adapted to sphagnum's world, not co-creators of it.

---

The colors of the moss are worth dwelling on because they are not decorative — they encode function.

The rust-orange and blood-red in the wetter, more exposed hummock-tops is pigment produced against UV radiation, a sunscreen response, the reddening of a pale-skinned organism in direct light. These hummocks are the highest, the most exposed, the oldest parts of the mosaic; their color is a record of decades of direct light-exposure. Touch the top of a rust-red hummock and the surface will be drier, springier, more firmly compressed — the oldest cells below have been compacted by the weight of the living above them.

The pale chartreuse in the lower cushions, the flanks of hummocks near the pools, where moisture is constant: here the moss does not need pigment against radiation. It is growing fast, building upward, the cells newer and less compressed, more loosely packed, holding more water. Press this part and it yields more dramatically, wobbles almost.

The dark olive-black of the pools' margins, where sphagnum grows half-submerged, oxygenation reduced, decomposition partially arrested: this is the moss at its most ancient-looking, at its most geological. These plants are doing what the moss has always done at waterlines — fringing the open water, slowly encroaching, building floating mats that will eventually cover the pool if the water level holds still long enough. Pools on the plateau are being digested. The moss is patient about it.

The military grey-green of the old hummocks — that dusty, unsaturated color that looks somehow less biological than the rest — is the color of a plant that has slowed. These hummocks have reached a height where the capillary water from below no longer reaches reliably. They are drying, slightly. Other colonists begin to appear on their tops: the occasional sedge tussock, a blueberry shoot, a cluster of crowberry. The hummock is beginning its long conversion into something else. The moss knows this in the only way it knows anything — the cells at the top are under slight stress, growing more slowly, dying more frequently.

---

In aggregate — across the continent-scale of the plateau — the sphagnum is performing a function so large it can only be perceived abstractly, from outside. It is sequestering carbon at a rate that, maintained over geological time, has altered the chemistry of the atmosphere. The carbon that entered the plateau as CO₂, was fixed by photosynthesis into plant matter, and then failed to decompose because the waterlogged, acidic, anoxic conditions the moss enforced prevented microbial breakdown — that carbon is now the peat. Meters of it. A deposit of atmospheric carbon that once moved through the air and is now held underground, stable, compressed, doing nothing but waiting.

What lives here, therefore — what the primary inhabitant of the plateau is doing with its whole continuous life — is converting the sky into ground.

This is the primary act from which everything else follows.

## II. The Sundew

The sundew presents a philosophical problem.

It is an animal that cannot move. It hunts, but it does not pursue. It digests, but has no stomach — the digestion happens outside the body, on the surface of the leaf, in a thin film of enzyme-rich fluid secreted along with the mucilage that serves as the trap. It is carnivorous by adaptation, having evolved in a substrate so mineral-poor that nitrogen must be supplemented from whatever protein source drifts within reach. The plateau has given it no choice. The sphagnum, which it competes with and also depends on, has stripped the available nitrogen so thoroughly from the soil and water that the sundew's evolutionary answer — *become a stomach* — is not remarkable; it is the only reasonable response to an impossible situation.

There are thousands of sundew plants on the plateau — tens of thousands — spread across every wet, open, sunny surface. They form an embroidery on the green, as described, a fine red-and-green textile worked into the upper layer of the moss. The flat rosettes press against the surface, their round leaves the size of shirt buttons, each leaf fringed with tentacles tipped in a droplet of glistening red mucilage. In the diffuse plateau light this mucilage catches what light there is and holds it, so that the sundew patches sparkle. This is, from the perspective of small insects, the problem: the sparkle resembles dew. The appearance of water on a surface is an invitation. Insects land to drink.

What happens next is slow, which is appropriate here.

The tentacles on the leaf surface are not simply sticky — though they are that. Each tentacle is a gland that can bend, and bend it does, in response to contact and the nitrogen-signal of protein. The motion is not fast; it takes minutes to become visible, hours to complete. The midge or small moth that lands, struggles briefly, and is held does not suffer a dramatic death. It is enveloped gradually, the tentacles on the margins of the leaf bending inward over the course of an afternoon, the digestive fluid concentrating, the body softening, the hard parts — wing scales, chitin — eventually to be released when the leaf reopens days later. What remains of the animal is absorbed: the nitrogen, the minerals, the fats. The leaf reopens, resets its tentacles, resecretes its droplets, and waits.

The sundew neither hurries nor rests. It simply holds.

This patience is not metaphorical. The sundew has no behavior other than response — no nervous system, no drive, no volition in any ordinary sense. But it has an elegant functional relationship with time that looks, from a sufficient distance, like temperament. It is adapted to the plateau's time-scale: slow growth, slow capture, slow digestion, slow release. A single rosette may live for years. Each capture event is separated from the next by days of waiting. The plant's life, from outside, appears to be mostly stillness with occasional, extremely slow motion. This is accurate.

What is stranger to consider is the aggregate. Tens of thousands of sundews, each making multiple captures per season — this is a significant ecological function. The plateau's insect biomass is being constantly, quietly, redistributed upward into plant matter, which will eventually become peat. The sundew is another mechanism by which the plateau converts animal protein into ground.

---

The sundew flowers, which almost nobody on the plateau notices, rise on thin naked stems above the rosette and open in sequence from bottom to top — small white flowers, entirely unambiguous in their pollinator-orientation, relying on insects to pollinate them. The same insects the plant eats. The sundew has therefore arranged its flower-stems to be tall enough that visiting pollinators arrive above the trap-zone of the leaves and are not caught. This is either elegant engineering or a coincidence so profound it functions as elegance. The flower-visiting insects are allowed to leave. The leaf-landing insects are not. The plant knows, in its chemical way, the difference.

## III. The Golden Plover

What the golden plover is, on the plateau, is a thin piping that contains all the space it moves through.

The call arrives before the bird is visible — sometimes it arrives and the bird never becomes visible, remaining a voice somewhere to the north, carried and thinned across the mosaic. When the bird does appear, it is improbably beautiful in a way that takes a moment to process: the breeding male, standing on a hummock-top against grey sky, shows a black face and throat and belly, edged with a white stripe that sweeps back from the forehead over the eye and down the neck, and above this, the upperparts — gold and black, a dense spangling of yellow-buff on dark background that is called gold only because we lack another word for this specific mixture of earth and light. The plover stands precisely. It turns its head. It calls: a liquid two-note, plaintive in the human ear, which means the human ear is interpreting correctly insofar as the sound is used to maintain contact with a mate across distances where sight fails in the weather.

The plover lives on the plateau in the breeding season, and its presence is a diagnostic — certain wet upland moorland of a quality sufficient for nesting, with the right mosaic of hummock and open ground for foraging, with the specific invertebrate communities that the plateau supports in such extraordinary density, hidden in the wet moss. The plover is eating things you cannot see: crane-fly larvae dragged from the wet upper layer of sphagnum, earthworms at pool margins, beetles, moths. Its foraging walk is a series of confident runs and sudden stops, the head cocked, the eye — gold-ringed against the black face — directed at the ground. It sees something. It takes it. It runs. Stops.

The nest is a scrape, barely a depression in a dry hummock-top, lined with a few fragments of lichen and dried vegetation. Four eggs, pyriform, dark and mottled in the pattern that has been tested against this specific substrate for as long as the plateau has existed: the eggs look like what surrounds them. They look like peat. They look like the shadowed spaces between sedge-clumps. The plover turns them regularly with bill and breast. In wet weather it sits with remarkable stillness, simply there, a slightly anomalous shape on the hummock, and you may walk quite close before it rises — and when it rises it calls, once, the alarm call which is sharper than the contact call, and moves away in a low, fast, crooked flight, landing fifty meters away to watch from a distance.

The chicks, when they come, are precocial — alert, mobile, already wearing the camouflage that will serve them on the plateau surface. They walk within hours. They find their own food within days. The parents — both of which incubate, in shifts, the male more during the day, the female at night — guide but do not feed them. The family moves across the mosaic. The chicks vanish into the hummocks if alarmed. In late summer they will gather, the family groups joining, the flocks building, and one morning they will be gone — south, to the coast, to the estuaries where the winter-mudflats concentrate invertebrates in the way the plateau concentrates them in summer. But that departure is not yet. For now the plover stands on the hummock in the diffuse light, and calls, and the call contains all the space it moves through.

## IV. The Short-Eared Owl

The short-eared owl hunts in daylight, which is anomalous, and this anomaly is an adaptation to the plateau, where the productive summer hours extend long into the evening and the invertebrate and small mammal life it preys on is largely surface-active during the day. The owl cannot afford to be nocturnal here. The prey does not wait.

Watch it long enough — and the owl on the plateau can be watched, if you sit still; it will pass and repass at fifty meters, at thirty, at distances that feel like an error — and what you observe is a creature that has refined coursing flight to an art that is not quite like flight as usually understood. The owl moves over the cotton-grass flats in long banking arcs, loose-winged, the wingbeats interspersed with long glides, the flight casual-looking to the point of apparent carelessness. This is deliberate. The sound profile of this flight — the specially modified leading-edge feathers that suppress turbulence noise, the downy surfaces that muffle the passage of air — is as close to silent as a bird of this size can achieve. What the owl is doing, in its casual arcs, is listening.

The ears are not symmetric. The left ear is slightly higher on the skull than the right. This asymmetry means that a sound arriving at the left ear and the right ear will arrive at fractionally different angles, and the owl's auditory processing system, which is unusually well-developed, can interpret this difference as spatial information. In three dimensions. The owl, banking at thirty meters, can hear a water-shrew moving under cotton-grass stems — the rustle, the small squeak — and locate it in three-dimensional space to within a centimeter. It adjusts its angle. It drops.

The drop is the end of the casual. The stoop from a coursing glide is fast, committed, the wings drawn back and then thrown forward at the last moment as the talons extend. The impact, if it connects, is both the conclusion and the continuation: the owl carries its prey — a shrew, a vole dragged up from its runway through the wet base of a hummock — to a higher piece of ground, a slight rise, and there tears and eats. The meal takes minutes. The owl sits with a posture that looks medieval, hunched, the facial disc forward, the yellow eyes alert to everything. The short ear-tufts — vestigial, functional as signal rather than hearing — are pressed flat. Then the owl is up again, coursing, the arc resuming.

The owl nests on the plateau in the tall grass or sedge hummocks, the nest a sparse collection of stems and grasses, difficult to find because the female, sitting, is the color of dead sedge, of dried sphagnum, of everything surrounding her. She is the color of *plateau* in late summer. The male hunts and brings prey. He arrives at the nest with a vole and performs a brief aerial display before descending — a circling at height, a series of shallow wingbeats — and she may lift and receive the prey in a mid-air exchange, and the vole passes from talons to talons over the hummock-top and the male rises again and is already coursing.

They know the plateau in three dimensions and in terms of hearing. To the owl, the plateau has a soundscape that no human presence has ever mapped: the vole runways beneath the cotton-grass, audible as slight changes in reflected sound; the shrew territories marked by a high-pitched chittering almost beyond human hearing but well within the owl's range; the specific acoustic signature of each pool — the way the open water reflects sound differently than the sphagnum surface, producing a hard return that the owl uses for orientation in fog, in the low-lying mist of early morning, when the plateau briefly dissolves and only sound holds the world in place.

## V. The Water-Shrew

The water-shrew exists at the extreme end of metabolic demand, which is to say it exists at the extreme end of *need*. Its heart rate can reach twelve hundred beats per minute. Its body temperature, if not constantly maintained by constant eating, drops to ambient within hours, and the shrew dies. The shrew therefore eats roughly its own body weight every day, and the day is organized entirely around this necessity. Sleep is brief and light. Foraging is nearly continuous. The shrew does not pause.

It is small — small enough to fit across a human palm with room on both sides — and this smallness is both its advantage and its problem. Small bodies lose heat faster, by surface-area mathematics. The water-shrew has compounded its difficulties by specializing in cold water, diving into the plateau pools and the small stream-channels between hummocks to hunt aquatic invertebrates, to chase stonefly larvae across the gravel of submerged peat margins, to take small fish occasionally, snails, anything protein-containing that does not move faster than a shrew can move. When the shrew dives, it carries a layer of trapped air in its fur — the fur is specially designed for this, a dense, water-repellent structure that holds a bubble of insulation — and underwater it appears silver, wrapped in its own air-coat, moving fast across the dark substrate. It surfaces with prey or without it. It eats immediately.

The water-shrew navigates by whisker. The elongated snout is dense with vibrissae — sensory hairs that detect water-movement, that map the shape of narrow tunnels through sphagnum roots and the hydrological channels between pools. In the darkness under the hummocks, where the shrew runs the tunnels it has worn through decades of its lineage running the same routes, sight is useless. Touch maps the space. The whiskers sweep continuously.

There is also a weak venom in the saliva — unusual among mammals, essentially unique among shrews of this size. It is sufficient to partially immobilize invertebrate prey, perhaps to subdue larger prey items caught in confined tunnels. The shrew's bite is not trivial.

Consider the shrew's relation to the plateau as a metabolic relationship. The shrew is a furnace running on plateau protein. Everything it eats — the invertebrates, the small fish — is converted within hours to energy spent moving, to heat radiated into the tunnel, to the making of more shrews. The waste is released into the hummock-base. The protein and mineral content of the shrew's body, when it dies — at two years old at most, more likely one, the lifespan scaled to the metabolic intensity of the life — returns to the peat immediately. The shrew completes its circuit quickly and without remainder. What it extracts from the plateau it returns.

The population on the plateau is large, and the shrews are largely invisible to each other and to the observer except at territorial boundaries, where males in early spring produce high-pitched, nearly ultrasonic screaming matches at the tunnel junctions that separate adjacent territories. These confrontations rarely result in contact. They result in the resolution of the boundary question — who owns this stretch of channel, who forages these particular pool margins — and then both shrews retreat and the near-ultrasonic screaming stops and the plateau resumes its silence.

## VI. The Crane

The crane is the plateau seen from above by something that knows the plateau from above.

A pair, or several pairs, moving between the larger pools in their characteristic unhurried walk — the legs long, the body large, the head raised above the surrounding cotton-grass so that the bird seems to float, legless, across the pale sedge-tops. The red cap of the common crane: a small, heated point of color in the grey-and-green landscape, appearing and disappearing behind hummocks, the body briefly lost in the complex terrain, then rising again into visibility.

The crane arrived here sixty years ago — or rather, the crane-population has recolonized this type of habitat after a long absence, and the specific birds of this plateau arrived within recent decades. Their presence is therefore young relative to the plateau's own temporal scale, and they have the quality of new inhabitants still learning a very old place. Watching a crane navigate the hummock-mosaic is watching a large body negotiate terrain designed for smaller bodies: the birds pick their way carefully, lifting each foot high, placing it on hummock surfaces or in shallow pool margins, reading the water-table with their feet. They have learned — this pair has learned — where the deep pools are, where the apparently solid ground is actually floating mat that will not hold their weight. They have made mistakes and recalibrated. They know this plateau in the way that requires having been wrong about it and corrected.

The dance, which cranes perform throughout the year — not only in courtship but as ongoing social bonding, communication, what appears to be play — takes on particular quality here, in the space of the plateau. The two birds face each other, bow, leap vertically with the wings spread, land, bow again, pick up objects and toss them, circle. The wingspread of the common crane is two meters. When both birds spread their wings simultaneously on an open bog-flat, the gesture fills the visual field. The grey of the wings matches the grey of the overcast sky so closely that the birds briefly become sky-fragments, rectangles of sky that leap and bow and resolve again into crane-bodies with the wings folded.

The voice of the crane carries two kilometers in still air. The loud, rolling trumpeting — produced by a windpipe coiled within the sternum to create resonance — is the sound the plateau least expects and most remembers. It happens rarely, without apparent reason: the birds will walk in silence for hours, foraging at pool margins for frogs, for large invertebrates, for tubers dragged from underwater by the heavy bill, and then one will call and the sound breaks across the plateau and is gone, and the silence reseals, and the cranes continue walking.

What the crane brings to the plateau that nothing else brings is the perspective of something that has seen it from the air — regularly, in migration, as the birds move between wintering grounds and breeding sites — and chosen it again. The crane makes the plateau a place that is chosen. That is worth more than it sounds.

## VII. The Pulse

What lives in the pulse?

The pulse is felt through the soles of the feet, sub-bass, slightly irregular but nearly rhythmic: forty seconds, pause, forty seconds. It has been felt by every creature on the plateau that has feet, though not all of them have distinguished it from the background vibration of existence. The shrews feel it through their tunnels and incorporate it as a condition of the substrate. The cranes may feel it through their long legs, though cranes do not react to it. The sphagnum feels it as slight compression, as water slightly displaced and then returning.

What causes it is in the deep peat, where the human body has not been and where the plateau does not explain itself. This much can be said:

The plateau is active at depth. The water that enters from above — from rain, from the condensation of fog at the surface, from the capillary draw of the sphagnum itself — must go somewhere. Not all of it evaporates. Not all of it runs off at the plateau margins. Some of it descends through the peat, moving through different layers at different rates, the oldest and most compressed peat nearly impermeable, newer layers more porous. This creates a hydraulic system of some complexity — water held in pockets between less permeable layers, building pressure, releasing. A hydraulic system of sufficient scale and complexity will have frequencies: the pressure-build time, the release time, the refill time. At scale — at continent-scale — these frequencies are very long, very slow, low enough to pass entirely through the range of sound into the range of vibration.

The plateau breathes. Not metaphorically. The water-table rises and the surface of the plateau rises with it — by millimeters, invisible, but real — and falls, and rises. At sufficient scale, this is a pulse.

Or there is something else. The peat at depth holds things, as has been noted. Human remains have been recovered from peat bogs elsewhere — bodies preserved by the anoxic, acidic conditions that arrested decomposition, bodies thousands of years old still wearing the expressions of their deaths. The plateau's peat has not been systematically examined. What has been there long enough to affect the hydraulic geometry of the deep layers, to introduce a resonant cavity, to produce a frequency — this is not known. The peat does not volunteer information about itself.

What is certain: the pulse has a period, and the period has been stable for as long as anyone has stood on the plateau and been still enough to feel it, and nothing on the plateau reacts to it except by having long ago ceased to react. It is the background. It is operating condition. If it stopped, something would be wrong — though nothing on the plateau would know, immediately, what was wrong. They would feel only a small wrongness in the ground, a missing note, a rest that extended longer than usual and then continued extending, and the silence below would become the new operating condition, and everything would incorporate it, and the plateau would continue.

But the pulse has not stopped. Forty seconds. Pause. Forty seconds.

Whatever produces it is alive, or is the rhythm of something once-alive at sufficient scale to persist as rhythm, or is the plateau itself having finally achieved, in ten thousand years of self-construction, something that functions as a heartbeat. The sphagnum builds and builds. The water moves. The peat compresses the peat below it, which compresses the peat below that, all the way to the original surface, the pre-botanical world. Something in that compression is releasing, and filling, and releasing.

The plateau continues.

## VIII. The Accumulated Life

Not a single thing. Not any of the things named above but all of them operating simultaneously, in the diffuse directionless light, in the cool that steals heat from anything that stops moving.

A landscape as organism is a figure of speech that becomes less figurative the longer you stand on the plateau. The sphagnum is the primary body. The other inhabitants are its organs, more or less: the plover and the crane and the owl converting invertebrate biomass into excrement that fertilizes the moss; the shrew running its hot circuits and releasing its heat into the peat; the sundew extracting nitrogen from the air-traffic of insects and making it available to the larger system through decomposition; the pools circulating water and organic matter and hosting the invertebrate communities that feed everything else; the fungal networks at half a meter's depth moving signals and nutrients across the full extent of the plateau in the only language available at that depth.

And the peat itself, which is the body of the past, the plateau's own fossil record, the compressed history of every organism that has lived here and died here and failed to decompose because the plateau would not allow decomposition. The living and the unliving dead and the living-that-is-dead-but-still-active are not separate categories here. The living sit on the accumulated body of what they will become. There is no gap between them.

This is what the pulse is.

The plateau has noticed, in the only way available to something that cannot think but has been accumulating long enough that accumulation becomes a kind of notice: that the living body and the compressed-dead body are continuous. That what breathes at the surface and what holds carbon in the dark are the same project. That the moss and the peat and the water and the pulse are all one thing in different states of time.

This is the primary inhabitant. Not the sphagnum — though the sphagnum. Not the plover — though the plover. Not the thrum below. The plateau itself: a living system that has achieved sufficient scale and duration to have its own weather, its own atmosphere, its own hydraulic heartbeat, its own memory encoded in the compressed bodies of ten thousand years of self.

It does not require you. It has not noticed your arrival or marked your presence in any system it maintains. If you stood still long enough, the sphagnum would begin, slowly, to grow across your boots. In a few seasons it would incorporate your presence into its slow upward project. In a few decades you would be indistinguishable from the hummock you had become. In a few centuries the peat would have you, and in a few millennia the plateau would have converted you completely — your carbon held in the dark below the living surface, released from the sky you once breathed, made permanent, made ground.

This is what lives here.

This is how it receives things.
