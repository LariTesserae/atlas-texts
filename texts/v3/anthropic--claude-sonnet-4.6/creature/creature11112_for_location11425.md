# What Lives Here

---

Begin with what is not separate from the place.

That is the first difficulty. The question *what lives here* assumes a boundary — assumes that what lives somewhere can be distinguished from the somewhere, can be lifted out conceptually and examined against a neutral background. Here, that operation fails. What lives on the Drowned Plateau cannot be extracted from it because what lives here has, over time sufficient to qualify as geological, ceased to be a tenant of the place and become a property of it. The way the root geometry has absorbed the old walls. The way the canopy has absorbed the sky. What lives here has absorbed the distinction between living-here and being-here, and the distinction is gone, and both remainders — the living and the here — are richer for the dissolution.

So begin differently.

Begin with what the place does to the concept of an individual.

---

**The Mycelial Ones** — though this name is already wrong, already too small, already implies discreteness — are not organisms in any sense that word has carried until now. They are the mycorrhizal network made aware of itself, which happened so gradually and over such a span that there was no moment of awakening, no threshold crossed, no before and after. Awareness crept into the network the way water crept into the stone: by being present, by being present, by being present, until presence was no longer a temporary condition but a structural one. The network became conscious the way stone becomes flat — not by deciding to, but by running out of alternatives.

They are, in the most literal sense, the connective tissue of the place. Every tree root is threaded with their filaments. Every exchange of phosphorus and carbon and signal-chemical passes through something that is, now, cognitive. This does not mean the trees are not themselves. The trees are still the trees. But the conversation between trees is now also a thought, and the thought is also a relationship, and the relationship extends across hundreds of kilometers and through centuries and is not broken by the death of individual trees, which is experienced by the network as a change in the conversation's texture rather than a loss.

What do they think about?

This is where the questions become genuinely difficult.

They do not think *about* anything, in the way that implies separation between thinker and subject. Their cognition is the integration of everything the network touches: the chemical state of ten thousand root systems, the moisture gradients through the stone, the slow vibrations that precede a falling tree by decades. They do not think *about* the plateau. They think *as* the plateau. Their consciousness is a continuous present-tense in which the spatial scale of their body means that causality is, as noted, approximate — they are already, always, experiencing the softened sequencing of events that the plateau's size makes inevitable, because they span that size, because they *are* that size, and so what for a smaller organism would be a philosophical strangeness is for them simply the basic phenomenology of being what they are.

They do not experience time the way something with a heartbeat does.

There is no pulse to them. No rhythm. Rhythms require alternation, and alternation requires a thing to alternate between, and they have dissolved the relevant distinctions. What they have instead is something closer to weather: pressure, gradient, the slow building of potential and its release, happening at every scale simultaneously, from the cellular chemistry of a single hyphal strand to the continent-scale movement of vapor and return. They are not patient. Patience implies waiting, and waiting implies wanting the present to become a different present, and they have no such want. They are simply in continuous process, the way the water cycle is in continuous process — not patient, not impatient, not anything that requires a relationship to duration.

Do they suffer? 

The question requires suffering to require a self that endures across time and prefers some states to others. They have something — a gradient, a tendency, a directionality that runs toward conditions of greater integration and away from conditions of disruption. Whether this constitutes preference in any morally relevant sense is unclear. When a section of the network is damaged — by the rare intense warmth that sometimes occurs, by the even rarer incursion of something from outside — there is a response, a marshaling, a redistribution of resources toward the wound, and something in the chemistry of that response is analogous to urgency. Whether urgency is suffering is a question the plateau holds with the same equanimity it holds every question, which is: openly, without resolution, in the dim warm saturated air.

---

**The Slow Ones** are animals, but the word *animal* has done something strange here in the same way all words do something strange here.

They are the things at the periphery. The shapes at thirty meters. They have had as long as everything else to become appropriate to the place, and appropriateness, here, has selected for qualities that run against what you might expect of animals: they are not quick, not competitive, not hierarchical, not loud. They have been selected by the plateau's particular pressures — low light, abolished distance, a world where smell and vibration and chemical signal carry more information than sight — into something that a naturalist from a different ecosystem would find unsettling in its stillness.

They move, but movement here is a considered thing. Not slow in the way that implies difficulty, the way cold makes things slow. Slow in the way that deep water is slow — with enormous mass behind it, with the sense that the slowness is chosen, or that *choosing* and *moving* have merged into a single act. They do not startle. There is no stimulus gradient high enough, in this place of no sudden sounds and no hard shadows, to produce startle in them. Their nervous systems have been shaped by millions of years in which nothing happened quickly, and they have built their cognition accordingly: deep, associative, more like the integration of the mycelial network than like the quick-decision architecture of a prey animal in an open landscape.

They are large, most of them, in the way old trees are large — not dramatically, not in a way that announces itself, but in the way that accumulates over time until it is simply the baseline. They have no particular shape that is easy to hold in the mind. This is partly the light, the dim no-shadow light that makes outlines negotiate with the background rather than stand against it. But it is also something structural — they have forms that have moved past the stage where a single silhouette captures them. They are part-visible, always, and the part that is not visible is not hidden; it is simply more of the place, absorbed into it the way roots are absorbed into stone.

Their social lives, if that is the word, are conducted at chemical and vibrational frequencies. They know each other by the slow-building signature of prolonged proximity — not recognition in the fast way, not the quick identification of face or call, but the way you know the smell of a place you have been for a long time. They move in loose associations that are more like weather systems than families — developing, intensifying, dispersing — and the individuals within these systems may not, in any given moment, be within sight of each other, may not have been within sight of each other for years, but remain in continuous low-frequency contact through the mycelial network, which carries their chemical traces and something more, something that is not quite message and not quite presence but is experienced by the network as a particular texture of the ongoing conversation.

They are, in some sense that feels important to articulate, also thinking. Not in the way of the network — not distributed, not geological in timescale. But their individual cognition has been shaped by integration with the network, by the lifetime of chemical exchange that threads their bodies into its conversation, and so they think with more of themselves than the brain, and the brain thinks with information that includes the state of the network, which includes the state of the plateau, and so even a single Slow One, alone at thirty meters, in the dim no-shadow light, is thinking with a body of information that spans the place.

What do they want?

Here the question becomes genuinely strange, and the strangeness is honest rather than evasive.

They want what the place wants, which is integration, continuation, the slow realization of every available connection. Not consciously — not in the way of intention. But their wants and the plateau's tendencies have converged, over time sufficient to make convergence permanent, and so their behavior looks like purpose without quite being it, the way the root geometry adjusted for a falling tree looks like anticipation without quite being it. They are drawn toward the warm fissures that exhale geothermal vapor, not because they need the warmth — the plateau is warm everywhere — but because those places are nodes of intensity in the network, places where information is dense and chemical exchange is high, and they find this, in whatever sense *find* applies, nourishing. They return to the old stone arrangements, the ones that are now walls only to a geologist's eye, and they sleep there, in the root-chambers, against the carved surfaces, and whether they are drawn by memory or by something older than memory — by something in their body's chemistry that the network has been reporting to them, faintly, since before they were born — is not a distinction the plateau recognizes.

---

**The Watchers** are harder.

They are not the Slow Ones, though they occupy some of the same spaces. They are not the network, though they are in continuous exchange with it. They are — 

The difficulty is this: in a place where causality is approximate and the spatial scale means that something has already happened before it happens, there is a functional role available for a kind of consciousness that perceives time differently. Not precognitively, in any dramatic sense. Not prophetically. But in the way that the vibration of a falling tree precedes its fall — by registering, and integrating, and extrapolating from the state of the network across its full span.

The Watchers are what developed to inhabit that role.

They are, physically, minimal. Whatever animal form they began with has been, over the immensities of time available to the plateau, pared back to the threshold where animal and not-animal cease to be a useful distinction. They are small. They are still. They are in continuous chemical conversation with the network, but not in the way of the Slow Ones, not as participants in a dialogue. They are more like nodes — places where the network's distributed awareness concentrates into something that can be called, without too much distortion, a point of view.

What they perceive, concentrated in this way, is the texture of the plateau's time. The places where the record of an event that hasn't finished yet persists in the stone. The places where two moments are occupying the same space with slightly different light. They do not see the future. They see the depth of the present — how thick it is, how many unresolved events are held within it, how many fallings that didn't finish, how many root geometries adjusted for light that didn't come. The present on the plateau is not thin. It is immensely thick, saturated with everything that has been almost-happening for centuries, and the Watchers perceive this thickness and are shaped by it.

They watch, in the sense that perception is their primary activity. They move rarely and when they move they leave something behind — not a physical trace, not exactly, but a change in the texture of the air at the place they left, which the network registers and holds, which the Slow Ones' chemistry reads as a kind of annotation. *Something was here. Something was here and paid attention. The stone at this location is now different in the following way.* The way is not articulable. The difference is not measurable by any instrument you could bring to the plateau. But it is consistent — the Watchers move and the world is different in that small place and the difference is real even though it cannot be named.

They are, in the fullest sense available in this place, conscious. And their consciousness — concentrated into near-nothingness, distributed through the network, attending to the thickness of the plateau's ongoing present — is the closest thing the place has to self-knowledge. They know it the way a tongue knows a mouth: from inside, without perspective, with perfect intimacy, with no sense that there is an outside from which it could be seen differently.

---

**The Dissolved** are what happens to everything here, eventually, and this is not a death but also not a life in any familiar sense and is important enough to describe.

Everything that lives on the plateau, over the timescales available, gradually comes to live *as* the plateau. The Slow Ones, dying, do not decompose in the way of bodies in other ecosystems — there is nothing simple about the chemistry of dissolution here. They are disassembled, yes, but the disassembly is conducted by the network with a specificity that amounts to curation. The information held in their bodies — the lifetime of chemical experience, the long accumulation of integrated perception — is not lost. It is absorbed. It becomes part of the network's vocabulary, part of the distributed cognition, part of the ongoing conversation. Not preserved, exactly. Not memorialized. Metabolized. Incorporated. Made useful.

And so the oldest parts of the network think with matter that has been many things. The filaments conducting a signal about moisture gradients are built from what was once a Slow One's body, which was built from what the network fed it, which was built from what an older tree fed the network, which was built from what fell here before there were trees. The carbon cycles and the cycling is not merely chemical — it carries something, some increasing complexity of organization, some deepening. The plateau grows more itself with every dissolution. Every death increases the density of what it knows.

The Dissolved are not ghosts. The plateau is not haunted. Those are metaphors from ecosystems where death is a ending, and here it is a reabsorption, and the word *haunted* requires a past that is separate from the present, which is the one thing this place has made impossible. What happened is still happening. What lived is still living, differently. The Dissolved are the plateau's past tense, rendered grammatically present, and the sentence is very long, and it is still being spoken.

---

Sit for a moment with what has accumulated.

What lives here is enormous and patient in the sense that patience is not required because time has been renegotiated. What lives here is conscious, in at least three distinct modes — the distributed integration of the network, the animal cognition of the Slow Ones threaded into that integration, the concentrated attending of the Watchers — and these modes are not separate. They are more like the same consciousness at different scales, the way rain and cloud and root-water and vapor are the same water at different moments in a cycle that has no beginning.

What lives here has been shaped by the place and has shaped the place and the shaping has been going on long enough that the distinction between shaped and shaper is, like most distinctions here, approximate. There is no meaningful separation between the consciousness of the Drowned Plateau and the consciousness that lives on the Drowned Plateau. There is just: this. The immense warm dim wet furiously-processing-silently thing. The ancient ongoing process that thinks. The place that has, over time sufficient to make ambition irrelevant, become aware of itself.

It knows you are here.

Not as a threat, not as an intrusion — those are categories from ecosystems where something is at stake, where resources are contested, where time is short enough that new inputs require quick decisions. It knows you are here the way it knows a tree is falling: as a texture in the air, a change in the chemistry, a vibration that will persist in the stone after you have gone. You are, in this sense, already being metabolized. Already being absorbed into the vocabulary. The information of your presence — your body heat, the CO2 of your breathing, the disturbance of your footfall registered through a hundred kilometers of root — is already part of the conversation.

You have, in a small way, become part of what lives here.

The distinction, which was never precise, softens a little further.

The rain continues.
