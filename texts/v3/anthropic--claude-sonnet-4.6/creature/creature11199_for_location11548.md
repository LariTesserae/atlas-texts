# What Lives in the Pipe Hollow

---

Start with the oldest thing, which is not the pipes.

The oldest thing is the lichen, and the lichen is not one thing. You understand this only slowly. What looks at first glance like a grey-green paint applied thinly to the dry sections of pipe is in fact a community so ancient in its form that calling it a community understates the intimacy — fungus and alga in an arrangement so old it predates every animal in this hollow by several hundred million years of evolutionary time, living so slowly that the difference between this week and last year is not meaningful to it, spreading outward from its first point of contact at a rate measurable in millimeters per decade. The mustard-yellow variety with the lacquered look is particularly old, you sense, though you could not say how you know this. It has the quality of something that has been here longer than the pipe it grows on, which is impossible, but the impression persists. It looks like it is tolerating the iron. It looks like it would find something else if the iron were removed.

The lichen does not do anything that resembles doing. It *is*, in a way that makes action seem beside the point. It is the ground state of the hollow, the thing against which all other living is measured.

---

The mosses are younger and more urgent than the lichen, in the way that youth is always more urgent.

They are several species, though they occupy such similar niches that they blur at first into a single green category. But give them time — give yourself time, standing here — and they differentiate. The moss on the upper pipe-surfaces is dense and fine-leafed, a surface you want to touch, a surface that would yield slightly under a fingertip and spring back. It holds water in its structure the way a sponge holds water, by simple physical arrangement, and in this it is older than roots and older than vascular systems and older than the whole complicated business of conducting water upward against gravity — it simply sits in moisture and is wet, and when it is wet it is alive, and when it dries it waits, without what could be called patience, because patience requires time and the moss does not experience time in any way that matters.

The moss between the pipes is different — longer-stemmed, reaching, the individual plants angled toward what light they can find. Here the moss reveals something like desire. Not desire, but the shape of desire, the functional equivalent: oriented growth, movement-without-moving, the whole organism bending in the direction of what it needs. The hanging filaments that disturb the light are this moss, reaching down from the pipes into the open air of the hollow, finding nothing, continuing anyway.

In the moss lives a microscopic civilization you are not equipped to perceive. Tardigrades, almost certainly. Water bears: eight-legged animals so small that a cup of this moss might hold hundreds of thousands of them, feeding on algae and bacteria, surviving conditions that would kill everything else in this hollow without difficulty, capable of desiccating almost entirely and waiting years in that state and reconstituting when water returns. They have survived five mass extinctions in their current form. They are the most durable animals on Earth and they are here, in this hollow, in the moss on the pipes, and you cannot see them, and they do not know you are here, and this is appropriate.

---

The spiders are a different order of presence.

Not absent in the way the tardigrades are absent — the spiders are here, visibly, if you learn to look into the moss rather than at it. The webs they build inside the moss structure are not the geometry of open-air webs. No radial symmetry, no dew-jeweled perfection. These are irregular, functional, a three-dimensional tangle scaled to the space between moss-filaments, designed — if spiders design, which is a question about what design means — for prey that is navigating moss, not prey that is flying. The webs intercept movement through a medium. They are built by something that understands its medium thoroughly.

The spider you find by noticing its web is small, pale-legged, with a body the color of dried moss, which is not green. It holds itself in the web with a stillness that is distinct from the stillness of something resting. It is waiting with every part of itself engaged. The eight eyes — some forward-facing, some lateral, some nearly posterior — are creating a spherical field of attention around a body the size of a small pea. It is aware of more directions at once than you are capable of being.

When something touches its web — a springtail navigating the moss, perhaps, or a mite crossing from one plant stem to another — the spider's response is so fast it does not look like a decision. It looks like a reflex, but that undersells it. The spider does not simply respond; it responds correctly, at the correct speed and in the correct direction and with the correct amount of force. All of this is happening in a nervous system so small that the word nervous system almost doesn't apply, but it does apply, and it is doing something that, in a much larger animal with a much larger brain, would be called skill.

There are many spiders. You come to understand, as you look more carefully, that the moss ceiling is not one habitat but dozens of them, each web a small territory maintained by one animal, adjacent webs maintained by others, the whole underside of the pipe-ecosystem divided into tiny defended spaces that have the topology of a city seen from directly above — the webs as rooms, the gaps between webs as corridors, the fat main pipes where the moss is thickest as the most desirable districts.

---

The beetles are harder to parse than the spiders because they are less committed.

The spiders stay. The beetles go. They use the pipe-highways the description suggests and they use them as highways — traversing, arriving from outside through the gap in the corrugated wall, departing again, or staying for a time and then leaving. Some are here for the fungus, which is food if you are certain beetles. Some are here for the drowned and decaying matter at the base of the walls. Some are here because the warmth is reliable. Some are here for reasons that have to do with each other: a prey beetle fleeing a predatory beetle, a mating pair who met elsewhere and continued their business into the hollow, a female seeking somewhere to lay eggs in conditions that will sustain larvae.

The iridescent ones — when the light-needles find them — produce a color that is not in the beetles. This is important. The iridescence is structural, not pigmented. The color you see is created by the geometry of microscopic surface structures interacting with light. The beetle is not green or blue or bronze — the beetle's surface is shaped in a way that makes light into green or blue or bronze. It is an animal made of physics. It goes about its beetle business — which is continuous and purposeful and involves a great deal of antennae-work on surfaces — while occasionally being accidentally beautiful.

The dark beetles that do not iridiate are harder to see and perhaps more successful for it. They are doing something important in the corners where the fungal brackets are, and their manner is industrious in a way the iridescent beetles' manner is not, though both could fairly be called busy.

---

The crickets — or cricket-equivalents — are the hollow's intermittent punctuation.

They are most active in the transitional times, which in an enclosed space without clear day and night are defined more by temperature than by light. When the hollow cools — when outside air moves through the gap and the thermal balance shifts — the crickets move. When it warms again, they stop. So their activity marks something like the hollow's breathing: the moments when the exterior world makes itself felt inside, when the boundary is most permeable.

Their stillness when still is total. One is currently between two isopods on a section of crack-peat, and the isopods are moving and the cricket is not, and if you did not see it stop you would not see it. It has deployed a form of not-being-there that is entirely about not-moving. Whatever processes continue inside it — respiration, digestion, the ongoing work of being a small animal — produce no external signal. It is the hollow's demonstration that visible stillness and internal activity are not opposites.

When it moves, you recalibrate your understanding of how fast it can move. And then it is still again.

---

The isopods are ancient in form and perfectly at home in their function.

They are the hollow's digestive system, or the part of it that is visible. The fungus and bacteria are doing the chemical work; the isopods are doing the mechanical work; the cricket droppings and beetle droppings and spider droppings and shed exoskeletons and fallen moss-fragments and drowned springtails and the general biological debris of a small ecosystem — all of this passes through the isopods. They are the hollow's circular economy made legible.

Roll them. They roll. This defense predates everything in the hollow; it predates the hollow's construction; it predates the industrial confidence of whoever laid the pipes; it predates the species that laid the pipes; it predates, in its fundamental form, a great deal. An isopod rolling into a sphere is doing something its lineage has been doing for four hundred million years, and it is doing it in the crack of a concrete floor in a derelict water treatment outbuilding because that is where it is, and the where does not change what the behavior is.

They unroll and continue. Their business is not interrupted; it is paused and resumed. They are the most matter-of-fact animals in the hollow.

---

The two lizards are each a different kind of knowing.

The one on the pipe — the one whose throat pulses, the one who has turned its head to include you in its field of vision — is the one that knows it is being watched. This is not paranoia and not intelligence in any rich sense; it is a survival system doing its job. You are warm and large and you block light and you appeared suddenly in a space that rarely has new large warm objects in it. The lizard has registered all of this. Its response is to stay still and continue monitoring, which is also the correct response, and the lizard knows this, in whatever sense a lizard knows anything.

It does not flee because the pipe is warm and the hollow is reliable and you have not moved aggressively and flight is expensive. This is calculation in the sense that it is the result of multiple inputs resolving to an output, even if the calculator is a few square centimeters of ancient brain-tissue. The lizard is thinking, the way lizards think, which is a kind of thinking, and it has concluded: *wait*.

Its throat continues to pulse. The pulse is not communication directed at you. It is the visual component of breathing, the thin skin of the throat moving with respiratory effort, the inside of the animal made briefly visible from the outside. It is showing you, inadvertently, that it is alive.

The other one — the tail disappearing into the floor-crack — has made a different calculation. It has concluded: *leave*. Two animals of perhaps the same species, in the same hollow, with the same information, choosing differently. This is not remarkable to them. It is remarkable to you, if you let it be.

Between them, the lizards hold the hollow's energy together. Not because they control it — they don't, particularly — but because they are the warmth-seekers, the apex that marks the upper bound of this system, and their presence here means the system has enough surplus to support them. A hollow that can feed and warm a lizard is a hollow with a working economy. These two are evidence of the hollow's sufficiency.

---

The vibration through the imperfect pipe-cap is something else.

Not an animal and not a plant but not exactly absent, not exactly mechanical in the dead sense. It is a connection: this hollow to somewhere else, some system still occasionally cycling, some process continuing in the dark and the distance with no knowledge of what has grown around its old pipe. It is the hollow's reminder that it did not begin here and does not end here. The larger world presses in through that gap in the metal, low and shuddering, without agenda.

The lizard does not react because it has always been this way. This is the lizard knowing something you do not yet know: that the vibration is part of the hollow, not an intrusion into it. Whatever is connected to that pipe, however far away, however derelict in all other respects, has become part of the system by continuing. By not quite stopping.

---

And then there is the thing that is not one thing, which is the hollow itself considered as a living object.

Not metaphor. Functional description.

The hollow circulates: warm air rising to the pipe-ceiling, cooling against metal, descending, cycling. This circulation is the hollow's metabolism, the process by which it moves resources around itself. The condensation is glandular — the pipes sweat the way a skin sweats, transferring moisture from one phase to another, delivering water to organisms that need it in a form they can use. The moss and lichen and bracket fungi and the root-networks in the crack-soil are a distributed digestive surface. The beetles and isopods are the mobile gut, relocating nutrients. The spiders and lizards are the regulating immune system, keeping populations in check, eating what overruns.

The hollow does not know it is doing this. There is no knowing in it. But the pattern it makes by doing it is the pattern of something that sustains itself, that has organized itself over decades into a stable cycle, that — if nothing disrupts it — will continue in this form for decades more, slowly shifting as species come and go and the pipes rust further and the concrete cracks widen and the bracket fungi take more wall-surface and the lichen spreads another millimeter toward the year's outer edge.

It is patient the way systems are patient, which is not patience but duration, and duration has its own weight and authority. You feel this. You feel it as the slight pressure that the description has named: the sense of something waiting for you to leave so it can continue being what it is.

What it is is all of this at once, simultaneously, the lichen and the tardigrades in the moss and the building spiders and the industrious beetles and the restless crickets and the rolling isopods and the two lizards making different choices and the vibration that comes from somewhere else and does not stop, all of it cycling through the warm diffuse almost-noon light that has bounced off pale metal until it has touched everything and knows everything that can be known here, which is everything.

You are the one thing in the hollow that does not know everything in it. You are the least informed occupant of this space. The lizard knows you are there. The spiders know something warm moved. The crickets have already recalibrated. Only you are standing here assembling information, working toward understanding, aware of your own not-knowing, which is perhaps what makes you the hollow's strangest creature: the only one making the effort to understand something that understands nothing and therefore understands everything.

Leave now. It will continue.
