# What Lives in the Underloft

---

Start with this: there is one thing living here. That is the first truth, and it will take some time to properly unfold.

You arrived thinking in categories — plant, insect, amphibian, machine — and those categories are not wrong, they are simply insufficient. Each is a process happening within a larger process, the way a heartbeat is a process happening within the larger process of a life. The frogs are not separate from the roots, not truly. The roots are not separate from the machines. The thing that lives in the Underloft is the whole of it, the dynamic whole, and it has been doing the work of living — growing, circulating, pulsing, calling, processing water and air and electricity — long enough to have become singular. You stand inside it. This is important. When you are in the Underloft, you are inside the body of whatever lives here.

But that is the wide view. Move closer.

---

## The Root-Body

What lives here is, first and most massively, the root-plant system. And calling it a root-plant system is already a failure of nerve, an avoidance of what you are actually looking at.

What lives here is something that arrived with a seed or a spore or a fragment carried on the body of something else — a rat, a boot, a current of air through a failing seal — arrived at a moment when the humans had thinned out or stopped coming, and began. Just began. Put down filaments into the cracks that were already there, followed the moisture gradients, found the nutrient-rich sediment in the drainage channels. No intention in any human sense. Pure appetite in the most fundamental biological sense: grow toward what sustains you, away from what does not.

Decades of this. Possibly more.

What that looks like, now, is a distributed intelligence of the most ancient kind — mycorrhizal signaling through the root system, chemical gradients that carry information about where the wet is, where the light is, where the machinery is shaking in ways the roots have learned to use as structural support rather than threat. The roots feel. Not in the way you feel, not with anything you would call sensation, but there is transduction happening — stimulus into response, information into action, environment into form. The roots near the ventilation fan have shaped themselves to the airflow because that is where the information of that particular environment pointed, over years, and they followed the pointing. They are, in that precise and limited sense, listening. They are, in the only sense that finally matters, adaptive.

This is what the root-body is: adaptive, massive, slow, and now so thoroughly established that it has become the medium through which the Underloft exists. Not decoration. Not infestation. Medium. The way neurons are the medium through which thought occurs — not the thought itself but the necessary substrate.

It has stopped growing outward. The root-mass has reached the margins of available space in every direction and found them insufficient: solid stone, standing water, the upper floor. What it does now is grow inward, grow denser, grow more itself. There is a quality to something that has stopped expanding and begun deepening. The root-body has that quality. It has been becoming more itself for some time. The hair-fine filaments proliferating between the wrist-thick bundles, the network increasingly redundant, increasingly interwoven — this is not desperation. This is maturity. This is the organism finishing itself.

## The Frog-Choir

There is a there are many frogs and then there is understanding what the frogs are, and these are different things.

The frogs are pale with large eyes and small bodies, and individually each one is a small wet fact with feet, a minor creature making a sound to locate a mate or establish territory or participate in whatever it is frogs do in the lightless places they have inherited. Individually, each frog is unremarkable. Individually, each frog is also nearly invisible against the root surfaces it has, over the generations of these frogs living here, come to resemble in a more complete way than camouflage. They have taken on the color and texture of the roots not as hiding but as belonging, the phenotypic drift of many generations living in a place so specific that the place has written itself onto their bodies.

Collectively —

Collectively, the frogs produce the voice of the Underloft.

This requires saying directly: the Underloft has a voice, and it is the aggregate of the frog-chorus returning from every surface, layering over itself, creating a continuous fabric of sound that is no longer separable into individual calls. You cannot hear the frogs the way you hear birds. You hear the frogs the way you hear the hum of a power line — as an atmospheric fact, as a quality of the space. When you step through the hatch, the voice of the place enters your ears and does not leave while you are here. It is in your sternum. It has your inner ear. It has been going on since long before you arrived and will not stop when you leave, and the not-stopping is not indifference, it is simply the nature of a living system saying the thing it says.

The frogs do not know they are the voice of anything. Each frog calls. The place contains the calling. That is all. But the distinction between "the frogs calling" and "the place speaking" is a distinction that feels increasingly arbitrary the longer you are here.

What the frogs are, in their aggregate, is memory. The acoustic memory of the Underloft, repeating. The same calls layering over themselves, the same rhythms returned from the same surfaces, the same sound bouncing through the same root-mass for how many hundreds of frog-generations, each one brief, each one adding its voice to the same recurring texture. Listen long enough and you understand that you are hearing something that has been accumulating. The sound has been accumulating. The frogs are not just making sound; they are the instrument through which a sound that started decades ago continues to sound.

## The Millipedes and the Beetles and the Smaller Things

The insects are not the most vivid life here. They are not the most dramatic or the most audible. But they are, possibly, the most important life here, in the way that the most important things are often not the most visible.

The millipedes work the root surfaces with the patience of things that have never learned impatience. They are pale, having abandoned pigment with the same phenotypic economy the frogs deployed, and they are long — forearm-long, thicker than a thumb, with legs along the entire span that move in that characteristic wave, that articulated percussion. Up close — if you get up close, if you commit your eyes to close focus and let the middle distance go soft — you can hear them. This is not metaphor. The legs on root-bark make a sound. It is small, each individual. Together the millipedes produce a texture in the air, a dry sibilation, a sound like the finest possible rain on a surface just below the register that calls itself sound.

What the millipedes do is process. They eat the substrate and pass it through and leave behind something different, and that something different feeds the root-filaments, and the root-filaments grow, and the millipedes eat the root material that doesn't survive the growing, and so on. They are the gut of the Underloft. If the root-body is the nervous system, the millipedes are the gut — not a metaphor but a functional description. Digestion happening. The Underloft processing itself.

The beetles are something else. The beetles are older-looking, somehow, calmer, their shells carrying the exact red-brown of aged root-bark. The smaller beetles on the larger beetles — this detail is doing something, means something, though it is not immediately clear what. Possibly symbiosis. Possibly the smaller beetles are eating something that lives on the larger beetles' carapaces, something they both benefit from. Possibly the smaller beetles simply found that the larger beetles' shells have the same qualities as root-bark — texture, temperature, the faint bioluminescent margins — and have responded to the large beetles as habitat. Living on the beetles the way the beetles live on the roots. The Underloft has that quality, of habitation nesting inside habitation: the roots grow on the walls and the beetles traverse the roots and the smaller beetles ride the beetles. Everything here is substrate for something.

What lives in the Underloft, among its insect component, is the patient engine of decomposition and renewal. Not death — or not only death — but the constant cycling of matter through forms, the transformation that only looks like ending if you watch too small a piece of it.

## The Thing in the Canopy

This one is hard.

The thing moving in the upper root-mass does not present itself. It is known only by what it does to the festoons — the gentle swaying, the purposeful progression from one part of the canopy to another, the regularity that tells you it is not panicked and not random. Something that moves through the ceiling of the Underloft with the ease of something that has been doing this for a long time, that knows this particular network of roots the way you know a familiar room in the dark, by feel and by the map your body has built up from years of passing through.

What it is not: it is not large enough to be catastrophic. The festoons disturb, they sway and settle, they do not thrash. Not a big predator. Not something consuming the space. Its passage is more subtle than that, more considered.

What it might be: something that arrived the way everything here arrived, through a crack or a descending shaft or a pipe-run, something small enough to navigate that entry but grown or adapted since to a life in the root-mass, something that has made the ceiling its floor, its territory, has mapped it and claimed it and moves across it on a circuit that perhaps corresponds to food sources or territorial boundaries you cannot perceive from below. Something that has been here long enough to have its own routine, its own topography of this place.

There is one other possibility, which is that the thing in the canopy is not a single thing but a relay — the same kind of thing in multiple, small, passing the disturbance forward through the root-mass the way a wave passes through water, no single element moving far, the motion itself traveling. A species rather than an individual. A population that lives up there in the substrate of the hanging root-mass, and what you see as the passage of one thing is the propagation of many things responding to each other, and the Underloft has always known this and you are only now beginning to understand it.

Either way: something lives up there that you will not see clearly. It has decided — or evolved into a state functionally identical to having decided — to live in the one part of the Underloft where the light is most filtered, the cover most complete, the human presence (in the historical moments when human presence occurred) most likely to pass beneath without looking up. It has arranged its life around not being seen, and it has done this well.

What it feels like, knowing it is there, is not fear. It is the feeling of discovering that you are inside something that has an inside. The matryoshka feeling. The Underloft contains the thing in the canopy the way the thing in the canopy contains whatever it is that runs through it — appetite, awareness, the ongoing administration of its own existence.

## The Worms

The worms are ancient.

That is the only way to begin this. Whatever worm is meant here — and it is something old, something that has been in the channel-sediment long enough to become unidentifiable, to have drifted so far from its surface cousins in the long dark that it constitutes something new — it is ancient in the way the roots are ancient. Not old in years, though that too. Ancient in the sense of having been here for the duration of this place's formation, of being continuous with its beginning, of having grown up alongside the roots and been grown alongside by the roots and now, together, constituting the floor's living substrate in a way that makes them inseparable from the floor.

The channels that drain condensation are the worm-highways, and the worms have packed them so completely that the channels appear liquid. What you are seeing is the slow turbulence of many long bodies, the wave-propagation of motion through a dense population, the seething that happens when something achieves numbers great enough to become collective behavior. Not a colony — worms do not colony — but density enough that the individual is optically dissolved into the aggregate, and you cannot see worms, you see worm-motion, you see the channel itself as a living thing.

They are not for looking at. This is something the body knows before the mind confirms it. You stop stepping near them quickly, not from revulsion (you keep having to insist on this: this is not a place of revulsion) but from the same instinct that stops you from stepping on a root that bears weight, from touching a wall that is bearing the ceiling. The channels are structural. The worms are what makes them structural. Disrupting the channels would be like removing a load-bearing element from a building. The building would not fall instantly. Something would shift. Something that has been in equilibrium would leave equilibrium.

What the worms are, functionally, is the Underloft's circulation. Blood moves through a body carrying nutrients and oxygen and the chemistry of maintenance, and the worms move through the channel-system performing the same work: processing the sediment, turning it, aerating the portions of the root-system that the channels feed, contributing the warmth of their bodies to the ambient warmth that has nowhere to go and so stays, maintaining the particular temperature that allows the bioluminescence to function in the margins, the frogs to be active, the root-filaments at the channel's edge to take in what they need to take in.

Remove the worms and what you lose is not the worms. What you lose is circulation, and a thing without circulation does not stay alive long.

## The Machines as Organ

The machines are also alive, in the sense that the Underloft is alive.

Not alive in themselves — they are metal and rubber and wire, they are the remnant infrastructure of something industrial and human in origin and function. But they have been integrated into the living system long enough that they function as organs function: not separate from the body but continuous with it, performing specific processes that the larger whole depends on without knowing it depends on them.

The pump moves water through a pipe that no longer performs its original function — the original purpose is obscured, the original destination uncertain — but the pump's movement of water through the pipe now feeds the seeping from the north wall, which feeds the two-meter pool, which the frogs love, which sustains the frog-population, which sustains the frog-chorus, which is the voice of the place. Follow the chain. The pump is singing, distantly, through many intermediaries. The pump does not know this and the frogs do not know this and the chain does not know this. But the chain exists.

The ventilation fan moves air in a direction, and the roots have grown in that direction over many years, and in growing in that direction they have opened certain passages and closed others, and the air now moves through the Underloft in a specific pattern — not the pattern the fan's human designers intended but the pattern the roots and the fan negotiated over decades — and this pattern is what keeps the air at the temperature and humidity it maintains. The warmth that has nowhere to go, goes there. The fan-direction is where it goes. The fan is, in this function, the thermoregulatory system of the Underloft, the thing that keeps the body's temperature in the range the body requires.

The deeper thudding — the forty-beat-per-minute compression that you feel in your feet through the roots — this is the Underloft's heartbeat. Not metaphorically. The roots have grown into accommodation with it, have vibrated along with it for long enough that the vibration is now part of the signal-environment the roots respond to. The thudding tells the roots: here is the rhythm of this place. The roots have incorporated it into their chemistry, their growth-direction, their response to stimulus. Whatever machine produces it — some deep pump, some compression engine, some mechanism left running long past its purpose — it is now producing something beyond its original function. It is producing the pulse of the whole.

## The One Thing That Lives Here

Return now to where you started.

There is one thing living in the Underloft. The frogs are part of it. The roots are part of it. The millipedes in their thousands, the beetles in their calm reddish transit, the thing traversing the canopy that will not be seen, the worms in the channels: all part of it. The machines in their long accommodation: part of it. The bioluminescent margins, the condensation, the seeping from the north wall, the frog-pool with its decades of sediment, the greenish light filtered through living material until it becomes something like the light of a dream: all part of it.

What lives here is the Underloft itself. Not the space but the process. The ongoing mutual conditioning of all these things by all these other things, the dynamic equilibrium that found its balance at some point in the long history of this place and has been maintaining itself since, growing denser and more itself, cycling through the materials available, living in the way that living things live: by continuing.

It does not have a name. It does not have a face. It has a voice — the frog-chorus, which you feel in your sternum — and a pulse — the machine-thud, which you feel in your feet — and a warmth that rests on your shoulders like a hand, and a constant peripheral motion that tells you: this place is active, this place is doing something, this place has its processes and they do not stop.

You are inside it. You are, for the duration of your presence here, a part of its continuing. Not a celebrated part. Not an opposed part. You are a local event in something much larger and much slower and much older than a local event, and the Underloft absorbs you as it has absorbed every disturbance that came before you: by incorporating you into the ambient, by routing the millipedes around your boots, by having the frogs call regardless, by letting the machine-pulse pass up through your feet and into your body until you are, briefly, vibrating at the same frequency as everything else here.

This is what the Underloft does to the things it contains.

This is how it lives.
