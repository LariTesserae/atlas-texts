# What Lives in the Breathing Canopy

---

Begin with what has no name for itself.

That is the first and most important thing to understand about what lives here. The inhabitants of the breathing canopy do not have the concept of inhabitation. They do not experience the plateau as a place they are in. They experience it as a condition they are continuous with, the way a pressure is continuous with the medium that carries it. To live here is not to be located here. It is to be an expression of here, a local intensification of processes that are running at every scale simultaneously.

This makes description difficult. Description requires the describer to stand outside the thing described, and the things that live here have no outside. But we will try.

---

**The Canopy Itself**

Start with the obvious, which everyone avoids because it seems too large to be a creature and therefore must be furniture: the trees are not a setting. They are the primary organism.

Not in a metaphorical sense, not in the loose way that people say a forest is alive. In the sense that the pale-trunked trees of the plateau, taken together, demonstrate behaviors that no individual tree demonstrates, that can only be understood at the scale of the whole, that seem to require the whole as a unit of action. The wave of settling after a wind-shift. The circumnavigation. The collective optimization of leaf-angle that happens faster than wind communication between individuals could account for. The root-chemistry that spaces the trunks so regularly, so precisely, that it looks like planning because at the canopy-scale it is planning, which is not the same as consciousness but is not entirely different from it either.

Each individual tree is something like a neuron in this arrangement, which is not an original observation but is an accurate one, and the canopy's aggregate behavior has the quality of cognition in the way that neurons in aggregate have the quality of cognition: not the property of any part, fully present only in the whole, vanishing when you look at it directly and only apparent from a sufficient distance.

What does it think about? This is the wrong question, almost certainly. But the answer, if forced: it thinks about light. It thinks about its own continuation and the direction of that continuation. It thinks, very slowly, in the currency of chemical signals and root pressure and the eleven-second breathing of the mat, about where it is going. It has been thinking this thought for longer than the genus *Homo* has existed, and it is not close to finishing it.

The canopy does not notice you. This is not hostility. It is a difference in scale so complete that noticing you would require something like a conscious decision to look at the very small, the way you might decide to examine the individual bacteria on your skin. It is capable of this decision in the same sense that you are capable of it: technically, with effort, and with no particular motivation to bother.

---

**The Circulators**

In the light-shafts, between the pale trunks, in the warm drafts that rise from the canopy's heat production: the things that circulate.

They are not insects. Call them circulators, provisionally, because circulation is what they do and what they are. They have evolved into the convective logic of the canopy's exhaust so completely that their bodies are optimized for drift rather than directed flight. Their wings — and wings is perhaps not quite right, since the structure functions more like a sail than a wing, more like a kite than either — are disproportionately large for their mass, which is itself almost nothing, which is the point. They have reduced themselves, over whatever time this took, toward the minimum mass that can still metabolize and reproduce. They are eating air. Not metaphorically: the chemical byproducts of the trees' accelerated chemistry are organic compounds, and the circulators filter them from the updraft through structures in their faces that are more gill than mouth, more net than jaw.

They have no eyes. This seems wrong until you understand the logic: in a light-shaft, you are either in the light or not, and the information you need is temperature and chemical concentration and the slight pressure-differential of the draft, none of which require eyes. What they have instead are sensors along their undersides, mechanoreceptors of extraordinary sensitivity, which read the texture of the air the way a hand reads a surface. They know the draft. They know the drift. They know when another circulator enters their column because the column's pressure changes, and they know how large it is, and whether it is ascending or descending, and what chemical signals it is emitting, and whether those signals mean competitor or mate or simply neutral.

They are social in the way that particles in a medium are social: structurally, inevitably, without preference. The columns of circulators are not communities. They are concentrations, temporary densities in an otherwise dispersed population, and they concentrate where the draft is strongest and the chemical richness is highest, which are the same places, which are always below the oldest and most metabolically active trees. The oldest trees make the richest air. The circulators have always known where the oldest trees are. They are the living map of the canopy's history.

Some are large enough to cast shadows. These are not a different kind but an older kind: the same body plan, carried further, allowed to accumulate mass over a life that might span decades if the draft is reliable. In older light-shafts, under trees whose pale bark is layered deep and whose warmth is profound, the large ones rise slowly and with what looks like deliberateness, which is not deliberateness but is the behavior of a very accurate sensor in a very consistent environment. They have read this column for so long that their adjustments are minimal, their drift economical, their presence in the light nearly motionless — hovering, the word might be, except hovering implies effort, and this is the opposite of effort, this is perfect fluency with a medium, the relaxation of a master.

Their reproduction happens in the drift itself. The eggs are so small and so well-adapted to aerial suspension that they complete a significant portion of their early development while airborne, carried on the updraft, feeding on the same chemical richness as the adults. What descends to the root-mat is not larva in the conventional sense but something almost ready, something that has already lived most of its early life in the column and needs only a brief period in the spongy dark of the mat to complete a transition that is, in the end, quite short. They return to the air quickly. The air is where they are from.

---

**The Canopy Things**

In the high crowns, forty meters up, where the fractal branching has become so fine that what you see from below is less structure than texture, there are things that have not come down.

They are large. That much is established by the shadows they cast when they move across the light-shafts, shadows that reset your sense of scale, shadows that arrive and pass and leave you recalculating everything. They are present in the canopy in numbers that are difficult to estimate because they move in the same convective logic as the circulators, riding the crown-level winds, using the canopy's surface the way an ocean creature uses the interface between water and air: as a zone of unusual richness, a boundary where two systems meet and their differences generate energy.

What they eat is not confirmed. The canopy produces everything: the chemical byproducts in the air, the bark, the leaves in their season of senescence, and also other canopy things, presumably, in whatever arrangements the canopy logic permits. The shadows suggest they are built for soaring, for economy of movement, for the exploitation of the canopy-wind that is nearly constant at that elevation. They do not flap, or flap rarely. Their shapes, read from below in shadow, are long and narrow and tapered, adapted for a life of adjustment rather than propulsion, for reading the surface of the canopy the way a hand reads a surface, taking what presents itself.

It is possible they are the canopy's own nervous system in the same way that mitochondria are a cell's: symbiants so ancient and integrated that the question of whether they are inside or outside the organism they live in has become unanswerable. It is possible that what they do in the crowns — the touching, the tasting, the landing and departing — is functional in the same way that root chemistry is functional, that they are carrying information laterally across the canopy in their bodies, in their movements, in the chemical signals they deposit and pick up. That the wave of settling after a wind-shift propagates partly through them, that the canopy's communication is not only chemical and root-pressure but also aerial, carried in living bodies between crowns.

This would make them messengers. They have not consented to this description. They are not aware of it. But they have been doing it, in all likelihood, longer than there has been anyone to propose it.

---

**The Root-Mat Lives**

Below the surface, in the compressed dark of the mat, in the warmth of the roots' own processing, in the eleven-second breathing of a system that has been breathing for centuries: the small lives that do not surface.

They are not visible. They are felt occasionally as vibrations distinct from the mat's own pulse, quick and purposive, and this is the only testimony available. What they do in the dark is what small lives in the dark always do, which is everything, which is consume and transform and excrete and die and become part of what they were consuming, the cycling that is the only real economy, the only process that scales indefinitely.

The root-mat is their ocean. It has depth, it has pressure gradients, it has zones of chemistry that shift with depth and with proximity to the roots, and they have explored it the way any ocean is explored: completely, over sufficient time, with everything that survives being an expert in some narrow band of the available conditions. Near the roots: things that live in the processing heat, that eat root-secretions, that manage to exist in chemistry that would stop most small lives. Deeper, in the compressed older mat that is becoming the not-quite-stone of the trailing edge: things slower, things that metabolize on longer cycles, things that may be approaching the mat's own timescale, may be living at the speed of the mat itself, may be so attuned to the system they inhabit that distinguishing them from it is an act of violence against the truth of their situation.

They feel the breathing. They time themselves to it, some of them, the way tidal creatures time themselves to tides. The eleven-second cycle is the heartbeat of their world, and they have hearted their lives to it, and the heartbeat has been there so long that it was there before them and will be there after, and they do not know this but are shaped entirely by it, are the eleven-second breath made flesh and motive.

---

**What the Place Has Made of Everything That Has Stayed**

Here is the summary, insofar as summaries are possible:

The plateau has had a long time to work on its inhabitants, and what it has done is consistent: it has made them efficient, which means it has made them small, or made them into surfaces, or made them into mediators between systems, or made them disappear into the systems they mediate until the line between organism and process requires a committee to locate. It rewards fluency and punishes insistence. The things that have insisted on being themselves in the way things elsewhere insist on being themselves — on having mass, on asserting their individual natures against the canopy's grinding logic — are not here, or are no longer here, or have changed enough that insistence is no longer the right word for what they do.

What remains is a collection of things that have learned to want only what the plateau offers: light (mediated through chemistry mediated through air), motion (the convective logic, the drift, the settling wave), and time (more of it than anything else, time in quantities that dissolve the distinction between living and being, between inhabiting and becoming).

The circulators are suspended between needing nothing and needing exactly what is there.

The canopy things carry messages they do not know they are carrying.

The root-mat lives transform the dead into the living into the dead with the patience of geology and the speed of biology, which is to say all speeds at once.

And the canopy thinks its slow thought, tends its slow arc, turns its slow turning.

---

They are, all of them, what the place has decided to do with the energy it intercepts.

They are the canopy's answer to the question of what else light can become.

They are at home here in the way that home means: not comfortable, necessarily, not safe, but shaped to, fitted into, unable to be elsewhere without ceasing to be themselves.

You are walking through them. You are an anomaly they have no framework for. The root-mat breathes around your footsteps. The circulators adjust their drift infinitesimally to account for the heat of your body, the disruption of the column. The canopy things may or may not register the warm mass of you below.

The canopy will not remember you were here.

But you will carry the eleven-second breathing in your feet for a long time after, a rhythm your nervous system took on without asking, a pulse that is not yours but has joined yours, and at odd moments in other places you will feel it again: the rise, the pause, the fall, the rise.

It does not mean anything. It is not a message.

It is just what living in that much process does to a body that passes through.
