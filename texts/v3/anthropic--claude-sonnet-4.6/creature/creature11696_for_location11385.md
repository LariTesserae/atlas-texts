# What Lives Here

---

Begin with what cannot be separated from the place: the things that did not come here. The things that became.

There is a distinction — fine but absolute — between creatures that arrived at a habitat and creatures that are a habitat's expression. A deer enters a forest. A deer can leave a forest. The deer carries its deer-ness intact across the boundary; it remains deer on the other side. What lives in the lowland is not like that. What lives in the lowland has been so thoroughly worked by this particular pressure and moisture and light and stability that removal would not reveal the animal underneath the adaptation. There is no animal underneath. The adaptation *is* the animal. These things are what the lowland thinks when it thinks about filling a niche.

This is the first thing to understand: the lowland is not habitat for its inhabitants. It is the substance from which they are made.

---

**The frog that becomes the sound it makes.**

Not any one frog — the frog-layer, the bass note of the biotic chord. But attend to a single one. This tree frog on its leaf: it is green in a way that is not the green of camouflage as a strategy but green as a state of being, green as what it means to be made of the same materials as leaves, green because the light that built it came through leaves and it could not have come out otherwise. If you moved it to a white surface it would become wrong without becoming anything else. It would still be itself. The self would simply be — incorrect. Like a word in the wrong sentence.

Its skin is permeable. This is not a vulnerability; it is a choice the lineage made, a bargain struck so long ago it is no longer remembered as a bargain. The moisture of the air passes through it in both directions. The frog is, in a meaningful sense, in conversation with the atmosphere at the level of its skin — not sealed against the world but continuous with it, a small node in the larger wet. When the humidity drops by three percent, the frog knows this not as information but as sensation, immediate and total, the way you know the temperature of your own blood.

It breathes partly through its skin. It drinks through its skin. The permeable membrane that should be a weakness is instead a direct connection: the frog has made itself available to the lowland in exchange for the lowland's provision, and the lowland provides. This has worked without interruption for an amount of time that makes the word "time" feel approximate.

Its eyes are gold. In the green-brown dimness they are startlingly gold, and they do not close, and they face in slightly different directions in a way that gives them nearly the full sphere of vision, though the sphere is not what the eyes are for. What they are for: the detection of moving things against the layered visual plane. The un-shadowed ambient light of the lowland floor is the precise condition these eyes evolved to parse. They are reading a text written by this specific lowland and they understand every word. In a different light — sunlight, directed, source-having — they would be confused by their own shadows.

It calls. The call is not a sound the frog makes; the call is the frog's way of *being in* the sound. When it calls, it joins something larger than itself. The other frogs are not responding to it; they are all producing, together, something no single one of them could make, something that the lowland requires in the way that a room requires air. The frog calling is not lonely and is not seeking. It is participating in the production of the lowland's voice. To not call would be to leave a frequency empty, and the frequency, this lowland feels, does not wish to be left empty.

When it is still, it processes. Not thought — processing. Comparing the incoming chemical signatures of the air to an internal library that is not memory exactly but something older than memory: recognition without recollection. It knows that what passed through the air four minutes ago was the shed skin cells of something that eats frogs, and it knows this not by thinking about it but by having the appropriate part of its nervous system light up in the appropriate way, and it has moved four centimeters to the left, and it has stopped. Whether it was afraid is the wrong question. Its response to threat is inseparable from what it is. The fear, if it is fear, is not felt above the level of action. The frog simply acts, correctly, always.

**The ant colony, which is a single thing.**

A single ant is a curiosity, a rounding error. The colony is what requires description, and the colony is not a collection of ants; it is an entity that has solved the problem of being large by being distributed. It has no center. Its intelligence is not located anywhere. If you removed the queen, the colony would mourn for perhaps thirty minutes in the form of disoriented movement, and then it would reorganize, because the intelligence is not in the queen. The intelligence is in the relationships between the ants, and the relationships continue.

The colony has been in this place — this specific location, these trails, this territory — for an amount of time that is not recoverable. The trails are permanent features of the lowland in the same way that rivers are permanent features of landscapes: carved by use, maintaining themselves through use, altering themselves at geological speed to remain optimal. A fallen log changes a trail the way a fallen log changes a river — the trail finds around it. The trail always finds around it.

The colony knows the lowland in three dimensions. The colony has been mapping it continuously for as long as the colony has existed, and the map is not stored anywhere; the map *is* the pattern of movement, the trails and their branches, the places where scouts have been sent and not returned, the locations of food resources held in chemical memory by the workers who visited them and passed that location to the workers who passed it to the workers who are now going there. The map is alive. The map travels on a hundred thousand legs.

There are workers who will spend their entire lives — measured in weeks, months — doing one task. There are ants whose task is to be living bridges, to grip each other and form a structure that other ants walk across, and they do this without impatience, without any sense that they are doing something unusual. Being a bridge is their work. Being a bridge is what they are, for now. Later — if there is a later for them — they will be something else, and they will be that with the same completeness. There is no self to inconvenience. There is only function, and function here is not a diminishment. Function here is a form of fullness.

The colony hunts. The hunting is not quiet: a raiding column of army ants through the litter layer is a seismic event at the scale of the debris, everything fleeing before it, the column moving with a purposiveness that individual ants cannot have but that the column has absolutely. The column knows where it is going. The column finds what it is going toward. The column does not hesitate. The intelligence of the column is not located in any part of it; it emerges from the rules followed by its components, and the emergence is reliable, and the reliability is indistinguishable from intent.

They farm. Below the main nest, in galleries maintained at precise temperature and humidity — maintained, not found at those conditions; the colony creates them, manipulates them, corrects them when they drift — the ants cultivate a fungus found nowhere outside of ant colonies. The fungus cannot exist without the ants. The ants cannot exist — at this population, with this stability — without the fungus. This relationship is older than the current members of the colony by millions of years. The ants do not know this. They tend the fungus because they tend the fungus, because the urge to tend it is not an urge but a constitutive drive so deep it precedes what we would call motivation. The fungus grows. The colony eats. This has been the arrangement since before anything alive today existed, and the arrangement is not breaking.

**The strangler fig, which is both murderer and monument.**

It began as a seed, deposited in the canopy by a bird, in the axil of a branch of a tree that was then living. The seed germinated in that debris, that air-garden, and sent roots down. Down is a long way from sixty feet. The roots reached the ground. When they reached the ground — months, a year, several years into the project — something changed in the fig's metabolism, some ancient chemical signal: *the connection is made, the circuit is closed.* The roots branched. The roots thickened. They sent up more roots from higher on the host tree, and those roots thickened. The roots anastomosed — grew together, fused where they touched, as if they recognized each other, because in a sense they did. The roots formed a lattice. The lattice encased the host.

The host died. Not quickly. Not from the roots themselves, precisely, but from the competition — from the fig's canopy, which grew over the host's canopy and took the light, from the roots that intercepted the water and the nutrients before the host's roots could reach them. The fig did not murder the tree. The fig simply used the tree correctly, and correctness in this context meant the host's death.

The host rotted. The fig's lattice is now structural. Where there was once the circumference of a living tree, there is now the circumference of the roots that encased it, a basket of fused growth, hollow at the center where the host decomposed, filled now with accumulated detritus and its own little ecosystem — beetles in the rot, a cavity that something nests in, a habitat created by the fact of the host's absence. The fig built a room out of a death and something moved into the room.

The fig does not know any of this. The fig is photosynthesizing. The fig is producing fruit — small, in enormous quantity, a strategy: not one large fruit that is easy to miss, but hundreds of small ones that birds and bats and monkeys take and carry and deposit elsewhere, seeding other branches of other trees, beginning other descents, other lattices, other encasements. The fig is excellent at what it does because what it does is simply what it is, and it is that completely.

Its roots go into the unweathered stone. This is new. The stone does not yet know what roots do to it. The stone has not yet been cracked by the freeze-thaw cycle that this climate does not have, has not been dissolved by the acid of a thousand years of rainwater. But the fig's roots produce their own acids, mild but patient, and they are finding the fracture lines in the young stone, the planes along which it can be disaggregated. The fig is beginning what will be, eventually, the conversation between this rock and the living world — the conversation that produces soil, that produces landscape, that produces the deep topography of a place that has had time to become itself. The fig doesn't know it is the pioneer. The fig doesn't know anything. The fig is simply doing what figs do, and what figs do is make themselves at home, and making themselves at home here means starting the slow negotiation with stone that will, ten thousand years from now, have made this place geological in the full sense.

**The epiphytic orchid, which has learned to want nothing.**

An orchid rooted in bark, not soil. No soil, no standing water, no reservoir. Just bark, air, occasional rainfall distributed through the canopy system, and light that by the time it reaches the orchid has already been translated through a dozen other organisms' photosynthetic processes into a quality more useful for orchids than for the trees above that intercepted it first.

The orchid has specialized roots whose outer layer is dead — not dead through failure, but dead by design, a sheath of hollow cells that capture water instantly when it passes and release it slowly when the air pulls at it. The orchid is a capacitor. It charges in the brief abundance and discharges through the long scarcity. In a climate where the moisture is constant but the delivery is irregular, this is genius — not the orchid's genius, not the genius of any individual, but the distilled wisdom of every orchid in this lineage that died before reproducing because its water management was wrong, and every orchid that survived because it was a slightly better capacitor, and the long accumulation of those survivals into something that looks, from the outside, like elegant design.

The orchid communicates its availability through color and shape and scent. The color is visible in wavelengths specific to its pollinator. The shape is the shape of the pollinator's entrance requirements, the flower a lock and the pollinator's body the key, a fit so specific that the orchid and its pollinator have shaped each other over such a long mutual history that the pollinator cannot easily pollinate other orchids and the orchid cannot easily be pollinated by other pollinators. This is dangerous — mutual dependency is fragility — but in the stable lowland, stability removes the danger. The pollinator is not going anywhere. The orchid is not going anywhere. The mutual fit can deepen indefinitely without the risk that attends narrow dependency in changing environments.

It flowers rarely. When it flowers, it is extravagant. All the energy stored in the slow patience of the capacitor-roots, all the photosynthate accumulated in the crassulacean metabolic patience of the non-flowering years — released into a single floral event that is, by the dim standards of the lowland floor, a violent brightness. The flower does not last. The flower does not need to last. The flower needs to be found, and it is found, and what needed to happen happens, and the orchid returns to its primary mode of being: simply persisting, wanting almost nothing, requiring almost nothing, surviving on air and patience and the occasional rain.

**The spider in its web, which is what patience looks like when patience has a body.**

The web spans three meters, which is large in the absolute sense but not anomalous here, where nothing is anomalous. The spider at its center is not at the center strategically; it is at the center because the web is an extension of the spider's sensory field, and the center is where all the signals converge. The spider is not sitting in a web. The spider is the web, functionally — the web is its nervous system externalized, its senses deployed spatially across an area the spider's body could not otherwise perceive. To be at the center is to have all the information. The spider knows exactly what is happening at every point of its web not by looking but by reading vibration, and the vibration is a language the spider has spoken since it first spun silk, which is to say since it existed, since silk glands and the behavior that uses them are not something the spider has but something the spider *is.*

It waits. Waiting is not the right word. Waiting implies consciousness of the passage of time, some awareness of duration. The spider is not aware of duration. The spider exists in a perpetual present, processing incoming information from the web and either doing nothing or doing something, and when it does nothing, it is not waiting; it is simply being a spider, which has no temporal dimension of expectation. This is why "patience" is not quite right either — patience requires the knowledge that something will eventually occur and the tolerance of the interval. The spider has no interval. The spider has now, and now, and now.

The beetle in the web is processed. The processing is swift and entirely confident — there is no hesitation because there is nothing to deliberate. Stimulus, response, completion. The spider is a function executing. The function is old. The function was written before the lowland existed, before this rock emerged, before most of what is currently living was living. The spider performs it without error, in the lowland's ambient light, without witnesses it cares about.

The frog in the web is also processed. The frog is harder. The frog has muscles and leverage and a frog's sudden desperate objection to being processed, and the spider works with this — not against it, not strategizing against it, but working *with* the struggle, the silk compensating for force, the spider's movements responsive without being calculated, because calculation would take time the spider doesn't believe in. By the time the processing is done, the frog is still. The spider is still. The web, damaged in one quadrant, is already being repaired — the spider spinning new threads with the same quality of presence it has brought to everything else, the same total investment in the present act, the same absence of anything that could be called reflection.

**The thing that moved at the edge of the visible.**

It was not nothing. But it is difficult to say more confidently what it was.

Something large enough to move the vegetation when it moved through it — not a frog, not an insect, not even a monitor lizard, though monitor lizards move here with a proprietary ease and are not small. Something that displaced the air-column in a way that you registered not quite as sound and not quite as feeling, a low-frequency presence that the biotic chord parted around briefly and then closed over.

It had been watching. Or watching is not the right word — *attending.* Attending to the new variable in the familiar pattern, the thing that had arrived and stood in one place longer than things usually stand in one place, emanating the particular chemical signature of an organism that did not grow up here, that carries scent-information from other places on its skin and clothes and breath. In the lowland, where every organism is known — not cognitively known but known in the aggregate sense, chemically recognized, incorporated into the pattern as a familiar element — a new scent is information, and information is responded to.

The response was attention. And then departure, when the attention was complete, when whatever it needed to determine was determined. Not threat. Not opportunity. Simply: *not-here, and now gone, and the pattern resumes.*

Whatever it was: old. Old in the sense that matters here, which is not years but tenure. Whatever it was has a place in the lowland's pattern, a role in the economy of processing and being processed, and the place is established enough that the other things know it and account for it and have been accounting for it long enough that accounting for it is not a learned behavior but an inheritance. The small lizards moved differently while it passed. The frogs at the edge of its trail shifted frequency. The ants diverted their column three centimeters before it arrived, forewarned by some information traveling through the substrate ahead of it — vibration, perhaps, or a chemical trace the column recognized.

And now it is gone, or gone from visibility, which in the lowland is the same as gone. The space it occupied returns to the pattern. The pattern accepts it. The pattern never actually lost its shape; the shape simply included the departure all along, because the pattern here is large enough and old enough to contain departures.

---

What lives in the lowland is not a population of individual creatures.

What lives in the lowland is a system that has, at each of its many scales and positions, solved the problem of living in the lowland, and the solutions have been running long enough to become stable, and the stability has become the lowland's primary quality, its deepest and least visible feature. The heat is felt. The moisture is felt. The density is seen. The stability is known only slowly, accumulated over the time of standing there — the sense, arriving late, that nothing is trying to become something else. That every species here is the result of a long negotiation with exactly these conditions, a negotiation completed, the answer arrived at, the species now simply *being its answer* in the hot green quiet.

The frog is the lowland's answer to the question of how to live at this moisture level, at this temperature, in this light, eating these things. The orchid is the lowland's answer to how to exist without soil. The ant colony is the lowland's answer to how to be large without having a large body. The fig is the lowland's answer to how to reach from canopy to ground and back again, enclosing space with roots. The spider is the lowland's answer to the three-meter gap between those two trunks, the question of what should occupy it, the efficient answer of silk and waiting and a body at the center of its own senses.

These answers do not know they are answers. They do not know anything about the lowland as a system. They know only their own imperatives, which are the same as what they are.

The lowland does not know it has produced answers. The lowland does not know anything. But if it did — if the heat and the moisture and the unweathered stone and the root mat and the mycelium and the bromeliads cupping their small lakes and the drip-sound every few seconds from somewhere indeterminate were gathered somehow into a perspective — what it would know about its inhabitants is this:

*Yes. These. Exactly these. For exactly this long. Continuing.*
