## The People of the Plateau City

---

They are not easily seen at first. This is not because they hide — they do not hide, exactly — but because they have learned, across so many generations that the learning is no longer learning but *shape*, to occupy space the way the city occupies the plateau: without announcement, without insistence, with a quality of having-been-here-already that precedes any particular arrival. You notice them the way you notice the ferns in the gutters: suddenly, as a whole fact, without having caught the moment of first appearance.

---

### The Body

They are, on average, neither tall nor short — a description that sounds like nothing but means something here, where the city's low doorways and narrow streets have applied, across centuries, a slow pressure on who flourishes and who does not. What stands out is something else: a particular quality of stillness. They can be motionless in a way that suggests not absence of impulse but *satisfaction with current position* — the way the limestone columns have decided not to fall. They stand somewhere and they are standing there, fully, with no portion of themselves already elsewhere. This is noticed by people who arrive from places where bodies always carry the residue of what they were just doing and the anticipation of what they'll do next. Here those traces are quiet. A person waiting is simply a person in a place.

Their skin runs toward the pale end of what human skin does — not dramatically, not uniformly, but as a population tendency, an effect of altitude and cloud-filtered light and the way the sun here comes as suggestion rather than fact. Not pallor in the sense of illness; more like the pale grey-white of the dry limestone, a palette that sits comfortably against it. Some are darker — the plateau has had long enough, and trade routes long enough, that it holds many lineages, and the tendency toward paleness is just that, a tendency, not a rule. Their eyes are often grey, often blue, sometimes brown, but there is a quality in the eyes that is consistent across all colors: a certain *patience with complexity*, an accustomedness to looking at something and not expecting it to resolve quickly. The mist teaches this. You watch the far rim either appear or not appear, and you learn a tolerance for the genuinely unclear.

They are often damp. Not conspicuously wet — not dripping, not rain-soaked — but persistently damp in the way the stone is persistently damp. The air deposits itself on whatever surface it finds, and so their hair holds a fine beading of moisture most mornings, their outer layers carry a slight darkening at the shoulder. They are accustomed to this the way people in other climates are accustomed to dust. It is not something they mention.

They move quietly. Not crept, not tiptoeing — their footfalls are present, real, substantial — but without the percussiveness of people who are used to making their weight known. The streets are stone, and they know where the stones ring hollow and where they don't. They walk with the foot meeting the ground over its whole surface rather than landing heel-first, a gait that took years to learn in childhood and is now simply theirs, a gait that negotiates the textured pavement rather than asserting itself against it.

### How They Speak

They speak at the volume the air here rewards. Loud speech in the wet grey air costs you something — the moisture absorbs echo, the sound becomes effortful, you work hard and get little back. What the air rewards is the middle register, the even projection, the voice that expects to carry only so far and calibrates accordingly. They speak at close range, or they don't speak at all. Shouting is for emergency and is therefore nearly unheard, and when it happens it stops everyone — not because it's prohibited but because it means something, the way a sudden bird call from above means something.

Their language has developed from older languages through a process that has made it more precise about certain things and completely without vocabulary for others. It has specific terms for the different qualities of wet — the verb-forms are multiple and discriminating: the seep from a wall is different from the pool-fed dampness on a floor is different from the mist condensation on a surface is different from the residual wet that follows rain. These distinctions are real differences to them, the way English distinguishes blue from green even where another language would use one word for both, and the distinctions matter for daily purposes: which kind of wet requires attention, which is the functioning of the system, which is a warning.

They have limited vocabulary for dryness. It is not that dryness is absent from their experience — the stone is dry in summer mornings sometimes, there are dry spells in particular seasons — but dryness is the exceptional condition, the thing that needs no fine vocabulary because it is simply *the temporary absence of the usual*. They have a word for it that translates approximately as "waiting to be wet again." They are not wrong.

They speak slowly by the standard of people from lower, warmer, louder places. This is consistently misread on first encounter as hesitation, as uncertainty, as a difficulty grasping the question. It is none of these. They are thinking, but more than that: they are allowing what they mean to arrive fully before they give it sound. There is a cultural pressure against saying a thing and then subtracting from it, against following a sentence with "well, except—" or "I mean, more like—". You say what you mean. This requires knowing what you mean first. Knowing what you mean first requires a moment. So they take the moment, and newcomers fill it with anxiety while the speaker stands in a comfortable stillness and waits for the right shape of thought to arrive.

Their humor is very dry. This is the standard joke, made by them about themselves, and they appreciate it moderately because it is somewhat true but also not quite right — the humor is dry the way the plateau itself is dry, which is to say: it is covertly wet. On the surface, little. Underneath, a reservoir. The jokes take a moment to work. Often they work in the body before the mind catches up: something tightens in the chest, a warmth, and then the understanding follows, and then the laugh, small and satisfied. They don't perform amusement. The laugh is the real thing and it is proportionate, no larger.

### Relationship to Time

This is where they differ most profoundly from people of faster places, and it is the quality that takes longest to see because it resembles, superficially, several other things — slowness, conservatism, stubbornness, incuriosity — and is none of them.

The plateau teaches a particular relationship to duration. The lichen speaks of centuries. The limestone speaks of epochs. The city's own walls, read in their lichen chronology, say: here is a stone laid before your great-grandparents were born, here is one laid sixty years ago, here is the grike this wall's foundation was quarried from and the grike is still there. Nothing in this environment models quick transformation. Nothing here suggests that a thing, once present, will be absent soon.

And so they have internalized a frame in which processes are trusted to continue. The water will come. The cisterns will fill. The cloud will lift and return and lift again. They plan across generations not because they are especially virtuous planners but because the horizon they operate against is simply longer than that of people in places where weather is more violent, more changeable, where the environment makes shorter arguments. When they make a decision — where to add a building, how to route a new channel, whether to deepen a cistern — they are deciding for fifty years or five hundred. The decision-making process is slower accordingly. This infuriates visitors on timelines measured in weeks.

They experience urgency differently. It exists — a person trapped in a flooded grike is urgent, a child lost in the mist at the plateau's edge is urgent, certain medical situations are urgent — but it is narrowly defined. Things that feel urgent to people from other places often arrive here and become, in the plateau's air, merely important. Important things are handled carefully and completely. There is no rushing of an important thing. Rushing an important thing treats it as urgent, and things that are not urgent should not be treated as urgent, because when the actually urgent thing comes you will have squandered your urgency capacity on lesser matters.

They are not without desire. They want things. Particular foods, particular weathers, particular conversations with particular people, the company of the ones they love, the small solving of the daily puzzle, the cold clean water in the pool you know well, the sound of your own voice talking about something you've understood for the first time. Desire here is just — proportionate. It rises toward what is possible. It does not spend itself on what cannot be.

### The Knowledges

They know the plateau the way the plateau knows itself: in depth, in system, in the relationships between things rather than the names of things. A child knows which pools are safe to drink from and why — the water that passes through a certain length of limestone develops a particular character, a particular mineral balance — and this knowledge is learned not from instruction but from accompaniment, from being beside an adult who is doing the thing correctly and absorbing the criteria over a hundred repetitions until the knowledge lives in the hand and eye rather than in consciously recalled rules.

They know stone. Not abstractly — not as a field of study, though some of them study it that way — but practically, intimately, with the hands. Which face of a block to split and which direction to apply force; where the grain runs; how to recognize a stone that looks sound but has a hidden void; how to read a wall for the crack that has been stable for a century versus the crack that is new, and why the difference matters. Children learn this young, running fingers along the walls the way children in places with trees learn to read bark.

They know water in the same way — its moods, its channels, the sound of a grike that is carrying more water than yesterday and what that implies, the sign in the stone that a new seep has opened, the timing of which cisterns fill first after a mist-period. The management of water is not done by specialists; it is distributed knowledge, everyone's responsibility, the way the common understanding of traffic patterns is distributed among drivers — no single person directs it, all maintain it, and the maintenance is mostly unconscious, mostly absorbed.

They know plants carefully. Sparse does not mean unimportant; the plants here are noticed, individually, in the way that rare things are noticed. The single flowering plant in the grike is known to the people who live near it — not sentimentally, not with the affect of nature enthusiasm, but with the attentiveness that people pay to things that are useful and things that indicate. A plant out of condition indicates a change in the water. A new plant in a place that was bare indicates that moisture is reaching a depth it didn't used to reach. This reading is done continuously, below conscious attention, the way you know when a room you know well has a piece of furniture moved without immediately knowing how you know.

Some of them know medicine, which here is the knowledge of what the plateau provides and what the plateau does to bodies and how to navigate both. The cold-and-damp operates on joints, on lungs, on the processes that require warmth. There are specific knowledge-holders for this, people who have chosen to carry it deeply rather than broadly, and they are consulted with the combination of respect and pragmatism that useful people receive in communities that cannot afford mystification. The knowledge is practical and it is written in the clay-tablet systems used here, because it needs to outlast individuals, but it is also memorized, because the tablets must be worked back from and the body presents faster than a tablet can be found.

They know each other. The city is vast in its horizontal spread but not unlimited in its population — the plateau supports what the plateau supports, and the plateau's slow hydraulics have determined this for longer than anyone remembers — and so the number of people is the number where a person can, over a lifetime, have meaningful acquaintance with most. They know the particular way a certain neighbor announces her presence before she speaks, a small sound in the throat, not a word, just a signal; they know the quality of the chough-caller two streets over, the one who has spent forty years at the edge of the plateau calling to the birds; they know whose wall shows a new course of stone this spring and why, and who helped them lay it, and what was shared in that work. This knowledge is not gossip, exactly — it is more like the knowledge of a system, the way you hold in your head the hydraulics of a watershed, how each channel feeds the next.

### What They Make

They make things to last. This is not an aesthetic choice in isolation; it is a logical outcome of their relationship to time. Why make a thing that won't be here in two centuries? The material cost of making it twice exceeds the effort of making it correctly once. And so there is a quality to what they make that is difficult to name without sounding like a tourist's description of craft as performance — it is not performed, it is not labored, it is simply executed to the standard that the standard requires, which is the standard of several lifetimes.

The ceramics are beautiful in the way the pool is beautiful — it is not that they try for beauty, it is that they have been making ceramics from this clay in this way for long enough that the making has been refined to a precision that produces, inevitably, a certain thing, and that thing is lovely. The forms are not elaborate. They don't need to be. A bowl to hold pool-cold water should be: cool to the touch, smooth to the lip, heavy enough to feel intentional, shaped so that the water sounds right when you pour it. These requirements, met completely, produce a bowl. The bowl is beautiful.

They make music. This surprises visitors who expected silence from people who seem to prefer it. The music is built for the wet air — lower in register than music from dry places, slower in tempo, with a particular interest in long tones and the ways they decay, the way the air takes them. There are instruments made from the bone and clay and plant-fiber the plateau provides, instruments that would not be recognized immediately as instruments because they look like ordinary objects — a particular bowl struck with a particular way of holding the hand produces one note, then another depending on how much water you put in it, and a person who knows this is a person who can, at a meal, make music with the vessels that are already there. The singing voice is treated as the primary instrument. It is developed carefully, not for performance but for the same reason they develop all knowledge: because having it well beats having it poorly across the span of a life and the lives it shares itself with.

They make records. The clay tablets are one system. There is another: marks on the walls of buildings, inscribed not on the surface but into the stone, because the surface weathers and the inscription must outlast the person who made it. These marks are not primarily decorative — they record the history of a wall, a cistern, a channel, a pool: when it was made, what it was made from, what was found when it was opened or excavated, whether anything was changed. A person reading the walls of their own building is reading several centuries of previous occupants' notes on the system they share. This is how things are passed on: not in ceremony, not in explicit instruction, but in record, in accumulation, in the long text of the stone.

### The Young

Children are difficult here and necessary and clearly loved in the practical way that things you depend on are loved — fully, without much performance of the love, because the love is in the way you hold the thing, not in the speeches you make about it.

They are noisy, in the specific way children are noisy when adults around them are quiet. The children's voices in the narrow streets echo briefly before the wet air takes them. Children run in the city's streets — this is tolerated, because children run, because they always have, because the time when they run is shorter than it seems when you are in it. They fall into the shallow pools and emerge cold and pleased. They press their faces into the grikes to see the small things in the dark. They call to the birds overhead with improvised syllables and consider it success when the bird does not leave.

The children are taught by the activity of the adults around them, primarily. There are people whose knowledge-role includes the deliberate teaching of children — not teachers in the sense of a profession that confers authority, but people who are known to have a way with the passing of things on, who are given time for it, who do it with the satisfaction of work that agrees with them. What is passed on first is the practical: water, stone, plant, weather. The vocabulary of the wet, the reading of the wall, the grammar of the pool. Later, the records; later, the harder knowledges that require a person to have lived long enough to understand why they matter.

Children are not shielded from the long view. They are brought to the very old parts of the city and shown the lichen chronology and told: this stone was laid before the knowledge of who laid it was lost. They are shown the tablets from centuries ago and someone reads to them — haltingly, because the old forms require work — the record of a cistern dug by people whose names are in the margin. They grow up knowing, in their bodies, that they are in the middle of something. Not at the beginning, not at the end, but in the middle of a long process that they are temporarily part of and will be part of after they are not present. This is not frightening to them. This is the basic condition of life as they understand it. The stone was here before and will be here after, and you are in it for this portion, and your portion is: take care of the portion.

### Their Relationship to Visitors

They receive visitors with attentiveness and without warmth, and by warmth I mean the performed kind, the welcome-to-our-city kind. There is no performance. There is instead a careful looking: who is this person, what do they need to know right now, what will be useful to tell them versus what will confuse them or worry them unnecessarily, where can they stand or sleep or eat that will cost the least and provide the most. The attention is not unfriendly. It is rigorous.

They find visitors exhausting in precise ways. Visitors speak at volumes that cost sound unnecessarily. Visitors are urgent about things that are not urgent. Visitors mistake the city's silence for emptiness and the people's stillness for absence, and then are startled when the stillness resolves into a person, and the startle disturbs the quiet, which disturbs the next ten minutes. Visitors ask questions whose answers require context the visitor doesn't have, so answering them is either a long process or a shallow one, and neither is satisfying. Visitors have opinions about the city's aesthetics — meaning well, meaning to compliment — and the opinions arrive as description from outside, as the city-as-object, and this is disorienting, because the city is not an object to stand before and have an opinion about, it is a system to be inside.

They also find visitors interesting. A new person carries different knowledge, differently formed — knowledge from lower altitudes, from warmer places, from faster timelines. This knowledge has uses that are not always predictable in advance. They listen to visitors more carefully than visitors realize, and what they are listening for is not the explicit information but the system behind it: how does this person think about time, about water, about the relationship between built and natural things. From that system you can begin to understand what their knowledge is actually worth, and what it cost them to have it, and what they don't know because they don't live here.

They ask visitors questions that seem simple and are not. *What is the water like where you come from?* This is a real question. It has implications for what the visitor's body knows about thirst and cold and taste, for what the visitor takes for granted about water as a resource, for whether the visitor understands the cisterns as a matter of course or sees them as infrastructure and therefore as choice, as effort, as something that could be otherwise. The answer tells you a great deal about who you are talking to. The visitor usually thinks it is small talk.

### The Old

Growing old here is growing into the stone. Not a metaphor — or not only one. The cold and damp work on bodies in the long run; the joints stiffen in ways that can be managed and in ways that cannot. The people who have lived here longest have accepted a progressive quieting of the body's range as a natural outcome of the duration, the way the city's older walls have accepted a progressive deepening of their lichen load. The body arrives at what it will arrive at. You manage what can be managed; you accept what cannot be managed with the same lack of drama with which you accept the mist.

The old are where the knowledge lives deepest. This is not an idealization — they are difficult sometimes, entrenched in ways that the present doesn't always need, slow in ways that exceed even the plateau's patience. But they have read more walls. They have seen more seasons of the particular kind this plateau produces. They know which of the city's systems will fail in which patterns, because they have seen the patterns complete at least once. A very old person, in this city, has the kind of knowledge that only arrives as accumulated experience of a specific place over many decades, the kind of knowledge that cannot be recorded fully because so much of it lives in the recognition, in the *that's not right* that fires before the reason for it can be articulated.

They are not feared, exactly. Death is the plateau teaching you one more thing about permanence — it is not a lesson that anyone else can explain to you in advance, and the people of the plateau know this and do not try overmuch. What they do is: sit with the dying person in the way they sit with most things, present and not urgent, available without demanding anything, quiet enough that the person in question can hear the particular silence they are approaching. The mist is the same temperature as the air. The stone is cool and damp. These are the final calibrations of experience here, and they are not wrong ones to end with.

### What They Believe

Not in any codified way that lends itself to summary. What they believe lives in the practices, in the way the practices have been performed for long enough that the performance is its own argument.

They believe in the system. Not as an article of faith but as an empirical matter: the system works, demonstrably, because it has worked, for longer than any account that begins with "I remember when". The water comes. The stone holds. The body, managed correctly, lasts a good while. The city, managed correctly, outlasts anyone's ability to claim credit for it. These are beliefs in the sense that they guide action, in the sense that deviating from them produces consequences that confirm their value.

They believe in the record. What has been written should be preserved; what is not written will be lost; loss is the exception worth preventing, not the rule worth accepting. They do not sentimentalize loss — things are lost, things end, this is true — but they resist it with the persistent resistance of water through limestone, finding the next channel when the current one is blocked.

They believe, loosely, in an idea that they do not have a word for in the way that English doesn't have a word for *saudade* and so must borrow it: an idea that the plateau is an ongoing process and they are part of that process, not the purpose of it, and that this is entirely acceptable. There is no theology, precisely, or there is nothing that wears theology's usual clothing — no named divine, no redemption narrative, no teleology that places humans at the end-point of a divine plan. There is instead a kind of structural humility that lives below conscious belief: we are the latest layer of what has been laying down here for a very long time. We are the most recent lichen on an ancient stone. We are inside a duration we cannot see the ends of. This is the condition. The condition is not a problem to be solved.

---

### What They Are, Finally

They are the conclusion the plateau kept almost-reaching. They are what the water became when it had long enough. They are what the stone made when it turned to building, and what the building made when it turned to people, and what the people made when they had lived in the building in the stone long enough to start becoming the stone a little themselves — covertly, persistently, cold at depth in the way of permanence, full of something they absorbed from the rock under their feet and the clouds forming overhead and the mist that is the same temperature as the air and the air that is the same temperature as the stone.

They are patient in the way that the system is patient: not because patience is a virtue they have chosen to cultivate, but because they have been trained by a landscape that rewards it, over enough generations that the reward is now built into the shape of how they are.

They are not serene. Serenity suggests that the inner weather has been stilled, and the inner weather here is as active as the plateau's weather: things form, things shift, things move in ways that are hard to track. What looks like serenity from outside is simply the absence of urgency about the inner weather, the same acceptance of cloud and mist and brief clarity that the plateau itself models. You are not required to resolve the weather. You are only required to continue through it.

They hold the cold water in the pale bowl. They look into the pool until the small movement becomes visible. They press the palm to the stone and feel the wet that is always there. They wait for the far rim to appear in the weather and are content if it does not.

They are here. They have been here. They will have been here, in the lichen and the record and the wall, after the particular shapes of them have joined the stone.

This, to them, is sufficient. The plateau asks nothing more, and they have learned, over the long time of learning it, that nothing more is exactly enough.
