# What Lives in the Lock

---

Begin with what is oldest, because it sets the terms.

Before the mussels, before the eels, before the jackdaws discovered that iron holds heat after noon and the cage lamp never goes cold — before any of that, there was something that came with the water when the water came, and has been here since. Not an organism. Not anything you could find in a field guide or scrape off a plate for examination. Something more like a disposition. A tendency the place developed toward itself.

Call it the Lock's attention.

It is the quality of noticing that a place acquires when it has been noticed-in for long enough. Every chamber that has held water and echoed sound and offered surfaces to cling to has eventually grown something like this: a gathered awareness that is not awareness exactly, that has no center, no organ of perception, but that orients. That turns, faintly, toward what enters. The way iron turns toward rust, without wanting to, without trying — just the nature of iron meeting the nature of wet air, a tendency becoming an outcome.

The Lock's attention is distributed through the whole chamber. It lives in the mineral stain on the walls — in the orange and umber delta-maps the water has made, running outward from every rivet head across the plates, because these marks are the record of the water's passage and the water has been passing for a long time and the record of a long passage becomes, eventually, a kind of memory. A map is already a mind of sorts. A map that drew itself over decades, in iron oxide, on iron, in a room that no one was watching — that is something further along.

The Lock's attention is not benevolent. It is not malevolent. It is the wrong category entirely. It attends. When the wagtail steps along the waterline, the lock attends. When a pike moves through from the lower pound, unhurried, the lock attends. When you stand on the gate beam and the sound of the place fills you, the lock attends to that too, in the same way it attends to everything: completely, without preference, without any implication that your being here is more significant than the gudgeon working the floor. This is not humbling in the way that vast things are humbling. It is humbling in the way that being accurately perceived is humbling.

---

The mussels are the Lock's oldest biological collaborators, if collaborator is the word, and it isn't, but there isn't a better one.

They came in when the lower gate failed, when the fallen leaf settled into the floor of the lower pound and the flow-through was established and the lock's character changed from sealed to permeable. The first ones were small, filter-feeding organisms doing filter-feeding things, innocent of anything like significance. But they have been here long enough, now, that the oldest layers of their colonization are below the living mussels, dead shells colonized by younger shells, the whole assemblage building up like geological strata — and in a place whose walls already think in strata, in layers, in the accumulation of one thing on another over time, the mussels are not merely decorating the iron. They are participating in the same project.

The project has no name and no goal and it does not benefit anyone. But it is what the lock is doing.

The mussels' specific contribution is patience, which they have in enormous quantities, and the transformation of suspended life into fixed structure, which is a kind of translation — taking what is dispersed and making it permanent — and if you watch the walls closely enough you can see that this is what the mineral staining does too, taking dissolved iron and making it pattern, and what the rust does, taking reactive metal and making it sealed, and what the old wood of the surviving gate leaf has done, taking rot and making it density. The lock converts. Everything in it is involved in conversion, in the slow alchemy of making what is temporary into what will last. The mussels are the most legible version of this but they are not the only version. They are merely the version that learned to breathe.

---

The perch.

The perch are not part of the lock in the way the mussels are. The perch are visitors who have stayed, which is different. They came through the open gate and found the chamber's still green water better than the restless canal beyond, and they have remained, holding in the shadow near the lower gate, their fins making the small corrections that hold a fish in a current that is barely a current.

What they have is the knowledge of interior.

Most fish live in the world of open water, where the definition of space is temporary and the boundaries are understood to be negotiable — where the edge of your territory is a matter of pressure and negotiation and can shift with the season or the competitor. The perch in the lock have learned a different thing: they have learned the edges of a room. They know where the walls are, in the sonar sense, in the lateral-line sense, in whatever sense a fish knows the geometry it moves inside. They carry the shape of the chamber in their bodies the way a river-fish carries the shape of the particular bend it has lived in for years — not as knowledge exactly, but as orientation, as the calibration of instinct to specific fact.

This makes them slightly different from other perch. A biologist, examining them, would find nothing different. The difference is not in their bodies. It is in how they move, in the slight additional economy of their movement, in the way they use the space in a room-like way — parceling it, keeping to their portion, using the walls as reference. Fish that know a room move in it the way people know a room in the dark: with confidence that is not sight-confidence but memory-confidence, the confidence of a body that has mapped a space in its nerves.

The gudgeon, by contrast, are pure function. They work the floor. They have no relationship with the lock except the practical relationship of mouth to sediment. They are the lock's janitors, if janitors is not too grand a word for small brown fish. They go about their business with the complete indifference of specialists. The lock neither notices them specially nor doesn't; they are simply part of what the floor does.

---

The eels.

The eels are the lock's nocturnal intelligence, if intelligence is the word, and it isn't, but there isn't a better one here either.

During the day you will not see them. The sediment carries the signs of their passage — the sinuous groove left by a body moving through silt, the displaced mussel, the disturbed patch where something was investigated and found uninteresting — but the eels themselves are in the iron darkness behind the corroded sluice housing, or in whatever gaps the rusted plates have opened between themselves and the ribs behind them, or simply buried in the floor sediment, which is deep enough in the corners of the lock to hide an animal of considerable size.

At night they come out.

What the eels do at night is not dramatically different from what the gudgeon do during the day — they feed, they investigate, they move along the walls and the floor looking for whatever is available. But the specific quality of an eel moving through dark water, in an iron chamber, with the lamp casting its orange warmth across the surface and the jackdaws still for once, is the quality of a needle threaded through cloth: purposeful, sinuous, intimate with the material it moves through. The eel does not push water aside the way a fish pushes water aside. The eel finds the water's existing movement and follows it, extends it, becomes temporarily part of the current that the gate leakage and the sluice seep have established.

There is one very old eel. This is not mysticism; this is biology. Eels can live for a very long time, in captivity or in a good settled habitat, and a lock like this is an almost ideal eel habitat — protected, food-available, undisturbed, the right temperature in the sediment. This one is old enough to be fat in the way of old eels, the yellow-brown of age rather than the dark of young muscle, and it is large enough that when it moves along the base of the iron wall the movement is visible from the gate beam above, a displacement of water, a slow shadow. It has not gone to sea. It should have gone to sea years ago, decades ago perhaps, made the migration to the Sargasso and the spawning and the death that all Atlantic eels make — but it hasn't, and the reason it hasn't is something the biology only partially explains. Landlocked eels, eels in enclosed systems, sometimes delay the migration indefinitely. Sometimes they simply don't go. They stay and grow old and heavy and they become the oldest thing in the water.

This eel is older than the jackdaws' claim to the chamber. It is older than the mussel strata on the walls. It has been here long enough that the lock's attention has settled into a particular relationship with it — not communication, not recognition, nothing that personal — but a familiarity of the kind that two things develop when they have been in the same room for a very long time. A co-shaping. The eel has learned the floor of this specific lock the way the lock has, over time, produced this specific floor, and these two processes are not unrelated, because the eel's movement through the sediment has been part of how the sediment has distributed itself, which is part of how the floor is what it is, which is part of what the chamber is.

The eel is not the lock's soul. But it is the oldest living thread in the chamber's continuous now, the longest unbroken line from the past to the present moment, and in that sense it carries something that nothing else in here carries: the accumulated experience of this particular water, this particular iron, this particular dark.

---

The jackdaws.

The jackdaws are not subtle. The jackdaws are everything the lock is not: loud in intention, quick in movement, social in the chaotic way of corvids, invested in the short-term negotiation of who gets which ledge with an intensity that makes the long patience of the chamber seem, briefly, absurd.

But they are the chamber's voice.

The lock is acoustically extraordinary — the iron walls, the water surface, the curved ribs of the ceiling all conspire to make a space that holds sound and returns it changed — and the jackdaws are the only residents that produce sound complex enough to reveal this. When a jackdaw calls from the conduit angle where the lamp meets the wall, the call comes back from four directions simultaneously, each return slightly different in character, the high-frequency elements more absorbed by the water surface, the mid tones more reflected by the plates. What the jackdaw hears coming back is a dispersed and multiplied version of itself, spread across the chamber, and it responds to this — calls again, tilts its head, processes the echoes with the spatial intelligence that corvids use to navigate complex environments.

Whether the jackdaws understand, in any sense, that they are inside a machine, is a question without meaningful content. But they know they are inside something. Their behavior in the lock is different from their behavior outside it — subtly, in ways you would only notice if you watched them in both places. Inside, they move closer to the walls. They use vertical surfaces more. They call more frequently but with shorter intervals between calls, as if testing the echo response habitually, the way a blind person might tap a cane. They have mapped the lock acoustically, over time, in the way that the old eel has mapped it in sediment and muscle-memory.

The grey wagtail is a single bird, and probably not always the same bird, and is not a resident in the way the jackdaws are residents. It comes to pick the waterline below the gate leakage for invertebrates and it leaves when it's done. But its presence is so consistent — there is almost always a wagtail at that particular waterline, in the splash-zone of the leakage, stepping neatly over the mussel shells — that it has become part of the lock's character the way a particular sound becomes part of a room's character. You would notice the absence. The wagtail is the lock's most effortless thing, the thing that moves through it with the most ease, without the perch's hard-won room-knowledge or the eel's long sedimented history. The wagtail just walks along the water's edge the way a wagtail always walks along the water's edge, entirely portable, carrying its own wagtail-world inside the lock's world, barely touching.

---

There is one more thing, and it is harder to characterize.

The lamp hums. The lamp has been humming for a long time. The hum is a ground tone, below the water sounds, and it has been continuous long enough that the other sounds of the lock have organized themselves around it, or been organized by it in some very slight way — the way that a continuous bass tone will influence what resonates sympathetically in a room, what gets reinforced, what gets damped.

The lamp is connected by conduit to somewhere outside. The electricity that reaches it has traveled through that conduit for as long as the lamp has been on, which is for a very long time, long enough that the conduit's own insulation has degraded in one or two places and there is a faint bleed of current into the iron wall where the conduit mounts, and from there into the water, and through the water, distributed evenly throughout the chamber.

This current is immeasurably small. It is far below anything that any organism in the chamber could detect. It is, in any meaningful sense, nothing.

But it is the industrial still speaking. It is the one thing the lock still does that it was designed to do: conduct electricity from outside to inside, transform power to light, assert the ongoing fact of its own connection to the world beyond the embankments. The hum is the machine's last word, held indefinitely, a single note sustained past the point of meaning, past the point of anyone listening, sustained simply because the circuit is closed and the current is flowing and that is what a closed circuit with current in it does.

The animals live around this hum. It is their weather, in a sense — omnipresent, not biological, not theirs, but the constant condition under which they go about their biological business. The mussels filter. The eels move through the dark. The jackdaws dispute their ledges. The wagtail walks the waterline with the delicacy of someone who knows exactly where the edge is.

And the lock attends.

It has been attending for a very long time.

It will continue.
