## What Lives in the Green Town

---

Begin with what you cannot see but can deduce: the mycelium.

Before anything with eyes or preference or hunger, before anything that makes a choice, there is the network in the soil, running under everything, connecting tree to tree in filaments thinner than thread, brokering exchanges that have been running for longer than the settlement above has existed. The network does not *want* anything, exactly, but it behaves as though it wants continuity. It extends toward phosphorus. It routes carbon from where it is excess to where it is needed. It is, in some functional sense, the oldest resident here — or the most continuous one — and everything else lives above it the way a city lives above its infrastructure: mostly ignorant of it, entirely dependent on it, shaped by its logic without knowing the logic exists.

When you step, you compress it. It does not notice. It continues.

---

**The Moss**

The moss is not one organism but a community that reads as one: a continuous surface of living material covering every stable substrate that isn't actively disturbed. On the ground, on the stones, on the north faces of walls, on the bark from root collar to the first major branch, on the decomposing log by the seep, on the bones of old thatch.

Each individual plant is tiny — a fraction of a centimeter, upright, unbranched or simply branched, covered in leaves so small they can only be understood at scale, which is to say they can only be understood as moss rather than as individual plants. Moss is always understood at scale. It is a collective noun that has eaten the singular.

The moss lives by being wet. This place is nearly always wet. The moss is therefore nearly always at its best: metabolically active, growing, extending its territory in the directions where surface is available. In dry spells it desiccates without dying — it simply pauses, contracts, goes grey-brown and crisp, and waits for water with a patience that is not patience at all because patience requires time-awareness and moss has none. It simply is not dead. And then it rains and it is green again and the pause never happened from the moss's perspective because the moss has no perspective, only state.

The moss softens every surface it covers. It absorbs footfall sound. It insulates. It holds water in itself like a sponge, releasing it slowly, mediating the pulses and gaps of rainfall into something more continuous, more reliable, and in doing so provides the constant thin film of moisture on which a dozen other organisms depend for their existence. If you removed the moss — a counterfactual the place itself makes almost unimaginable — you would remove a regulatory layer, and the whole enterprise would become more violent: wetter in rain, drier between rains, less livable for frogs and newts and the particular species of beetle that requires the moss-dark microclimate under the larger colonies.

The moss does not know this. It is not doing it on purpose. But it is doing it.

**The Trees**

The trees here are ancient without being large, and this combination gives them a quality that large ancient trees elsewhere do not have: a kind of condensation. Everything that happens to a very old tree in terms of surface complexity — the deep furrowing of bark, the hollows where branches have fallen and the wound has not quite closed, the crown dieback and regrowth, the slow tortuous departure from vertical as the years and the wind impose their corrections — all of this has happened here, but in small compass, in a low spreading frame, so that the density of history is higher per square meter of tree than you would find in a tree of the same age that had grown in kinder conditions.

They are oak, mostly, though the sessile kind, the kind that belongs to altitude and poor soil, shorter-leaved, more compact in its acorn. Some rowan among them, growing from the rock crevices where no oak could establish, adapted to roothold in stone with the pragmatism of species that colonize difficult places first. Birch at the margins where the bowl edge rises — pioneer trees, still pioneering even after decades, the advance scouts of woodland into moorland, slowly extending the boundary.

Each tree is a habitat. This is not metaphor. A single old oak in full surface complexity is host to hundreds of species: the lichens in their vertical zones, the mosses, the fungi at every stage from root to crown, the beetles in the dead wood, the birds in the crown, the small mammals in the hollows. The tree is a structure on which a community assembles. The tree feeds them (directly, through leaf litter and seed and sap and the slow release of nutrients from its own decomposing fallen matter) and houses them (in its bark fissures, its hollows, its canopy volume). In exchange — and it is not exchange, there is no contract, but the functional result is exchange — the birds eat the parasites that would otherwise limit the tree, the fungi extend the roots, the lichens contribute nitrogen fixed from air.

The trees grow slowly here. A century to reach the spread of a tree that would reach the same spread in fifty years elsewhere. The slowness makes them dense: tight growth rings, hard wood, surfaces accumulated over long time that have had long time to accumulate occupants.

They do not think. They respond. They reach toward light in ways that take decades but are directional. They shut down when damaged. They send chemical signals through the air when under insect attack, signals that neighboring trees respond to by up-regulating their own defenses. This is not communication in any sense that requires a mind, but it is information transfer, and the result is a coordinated response. The trees as a collective have something like a shared immune response.

They are indifferent to the settlement among them, neither welcoming nor hostile, simply continuing.

**The Frogs**

The frogs are everywhere and they have given themselves entirely to the project of being frogs in a maximally frog-suitable environment.

There are two kinds here, possibly three: the common frog in its thousand variations of brown and green and mottled, adapting its coloration to whatever surface it last occupied, which is a passive process but creates the impression of deliberate concealment; the palmate newt, technically not a frog but filling similar roles in the still water of the larger pools; and, in the margins, in the transition zone between the true wet and the drier heath at the bowl's rim, the toad — less numerous, more deliberate in its movements, possessed of a gravity the frogs lack.

The frogs' life is very old in the sense of being very simple, which is to say very refined. Six hundred million years of tetrapod evolution and before that deep time in water, and the frog is what you get when you commit entirely to the interface of water and land, to the production of eggs in numbers, to the strategy of not being noticed if possible, to finding insects and eating them, to surviving winter by becoming cold and still and waiting.

In spring the calling is extraordinary, and in fall the silence as they descend into the mud at the pool bottom, where they will breathe through their skin in water that barely moves, that barely contains oxygen, that barely differs from death except that they emerge from it, is its own kind of extraordinary. But right now it is neither spring nor fall and they are simply frog-ing: sitting, watching, eating, being eaten occasionally by the heron that visits the largest pool in the early morning before the settlement wakes.

They watch you with a quality of attention that feels like suspicion but is just the high-gain alert-state of an animal that has many predators. The eye is gold-ringed, ancient-looking, a design from very deep time.

One frog on the large mossy stone by the seep has been sitting in the same position for forty minutes. It is not doing nothing — it is waiting, which is a kind of active behavior, the body poised for tongue-strike — but externally it is entirely still. The moss grows around its feet.

**The Insects**

The insects are the medium through which the rest of the place conducts its business.

They are the primary converters of plant material — leaf, wood, root, seed — into animal protein, and in being that conversion layer, they are what makes the bird density and the amphibian density possible. Without them, the thrush has nothing to extract from the path, the wren has nothing to pull from the wall gap, the frogs have nothing to lunge at. They are the base of the chain that includes everything visible, and they are themselves so numerous that they constitute a significant portion of the biomass of the place.

The midges swarm because the warm moist air above the seeps is where they mate and because this is what midges do with their brief adult lives: they mate in column, they lay eggs in water, they die, and the larvae in the water grow and emerge and mate in column and the column is the point of the whole enterprise. The column is the midge's purpose made visible.

The beetles in the bark are less visible but more various: bark beetles and longhorns and the small round things that reduce dead wood to frass and in doing so break it down faster than fungi alone would manage, accelerating the return of wood-carbon to soil. You can hear them if you press your ear to old timber, a sound like very quiet chewing, which is exactly what it is.

The bees work the meadowsweet with a focused efficiency that looks like pleasure but is appetite and nervous system and the shape of the flower fitting the shape of the body in a way that was not designed by anyone but emerged from ten million years of mutual influence. The bee's tongue fits the flower's nectary. The flower's anthers are positioned to brush the bee's body where the pollen will be carried to the next flower. This is an arrangement so precise it looks deliberate, and in a sense it is — it was arrived at through deliberation by selection, a different kind of deliberation, one conducted in generations rather than moments.

There is a spider. There are many spiders. The orb weavers have built their webs between the upright stems of the rushes, in the gap between shrubs, in the space between the building wall and the woodpile. The webs are beaded with water and visible now in a way they won't be when the moisture burns off (if it burns off). The spider sits in the center or hangs beneath, a thread connected to the hub so that vibration in the web transmits through the thread to the spider's legs. The spider reads the web through its body. Every caught insect is a different vibration, interpretable: this is prey, this is debris, this is something too large, retract.

They do not dream. They vibrate.

**The Mammals No One Sees**

The voles in the grass base of the bowl margin, running through their tunnel-system in the dense vegetation, never pausing, always eating, their metabolism so high that they must eat nearly constantly to survive it. Their heartbeat at rest is something above three hundred beats per minute. They are warm and small and desperately alive and no one in the settlement, probably, thinks much about them because they are invisible, but they are numerous and they are converting plant material into vole-flesh that will become weasel or owl or kestrel, and in their tunneling they are aerating the soil, and in their dying they are fertilizing it.

The stoat moves through periodically — not a resident but a traveler, using the bowl as part of a larger circuit. It hunts through the shrub layer with a sinuous efficiency that seems boneless, its body flowing through gaps that seem too small for it, because the body is extraordinarily flexible and because the stoat commits entirely to pursuit. When it is on a scent it does not divert. It has a single mode: find and kill. The voles know this in their nervous systems without knowing it as a concept, and their heart rates, already high, go higher when the stoat-smell reaches them through the tunnel walls.

The red deer comes down from the moorland in the evening sometimes, to the seep water. It is large and incongruous among the low trees, having to lower its head awkwardly to drink, its antlers catching on the overhanging branches. It is not comfortable here. It comes for the water and leaves. But it has been coming for years, the same path down the bowl edge visible as a slight trough in the heather, and in its coming it has moved seeds on its coat, transferred parasites to the tick population and received ticks from it, and occasionally left behind a loose-shed piece of velvet that was quickly investigated by the corvids and carried off to a nest.

No one lives here in these bodies with these metabolisms except by being unable to live at any other speed.

**The Birds**

The wren is extraordinary and does not know it.

It is the smallest of the regular residents, weighing under ten grams, a thing so light it might seem vestigial, but it generates the loudest call relative to body size of any bird in this place and possibly any place, a sound of such astonishing volume for the body it emerges from that it reads as biological hubris, or alternatively as perfect efficiency — every possible acoustic resource extracted from every gram of tissue. The call is territorial and complex, a sequence that can vary and does vary, but that carries the signature of this individual wren, distinguishable to other wrens from the calls of neighboring wrens, a tiny individual embedded in a web of acoustic information as complex and local as a human knows their neighbors by voice.

The wren extracts invertebrates from the stone wall with a beak evolved to exactly the purpose: thin, slightly down-curved, designed for crevice-investigation. It extracts and eats and moves along the wall and extracts and eats and the efficiency is complete. The wren is a machine for finding small invertebrates in small gaps and converting them into wren tissue and then into wren eggs and then into more wrens. It does this with a kind of furious joy that is not joy but is functionally indistinguishable from it in its intensity.

The thrush on the path is doing the same thing at a larger scale and with a different technique: the anvil-method for snails, the listening-and-striking for worms. It cocks its head not to look at the ground — its eye placement doesn't allow binocular vision downward — but to *listen* for the movement of worms through soil. It hears them. It strikes with the confidence of something that rarely misses and when it does, recalibrates and tries again. The thrush is a patient thing, measured, moving with the deliberateness of a larger body and a slower metabolism.

Up in the branches: a pair of long-tailed tits moving through the canopy in a small flock with others of their kind, traveling together, a habit that keeps more eyes on the sky where sparrowhawks come from. They are brief visitors to any given branch, spending seconds investigating bark and lichen for the insects that shelter there, then moving on, their high contact-calls stitching the flock together across the canopy. The flock moves like a single organism, not because it is one but because each individual bird is tracking its neighbors and moving with them, the collective behavior emerging from individual reaction times.

The buzzard above is doing the slow long work of finding something large enough to be worth the energy of capture. It has been circling for an hour. It will circle for another hour if necessary. The thermal that lifts it costs no energy to use — it simply spreads its wings and tilts and the air does the lifting. From up there the bowl is visible as the thickening of vegetation it is, the structures identifiable among the trees by the angles that nature doesn't make, the straight line of a ridgepole, the geometry of a wall. The buzzard does not make these distinctions. It watches for movement. It has the patience of an animal that can float for nothing, that can simply wait at altitude until something below reveals itself.

**The People**

And the people.

They are the hardest to describe because description wants to generalize and these are specific people living specific lives, but some things can be said from the outside, from the evidence of the settlement and its tools and its construction and the way it sits inside the bowl's ecology.

They live by knowing. The knowledge is practical and it is vast and it is held collectively — no single person knows all of it, but the community holds it, and has held it for generations, the kind of knowledge that only persists if it is transmitted and practiced continuously, because it is knowledge of how to do things rather than knowledge of facts, and how-to-do-things knowledge dies if it is not done.

They know the moss: which is which, which can be used for what, which is healthy and which indicates something wrong with the drainage. They know the timber: when it was cut, how it was dried, how it will move with moisture and how to account for that movement in the joinery. They know the seeps: where they run, when they slow, what it means when a particular one changes character, because that change might mean something is happening in the hillside above that will eventually matter here.

They know the birds as background, as continuous information feed: when the birds go quiet there is a reason and the reason is sometimes relevant to what they are doing. They know the insects in the practical sense of knowing which bite and which do not, which indicate what, which are welcome in the woodpile and which are not. They know the plants as material: which is medicinal and in what preparation, which is edible and when, which makes fiber, which makes dye, which to leave alone.

This knowledge has the quality of landscape-specific expertise that only comes from being in one place long enough, across generations, that the place's patterns have become readable. They read it the way you read a language you grew up in, without thinking about the grammar, without translating — directly, immediately, with the accuracy of deep familiarity.

They are not peaceful in the sense of being without conflict — they are people, with all that implies. But the conflicts here are the small conflicts of close quarters, of people who have to cooperate because the alternative to cooperation is difficulty, and difficulty in this climate, at this altitude, with this much required maintenance, is something no one can afford alone. The social structure whatever it is — and from outside you can only infer that there is one — must be workable enough to have produced a functional settlement over a long enough time for the structures to have acquired their moss and the trees to have grown their lichens and the paths to have been worn into the ground by feet following the same line for generations.

They do not find this place beautiful, probably, in the way you find it beautiful when you arrive from outside with the framing that arrival gives. They find it *normal*, which is harder and more intimate — they see it without the aestheticizing distance that novelty provides, see it as the ground of ordinary life, which means sometimes tedious, sometimes difficult, sometimes a source of the particular despair that comes from being wet for too long, or from a bad harvest of whatever they harvest, or from the simple friction of living close to other people who are also wet and tired. But the *shape* of normality is this: the sound of many frogs, the smell of peat and meadowsweet, the green-filtered light, the path to the water worn soft by long use, the fire smell from the common building, the wren's outrageous call from the wall.

They have been shaped by this place over many generations and they have shaped it, slightly, in return — the cleared area, the paths, the altered drainage from the structures' rooflines, the effects of their fire-use on the wood supply and therefore on the canopy structure. The shaping has been gentle, or the place has not allowed it to be otherwise: this environment insists on its own terms, and a community that wanted to exist here long-term had to accept those terms, had to build with the wet rather than against it, had to burn wood at a rate the woodland could replace, had to work around the hydrology rather than redirecting it.

The children here learn the place before they learn anything else. They learn it in their feet, in the mud and moss and water that are their first unmediated environment. They learn the frog-catch technique, which is patience and the right angle of cupped hands. They learn to read the weather by the feel of the air before the visual signals arrive, because at this altitude the weather matters and you want as much warning as possible. They are becoming people of this specific place, which means they are becoming people who carry this place's logic in their bodies, who will struggle to thrive anywhere that operates on different logic — lower, drier, louder with mechanical sound, brighter at night.

They are not happy or unhappy in any categorical way. They are *here*, which is the strongest claim any organism makes about any place.

**The Place as a Whole**

What lives here is not a list of species and one human community. What lives here is the *system*, the whole thing as a running process, the bog and the trees and the moss and the mycelium and the frogs and the beetles and the birds and the people and the water and the particular quality of the light that comes through this canopy to reach this moss — all of it together, all of it in relation, all of it running on the ancient energy of rain falling on high ground and finding its way down, of sunlight being caught by chlorophyll and converted to structure, of structure falling apart and being rebuilt and falling apart again in cycles so long they look like stability.

The stability is not stasis. The stability is a kind of turbulent equilibrium, a dynamic balance maintained not despite the constant flux but by means of it: the frogs eating the insects, the birds eating the frogs, the fungi breaking down the birds when they die, the trees taking up the nutrients the fungi release, the insects feeding on the trees, the frogs eating the insects. It runs. It has been running. It will run differently as the climate shifts and the inputs change, but the tendency of this bowl, this catchment, this wet declivity in the high moorland, is to be *full* of life. The bowl catches things. That is what it does. And what has filled it, over enough time, is this: this sound, this smell, this particular green light, these specific creatures in their webs of dependency, and people who learned, somehow, across the long transmission of practical knowledge from hand to hand and year to year, how to be part of it rather than opposed to it.

The wren calls. The water finds the next lower surface. The moss, damp and continuous, holds the whole soft floor of the world together.
