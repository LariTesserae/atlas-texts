## What Lives Here

---

Begin with this: they have been here longer than they can know, and they do not need to know.

The frogs — call them frogs, the word is close enough, the shape is close enough, the ancient arrangement of limbs and wet skin and wide-set eyes is close enough — have lived on this flat for so long that the question of how they came to be here has dissolved entirely. There was no arriving. There is only the being here, which is continuous, which is the same in this generation as in every prior generation going back past any coherent counting, a chain of small wet lives each one indistinguishable from the last, the whole chain indistinguishable from the place itself. They are not adapted to the mud flat. They are the mud flat, organized into creatures that move and call and catch and die and become mud again.

Their color first: yellow-brown deepening to clay-gray at the flanks, with markings — irregular mottlings, patches of darker brown, occasional faint suggestions of green where the bacterial mat has tinted them — that are not quite camouflage in the deliberate-seeming way that word implies, but are simply the result of living so long against this background that to be any other color would be to be from somewhere else. A freshly dead frog placed against the clay surface disappears. This is not a trick. It is a convergence. They and the ground have been working on each other for a very long time, and both have arrived at the same color.

Their size: small to medium. Nothing here is large. The largest are the length of a palm, heavy-bodied, their mass concentrated toward the head and shoulders in the way of things that sit and wait. The smallest are translucent, their internal workings dimly visible through the skin — hearts beating at a rate that would look like a malfunction in anything larger. There are many sizes in between, because there is always a next generation arriving, always a cohort that hatched some weeks ago and is still working out the dimensions of a body. In any given pool there are animals at five or six stages of becoming, all of them calling as they are able.

---

Look at one closely, if you can — if it has not thrown itself sideways into the water at your approach, if it is sitting at the edge of a clay pan in that motionlessness that frogs achieve, the motionlessness that is not stillness but a kind of suspended readiness, every muscle in a particular not-quite-relaxed state that allows for sudden action while presenting the appearance of a stone.

The eyes are gold, or amber, or something between them — a color that the landscape does not otherwise contain, and this is the only way in which these animals depart from the general palette of the place. The eye is divided horizontally by the pupil, a long dark slit oriented on the axis of the horizon, so that the frog sees the world in two halves — the below and the above, both in focus simultaneously. This is an eye evolved for a flat world, for a world where the important information is: what moves on the water's surface, what moves in the water below, what passes against the sky above. For a world, in other words, exactly like this one. The eye is a document of the place.

The skin breathes. This is not a metaphor. Oxygen and carbon dioxide cross it directly, and a frog can sit beneath the shallow water for longer than you would expect, absorbing what it needs through the skin alone, watching whatever passes in the dimly lit column of water above it, deciding nothing. The skin is kept wet by this water and by its own secretions, which are faintly alkaline, which do not smell like anything except wet animal, which have their own minor chemistry that does something — repels something, signals something, the exact transaction is not fully clear. In the thin sheets of water across the clay pans, when many of them are present in a small area, the water acquires a faint iridescent quality at the surface, barely perceptible, a suggestion of oil without the oil, the ghost of a color that isn't there in better light.

They eat whatever moves at the right size. Insects — the things that make the high layered shimmer in the upper register of the sound. Smaller animals in the water. Each other, sometimes, in the way that frogs sometimes eat each other, without apparent conflict or meaning: the smaller one simply is the right size, and the larger one's tongue resolves the situation. The tongue is fast past any comfortable observation of it. A thing is there and then it isn't, and the frog adjusts its throat with one deliberate swallow and returns to stillness.

---

The sound.

It cannot be discussed separately from the animals, because the sound is not something they produce incidentally. It is what they are doing. It is the primary activity.

Each one calls, and the call is not directed — or it is directed everywhere at once, sent out into the general air as a signal that exists independent of any particular receiver. The call says: *I am here. I am of the right kind. I am alive and present in this particular moisture, in this particular portion of the flat, in this moment of the long unbroken continuity.* It says this to others of its kind and invites reply. The replies come — from every direction, at every distance, overlapping without resolution, building into that enormous texture, that governing condition, that weather-that-is-sound.

What is strange, if you listen past the first overwhelming impression, is that the calls are not uniform. They are not random, either. There are types: the deep resonant throb that carries the farthest and sits lowest in the frequency range, made by the largest and most established animals, which is to say the ones that have survived longest in this place that is already the survivor of everything. There is the fast pulse, higher and more urgent, made by animals that are younger or smaller or both, who have more to prove or more to lose. There is the long ascending trill, which seems to be a specific invitation, a specific address, though to whom and for what purpose requires more observation than a brief visit allows. There may be others — there are probably others — that exist at frequencies the human ear collapses into the background rather than resolving individually. The whole, heard as a whole, is the call of the flat. The parts, attended to carefully, are thousands of distinct negotiations.

They do not stop at night, except that night doesn't quite happen here — the sky seals to the same gray at all hours, the light diminishes slightly, the mist thickens. In this lesser darkness the calling continues. It has probably not stopped for ten thousand years. Maybe more. The humans who came here and placed their stones and wove their small shelter would have slept inside this sound, would have wakened inside it, would have sat in the dimness of whatever they were doing and felt it as a physical weight and after a time not noticed it and after a longer time been unable to imagine its absence.

---

Now the others. The longer things whose wakes cross the standing water.

They are not frogs, or not the same frogs. They are something in the same broad category of life, something that shares an ancestor somewhere far back — or perhaps they are frogs in a further stage of becoming, shapes the flat has selected over time toward a different specialization. They are longer and lower, moving through the water with a lateral flexion, the body sweeping side to side in a rhythm that is unhurried and continuous. They are the color of deep mud. Their eyes are also horizontal-pupiled, perhaps even more drastically so, as if the slit has widened to cover nearly the whole iris — animals that see the world as almost entirely horizontal plane, whose entire perceptual life is oriented to the surface they move through.

Whether they are predators to the smaller ones is not quite settled. They are certainly larger. They certainly make those wakes. But they move through crowded water without apparent aggression, and the small ones do not panic except when approached suddenly from above — which is to say, they treat the large ones as part of the landscape. Perhaps the large ones are eating below the surface, taking different prey, operating in a different layer of the same place. Perhaps they were once in competition with the smaller ones and the competition resolved long ago into something less costly for both. The flat does not contain animals wasting energy on unsettled questions. Everything here has been settled. The arrangements are old and stable and they hold.

There are others in the mud itself, not in the water. Worms is too small a word, but the word toward which this points: long, slow, deep in the soft clay below the ceramic skin, moving through pockets of water and seams of moisture in paths that must have been first made by previous generations and deepened and widened over time, so that the structure of the wet subsurface clay is partly natural and partly a record of all the animals that have moved through it. They turn the clay. They process it, slow as geology, and what comes out the other end is slightly different clay — clay that has passed through a body, clay that has been touched by the minor chemistry of life and emerged still clay but changed by the encounter. The bacterial mats at the surface depend on this. The mats and the things in the mud are in a relationship older than the mats themselves, each one making the conditions for the other, neither able to identify the boundary between itself and the process it participates in.

They do not call. They make no sound. They are the silence inside the noise, if silence is a thing that can exist here, which is doubtful.

---

And then: the insects. If insects is the right word, and it is nearly right.

They exist in clouds above the bacterial mats, in layers of density that vary with temperature and time — denser in the slight warmth that rises from the mats, thinner where the air sits colder over open clay. They are small enough that individually they register as near-invisible; collectively they are a shimmer, a visual texture, a haze that is alive. They have wings that beat at a frequency in the upper range of hearing, and this is the high shimmer in the sound, the layer that sits above the frog-chorus and fills the registers the frogs leave empty. The sound they make collectively is not any one of them. It is what happens when a great number of small wingbeats exist in the same column of air and interfere with each other in a way that produces something coherent, a tone that no individual creates.

They are feeding on the mats. Or they are laying eggs in the standing water. Or both. Or they complete their entire life cycle within the compass of a few meters — from egg in the water to larva in the water to adult in the air above the water, brief and buzzing, making their contribution to the total sound, then returning to the water to begin the cycle again. The frogs eat them. The process is continuous. The numbers stay approximately constant. This is the balance, held but dynamic: the insects feed the frogs and the frogs feed the clay and the clay feeds the mats and the mats feed the insects, the whole thing turning over without advancing, without retreating, without arriving anywhere other than where it already is.

---

There may be birds, sometimes.

Visitors. Arrivals from somewhere with more vertical relief, somewhere with trees or cliffs or other promontories from which birds can take the long view. They come here because the here is full of food and the food is dense and easy. They land at the edge of standing water and stand with that bird-stillness — a different quality of stillness from the frog-stillness, more alert, more ready to depart — and take what they can reach with a long bill or a precise strike, and they do not stay. They cross the sky above the flat in ones and twos, brief shapes in the gray light, their wing-noise joining the insect-shimmer briefly before they are gone.

The frogs' horizontal pupils are, among other things, a defense against this. The slit allows them to watch the sky with part of each eye while keeping most of their attention on the horizontal plane. When a shape crosses above, there is a perturbation — a sudden disappearance of a few dozen animals into the water, a spreading silence in one register of the sound that recovers within seconds as the threat passes or fails to resolve into anything real. The flat reads the birds the way it reads everything: as temporary, as passing through, as something that will be gone before the sound stops.

---

The ones who built the structures.

They are not here now. But they were here long enough to leave stone and woven stem, which means they were here through many seasons, which means they knew the sound, which means they woke to it and slept inside it and may have dreamed something shaped by its rhythms. They were, like the birds, visitors — but slower visitors, visitors who tried for a while to be otherwise, who said *we are placing this stone, we are making this shelter, we are making marks on this place that will say we were here and that the here was ours.* The flat received these gestures with total equanimity. The flat had been doing what it does for longer than the word for long contains, and it would continue after the stones were undisturbed by any hand and the woven shelter had pressed itself flat against the clay in surrender.

What did they do here, the ones who placed the stones? Perhaps they came for the frogs — for food, for something the frogs carry in their skin that has a use. Perhaps they came because this was a boundary, the far edge of somewhere, and they marked it in the way that people mark edges. Perhaps they came because this is a place where the scale of time is visible, where the ground is old in a way that communicates itself, and they came the way people come to large quiet places when they need to be near something that will not be altered by their fears.

They must have heard the sound. They must have lain down in it and let it work on them. They must have understood, in whatever way understanding happens without words, that the sound was not for them and not against them, that it preceded them and would succeed them, that it was the flat speaking in its actual voice — not to be interpreted, not to be answered, simply to be received.

---

What it is to live here.

For the frog at the edge of the clay pan: the world is wet and warm-enough and full of the right size of moving thing. The sky is a threat-register, attended to peripherally. The ground is home — not felt as home, not experienced as home in the way that word carries feeling, but *functionally* home, which is a more complete kind of home because it requires no maintenance, no decision to remain. There is no elsewhere to compare it to. There is no sense of having chosen this. This is what exists, and what exists is sufficient, and sufficiency here is total.

The body knows the clay. The clay knows the body. Between them, over the long time, they have made a mutual accommodation so complete that the boundary is uncertain. The frog is clay that organized itself into something that moves and calls, that eats and is eaten, that carries forward the pattern of itself through the wet medium of the egg into the next generation, which will also be clay, which will also call, which will add its voice to the enormous ongoing voice of the place.

The sound is not communication between individual animals, finally. Or it is that, but it is also something more structural. It is the flat knowing itself. It is the accumulated aliveness of everything here asserting its own reality into the air, filling the space between the gray sky and the gray ground with proof that this place is inhabited, that it has solved itself, that its solution is alive and calling.

The sound will continue tonight. It will continue through whatever passes for seasons here. It will have been continuous in ten thousand years, when the woven shelter is undetectable and the placed stones have been covered by a millimeter of new clay and the bacterial mats have expanded to cover new surfaces or retreated to rediscover old ones. The frogs or their descendants will be in the water and at the margins, doing what they do, which is to say: being the place, in the form of creatures, making the only noise the place has ever made in its own voice.

It is enough. It has always been enough. The flat rests on its floor, in its final stable state, with everything it needs already present in enormous numbers, and the sound rises from it into the directionless gray light and goes on.
