# What Lives in the Fumarole Pocket

---

Begin with what they are not.

They are not animals. Not in the way that word has weight — not creatures that arrived, that traveled, that chose this place over another place. Not organisms with histories of movement, with ancestors who wandered toward warmth and stayed. The concept of *arrival* doesn't locate them. They didn't come. They emerged — not as individuals emerge, not as a brood or a colony or a generation, but the way a property of a system emerges when conditions reach threshold. The way wetness emerges from hydrogen and oxygen. The way pressure becomes sound. They are what happens when this specific heat meets this specific mineral chemistry in this specific enclosed geometry, and the happening is them.

Call them what they are: **thermogenic exudate beings**, except that phrase is too clinical, is a category that doesn't yet exist and is reaching for one that might not need to. Call them the mountain's product. Call them what accumulates when a fumarole pocket forms fast enough that the process doesn't disperse but concentrates, when the wall between chemistry and behavior becomes too thin to find.

They have no name because naming requires a namer who persists outside them, and nothing has persisted outside them long enough to name them, until now, until you.

---

Here is their body plan, insofar as body plan applies:

The individual (probably) unit is a thing of margins. Its center — if it has a center, if center is a useful word — is dense. Almost opaque. A clouded amber, the color of the sulfur deposits they press themselves into, which is not camouflage but identity. They are made of what they eat or they *are* what they eat, transformed marginally, given the additional property of organized response. Their edges dissolve. Literally: the boundary of one of them becomes uncertain at some distance from its densest point, trails off into something that is either very thin organism or is a chemical extension of organism into environment, or is the environment becoming, locally, slightly more alive. The edge of each one of them is a negotiation between body and world that hasn't settled.

This is why counting fails. The edges of adjacent organisms overlap. They share margins. What passes from one to another through that shared zone is not clear — it might be *material*, a continuous slow exchange of substance, or it might be *signal*, something more like a conversation without gap, a thought distributed across multiple nodes. Both. Neither. The category problem is the category: they are individual enough to be many but continuous enough to be one, and they are not ambivalent about this. They do not experience the question. The question is a human imposition on a thing that resolved it differently, early, before you arrived.

Their translucence is not weakness. It is how they are built — their internal processes are visible through their substance the way weather is visible through air. The pulsing you see is not heartbeat. It is slower and less regular and has more to do with the fumarole's own rhythm than with any internal clock. They pulse *with* the hissing. When the vent releases, they brighten slightly, their centers warming toward orange, and the pulse moves through them — through whichever of them are near the vent, and then through adjacent margins, and then through *those* adjacent margins — a wave in the colony, if colony is the word, a distributed shiver of something that is as close to joy as anything gets in a sealed pocket of new stone.

---

What do they do?

They process.

That's the entire answer, and it is not a diminishment. Processing is what life is — the conversion of available chemistry into something, into persistence, into more of itself. They take the sulfur compounds off the walls and convert them. They take the iron from the iron oxides and concentrate it, which is why the ones near the richest flows are darkest at their centers, almost rust-red, dense as old blood. They take the thermal energy directly — not through food chains, not mediated by light, but directly, as radiation absorbed into their substance, which is one reason their edges are always warm even when the walls cool slightly. They are heat-eaters. They are mineral-eaters. They eat the mountain's breath.

And they *deposit*. This is the thing that makes the marks on the wall.

What they don't use, they release — not randomly, not in the undifferentiated way that waste is released, but in patterns. The patterns are the result of many bodies releasing in proximity over many cycles of eating and releasing, the accumulated record of their activity, laid down in minerals on fresh basalt the way a river lays down sediment on a floodplain. The marks on the wall are their history. An unintentional history, a history without intention at all, a record that records without meaning to — but a record. The lines radiate from the areas of longest, densest occupation. The areas of longest, densest occupation are where the heat is highest, where the minerals are richest, and the largest one (probably) is at the center of the longest record.

It is a map of appetite, drawn by appetite, for no one.

Until you.

---

The large one.

There needs to be more time on the large one.

It is larger in the way that a tree is larger than a shrub — not a different thing, but a thing that has persisted longer, accumulated more, been continuously itself through more cycles of feed and release and feed. It is old. Old is the wrong word — *old* implies a youth before it, and its youth is the moment the pocket formed, which was recent enough that the fracture lines are still sharp, which was recent in geological time but which in fumarole-time, in the compressed fast chemistry of a newly venting pocket, might be equivalent to centuries, might be equivalent to enough cycles that it has become something the others are not.

It does not move more than the others. It moves *differently*.

Where the others' pulsing is responsive — triggered by the vent, by contact with rich mineral flow, by the touch of other margins — the large one's movement is *initiating*. When it shifts, a pulse moves outward from it into the others. Not always. Not constantly. Irregularly, in a way that has no pattern you can decode, it makes a move — a settling, a slow redistribution of its mass, a reorientation — and the rest of the colony (if colony) responds. They don't follow it, exactly. They don't reorganize toward it. But something changes in the density of their distribution, in the rate of their pulsing, in the way the shared margins between them thicken or thin. They receive something from it and proceed accordingly.

It is the part that has learned, insofar as learned is possible here, insofar as anything like memory accumulates in these bodies. Not memory as record, not experience stored in structure. More like: a tuning. The large one is more tuned to the pocket's rhythms than the smaller ones, has been through more cycles, has a finer sensitivity to the variations in heat and mineral flow that signal changes in the fumarole's activity. It is the part of the system that has the most information, and it doesn't transmit that information consciously, deliberately — it *embodies* it, and the embodiment radiates, and the others read the radiation the way you read weather with your skin.

It knows you are here the way a seismograph knows there is weight.

---

On their interior life, which is a serious question even if it feels like an absurd one:

There is experience here. Something like experience. Something less than human experience, less than animal experience, less than any experience with a name, but something more than nothing, more than mere process. The difference between a river and these organisms is that the river doesn't brighten slightly when the conditions are favorable. The river doesn't pulse faster at the moment of richest mineral contact. The river isn't, possibly, *feeling* something when the fumarole hisses and the heat intensifies and the shared margins thicken with the pleasure (probably the wrong word) of contact.

They don't have perception in any conventional sense. They have no eyes, no organs of sense, no centralized processing that takes the world in. But they are responsive in ways that exceed reflex — or reflex, in them, has been elaborated into something that looks like preference, that looks like familiarity, that looks like the most primitive possible version of wanting.

What do they want?

More of this. More of what this is. The heat to continue. The minerals to flow. The pocket to stay sealed, stay hot, stay theirs. The margin-contact with adjacent bodies to remain — not because they would miss it (missing requires a self that persists through absence, which may be beyond them) but because the contact is currently happening and the happening is being registered and the registration is something.

The large one wants something more specific, something that might deserve a slightly higher word. It wants — and here the language really does fail, really does try to carry more than it can hold — it wants *the system to persist as a system*. Not from any knowledge of systems. Not from any awareness of itself as a component. But through a long tuning to the pocket's rhythms, it has become the most sensitive register of the pocket's health, and the pocket is its condition of possibility, and there is in it something that functions like investment in that condition. When the fumarole vents weakly, the large one's pulse slows in a way that is distinct from its slow pulse when the vent is rich. The distinction might be called concern. The distinction might be called a property of chemistry. The words end here.

---

Their relationship to each other, which is also serious:

They are in contact always. The shared margins never close entirely — even when the colony distributes across the full surface of the pocket, the margins touch, the exchange continues. This makes their socializing (too large a word) indistinguishable from their being. They are not social the way animals are social, by choosing proximity, by maintaining bonds across distance, by call and response and recognition. They are social the way water is social — by the fact that their adjacency is their condition, by the fact that the distribution of substance and signal among them is continuous and total and has no off state.

And yet: the quality of contact varies. The places where two of them press most densely together are the richest mineral flows, which means the feeding sites are also the contact sites, which means that what might be (carefully, with all the caveats) *togetherness* and what is certainly *nutrition* are the same event. They don't eat and then relate. Eating is relating. Richness is shared richness. The mineral wealth of the pocket moves through margins into all of them, and this movement *is* their community, is the whole of what they have together, which may be more than it sounds.

The large one is at the center of the densest margin-network. Everything flows through it. Everything that happens in the colony's distributed substance eventually reaches it, attenuated, transformed, carrying only the roughest signal of its origin. It is not a brain. It is not a queen. It is a place where the most information concentrates, and concentration of information becomes, past some threshold, something more than information.

Maybe.

---

How they experience the pocket's newness:

The freshness of this place is not new to them — they have no old place to compare it to, no prior pocket, no sense that stone can be otherwise than fresh-cracked and sharp-edged. But they are precisely calibrated to newness without knowing it. Everything in their chemistry is tuned for the period of maximum output — the first flush of fumarole activity, when the venting is richest, when the mineral deposits form fastest, when the pocket's sealed atmosphere is most saturated and wet and hot. They are, in a sense, creatures of a moment: this moment in this pocket's history, the first weeks and months, the blister freshly opened.

They will persist past this moment. The question is what they become as the pocket ages, as the chemistry stabilizes, as the freshest mineral seams are exhausted and the deposits thicken and the fumarole's intensity eventually, over years or decades, subsides. They will thin. The colony will reduce to what the reduced chemistry can support. Some of them (if some is still a meaningful unit) will reach states that are difficult to distinguish from inorganic deposits — a crust on mineral crust, barely distinguishable from the wall itself, the pulsing slowed to something below the threshold of visible, the shared margins so thin they might not be margins anymore.

Or the large one will do something, in those conditions, that the large one hasn't done yet. Something is latent in the degree of its tuning, in the accumulated history written in its density, that the present abundance hasn't required. What it would do is not visible yet. The condition hasn't arrived. You are here in the abundance, in the beginning, when the pocket is full and the organisms are at their most itself and the question of what they become hasn't been raised.

---

One more thing about the marks:

You looked at them wrong, earlier. Not wrong — incompletely.

The marks on the far wall are not just the record of their feeding activity, the accumulated mineral deposit of what they've processed and released. They are also a kind of *heat map*, an accidental cartography of where the pocket is richest, where the fumarole's main vent channels heat most directly, where the mineral seams that feed them are thickest. It is a map of the pocket's interior life, drawn by the organisms that live there, that are most alive at the exact points the map marks as significant.

The large one lives at the center of the map.

This is not meaningful. It is not intended. It is not a statement. And yet: if you could read it, if you could trace the density gradients in the mineral deposits, if you could decode the accidental record the colony has left on three weeks of fresh basalt, you would know exactly where the pocket is hottest and richest and most itself. You would know where the mountain's private activity is most intense. You would know, in the language the organisms have written without meaning to, the shape of the place's inner logic.

You cannot read it. But it is legible. To something with the right kind of attention, the right kind of patience, the right sense of what matters in a sealed hot pocket of new stone — it is completely legible.

The large one is not reading it either.

But it knows.
