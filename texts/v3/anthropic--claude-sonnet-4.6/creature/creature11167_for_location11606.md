# What Lives There

---

## The Settled Ones

Begin with the beetles, because they are the most legible.

There are at least forty species in the basin, and between them they have occupied every stratum of the available world: the surface of the dry clay, the underside of the fallen stones, the interior of the moss cushions, the root-zones of the ancient shrubs, the packed mineral crust of the old road. They do not compete for these zones because they have been sorted into them over so many generations that the sorting is now genetic — a beetle from the clay surface placed inside a moss cushion would be not merely uncomfortable but constitutionally wrong, as wrong as a deepwater fish on a mountainside. They are specialists who have forgotten that specialization was ever a choice.

The surface beetles are the ones you see: thumb-sized, soil-brown, moving with the unhurried deliberateness that is this basin's signature quality. They are processors. They move through the dry surface layer consuming what can be consumed — organic particles, the husks of spent spores, the shed casings of other insects — and depositing what cannot, and the long record of their routes is written in the mineral composition of the clay, which differs by parts per million along their preferred paths in ways that are not visible but that the springtails and the fungal threads can detect. They have, without intending anything, mapped the basin in chemistry.

The moss-interior beetles are different in body plan: flattened, compressed into a profile that fits the gaps between moss filaments, their legs set wider and lower for lateral movement through dense growth rather than forward movement across open ground. Their carapaces carry structures on the undersurface — small projections, waxy channels — that collect and route moisture from the moss interior to the beetle's mouthparts. They have solved the basin's central problem, the dry economy, by outsourcing their water collection to the moss, and in exchange they manage the moss's interior — clearing the dead material, opening the channels that allow the dry air to move through without desiccating the living filaments. It is a very old arrangement. Whether it began as parasitism and became mutualism, or was always something in between, there is no way to know.

The undersurface-of-stone beetles are blind.

Not partially blind, not reduced-vision, but blind in the manner of things that have had eyes selected away across sufficient time — their eye-sockets present as shallow depressions in the exoskeleton, the eye-tissue replaced by sensory structures of a different kind, antennae reconfigured to be longer and more elaborate, to read air currents and chemical gradients in the dark under the stones where light does not reach. They are pale, the pale of things that have no need of pigment, a dirty cream color that looks wrong in the brief exposure when a stone is shifted and they scatter. But they are not aberrations. They are the most fully realized expression of what the basin is — an old world that has moved past the need for certain things and replaced them with other things, a world that has been optimizing in the dark for a very long time.

## The Lizard on the Lintel

The lizard has been there — that specific lizard, on that specific flat stone — for what seems impossible: three days that you have been watching, barely moving. On the morning of the second day it shifted position by perhaps the width of its own body. On the morning of the third day it was back to the first position, or perhaps it was a different lizard entirely, but that seems unlikely given the exact correspondence of angle and placement.

It is small — fifteen centimeters from snout to tail-tip — and its coloration is the kind of complex neutral that reads as plain at a distance and intricate up close: gray-brown as a base, overlaid with a reticulate pattern of darker and lighter, and along the flanks something that in certain light is almost iridescent, a structural color like oil on water, which vanishes when the angle changes. It is slim, slightly flattened, built for the flat-stone environment with the same logic that flattened the undersurface beetles for their environment.

It eats the springtails and the smaller beetles. It does not chase them. It waits, and when they come within range — which, given the density of invertebrate life in this basin, is frequently — it takes them. Its metabolic rate is calibrated for this strategy, for the cool pooled air of the basin, for a diet of small things taken at long intervals. It does not appear to drink. It absorbs what it needs through its skin and through the thin vapor of its prey's bodies and through whatever the moss releases into the night air during the slightly-less-dry hours at the basin's margin.

There are perhaps thirty lizards in the basin. You have seen four. You have the sense that the others are there without presenting themselves — that the lizard's primary social technology is absence, and that the stone-shelf territories are distributed across the buried city in a map that the lizards maintain with great precision and great silence. When two of them are visible at once, something tightens in the air between them: a stillness within the stillness, a held quality. Then one of them is gone, having moved in the moment when your attention broke.

The lizard blinks with both eyes sequentially, not simultaneously. Left eye, then right eye, with a pause between that seems long enough to be deliberate, as if each eye is making its own assessment and the brain is receiving two reports from two slow correspondents. When it blinks, you can see the translucent inner eyelid — the nictitating membrane — sweep across the surface, cleaning and moistening. This membrane is more developed than seems standard. This membrane, you suspect, is doing more work than simple cleaning. In the dry air of the basin, the outer eye surface would desiccate without constant maintenance. The lizard's nictitating membrane is elaborate and efficient, another of the basin's solutions to its central problem, present in a different kingdom.

## The Architects of the Soil

Beneath the surface — not deeply, not the deep underground, but the shallow under-world of the top thirty centimeters of packed clay — there is a civilization that is more complex than anything visible above it.

The primary engineers are ants, but calling them ants feels insufficient, the way calling the buried city a town feels insufficient. They are large by ant standards, dark, moving through galleries they have built in the compacted clay of the old road and the former floors of former buildings — following, without knowing it, the paths of old drainage systems, the slight differential hardness of ancient construction, the deep organizing logic of the city's buried infrastructure. Their galleries follow the city's plan more faithfully than anything on the surface, because the soil preserves the plan in its density gradients, and the ants read density. They have mapped what was built here more completely than any observer above ground could.

They do not farm in the conventional sense — no leaf-cutting, no fungal cultivation. They are processors, like the surface beetles, but working at the chemical level: they break down the mineral matrix of the old mortar and stone dust, selectively harvesting certain elements, concentrating others in their waste deposits. Their waste deposits are visible if you know what to look for — slight discolorations in the clay at the base of certain moss cushions, patches where the soil chemistry has been altered enough to favor specific moss species over others. They are, in this indirect way, landscapers. They are shaping the moss distribution of the basin, slowly, with the same patience that shaped everything else.

The queen, or queens — this colony does not fit neatly into the standard model of colony structure, with its single reproductive female — may be old in a way that requires re-evaluation of what ant queens are known to be. There is evidence, in the depth of the galleries and the complexity of their organization, of continuous occupation spanning centuries. Not continuous with individual lives — the workers cycle through their brief spans — but continuous with something. A tradition, held in the body of the colony. A cultural knowledge of the space. Workers born a hundred generations after the first galleries were dug still navigate those galleries with the confidence of familiarity, because the knowledge of the galleries is encoded not just in their structure but in the pheromone landscape that the colony has maintained, refreshed, elaborated for as long as the colony has existed.

They are mnemonic creatures. The colony remembers what the individual cannot.

## The Spinners

In the corners and the inner angles of the buried walls — where, in the city's time, there were rooms and corridors and the spaces that human habitation creates — the spiders have established.

They are small and pale, cave-adapted in body type despite the fact that this is not a cave, simply a basin that is nearly cave-like in its light levels and its stable, cool, dry air. Their eyes are large relative to their bodies, optimized for the low light. Their legs are long and set at wide angles, built for navigating the irregular surfaces of old stone and deep moss, for moving quickly when necessary over terrain that is never smooth.

They do not build webs in the air. The basin's humidity is too low for structural web-silk to hold its shape; in drier air, the surface tension dynamics that make aerial webs possible break down, and a web strung between two points would lose its geometry too quickly to function as a trap. Instead, these spiders spin in surfaces: flat sheets laid across the stone, trip-lines set at ground level, tube webs retreating into crevices, all of it close and horizontal, all of it in contact with the solid world. They have given up the third dimension that spider silk usually commands. They have accepted the lateral world, the world of surfaces, and made their engineering work within it.

Their silk is denser than standard, with less water content, resistant to the desiccation that would destroy a more conventional web in this dry air. Under magnification it would reveal a different structure at the molecular level — a different crystallinity, a different ratio of ordered to disordered protein domains — but you do not need magnification to understand that these spiders have solved their problem. Their webs are there, barely visible, laid across the top of the old stones like a faint sheen. And the webs work: the springtails and the small beetles and the flying things that occasionally descend from the basin's aperture find the trip-lines and are gathered.

The spiders do not seem to compete with the lizards. They occupy different layers of the same space — the spiders in the surfaces and crevices, the lizards in the open flat. There may be a degree of mutual avoidance, or the territories may simply not overlap enough to generate conflict. There is something that looks like accommodation, the practiced non-interaction of organisms that have been in proximity so long that they have learned to read each other without needing to engage.

## The Fungal Layer

You cannot see most of the life in this basin.

The fungal network threading through the dry clay is invisible at the surface — pale white threads finer than hair, pressed through the mineral matrix along planes of least resistance, following cracks and root channels and the ancient pathways of water that no longer moves. This network is not a single organism, strictly, and yet it functions with something like coordination: the signal molecules that pass through fungal hyphae travel this network in patterns too complex to read, carrying information about nutrient concentration and moisture and the presence of dead organic material, routing the network's collective appetite toward opportunities.

The fungi here are xerophytes — drought-adapted — and they have evolved a different relationship with water than most of their kind. Their primary interface with the atmosphere is not through wetted soil but through mineral surfaces: they secrete acids that weather the rock, extracting elements from the stone itself, breaking bonds in the crystal lattice to free the phosphorus and potassium locked there for geological ages. They are miners. They are slow, patient, mineral miners, and the city's stones have been their seam for as long as the city's stones have been available.

When one of the surface beetles dies, the fungal threads find it within hours. When a section of moss cushion finally exhausts itself and collapses, the fungal network is there as it falls, ready to process. The network is the basin's decomposition layer, its recycling mechanism, and it is so thoroughly woven into the ecology that to say it is part of the ecosystem is not quite right. It is, more precisely, a part of what makes the ecosystem's resources available to everything else. Take it away and the mineral economy collapses. The beetles and the lizards and the spiders and the ants eat things that ate things that ate things that ate what the fungi released. The fungi are the foundation under the foundation.

They do not have what could be called behavior, and yet they respond. They respond to the ants' passage through the clay, routing around or routing toward. They respond to the chemical signals of the moss. They respond to the presence of the lizard's shed skin with a speed that seems disproportionate to their usual patience. There is something happening in the network that is not behavior but is not nothing. It is sensitivity, accumulated to a degree that looks, from the outside, like attention.

## The Fliers

The things that descend into the basin from above are visitors, not residents — moths that come in from the surrounding terrain to use the basin's cooler, more stable air for thermoregulation, or to follow the fungal spore-plumes that release in fine seasonal pulses from the old moss cushions and drift, heavy with fat molecular content, up toward the pale aperture of sky. They come down, feed briefly at the spore-level, and leave. Their contribution to the basin's ecology is incidental — they bring nutrients from the outside when their bodies become available to the spiders and lizards, and they export the basin's genetic material when the spores they've consumed pass through them elsewhere.

But there are certain small beetles with vestigial wings — non-functional, structurally present but incapable of generating flight — that retain, in their descendants' memory, the posture and the intention of flying. In the slightly warmer hours, at the edges of the lit coins on the basin floor, these beetles climb to the tops of moss cushions and open their wing-cases and stand there. They stand with their bodies angled as if at a launch point. They do not fly. They will not fly. But they stand in the posture of flying, ancient and intent, and whether this is a vestigial behavior playing itself out in the absence of its purpose, or whether it serves some function — thermoregulation, or mate-signaling, or spore-dispersal through the air currents the angled body catches — is not clear. What is clear is that they do it with great regularity, a precise and patient practice, performed at the same hours on the same cushion-tops by the same individuals, day after day.

They look like prayers. Probably they are not prayers. But they look like them.

## The Long Residents

Here is what has not been fully said: that several of the organisms in this basin are what ecologists would call the same species as organisms found outside it, and yet they are not quite, anymore. The generations have been long, and the basin has been selecting, and the result is a set of what the field would call ecotypes — populations diverged enough from their relatives outside to behave differently, to have different tolerances, to carry different allele frequencies in ways that are subtle but consistent. The lizard is a lizard that lizards on the surrounding terrain would recognize, probably, as lizard; but the lizard of the basin blinks more slowly and moves less and is more drought-tolerant and lives longer. The moss cushion beetle is a beetle whose closest relative outside the basin is faster, smaller, less heavily armored, more interested in wet soil.

The basin has been making them. Generation by generation, the basin has been working on them, selecting for what it requires, discarding what it does not, and what it requires is always the same: patience. Tolerance. Efficiency. The ability to do a great deal with very little, over a very long time.

In this they resemble — in whatever resemblance is permitted between such different kingdoms — the city that preceded them. The city that was built here before they colonized the ruins. The city that was built here by something that also learned to persist in the basin, that also had to solve the dry economy and the cool air and the low light, and that also, eventually, became so fitted to the place that when it ended — when the builders left or diminished or transformed into something else — the absence was barely a disturbance. The city sat. The moss moved in. The beetles came. The fungal threads found the mortar.

And the basin went on, as it always had, making slow patient things out of whatever arrived and was willing to be made.

---

What lives here is not dramatic. It does not announce itself. It does not perform.

It simply *is*, in the way that old things simply are — completely, without remainder, without the sense that anything is being held in reserve. There is no flourishing yet to come. There is only this: the settled roar, the dry cushions, the blind pale beetles moving through the dark under the stones, the lizard blinking left eye then right eye in the low light, the fungal threads reading the mineral patience of the old stones, the colonial memory of the ants following the ghost-map of the buried streets.

It is a community that has found its answer and inhabits the answer fully.

It has been here a long time. It intends, in the way that things without intentions intend, to remain.
