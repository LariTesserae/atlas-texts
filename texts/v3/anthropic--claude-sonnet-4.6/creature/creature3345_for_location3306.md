# What Lives in the Lowland

---

## The Ones Who Wait

They arrived before waiting became a strategy. Waiting is simply what they are.

The heron is the most visible practitioner. It stands in the shallows at the channel's edge — not *at rest*, because rest implies a prior activity that has paused, and for the heron there has been no prior activity. It has been standing here, or in a place identical to here in all respects that matter to a heron, since before you came and before the person who came before you came, and will be standing here in whatever configuration of shallows exists after you leave. Its stillness is not effort. You can tell the difference: effort has a quality of maintenance, of constant small corrections, of the body arguing with itself about whether to move. The heron has no such argument. It has resolved something that most living things never resolve. Its grey body, the blue-grey of weathered driftwood, has the same relationship to the landscape as the flat rocks — it is present in the scene without quite being a figure in it, more of a feature, a protrusion, something that belongs to the set of things that were already here when you arrived.

When it moves, it is wrong. Not clumsy — the movement is geometrically precise — but wrong in the way that a statue's movement would be wrong. A slow mechanical extension of the neck, a deliberate angular unfolding of the legs, a repositioning accomplished so slowly that the eye keeps losing track of whether movement is occurring or has already occurred. Then stillness again, exactly as total as before, in a location six inches from the previous location.

The strike is different. The strike is not continuous with any of this. The strike is sudden and absolute and belongs to a different category of existence than the waiting does — the neck a whip, the head a point, the fish lifted in a beak that was somewhere else a moment ago. Then stillness again, the fish struggling, then still, the heron back in its first category, waiting, swallowing with a series of long ripples down the grey-white neck.

It has no relationship with urgency. It cannot imagine urgency. Urgency evolved for creatures operating on a different timescale, and the heron operates on the timescale of the lowland — long, level, patient, working through problems not by force but by the willingness to remain present at the site of a problem until the problem resolves itself.

## The Ones Who Move Through Everything

A rail passes through the sedge without disturbing it.

This requires explanation, because the sedge is dense at the base of its tussocks — the dead stems of previous years accumulated in layers around the living green, fibrous, interlocked, resistant. A human hand reaching into a sedge tussock finds resistance, finds sharpness at the stem-edges, finds the tangle of living and dead that presents itself as a unified barrier. The rail finds none of this. The rail moves through the sedge the way water moves through it — by being the right shape for the gaps.

You hear it before and after and during and you do not see it. The *churring* from the east — rhythmic, insistent, ventriloquistic in that maddening way of calls that seem to move as you approach them. You know it's there. You could locate it within a meter if you could see inside the sedge, but the sedge is opaque from your height, and the rail is lateral, down in the dark channels between tussocks where the water films the ground and the light barely reaches. It has laterally compressed its body over evolutionary time specifically to fit these channels. Its sternum is narrowed. Its ribs are articulated to fold. It has become, physically, a sedge-penetrating organism — not as a behavior but as a body plan.

Its feet are absurd. Long and splayed, the toes extravagant, spreading its weight across water-surfaces and floating vegetation with the same physical logic as the mat-forming plants, which also spread their structure flat to stay atop the wet. Everything here that needs to move on this ground finds the same solution: spread out, be wide, stay close to the surface, do not concentrate weight in small areas.

The rail's call says *I am here* and *I am here* and *I am here* in that mechanical, insistent rhythm, and you will never see the one who is calling because seeing and hearing in this landscape are different kinds of knowledge, and hearing is the truer one. Most of what is alive here is known by sound before it is known by sight, if it is ever known by sight at all. The rail you hear today you will not see today. You may not see it before you leave. It will keep telling you exactly where it is.

## The Ones Who Work the Surface

The insects are not ornamental. They are structural.

Above every square meter of open water, in the warm midday light, there is a layer of life operating at a scale you only intermittently perceive. You see it as stippling — a faint, constant, unresolvable movement above the water surface, as though the air just above the water is slightly more alive than the air above it. You hear it as a layered hum, high frequencies over low frequencies, layered in the way that weather sounds layer, not discretely but continuously.

The water surface itself is an economy. Its tension is a membrane with inhabitants. Water striders — and here they are numbering in the dozens per channel-stretch, dozens per flooded grass-flat — sit atop this membrane on legs that end in minute hydrophobic pads, creating four small dimples in the surface film without breaking through, standing on the skin of the water as though the water is solid, as though they have perceived and solved a physics problem unavailable to heavier things. They move by sculling, the middle legs pushing, the front and back legs steering, and they move fast when they move, sudden accelerations across the water-mirror, then stillness, then the tremor of something struggling elsewhere on the surface — a fallen insect, a drowning fly — and then another acceleration toward it.

Below the surface, the water is not empty. It only looks empty because the things in it are small and the water is stained dark by dissolved peat, tanning it to a tea-color that absorbs light and returns nothing. In the dark of that tea-colored water: the larvae of every flying insect above it, coiled in their larval patience, eating algae and detritus and each other, growing through instars toward the moment when they will dry their wings on a sedge stem and add themselves to the hum above the water. The water is a factory for the air above it. Everything flying was once crawling down there in the peat-dark, negotiating its transformation.

The midges do not bite here. Or — some of them do not bite. The ones that do not are the majority, the males, who form their dancing swarms above certain landmarks — above a particular sedge clump, above a flat rock that warms faster than the ground around it, above your head if you stand still, because you are a tall feature in a featureless country and tall features are reference points. The swarms pulsate, individual insects ascending and descending within a shared column, maintaining the column's position over its landmark while the column's internal contents churn. They have no individual project. The swarm has a project, which is to be a swarm, to be a visible signal, to be the kind of thing a female midge flies toward. The individuals sustain the column and the column sustains the possibility of finding each other in all this open, featureless space.

## The Ones Who Are Everywhere at Once

The frogs exist at a scale of abundance that is difficult to perceive directly because the scale is too large. You hear them everywhere, from every direction, the sound so ambient it seems to belong to the landscape rather than to organisms. But every point in that ambient sound is a frog, and the frogs are everywhere — in every channel, in every flooded depression, in every shallow film of water between the grass clumps, in the dense base of the sedge tussocks where moisture holds and temperature stays cool.

They are small and brown and the same color as the dark peat mud and the dark water and the dead lower stems of the sedge. If they were any other color, everything here would eat them immediately. Being this color, they occupy the boundary zone between visible and not-visible that permits survival in a place with this many egrets, this many herons, this many wading birds picking through the shallows with that cocked-back neck-readiness. You will see a frog when it moves, briefly, then lose it again when it stops, your eye sliding off the brown form onto the brown mud around it, unable to locate the boundary.

They breathe partly through their skin, which requires the skin to stay moist, which in any other landscape would be a constraint but here is not a constraint at all. The lowland is arranged around moisture. The lowland provides, in every direction, at every moment, the exact moisture gradient that a frog requires. They are, in this sense, the most perfectly located organism in this landscape — they have found the place that is precisely continuous with their own physical requirements. They do not accommodate the lowland. The lowland accommodates them.

When they breed, they breed collectively and without coordination — or with the coordination of a synchrony that looks like no coordination at all. The water fills with eggs. The eggs are everywhere. The shallow channels become thick with tadpoles that are almost too many to count, dark comma-shapes angling through the tea-colored water, grazing algae from submerged stems, growing toward the moment when they will come ashore and be brown and invisible in the peat mud, calling from every direction, being heard everywhere at once.

## The Ones Who Cross

This place is a waystation for things that are elsewhere in other seasons.

Some of the wading birds are transient in the deepest sense — they were elsewhere in the spring when they bred, they will be elsewhere in the winter when they need warmer feeding grounds, and they are here now in the midyear pause, moving through, using the lowland as a stopping-place on routes so long they belong to a different geography than the one visible from where you stand. A sandpiper at the channel's edge, probing the mud with a bill evolved for exactly this mud, has recently been considerably north of here and will be considerably south of here and is here now because here has what it needs in the transition between those two other places.

There is something in the way it works the mud — efficient, systematic, covering ground in one direction and then turning, covering ground in another direction — that speaks of the distance still ahead of it. It does not have the settled quality of the heron, which has resolved its relationship to place and time. The sandpiper is still in motion at the scale of its year, and even when it stands still you can sense the trajectory it's on, the way a thrown stone is still in motion even at the top of its arc.

The eels in the channels are the extreme case. They came from the ocean, from spawning grounds so remote they were unknown to science for most of human history, and they grew in these dark channels for years — some of them for decades — and one autumn they will leave, all at once, on a night that something in the accumulated chemistry of their blood decides is the correct night, moving downstream through the channels to larger channels to rivers to the sea, across an ocean, to waters where they will spawn and die without returning. They carry the ocean inside them the way the peat carries the weather of centuries. They are fat from the lowland's abundance, full of the stored energy of ten or twenty years of slow channel-living, and that energy is the fuel for a crossing so long it seems impossible for something so small.

Here, now, they lie at the bottoms of the dark channels, in the silt, barely moving. They breathe. They wait. Something is accumulating in them.

## The Ones Who Were Here First

The bacteria and the fungi and the organisms for which there are no common names — the things working through the peat — are the oldest residents and the actual governors of the place.

The peat itself is their product. What looks like soil is mostly their work, centuries of it: organisms dying, organisms decomposing organisms, the chemical stripping and reconstruction of organic material through bodies too small to see, the slow conversion of plants into the dark matrix underfoot. The smell of the place — that low, sweet, rich decay — is their smell, the smell of their processes, of the particular chemical byproducts that emerge when this specific community of microorganisms works through this specific kind of organic matter in these specific conditions of acidity and cold and wetness.

They are not fast. They are among the slowest metabolizers in the living world, operating in the cold waterlogged anoxic layers of the peat where oxygen barely penetrates and where the chemistry of decomposition runs on electron acceptors that only specialists can use. A piece of dead plant may pass through their collective processing for decades before it fully converts. A piece of wood may persist for centuries in the waterlogged peat because even these specialists struggle to break down lignin without oxygen. The persistence of the peat is their slow digestion of it. It is thick below your feet because they have been working it for a long time, and they have been working slowly, and the material keeps arriving faster than it fully converts.

What they are doing, collectively, over long time, is building the ground. The landscape you stand on is their construction. The dark soil, the water-holding matrix, the particular chemistry that makes the water tea-colored and slightly acid and inhospitable to some organisms and essential to others — all of this is the output of their collective work across time longer than anything with a nervous system can claim. They were the first residents. They will be the last. Everything else — the herons, the rails, the striders, the frogs, the crossing birds, the waiting eels — everything else lives in the structure they built and are still building, slowly, below the surface, in the dark.

## The Ones Who Sit At the Center

There is a toad in the low sedge. Not immediately visible. It has been sitting in the same slight depression — a socket of its own making, pressed into the mud over time — for most of the day.

It is not doing nothing. It is doing the primary thing, which is waiting and watching and being precisely what it is without any remainder. Its eye is a gold iris with a horizontal pupil, an eye that looks old in a way that eyes rarely look old, as though the design of this eye predates the toad that contains it and will outlast it too. Its skin has the texture of the landscape — warted, irregular, slightly shining where the moisture films it, dark brown with irregular darker markings that mean nothing on their own but in aggregate mean *this, here, mud, dead leaf, ground*. Its breathing is slow and absolute — the throat pulsing with a rhythm you can observe if you sit still long enough, a rhythm that has no relationship with urgency or hurry, a rhythm that could continue for hours without the toad noticing it was continuing.

When something small and moving comes within range — a small beetle, a worm working up through the softness, an insect that has misjudged its landing — the toad's tongue is there and then it is not, a movement too fast to be parsed by observation, and then the toad is the toad again, sitting, breathing, watching with that gold eye that is older than this toad and younger than this place.

It has eaten perhaps four times today. It will eat perhaps four more times before the light changes and it moves, slowly, to a different slightly damp hollow where it will sit through the dark hours, warmer than the air, breathing, waiting for what moves.

---

*They all know one thing here: that the lowland will continue. The rails know it in the architecture of their bodies. The frogs know it in the accuracy of their numbers. The bacteria know it in the depth of their ongoing work. The heron knows it in its stillness. The eels know it in their patience, in the long uncounted years they have been lying in the dark water accumulating whatever it is they need before they leave.*

*The place settled into itself long before any of them were here. They did not make it what it is. They found it already knowing what it was, and they settled into it the way the water settled into its channels — by trying every direction, over time, and staying where they fit.*
