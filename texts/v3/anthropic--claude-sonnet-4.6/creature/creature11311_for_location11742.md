# The People of the High City

## Body

They are compact people, and this is not incidental.

The body that thrives here is the body that loses heat slowly — the body that presents less surface to the cold air relative to its volume, that keeps its vital warmth at its core, that does not waste itself on length. Over the generations, the plateau has made its preferences clear, and the people have obliged, and now the preference has become the norm and the norm has become the body. A tall person here looks subtly wrong to their neighbors, slightly wasteful, like a wall built thinner than it needs to be. This is not said. It is felt.

Their hands are the most telling part. Wide hands, with short dense fingers that have retained their sensitivity despite the cold — because sensitivity in the hands is not a luxury here, it is a professional requirement, it is how a stoneworker reads a face of rock, how a weaver judges tension in a thread, how a cistern-cutter finds the fault line in the substrate. The cold has tried to take that sensitivity away; the body has refused to surrender it. The compromise is pain in the early morning, before the hands warm, before the work has warmed them — a kind of deep interior ache in the finger joints that is so universal it requires no comment and receives none. Everyone's hands hurt in the morning. This is what morning is.

The lungs have also been in negotiation. Larger than they look from the outside — the chest of a plateau-born person has a slightly different architecture than that of someone from lower elevations, the ribcage carrying a greater volume, the diaphragm working a slightly different range. Strangers who come up from below breathe quickly and too shallow, their bodies doing something like panic. The people of the plateau breathe slowly and fully, making more of less, filling what they have built to hold it. Children are told this early: breathe deep. Take what there is. Don't waste it on shallowness.

Their skin is windburned and UV-struck — the thinner air does less to filter the sun's radiation than it should, and the sun, when it comes, does its work on any exposed surface with quiet efficiency. The face, particularly, develops an early weathering, a thickening and roughening of the skin at the cheeks and the brow and the bridge of the nose. Lips crack. Everyone has a practiced habit of working moisture back into the lips with the tongue, a small repetitive motion visible in conversation. Young children's faces are extraordinarily vivid, the color high and constant, as though the altitude has concentrated something in them. Old faces are something else: layered and dense, creased by decades of cold-wind-sun into a terrain as readable as the plateau itself.

Their eyes — and this is noticed by the strangers who come up and get past the headache long enough to look — their eyes are open in a particular way. Wide-held, habituated to scanning large distances without landmarks, trained on a landscape that offers only subtle variation and requires that you find it. On the plateau there is no forest to hide things, no sudden obstacle. Everything visible is visible for a long way. The eyes have learned to work in that register, to catch small movements at great distances, to track a raptor's spiral a kilometer away, to notice the darkening of a water-pocket against the light of early morning. Brought into a small interior space, those eyes take a moment to relearn the scale. In a conversation close-up, they can feel to a stranger like they are seeing further than they should.

## The Social Body

The unit of life here is the household, and the household is not the nuclear family of other traditions.

It is larger, and less defined by blood, and more defined by the cistern.

The cistern is the point. The cistern, cut by someone's ancestor into the rock beneath the floor of the main structure, fed by the inward-sloping rooftops of every building in the cluster, maintained by communal effort and governed by communal right — the cistern determines who belongs to whom. To share a cistern is to share survival. The household, however many people it contains — and it may contain six or twenty, arranged by age and skill and history and the accumulated decisions of several generations — is the group that shares this water and takes collective responsibility for its management.

The head of the household is not exactly a leader. The word the people use for this role translates more accurately as *keeper* — the one who keeps account of the water level, who keeps the social debts of the household in memory, who keeps the relationships with other households in functional repair. The role is understood to be difficult and slightly thankless and essential. It passes not by birth but by recognition — the household observes its own members over time and eventually arrives at a rough consensus about who has been doing the keeping whether or not they were formally appointed to it. Appointment is often retroactive.

What this means in practice: the plateau-born are accustomed to shared space in a way that can unsettle strangers. They are not unaccustomed to privacy — privacy exists, it is valued, it is managed through a set of understood signals that are obvious once you know them and completely invisible before — but the baseline is proximity. The baseline is other people's breath in the same room, other people's warmth contributing to the collective heat budget, other people's sounds as the texture of sleep. Solitude, when it comes, is a kind of pause that the body notices the way it notices the absence of wind after a long presence — as a difference in pressure, a slight ringing in the absence.

They are patient with each other in the way that people are patient when they have no alternative. Conflict in a shared-cistern household cannot be resolved by leaving. You can go outside, in the cold, and stand there among the sharp-edged rocks for a while. But you will come back. Everyone knows you will come back. The conflict must therefore be managed toward something livable. Not necessarily resolved — resolution is perhaps too clean a concept for the kind of accumulated minor friction that constitutes most domestic difficulty — but managed. Accommodated. Given a shape that people can move around without injuring themselves on it daily.

There are fights. There are grudges held for decades, and grudges held so long they calcify into something almost architectural, a feature of the household space that everyone steps around without discussing. There are also — and this must be said — long tenderness, the kind of tenderness that grows between people who have been cold together many times, who have watched each other's hands hurt in the morning many times, who have tracked the same water level together through a dry season and felt the same specific fear and arrived together at the wet season on the other side.

## What They Know

The knowledge here is old and has been refined toward usefulness.

Useless knowledge is not exactly discouraged — the plateau people have their poets, their people who remember the old stories that have no practical application — but it occupies a different category from the knowledge that keeps people alive, and everyone understands which category any given piece of knowledge falls into, and everyone understands that the useful kind must be passed on intact.

Stone knowledge first, because it is first. The ability to read a surface, find a grain, predict a break. This takes years to develop and cannot be adequately conveyed in words — it lives in the hands and the eyes, in the accumulated experience of many strikes and their results. A master stoneworker teaches by demonstration and by correction, by handing the tool to the student and watching and saying very little. The knowledge transfers through the hands. The vocabulary for it is limited because the vocabulary was never the point. You can describe a good strike all day; you cannot strike a good strike until you have made ten thousand mediocre ones and felt the difference.

Water knowledge: the reading of condensation patterns, the prediction of the rain season's start and depth, the signs that a cistern's wall is beginning to fail, the management of the seasonal channels to slow their loss, the recognition of the underground moisture variations that make one place better than another for a new cistern cut. This knowledge is partly ecological, partly geological, partly something more intuitive — a feel for where water wants to be in a given formation, a sense accumulated over many seasons of watching.

Animal knowledge: the behavior of the herds, the social structures within them, the signs of illness, the management of communal grazing rotations across the plateau's vegetation zones. The herd knowledge is distributed — many people hold pieces of it, and the full picture requires consultation among specialists who each know one part deeply. The people with the deepest herd knowledge have a particular quality of attention when they watch the animals: not distant, not analytical, but absorbed, the way a person looks when they are listening to something in a language they know but have to concentrate on.

And then the sky. The plateau's enormous unobstructed horizon means the sky is always present, always available for observation, always offering information about what is coming. Not astronomy in any formal sense — though the stars are so clear at altitude that everyone has an unconscious familiarity with their patterns, a navigational awareness they would not know to call that. What the sky is read for is weather: the direction and quality of cloud, the color of the horizon at morning and evening, the behavior of the birds, the feel of the air on the skin of the face. This is not a formally structured body of knowledge. It is more like a background awareness, always running, noticed only when it produces a conclusion worth speaking aloud. *We should finish this now and go in.* Or: *The rains are late.* Or — and this is a particular phrase with a particular weight — *It will be a dry season.* That phrase, spoken in the early spring when the snowmelt is less than hoped, produces a particular quality of silence in whoever hears it. The silence of already beginning to calculate.

## The Life Stages

Children here learn early that the plateau does not make exceptions.

This is not taught cruelly — the teaching is gentle, patient in the way the adults are generally patient — but it is taught. You do not explain to a child that the rock edges are sharp. You show them the edge, you let them understand the sharpness at a level that does not damage them, and you do not prevent every small cut because small cuts teach faster and more permanently than any instruction. By the time a plateau child is five they move through the city with a particular care — not timid, not slow, but precise. They know which surfaces to trust and which to approach slowly. They move like people who understand that inattention has a cost.

The question of education is not separate from the question of work. The child who is learning to spin fiber is learning to spin fiber — this is the lesson, and the skill developing in the hands is the point, and there is no distinction drawn between the learning and the doing. Children work alongside adults from early, not as a burden but as an apprenticeship that is so constant and normalized that it does not feel like either. The transition to adult responsibility is not marked by a single ceremony but by a gradual increase in what you are trusted with. The moment varies by person and by what they are being trusted with. A child might be trusted to manage the morning water collection before they are trusted to read a cistern wall, because those require different developments of different capacities.

There is, though, a ceremony at the first blood and at the first stone cut — at the first time a young person cleaves a rock correctly, intentionally, producing the clean face rather than the shattering failure. The cut stone is kept. The household keeps it. It is sometimes used — built into a wall or set as a threshold — and sometimes not. What matters is that it was kept.

Old age here is the old age of people whose bodies have done real work in cold air for decades: the joints that have already been in negotiation for years lose the argument more completely. Movement slows. But the knowledge does not slow. The oldest members of a household — the ones who have seen three or four dry seasons in living memory, who remember the winter when the southwestern ridge's outer wall collapsed under ice-shift and had to be entirely relaid — these people are the archive. They are not revered in any formal sense, not kept separate and sacred. They are simply consulted, the way you consult a particularly reliable instrument. Their memory is part of the household's equipment.

## What They Believe

The plateau people do not have priests, precisely. They have people who specialize in the relationships between the human community and the other forces present on the plateau, which is a different thing. The distinction matters to them.

A priest intercedes. These specialists — and there is no consensus on what to call them in translation, every attempt loses something — these specialists *attend*. They are present at moments when the borders between categories become important: at a death, at the beginning of the dry season, at the first of a newly-cut cistern's water, at the return of the migration in spring. They attend not to change the outcome but to witness it properly. The witnessing is what they offer. The idea seems to be that certain transitions require human acknowledgment to be fully real — or, perhaps more accurately, that the failure to acknowledge them risks a kind of unreality, an incompleteness in the event that may have consequences the way structural incompleteness has consequences.

The plateau itself is understood as something with interiority. Not a god — not a being that can be petitioned or appeased — but something that has its own condition, its own ongoing experience of itself, which runs at a scale and timescale that makes human interaction with it nearly impossible except through the accumulated long attention of many generations. The specialists are in some sense translators of this experience: they observe and report on what the plateau is currently doing, what it seems to be in the process of, what attentiveness it may require. They are the people who have noticed that the survey marks no longer align. They are the people who track the slow extension of a crack over years, who have learned to read the pale faces of fresh fractures as signs of something — not a narrative, nothing so interpretable — but signs of a state, a condition, an ongoing process that the community should be oriented toward even if it cannot act on it.

Death is understood as a return. The body goes back into the rock — not metaphorically, literally: burial is below, cut into the substrate, the body placed in the stone and the stone closed over it. The moisture from the body feeds the substrate. This is not spoken of as a spiritual process exactly but it is not spoken of as a merely physical one either. The category the plateau people might use is something closer to *appropriate* — death handled this way is appropriate, it completes something that was begun when the person first drew air at this altitude, first ate the food grown in the thin soil at the base of the ridge grasses, first drank the condensation water. The body has been cycling through the plateau for a whole life. Returning it is not loss but continuance.

There is no word in their language for the sky-and-stone landscape that means *nature* in the sense of a thing separate from human life. The word that outsiders sometimes translate that way means more accurately *the conditions* — the given facts, the parameters within which life occurs, which are not other-than-life but are the medium of it. You do not stand *in* nature on the plateau. You are *part of* the conditions, briefly, and then you are part of different conditions, and the rock is still there, sharp and rising.

## The Texture of Daily Life

They wake to the cold. This is not a new thing every morning; it is simply the morning's first information, the way the return of one's own name is the mind's first information on waking. The cold means: begin. The cold means: the water collection window is now, because in an hour the sun will be burning off the condensation that has gathered on the roof surfaces and the collection will be diminished. Several members of the household are up before full light, moving quietly along the roof channels with collection vessels, doing this with the focused attention of people who understand exactly what they are doing and why.

The fire is rebuilt. Not a large fire, not a dramatic one — fuel is too scarce for drama — but sufficient: enough to take the edge from the interior temperature, enough to heat the morning preparation of food, enough to gather around in a circle of small warmth while the day begins. The morning meal is the longest meal, the most communal one. People who leave the household for outlying work — the herders, the cistern-cutters working a new site at the edge of the settlement — eat before they go, and the meal stretches to accommodate their departure times. The meal is grain-based, almost always, the grain being the plateau's primary cultivated product, grown in the shallow-soiled depressions where organic matter has accumulated over generations, tended with the same care as the cisterns.

The workday is organized by task rather than by hour. There is no timekeeping in the abstract. There is: this wall section needs to be done before the temperature drops further and the hands lose sensitivity. There is: we need to reach the eastern water-pocket and return before the afternoon wind picks up. Time is relational, contingent, understood in terms of what it makes possible or forecloses. The sun's position across the sky matters as an indicator of these conditions, not as a clock.

The middle of the day, if the sun is out, is when people stop and face toward it. This is not worship, though it is not nothing either. It is the body's honest response to warmth. You see it across the city during those still minutes: the inhabitants of the plateau, between tasks, turned toward the sun with eyes closed, faces offered upward, absorbing what there is. It looks, from a distance, like a kind of prayer. From closer it looks like a population doing something utterly necessary with complete simplicity, without self-consciousness. Like drinking when you are thirsty.

Evening is the contracted time. The cold returning, the shutters drawn, the interior spaces consolidating their warmth. This is when the talking happens that isn't about work: the stories, the instruction of children in things that require words rather than demonstration, the music that the plateau people make with instruments designed for indoor acoustics — not to project or to fill a landscape but to be heard in a low-ceilinged room by nearby people, to be felt as much as heard, the resonance of drum and the particular overtones of their stringed instruments designed to be pleasing in a space that has stone walls and a roof low enough to touch. The music is not loud. It doesn't need to be.

They sleep well, as a rule. The altitude helps, in this one way: the body at altitude sleeps deeply, the systems slowing in proportion to the available oxygen, the kind of sleep that leaves you with a sense of having been somewhere. The dreams, if you ask them about dreams — and they will answer, they are not guarded about dreams, they treat dreaming as simply another kind of experience that happens to a person — the dreams are often of edges. Of coming to the edge of the plateau and seeing what is below. Of the valley air rising warm against a face that has been cold since birth. The content of these dreams is not interpreted as desire. They are interpreted as the body processing something it has only heard about, trying and failing to construct it with insufficient information. 

*You can't dream the edge*, they say. *You've never seen it.* 

But the body tries anyway, in sleep, and produces something — a feeling of exposure, of the ground falling away, of air that is too warm and too thick and almost wrong in the mouth. The dreamers wake into the cold room and the familiar dark and the sound of other people breathing and let the feeling fade, because it has nowhere to go here, because the edge is mythological, and this is the plateau, and the plateau extends in every direction as far as the world goes.
