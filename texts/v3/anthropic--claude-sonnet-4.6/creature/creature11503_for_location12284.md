# What Lives Here

---

Begin with what cannot be seen.

The warming of the ground is not passive. Something below moves—not locomotes, not travels, but moves the way breathing moves, the way a sleeper's chest moves: without decision, without cessation. What lives deepest here has no surface. It is a distributed presence in the rock itself, or in the water threaded through the rock, a metabolic fact so ancient it has become geological, something that was once distinct from the substrate and has since negotiated its way into identity with it. It is not the rock. But the rock is not fully itself without it. You could not culture it; you could not name it to kingdom. It gives off warmth the way a story gives off meaning—as a consequence of its internal organization, not as an intent.

From this unnameable root, everything else proceeds.

---

The moss is a mind, or the nearest thing to a mind that doesn't have one.

Not one organism—never that—but a collective so dense in its mutual arrangements that the distinction between individual and community has been composted away, broken down to something that feeds the whole. The near-black of it in shadow is not absence of color but presence of depth: layer upon layer of incremental growth pressing down against itself, the bottom layers long dead and long unrotted, preserved in the permanent wet, a record in organic matter going back beyond what you'd believe if I told you. What lives in the moss is the moss, and also is not the moss, and this is the first lesson the clearing offers to anything that stays long enough to receive it.

The collembolans that leap between stems have lived in mosses for four hundred million years. They are not primitive. They have simply finished. They solved the problem of being a collembolan with extraordinary thoroughness and have had the grace, ever since, to hold still in their solution. They are among the most successful arguments against the idea that progress is the point. They eat the fungal threads between moss cells and are eaten by the mites and the mites are eaten by the pseudoscorpions you have not seen but who are there, moving through the canopy of moss stems like tigers through grass—patient, cold-blooded, carrying their tiny chelicerae before them like offered gifts. The pseudoscorpions are encountered by almost nothing that would write about them. They live and die in the moss-world completely, and the only record of their dynasty is the dynasty itself, still ongoing, still conducting its minute predations in the millimeter-high forest.

What lives in the moss lives in geological time by accident. By staying small enough, by wanting little enough, by being willing to go on doing what they do in the water film between one leaf and the next—they have slipped through every extinction like water through cupped hands.

---

The water that comes up is inhabited.

Not metaphorically. In the seeps where the water table and the surface meet in their quiet agreement, there is a biofilm on every grain of rock that the water touches—a skin of bacteria and archaea so old, some lineages of them, that their ancestors were here before the first eukaryote drew a nucleus around its DNA and declared itself a new kind of thing. They do not know they are old. They process sulfur compounds, iron, the trace minerals the water carries up from below; they fix nitrogen in the margins of the pools; they make the rock, incrementally, into soil, though on a timescale that makes *incrementally* feel like comedy.

The biofilm is the base. Everything else is the elaboration.

Nematodes thread the water film around every moss stem with the blind insistence of their kind—nematodes are everywhere, in numbers that would disturb you if you could see them all at once, in the soil, in the water, in the bodies of other organisms, occupying niches so varied it sometimes seems their solution to speciation was simply to be willing, to be small and willing and equipped with a mouth. Here they are fine-adapted to the slow upwelling water, to the specific mineral load, to the particular temperature the deep warmth produces. Their bodies are transparent. If you could see one—and you cannot, they are far too small—you could watch the food moving through it, watch the eggs arranged inside. It would look like a diagram of itself.

---

The frogs have made patience into a philosophy, if a philosophy can be embodied in meat and stillness.

These are animals that became rock-colored not in the way that camouflage is usually discussed—not to hide from predators, though that is also true—but in some deeper sense that the clearing seems to encourage: a general tendency toward harmonics with the substrate, toward resemblance with what is ancient and worn and present. The frogs sit at pool edges with a quality of duration that exceeds what frog-bodies should contain. They are waiting, but *waiting* is not quite right because waiting implies an orientation toward a future moment, and these frogs seem to have evacuated that orientation entirely. They are simply here. The tongue fires—not *will fire*, not *has fired*—is the only tense available to them. Then the tongue returns. Then the here again.

A frog in this clearing has a circulatory system, a nervous system, gametes, a hunger that can be measured in caloric terms. It has a species name in some taxonomy, or the thing it has become would have a species name if anyone had named it. It has parents and will have offspring, or has had, or is one of the last, which also happens. None of this is inconsistent with the quality it projects, which is the quality of a stone that has accepted being a stone so thoroughly that its stonehood radiates.

When they enter the water they become part of the pool's mirror briefly—a disturbance, a clouding—and then they are at the bottom, in the centimeter of depth, and the mirror reforms above them, and they are under the sky without being in it, held in the cool upwelling, converting oxygen at the rate that their metabolism requires and no more.

---

The dragonfly that holds station over the pool is doing something you might call hunting but which looks from the outside more like sovereignty.

It has been here—this species, this lineage, in this clearing or clearings like it—for longer than the word *species* usually implies. The basic form of it was established so long ago that its persistence begins to resemble a natural constant, like the speed of sound in still air. And yet there is nothing archaic about this individual. It is precisely configured for this moment: its compound eyes parsing the polarized light off the pool's surface, detecting the micro-turbulence of a midge's descent before the midge has fully landed; its wings making the calculations—though *calculations* is a metaphor for something that requires no calculator, that is simply what wing-muscles do when they are these wing-muscles in this air—to intercept at the correct angle, at the correct instant.

When it strikes, it strikes with a violence that seems improbable against the clearing's general tempo. There is the stillness, and then there is the intercept—an arc, a snap of forelegs, a brief stall in the hover—and then the stillness again, and in the stillness the eating, the dragonfly facing into a light breeze if there is one, or held in place by its own precision, working at the midge with its mouthparts methodically. The pool reflects an empty sky. The dragonfly is above it, present in itself.

---

The carabid beetle crossing the open rock toward the moss-edge is alone in the way a sentry is alone: singular representative of a purpose the collective has distributed into individuals and sent out to the perimeter. It is looking—*looking* is wrong, it has no concept of looking, but its antennae are receiving, its sensory apparatus is forward-deployed, and this functions like looking from the outside—for whatever it is that carabids in this clearing eat. Possibly the collembolans at the moss margin. Possibly small worms. Possibly other beetles.

Its underside, pressed close to the rock, is in contact with the warmth from below, and this warmth regulates it in ways it does not experience as regulation. It is simply appropriately active. It moves at the pace that the ground's temperature licenses it to move. The clearing controls its tempo without touching it; the warmth is the clearing's way of speaking to the beetle's metabolism directly, prior to anything the beetle might call experience.

When it reaches the moss and enters it, it disappears into a world that has its own interior dimensions. The moss, from above, is a surface. For the beetle, it is a volume—corridors between stems, clearings between hummocks, vertical climbs and descents within the space of a human handwidth. It will move through this for some time, and the civilization of the moss will move around it, some things fleeing, others ignoring, the pseudoscorpions recalculating. The beetle is the largest thing in that world, for now, and is briefly a weather event—a pressure, a shadow—that the world adjusts to and recovers from, and by the time the beetle has passed through and out the other side, the moss-world is as it was, or as close as it gets.

---

The bird in the flat canopy is watching in the way that the clearing watches: not scrutinizing, not waiting to act on what it sees, but maintaining the kind of attention that has no immediate object. It is a receiver with the gain turned high. Its call, made once, was not a territorial claim—or was not only that; or was that plus something the territorial-claim explanation doesn't account for, some quality in the single note that functions as orientation, as *here* spoken aloud for the clearing's benefit, as if the clearing needed to be reminded and the bird has appointed itself to the reminding.

It has been alone in this canopy for some time. Not lonely—*alone* doesn't map onto loneliness in this bird the way the word implies. It is simply the solitary node, the one that takes the high perspective while the work below continues. Whether there are others of its kind, nearby or elsewhere, is a question the clearing doesn't answer. The bird does not answer it either. It watches. It will call again or it will not; it will descend or it will not; it will be here tomorrow or something will eat it, or it will eat something, and the process will have included it and moved on.

Its grey back against the pale bark makes it, from certain angles, nearly continuous with the tree. This might be coincidence. In a place old enough, coincidences accumulate until they look like plans.

---

And then there are the ones that don't fit the taxonomy you arrived with.

The slim-bodied slow-winged things drifting at head-height: they are insects, clearly insects, bilaterally symmetrical, six-legged, winged, but assembled in proportions that don't correspond to any Order you'd reach for first. They are too long for their wing-load. Their wings are too soft-looking for the weight they're carrying. They move as if moving through something denser than air—but they are not moving through something denser than air, the air here is the same air—and you realize, watching them, that what you're reading as difficulty is actually precision: they are not struggling through the air, they are threading through it, selecting particular channels of stillness between the faint thermals rising from the warm rock, and what looked like labored flight is in fact extremely competent flight, flight so efficient that it looks inefficient to anything calibrated for urgency.

They don't land while you're watching. Or one lands, once, briefly, on the waxy-leaved flowering plant at the pool-edge, and stays long enough to do something—exchange something, deposit something, extract something—and then rises again and drifts back into the column of warm air over the seep and hangs there, not doing anything that you can name.

They are the part of this place that is most legibly *other*, most clearly from a different history than the one that produced the things you have names for. And they are also, in the context of this clearing, simply present. The clearing has made room for them. The clearing makes room for everything that has arrived at its equilibrium and stayed.

---

The objects that are warm like the ground, that are smooth in the way geology is not, that carry their iridescence without reference to the angle of light—

The objects are inhabited also.

Not by anything you'd expect. Not by insects nesting in a crack, not by moss colonizing the edges, though the moss *has* colonized the edges and this is its own kind of information. The objects are inhabited by a function still executing. Something still running in the material of them—a process that takes in inputs you can't measure and produces outputs you can't detect, or whose outputs are what you've already been experiencing as the warmth, as the quality of attention, as the agreement between water and surface to meet here rather than anywhere else.

They were left by something that understood this clearing—had studied it, or made it, or simply knew it well enough to know it could use it. Whatever left them is not here. Has not been here for long enough that the objects have entered into the ecological community without disruption, have become part of the substrate the moss measures itself against, have been incorporated into whatever calculations the dragonfly makes when it decides the pool is sovereign territory worth defending.

What they do is maintain something. Whether that something is the warmth alone, or the warmth plus the upwelling, or the warmth plus the upwelling plus some more specific condition that the objects are monitoring and adjusting with a patience that only looks like inertness—this you cannot determine by observation. You can determine only that they are doing it, have been doing it, and are disposed to continue.

The clearing was found, or made, or found and then made more precisely what it was already becoming. The objects are the record of that intervention, fully assimilated now, warm under the hand, iridescent without cause, carrying out their old instructions in a place that has long since arranged itself around those instructions without knowing it was doing so.

---

What lives here, finally, is equilibrium itself.

Not a stable equilibrium—*stable* implies that a force applied would be resisted and the system would return to its previous state, and this is not quite that. More a dynamic equilibrium: a process in which everything is changing slowly and simultaneously and in response to everything else, so that the net result is a constancy that is actually motion viewed from far enough away. The moss is becoming. The rock is being unmade into soil at the moss's base. The frogs are doing what the frog-lineage has been doing for longer than this clearing has existed and will continue doing after the clearing has changed beyond recognition. The beetle-civilization and the midge-civilization and the film-of-archaea-civilization are running in parallel and in exchange, and the objects that maintain the warmth are part of the exchange now, and the bird in the canopy is part of the exchange, and you are briefly part of the exchange—your breath, your heat, your weight on the stone—and the exchange takes all of this in, adjusts, and continues.

What lives here is not any individual thing. What lives here is the living-here. The process of it. The continuation of it.

The ground is warm. The water comes up. The dragonfly holds position. The moss has no ambitions but has its depth, its dark layers, its memory in organic matter of every wet season going back past counting.

It is paying attention because attention is what is happening here: everything attending to everything else, calibrated across scales from the nematode to the silver-barked tree to the object whose function you cannot name, all of it constituting a watching that has no center, no eye, no self that watches—

Only the watching.

Only the clearing, regarding itself through its ten thousand simultaneous processes, in the bright mid-day, in the warm rising water, in the dragonfly's compound eye which holds, as it holds everything in its field of view, a small inverted image of the sky.
