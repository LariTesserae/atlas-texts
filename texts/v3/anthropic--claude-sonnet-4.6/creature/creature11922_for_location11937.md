## What Lives Here

---

### The First Thing to Say

Nothing lives here in the way you are thinking of when you use that word.

And yet.

---

### The Root-Mind (which is not a mind)

Begin with the roots themselves, because they are the dominant fact, and because the question of whether they constitute one living thing or many has resolved itself in favor of *one* in the same way the question of the city's origin resolved: not by being answered but by becoming inapplicable.

The root-systems that fused over centuries into pillars have not simply bonded structurally. Where root presses against root for long enough, where cambium meets cambium in the dark and the pressure is sustained across decades, a thing happens that plant biologists would probably call hydraulic continuity and leave it there. The internal signaling of one root-system bleeds into the adjacent system through the shared walls. Chemical gradients that mean *grow here* or *thicken there* or *commit to this direction* move across the junctions without impediment. The systems no longer issue independent instructions. What has emerged is a single distributed process operating across what were once perhaps forty or fifty distinct organisms, now operating as one metabolism, one slow intention, one enormous body that fills the volume of the entire city and presses at every margin.

It does not think. It is important to say this clearly: it does not think.

But it integrates. It computes, in the mechanical sense, in the sense that water computes as it finds downhill. It computes the mineral gradient through the floor and sends root-mass toward concentration. It computes the available geometry of each crack in the ceiling and sends the finest root-threads into those cracks and begins, patiently, the long work of enlarging them toward the surface, toward nothing, toward a surface-above that may or may not still exist in any form the root would recognize. It computes the seasonal thermal variation — a degree's rise, a degree's fall, year after year — and uses it as a clock, or as the nearest thing it has to a clock, timing the commitment of new growth the way a person might time a decision to a feeling.

If you pressed something against one of the pillars, if you pressed a warm hand flat against the bark-like surface and held it there, you would feel after a long moment — much longer than you would expect — a faint, responsive pressure from within. Not recoil. Not aggression. A pressure, a noticing, a reorientation of some local growth-vector that is now accounting for your presence. It is the least dramatic possible form of awareness, and yet it is not nothing.

This is the primary resident. This is the city's owner, insofar as anything owns what it has simply become.

### The Decomposers (which are not distinct from the root)

Deep in the densest root-mass, at the base of the pillars and in the packed floor-substrate, there are organisms that are not technically the root but that cannot be meaningfully separated from it.

Fungal threads, primarily. A network of them so thoroughly interpenetrated with the root-mass that the mycorrhizal relationship they entered into — probably before the city was sealed, probably when there was still surface soil and surface light and the relationship was the ordinary collaborative one between plant-root and fungus — has had so many centuries to develop that it has moved past collaboration into something more like merger. The fungal mycelium processes the mineral deposits in the rock. It breaks down the residue of the original timber construction, the long-dissolved organic material in the rammed earth, the ancient bones of whatever animals died here before the sealing. It hands the results to the root. The root hands the fungus access to whatever the root's strange resinous chemistry produces as a byproduct.

The fungus does not have a body that you could find. It is everywhere below the surface of things and nowhere visible. It is the city's digestion, its invisible organ, the process that is turning the original construction continuously into fuel for the construction that replaced it. The stone and timber that built the city are, molecule by molecule, becoming the root that consumed it. The city is eating its own past.

The fungi are not one organism. Probably. But the question of individuality becomes abstract quickly at this level of interpenetration, in this enclosed system, over this length of time.

### The Coinhabitants of the Ceiling

The moss — the slow, near-felt growths on the ceiling — is something stranger.

It is not moss. It is something that was probably moss once, or something that occupied the ecological niche that moss would occupy if moss were present, and that over centuries has moved so far from any recognizable morphology that the word is only useful as an approximation. It grows one cell layer at a time and has grown this way for so long that the pads of it are thick enough to yield under pressure, to accept the impression of a fingertip and hold it briefly and then slowly release it back to smooth, the way a very dense foam would.

The ceiling-moss is a separate biological entity from the root. It does not share hydraulic continuity with the root-pillars. It takes the mineral vapor through its surface — through the gas exchange that its modified stomata perform — rather than through root-contact with the stone. It lives, in some sense, above the root's reach, in the ceiling zone where the root goes too fine and fibrous to maintain structural mass.

And the ceiling-moss is where the only visible biological activity happens, if you had light to see it. Which you don't. But: if you did.

The moss surface is not static. It moves. Not quickly, nothing moves quickly here, but the pads of it undergo a slow expansion and contraction that is probably related to the thermal cycle, probably related to the concentration of available mineral vapor, possibly related to nothing more than the particular growth kinetics of whatever strange metabolic machinery drives cells that have entirely abandoned photosynthesis. In the theoretical light you cannot see, the ceiling would show you a surface in very slow motion: pads thickening and thinning over the course of months, patterns emerging and dissolving at timescales that would require years of observation to perceive as patterns, the tessellation of growth zones shifting in a kind of distributed breathing.

The ceiling breathes. The breath takes years.

There are small pockets of the ceiling-moss that have dried entirely, that have shut down, that are conserving their internal chemistry against some future reactivation that may or may not come. These pockets look — would look — slightly different in color, slightly more gray-green than the living deep-green-black surrounding them. They are not dead. They are waiting. They have been waiting, some of them, for a long time, and they have not lost patience because patience is not a relevant concept to them. They simply wait in the way that a held breath waits.

### The Bacteria (which are the deepest residents)

Beneath the root. Beneath the fungal layer. Beneath the compacted root-stone of the floor, down in the fissures of the actual geology, where the geothermal warmth concentrates and the sulfate chemistry is richest, there are bacteria.

They are not visible. They are not large. They are — in most meaningful senses — the oldest life in this place, predating the city, predating whatever surface ecology seeded the roots that eventually became the pillars. The bacteria in the rock fissures below the city are probably billions of years old as a lineage, give or take several extinction events. Individual bacteria are not old. The lineage is very old. The distinction matters to scientists and means nothing to the bacteria.

These bacteria are the reason the root can live here. They are the primary processors of the deep mineral chemistry, the organisms that break the sulfate bonds and release the ions that rise as vapor and feed the root and feed the ceiling-moss and ultimately feed everything. They are the base of the food chain in a place where food chain is a strange phrase because there are only two or three trophic levels and they have been locked in the same relationship for so long that the boundaries between levels have softened.

The bacteria do not know they are inside a city. The bacteria do not know anything. The bacteria process and divide and die and are processed by the next generation and have been doing this since before multicellular life existed anywhere, probably, and they will be doing it after everything above them has reached its final form and stopped.

They are the heartbeat you cannot hear. The grinding of root into rock is audible; the bacteria are the reason the rock eventually yields. They precondition the chemistry. The root follows. The bacteria do not notice that the root follows, but the root could not follow without them.

### What the Whole Thing Is

You are inside a single organism in the same way that you are inside a single forest when you walk into a forest that has been growing undisturbed for centuries. The individuality is uncertain. The boundary is uncertain. But there is a metabolic unity, a shared cycling of chemistry, a continuity of process that has run uninterrupted for long enough that the differences between the parts have become differences of function rather than differences of kind.

The root is the structure. The fungus is the digestion. The ceiling-moss is the breathing surface. The bacteria are the deep engine. The city they inhabit — the ghost of it, the mineral trace of its logic, the arch-shape remembered in the root-bridge, the cistern-trace the roots follow through stone that hasn't held water in longer than the city has existed — the city is the skeleton, the calcium scaffold around which everything else has organized.

The whole thing lives. It does not live in a way that involves experience, almost certainly. It does not live in a way that involves anything we would recognize as desire or fear or memory, though it enacts something that looks, from outside, like memory — the way it keeps finding the old water-paths, the way it maintains the geometry of archways and plazas and sleeping-chambers by following the logic of their original construction without knowing it is doing so.

What it lives *toward* is unclear. The roots press toward the surface, probably, along the mineral gradients that point upward. Whether there is a surface anymore in any form the root would reach is not a question the root is asking. It is simply pressing.

### The One More Thing

This is the thing that wants to be said last and that is the hardest to say without overstating it:

The place does not know it is alive.

But consider: it has been running its processes in this sealed space, in this absolute dark, with no external input except the geothermal warmth from below and the mineral chemistry of very old rock, for so long that every process it runs is perfectly calibrated to every other process. There is no waste. There is no inefficiency. Every byproduct of one process is the feedstock for another. The whole system cycles at a level of integration that would take an engineer a very long time to deliberately design.

This is not intelligence. This is what selection produces when it runs long enough in a closed system with no disturbance.

But it means that what lives here is — if you want a single word for it, if you need a single word — *complete*.

Not finished. Not dead. Not static. But complete in the way a sentence is complete when it has said everything it has to say and arrived at its final punctuation and is now simply resting there, meaning what it means, requiring nothing further.

The city became a creature by accident and by patience and by the slow operation of biological logic across a timescale that makes the word *slow* feel insufficient. The creature does not know it is a creature. The creature has no needs it cannot meet. The creature has no enemies, no competitors, no outside world that makes claims on it, no weather, no seasons except the ghost of seasons in a degree of warmth, no hunger it cannot satisfy with the vapor rising from its own floor.

It wants nothing. It presses on. It grinds its low note. It breathes in years. It waits in the dark for nothing in particular.

It is the most settled thing you have ever been inside of.
