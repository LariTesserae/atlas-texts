## What Lives Here

---

Start with the pulse, because everything else orients to it. Every four seconds. Something in the district has a heart — not the pump, or not only the pump — and it is from this rhythm that most of what lives here derives its structure, the way shore life structures itself around the tide. Not adapting to the rhythm. Arrived in a place that had one, and arranged itself accordingly.

---

**The iron bacteria** are the first answer and the truest one, the baseline, the thing from which all the other living derives. They are in the water and on the iron and *inside* the iron, working in layers, the outer colonies running rich orange-brown films across every vertical surface, the inner ones deeper, slower, feeding in the anoxic zones behind the crust they build and die into. They are not remarkable. They are not strange. They are the condition of the place, the way oxygen is a condition elsewhere — something so pervasive that singling it out feels like a category error. But they built the orange fur of the pipes. They built the films on the Flemish bond. They are still building. They will outlast whatever comes after the pump finally stops, which will not be soon.

Their scale is the scale of the district. Not creatures *in* the district — the skin of the district itself.

---

**The pale things on the undersides of machines** — the almost-snails — are worth longer attention than most people give them, though only a particular kind of person would pay it.

There are perhaps thirty of them in the largest building, fewer in the others. They are not quite identical: small variations in the pattern of their shells (if shells are the right word — calcified structures, spiral, pale), variation in size, in the degree to which they have become translucent. The oldest ones are nearly transparent. What they contain — organs, mass, the infrastructure of living — is visible as a faint shadow-complex inside the shell, a ghost-architecture. You can see them thinking, in a sense. You can see whatever passes for thought in whatever passes for their nervous system.

They move along the most protected surfaces: the undersides of machine castings, the inner faces of window frames where the frame has survived, the underside of a broad iron flange where a pipe emerges from a wall at shoulder height. They move across surfaces that are covered by a thin layer of something — the bacterial film, mostly, and whatever accumulates in it: organic particles sinking from above, the shed material of other organisms, dead bacteria and the chemicals they leave behind. This is what they eat. This is why they are here and not elsewhere. Nowhere else in thirty miles has iron bacteria in quite this density, at quite this depth, in quite this cold.

They do not perceive you. Not in any meaningful way. There may be pressure sensitivity — a slight registering of your presence as a change in fluid dynamics as you pass through the water near them — but if so, they do not respond to it. They continue on whatever vector they were on. They are ancient optimizers, refining movement toward food and away from nothing, in a place that has very little of either.

What is strange about them: they are *old*. Some of them are older than anything should be, that small, that simple. The low temperature, the low metabolic cost of the life they live, the absence of predators — there is almost nothing here to kill them. Some of the largest, most translucent ones may have been here for decades. It is possible that the ones on the largest press, which has not been disturbed and has no particular reason to be disturbed, have moved across that single casting for the span of a human adult life. Moving, eating, persisting. The oldest ones may have been born before the place was submerged, carried in on the water that rose through the floors, already adapted to cold and iron in some prior flood, some prior industrial channel, finding here a perfected version of everything they needed.

They do not respond to the pulse. Their four-second cycle does not sync to it. They are the one thing in the district that doesn't orient to the beat.

---

**The fish are infrequent.** Visitors. The district sits within a larger body of water — some canal system, some harbor fringe, some drowned industrial margin — and occasionally fish work their way in along the channels that were alleys, moving between buildings, passing through the window-holes into interiors and out again. They do not stay. The food density is too low for anything with their metabolic requirements. They come in, work along the walls, take what they find, go back.

One kind returns more than others: a species of small perch, dark-barred, wide-eyed, adapted to low light. They move in groups of three to seven and they travel the alley-channels in a particular direction — west to east, generally — then spiral into the courtyard and hold there for minutes or hours, apparently doing nothing, then disperse back the way they came. Whether the courtyard is territory or landmark or something experienced as shelter is not determinable by watching. They are not feeding while they hold in the courtyard. They are not displaying. They appear to be *waiting*, though this is projection. They are simply in a place, briefly, the way a thing can be in a place before the thing that drives it moves it somewhere else.

They do not acknowledge the mineral formation. They pass close to it without deviation, without the sideways drift that fish sometimes show near novel objects. Either they have been coming long enough that it is familiar, or it does not register as novel to whatever their novelty-detection is.

When the pipe-light pulses through the courtyard's iron, they flash. Their scales catch it. For half a second, every four seconds, they are lit — irregular darts of silver in the darkness — and then gone again, invisibly present in the black water, and then lit again, in slightly different positions, tracking the same general formation.

---

**The bioluminescent colony in the silt floor** is less of a colony and more of a network — a diffuse web of bacterial threads occupying the upper twelve centimeters of silt, patchy, densest in the center of the main floor and thinning toward the walls. It is, almost certainly, several species in a relationship: sulfur-reducing bacteria in the deeper pore spaces, and above them something that produces light as a metabolic byproduct under conditions of low oxygen and organic richness, and the silt itself is rich with the centuries of settling that brought organic particles down from the surface in the decades since submersion.

The glow is not constant. It is responsive to something, and the something is not clear — could be pressure-differential, could be chemical gradient, could be temperature change, could be the pump's influence on dissolved oxygen concentrations carried through the water. It brightens and fades over a period of hours. The period is not fixed. Sometimes the glowing lasts most of a day; sometimes it collapses quickly. It is doing something with its luminescence that has nothing to do with you, nothing to do with the fish, nothing to do with the signal-system of the pipe-network above it. It is communicating with itself, probably — gene expression, some quorum-sensing chemistry that uses the light as a byproduct of signaling, the colony-parts tuning themselves to each other across the silt-space.

By the time you are present, the colony may be decades old. The threads are slow to extend and they have been extending. In the dark beneath the silt, below the depth of its light, the threads run deeper than anyone has measured, following chemical gradients down toward the rock below the silt below the water, a slow quiet reaching that has been underway since something arrived here — on the foot of a migrating waterbird, possibly, years after submersion, deposited in the shallows of a time when the surface was closer — and found a place with everything it needed.

---

**The attached things on the outer walls** — the kelp's empty holdfasts, the film-colonies, the accumulated mineral crust — are not all dead. Among the holdfasts, several are occupied by something that is not kelp: small colonial invertebrates, bryozoans or hydroids, something with a budding structure that built itself into the concave space of an old holdfast and has been expanding from it at a rate visible only over seasons. They are the color of pale stone. They fan out from their base in a radiating structure that in brighter water would look like a plant, a pale weed, but here looks like a mineral formation or a crack or a shadow. They are not mistaken for these things, as far as anyone can tell — no fish attempts to eat them, but this may be because they taste wrong, not because they are undetected.

They have reproduced. Along the east outer wall of the southernmost building there are twelve distinct colonies, most within a meter of each other, connected now by the overgrowth where their edges have touched. This merger happened recently — the growth margins are still visible, slightly different textures, the line between original and absorbed still legible. In another decade it will be a single organism occupying the lower east wall of a building that no longer needs a wall for any purpose it was built for.

---

**Then there are the things in the pipes themselves.**

This requires care, because it is easy to get wrong — to overstate or to understate.

The pipes are not hollow. Not all of them. Some of them are corroded shut from the inside: iron-oxide and mineral concretion, layer on layer since the water stopped flowing with enough velocity to scour. These are sealed, airless in the relevant sense, filled with compacted material that is chemically very different from the surrounding water. These are their own sealed environment, a pipe-interior that has had no exchange with the outside for decades, and inside them — in the anoxic mineral matrix — there is chemistry happening, chemistry distinct from the chemistry of the open water, and whether or not the chemistry involves anything organized enough to be called *living* is exactly the kind of question this place refuses to answer cleanly.

Some of the open pipes — the ones burst or corroded through — have been colonized from the outside. The holes allow ingress: small worms, annelids, the thin red-thread kind that weave through confined spaces and are rarely seen in open water, live in the mineral sediment inside the pipes, feeding on the iron-bacteria colonies that have established on inner pipe-walls where the chemistry is favorable. These worms are not rare; they are common in industrial sediments, in harbor bottoms, in similar places. They are here doing what they do elsewhere, untroubled by the strangeness of their substrate, unbothered that the structure they inhabit was built for a different purpose. They are a purpose unto themselves. The pipes are useful to them. This is all.

The worms are most dense in the pipes that run between the main building and the adjacent one to the north — the pipes that the pulse travels through visibly, the ones that flash. Whether the pulse affects their behavior is unknown. They are photosensitive in the theoretical sense — they have light-sensitive structures — but the flash is very brief and the worms are deep in the pipe. If the light reaches them, it lasts less than a second. What a worm does with a half-second of faint phosphorescent light every four seconds, in an otherwise lightless pipe, is a question without a good answer.

Perhaps: nothing. Perhaps the four-second rhythm structures something in them that we would not know how to look for.

---

**The formation in the courtyard does not live.** This should be said. It is calcium carbonate and iron oxide, mineral patience, the accumulated residue of water-chemistry operating over years on a point of pressure and temperature. It is the most fully realized thing in the district — the most complex visible structure, the most stable, the most finished — and it is not alive.

This is worth sitting with. The district has been running its slow process for decades, the bacteria building their films, the worms threading the pipes, the pale organisms crossing the machine-faces, the light pulsing and the chemistry cycling, and the culminating *form* of all of this, the thing that looks most like a presence, is mineral. Is the district's unconscious production of something shaped like waiting.

It looks like what it looks like. It looks like it has been in this courtyard for a long time, waiting for something, hunkered into itself against the cold.

Everything else here is alive and looks like nothing. The formation looks like something and is nothing.

---

**What lives here, taken whole:**

A chemistry. An infrastructure of organisms fitted to each other in the specific way that organisms fit to conditions that have been stable long enough to become reliable — not obviously, not dramatically, but with the efficiency of a solution arrived at over many generations. The iron bacteria at the base. The pale snails at the next layer, eating what the bacteria make available. The worms in the pipes. The diffuse colony under the silt. The hydroids on the outer wall. The visiting fish. All of it running in parallel, each thing doing its particular thing, most of them not interacting with the others at all.

And the pulse, which is not alive but which everything here has been shaped by — calibrated against, accommodated to, organized around without organizing in return. The pump's four-second beat, the pipe-light traveling the network. The district's heartbeat, which preceded all of them and will outlast most of them and which none of them required but all of them have come to need, in the way that anything living in a rhythmic environment eventually incorporates the rhythm, uses it as a clock, derives from it whatever stability the environment provides.

This is the community of the Sunken Works. Not impressive. Not dramatic. Not the life you were perhaps expecting to find in a place this strange, this old, this pressured and dark and cold. You were perhaps expecting something worthy of the atmosphere — something singular, something evolved specifically to be here, something that could only exist in this exact convergence of iron and water and pulse and depth.

What you get instead is: small things persisting. The ordinary extremophile life, the life that does not need grandeur, that needs only consistency and time and the absence of disruption. The life that the district has been, without meaning to, providing.

It has been enough.
