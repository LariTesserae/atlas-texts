# What Lives in the Undercroft of First Growing

---

## The Firstborn Knot

At the oldest point of the system — the place from which the advancing edges have advanced — there lives something that cannot be found by looking for it. You will walk through the chamber it occupies many times before you understand that the chamber is it.

Call it the Firstborn Knot.

It is the original fusion, the one from which all subsequent fusions have been calibrated. Two roots, or what were once two roots, have been merging for longer than anything else here has existed, and the merger is not complete and will not be complete: they have grown into each other in layers, ring upon ring of joining, and the result is a mass at the chamber's center roughly the shape of a large sleeping animal, though its surface is all bark and root-cord and the amber sap that seeps from active fusion-zones. It does not move on any timescale you can watch. You put your hand on it and it is the warmest thing you have touched here — warmer than the ceiling-slabs, warmer than the air, warm in the way that centers of process are warm, the way a heart is warm, a furnace's wall, the inner chamber of a hive.

The Firstborn Knot does not eat. Does not perceive, in any sense you can confirm. But the roots of the whole system trace back to it in the way river tributaries trace to a watershed, not a source exactly — the source is somewhere above, in the world of light — but a *decision point*, a place where the first choice was made about what direction, what density, what architecture. The billion subsequent fusions are all, in some sense, doing what happened here first. They are iterating the original event.

What lives in the Firstborn Knot: the pattern. Itself. The first version of the logic this entire place is running.

Small creatures attend it constantly. Not the eyeless vole-things — those stay in the moving surfaces, in the shallow root-hair shallows. These are different: slow, dark despite the general paleness of the undercroft's fauna, each about the length of your forearm, moving with a deliberateness that looks curatorial. They work across the surface of the Knot with appendages that are too fine to see individually but produce a visible effect — a polishing, almost, a maintenance of the fusion-zones, a clearing of whatever accumulates at the margins of the merging. Whether this is feeding or tending or both at once or something that hasn't been named: unknown. They do not leave the Knot. You watch for an hour and not one of them travels more than a body-length from its surface. They are monks. They are the custodians of the first idea.

## The Calibrators

There is an animal that lives nowhere and everywhere, that you will encounter three times in an hour if you move through the space and thirty times if you hold still, that has no permanent residence but is constantly in motion on a circuit that appears, over the course of a long observation, to cover the entire accessible system.

It is the size of a hand. It moves on six legs that end not in claws or pads but in something like root-hair — a grip that works on the rounded, continuous surfaces of the undercroft by conforming to them, by becoming, briefly and locally, part of the texture it traverses. The body is flattened, the color of old cream, and it carries on its back a structure that is still being determined: an irregular accumulation of material — sap-resin, framents of root-cord, the secretion-silk of the aerial fauna — assembled into something that has the character of a structure-under-construction rather than a finished form. No two Calibrators carry the same configuration. Some of the configurations are large, absurd, seemingly impractical — a tower of silk and resin rising half the animal's body-length again from its back, swaying as it moves, apparently not minding. Others carry small tight packages close to the body. The configurations are changing. You watch one Calibrator for twenty minutes and it has added two strands of silk-secretion and removed, deliberately, a segment of root-cord that it dropped, apparently with intention, at the base of a structural root.

They are measuring. This is the thing you understand slowly: the accumulations on their backs are not dens, not portable homes, not mate-attractants. They are instruments. Each Calibrator is carrying a record of conditions — humidity (low; the resin behaves differently at different humidities), temperature (high; the silk-secretion sets at different rates), the precise mechanical properties of the surfaces traversed (the root-cord fragments are samples). When a Calibrator encounters another Calibrator, which happens constantly and without apparent planning, there is a pause — two seconds, three — in which they orient toward each other, in which the structures on their backs come briefly parallel, and then they separate and move on. An exchange of information. A comparison of readings. A calibration.

What they do with this calibration, where the information goes, what acts on it: unknown. But you notice, over the course of a long visit, that the pattern of root growth in the zones through which Calibrators travel most frequently differs subtly from the zones they visit less often. The ceiling in their high-traffic areas fuses more evenly. The floors there have a more consistent depth of root-hair. It is possible the Calibrators are not just recording the conditions of growth but participating in directing it — that their presence, their chemistry, their small deliberate droppings of material at specific junctions, constitute a form of signal that the root network reads and responds to.

In which case they are not passengers in this system. They are part of its nervous function. They are, in some sense, how the growing thinks.

## The Aerial Fauna, Whose Name Is a Process

The things that undulate through the air without flapping — that trail the silk-secretion, that cast their slow phosphorescence across the vault — they do not have individuals in any way you can confirm.

You see what look like individuals: bodies, discrete, separated from each other by the open air between them. Bodies of a consistent form — elongated, pale, with a suggestion of segments that are not quite segments, not quite the rings of a worm but not the fused tube of a fish, something between, something that hasn't decided. Each body is perhaps a meter in length. Each trails its secretion-silk from the posterior end. Each moves with that lateral undulation, the same rhythm in each of them, a rhythm that strikes you as familiar and then you realize why: it is the same four-second interval as the pulse in the ceiling roots. They are moving in time with the system that houses them.

But when you watch two of these bodies come close to each other — and they do, they cross paths, they pass within centimeters — they do not acknowledge each other. There is no adjustment of course. No communication. They pass through shared space like things that cannot perceive each other, and behind them their secretion-silk mingles in the air and does not differentiate, does not retain a sense of which strand came from which body. The silk is continuous. The silk is the same silk. The separate-seeming bodies are issuing the same substance with the same chemistry from the same process.

They are not a swarm in the sense of many individuals collectively behaving. They are one thing doing one thing in several places simultaneously, and the places are not communicating with each other because they do not need to — they are not separate processes coordinating but one process distributed. The bodies are how the silk gets laid where the silk needs to be laid. The silk is what is alive here. The bodies are its method.

The silk, when you examine it — when you hold a strand of it against the bioluminescent glow and look — contains, suspended in its structure, particles. Small particles. They are not debris; they are too evenly distributed, too consistently sized. Seeds, possibly. Spores, possibly. Some stage of something that needs to be somewhere other than where it is. The silk is a delivery system. The distributed organism is a seed-spreader, a colonizer, laying the material basis for the next thing in the spaces that the root network has not yet grown through, carrying the future into the parts of the substrate that are still raw concrete-colored and historyless.

This organism is not new to this place in the sense that the vole-things are new, that the Calibrators are new. It is older. It is, possibly, part of why this place exists at all — part of the mechanism of the place's expansion. If the Firstborn Knot is the memory of the first decision, this is the action of the decision still being enacted. It is the growing's advance agent.

It has no face. It has no center. If you removed one of the bodies from the air, the remaining bodies would not react. The silk would continue to be laid. Nothing that is the organism would know.

## The Vole-Things and What Tends Them

The eyeless cream-white things that move through the root-hair layer like fish through weed: they are not mysterious in themselves. They eat root-hair. They produce, in eating it, an acceleration of root-hair replacement — the root-hair grows back faster where they have been than where they haven't, a grazing benefit, the way certain grasslands thicken under the pressure of certain grazers. They are small enough to hold in a cupped palm, and when you hold one — when you catch one of the ones whose noses surface through the root-hair — it is warm and moves constantly in small rotations, not panicked, not calm, simply *operational*, its body a continuous motion-machine performing root-hair consumption.

They have no eyes because they do not need to navigate by light; they navigate by the texture of the root-hair and by the chemistry of the roots beneath, following gradients of sucrose and growth-hormone the way creatures in lit worlds follow paths. They cannot go wrong. There is nowhere wrong to go. The whole floor is food and home and road simultaneously.

But the things that move in the structural root-lattice below — the leggy patient things seen in silhouette against bioluminescent patches — they are not hunting the vole-things, you eventually determine. The evidence: the vole-things do not flee the vibrations caused by the leggy things moving below them. The leggy things do not accelerate when a vole-thing passes through the ceiling of their world. They coexist without predation, which in this place of new animals, first-ideas of animals, may mean simply that predation has not been invented yet between them, or may mean that they are in some relationship that looks like tending.

Tend is the right word, you think. The leggy things move through the root-lattice in patterns that trace the zones of highest vole-thing activity, and in their wake — in the root-lattice through which they have recently passed — you notice that the structural roots are slightly more intact. Where the leggy things have not been, there are small instances of damage, root-cord fraying, fusion-zones improperly sealed. Where they have been, these are corrected. Not by the leggy things directly — you cannot catch them in the act of direct repair — but as a consequence of their presence, their chemistry, the signals they leave in the system.

They are the health of the floor. The vole-things are the hunger of the floor. Between these two things, the floor is maintained, fed, growing, alive. You are walking on an ongoing negotiation between appetite and repair, between consumption and restoration, and both sides of that negotiation have bodies and move through space and have, for the moment, found a working arrangement.

## The Swarms

The ten thousand small pale-winged things spiraling around sections of ceiling root: they are not ten thousand individuals in the way the word *individual* wants to mean. They are ten thousand units of a process that has a memory. You can watch this:

When a swarm finishes stripping the outer layer from a root section — a process that takes roughly forty minutes per section — it does not disperse. It relocates. It lifts, all at once, from the finished section with a sound like a single exhalation, a rush of wings resolving into silence over the course of a second, and it moves to a new section. The new section is never random. The new section is always, when you observe long enough, a root section whose outer layer is at a particular stage of development — not too young, not too thick, but at a specific moment in its growth cycle when stripping accelerates rather than damages the root's further development. The swarm is farming. It strips what is ready to be stripped, and the stripping is a service, and the swarm has a map, a calendar, a knowledge of where it has already been and what is ready now and what will be ready next.

This knowledge does not live in any one of the ten thousand bodies. If you remove a hundred of them — if a cave-in, a root-fusion, any accident takes a section of the swarm — the remaining bodies do not reorganize. They continue the spiral at exactly the same rate, the same density-per-meter-of-root, the same collective calendar. The knowledge is in the configuration. In the particular distances between bodies. In the frequencies produced by ten thousand wings at particular intervals. The swarm *is* the knowledge; the bodies are where the knowledge stands while it works.

What is stripped from the roots is not simply discarded. The material carried by those subgroups that detach and descend to floor-level — carried in pieces, in secretion, in whatever transactional explosion of wings delivers it to the waiting floor-groups — is being used. For what, the construction of something: you see it accumulating at the bases of the structural roots, a layered deposit, pale and dense, neither plant nor mineral, something new. It is being built in rings. The rings are consistent in their geometry across different root bases, consistent in ways that imply a shared plan, a shared measurement.

The rings are foundations. Something will be built on them. What, you cannot see yet; this place has not gotten there. The swarms are working on a project whose second stage has not yet begun, and the foundations are being laid from material the swarm farm harvests from the ceiling, deposited at the bases of the floor, and the interval between first stage and second stage may be measured in weeks or months and you will not be here to see it.

You leave the swarms to their calendar.

## The Pale Leggy Creature in the Bowl

The creature that lives in the resin bowl in the human-made dome — the one that does not move when you lean close — is not sleeping. It is waiting.

It is the size of a large spider, and it has the legs of a spider in number but not in proportion — the legs are too short for the body, not built for speed, built instead for grip, for staying in one position against forces that might dislodge it. The body is the color of the resin bowl it occupies, by coincidence or by some process of pigmentation that responds to substrate, you cannot tell. It does not move because it has learned, in its short life, that the bowl does not move; and that stillness in the bowl is safety; and that what it needs will come to it if it remains still, in the place that is its place, and waits.

What it needs: the small condensation of biochemical residue that accumulates in the bowl's curve from the air's chemistry, the traces of whatever was once held in the bowl. Whatever was drunk from it or stored in it by the human who made it, some residue of that persists, and the creature is living on that residue, on the ghost of human storage, on the afterglow of a human use for a human object in a place that is busily making human presence irrelevant.

It is the most contingent life in the undercroft. Everything else here has a function, is doing something for the system, is part of a negotiation. This creature has found a coincidence: a bowl-shaped thing that accumulates what it needs, and it has staked its life on that coincidence, and so far the coincidence holds. When it fails — when the residue is exhausted, when the bowl is grown over, when the contingency ends — this animal will either move or die. It is betting, in the way that small animals bet without knowing they are betting, on the continued availability of a specific found thing in a place that finds and incorporates and eventually eliminates all found things.

It is, you think, the most human creature in this entire system.

## What Else Lives Here That Has No Body

The growing itself. The pattern of the growing. The first decision being iterated a billion times in two directions simultaneously — outward into unhistoried substrate, and inward into further and further complexity of fusion.

It lives here in the way that a conversation lives in a room — not as a thing you can hold but as the thing that all the things in the room are doing together. The Firstborn Knot is its first word. The Calibrators are its syntax. The distributed silk-organism is its forward motion. The vole-things and their tenders are its maintenance of itself. The swarms are its long project, its future tense, its foundations for something not yet begun.

And you, standing in it, warm, dry-lipped, the thunder of fusions coming from every direction at three or four per second — you are a feature of the landscape being noted by processes that cannot note, a warmth and a stillness and a particular chemistry that the root-hair below your feet is already considering, that the Calibrators passing your boots have already measured and added to their records, that the root network has already begun to factor into its growing as a resistance, a presence, a fact.

You are being incorporated. Not consumed — not yet, not in any time that matters for you. But noted. Added to the record. The stake is being grown over. The dome is becoming more than was intended. The bowl-creature waits in its coincidence.

You are a new thing in the undercroft of first growing, and the undercroft has been expecting new things, and it is, in its patient, thunderous, comprehensive way, making room.
