# What Lives Here

---

Begin with what is not here, because absence is a kind of presence on the plateau.

There are no large mammals. You might expect there to be — something furred and heavy-footed, something that could weather the cold band and the thin air and the surgical sun — but the plateau declines this. Whatever rule organizes the place has determined that large mammals are incidental, visitors tolerated but not accommodated, and so none have stayed. The smaller mammals are present but barely: a few species of vole or pika living in the debris fields between boulders, almost entirely hypothetical, known by evidence rather than by sight. Droppings the size of apple seeds near the cushion plants. A small oval of slightly flattened vegetation that might be a sleeping hollow. A flash of brown between stones that could be a rodent or could be a trick of the changing shadow when a cloud crosses.

If you sit still for long enough, you see them. But they don't want to be seen.

The pika — because let's say it is pika, the plateau being the kind of place that suits them — is a small animal that looks like what a rabbit might become if it decided to take altitude seriously. Round-eared, densely furred, with a face that appears startled but is simply the face they have always had. They live in the rock piles, in the fracture-spaces between boulders where the temperature is slightly regulated by the thermal mass of the stone and the depth keeps out the wind. Their bodies carry an extraordinary amount of heat relative to their size, which is necessary because they do not hibernate. They stay. They stay through whatever this place does in winter, which the present midday light does not show you but which you can infer from the way the pika behave: with a kind of urgent, perpetual industry that suggests they are always aware of what's coming, always preparing, always adding to the hay piles they build in sheltered chambers beneath the rocks.

The hay is made from the cushion plants, the sedge, the prostrate willow leaves, the tiny grass growing in the crack. All of it is cut and carried and dried and stored by the pika in quantities that seem excessive for such small animals. But the winter is not a small thing here, and the pika understand this in the only way an animal can understand something: entirely, without reflection, through the complete commitment of their bodies to the problem.

They call to each other. Short, sharp barks, very high — a sound that fits into the upper register of the plateau's roar without competing with it, surfacing briefly and sinking back. The call serves as alarm but also as something else, something territorial or communicative or simply present: *I am here, I am here, I am here.* They say it constantly. You could interpret this as anxiety. You could also interpret it as satisfaction.

---

Now the birds more fully, because they are the reason for everything.

The cliff colonies are guillemot-like — not guillemots exactly, but filling the same shape in the world's logic: alcid-bodied, short-winged, designed for diving rather than soaring, birds that are genuinely excellent at two things (flying through water, producing a sound like the sky breaking) and merely adequate at the third thing (flying through air) that gets them between the cliff ledges and the sea. Except there is no sea here. The plateau is too high and too far inland for there to be sea. And yet here they are, in their thousands, on ledges they have used for generations, producing eggs they shelter under their wings the way their coastal relatives do, calling in the way they have always called. They look slightly wrong here, these colonial seabirds at extreme altitude, their navy-and-white coloring incongruous against the gold and pink stone. But they don't look lost. They look like they were here first.

The updrafts explain some of it. The plateau generates reliable thermals along its cliff faces, and the approach routes to the colony are consistent with what birds built for ocean flying could manage. The fish — because they still need fish — are in the lakes below, and the birds make long daily flights down and back, descending thousands of meters and climbing back, doing it so routinely that they don't waste energy on it. Watching them come in to the cliff from below, you would see them transform: awkward in level flight, but riding the updraft along the cliff face with sudden, startling grace, turning and braking and landing on their designated inch of ledge with a precision that suggests the ledge has their name on it. Which, in every sense that matters, it does. The ledge is inherited. The egg laid on it is the latest in an unbroken line. The voice added to the roar is the voice the lineage has always contributed to this specific frequency of this specific sound.

They know each other. That is the thing that takes time to understand, watching the colony from close enough to see individual birds. In the chaos of ten thousand birds on ledges forty centimeters wide, there is complete social precision. Each pair knows its neighbors. Each bird returning from the lakes finds its mate on the correct ledge among thousands of identical ledges, by a recognition so quick and certain it looks like the bird is falling toward the right place the way a stone falls — not choosing, but obeying. They touch bills. They exchange calls at close range, a different call than the colony roar, lower and more complex, a murmur between two specific birds that has been going on for years. The pair bond is long. They return to the same ledge, the same partner, the same egg-position, year after year for as long as both of them are alive.

The colony is made of thousands of these private conversations conducted simultaneously in very close proximity, producing the roar.

---

The raptors that circle high are not hunting the cliff birds, or not usually. The cliff birds are too dense a target, too defended by their own numbers — a raptor that stoops into the colony would be mobbed in ways that could actually be dangerous, the air suddenly full of heavy-bodied alcid-shapes moving in all directions simultaneously. The raptors have learned this, or the raptors that survive have learned this, which is the same thing.

What they hunt is the small bird — the finch-flock, the ground nesters. And also the pika. The pika, sitting on a sun-warmed boulder with a mouthful of sedge, must look from six hundred meters up exactly like the meal a bird that size needs.

The raptor that lives here — and one does live here, a permanent resident rather than a visitor, distinguishable from the others by its slightly different wingbeat and by the fact that it is always present — is large enough that its wing-shadow crossing a boulder is briefly alarming even to you. It has, over years of living here, developed hunting methods suited to this specific plateau: the slow orbital survey at height, the abrupt stoop that begins so far up the descent seems impossible until it is very nearly complete, and then the pull-up and carry-away that happens so fast you have to reconstruct what you saw after it's over. It is efficient in the way that something becomes efficient after years of operating in a specific landscape: it wastes nothing. It makes no unnecessary calls. When it is not hunting, it sits on a particular boulder on the highest shoulder of the plateau and is still, conserving heat, watching everything below it with the calm attention of something that has decided it belongs.

Its mate hunts separately. Their territories overlap but their hunting patterns are timed to avoid competition, a coordination that has no central organization, that emerges from two intelligent birds living in close proximity over many years and learning each other's movements the way water learns the shape of stone. They roost near each other. They are not demonstrative. They are simply, reliably, present in each other's range, which is its own kind of intimacy.

The nest is on a cliff face, in a place that has been a nest for longer than any individual bird can account for. The material is accumulated over many generations: grass and lichen and the bones of small animals and the dried cushion-plant stems the pika left unguarded. It has the consistency of felt. The eggs, when there are eggs, are a mottled brown that disappears against the stone, which is not an accident.

---

The finch-flock moves and moves and never quite settles.

They are small birds with thick bills designed for seeds, though here at this altitude the seeds are hard to find and they supplement with insects and with whatever the cushion plants offer in the brief weeks when the cushion plants offer anything. Their plumage is not exciting: streaked brown and buff on the back, slightly lighter below, with the males showing a wash of rose at the breast that looks, in the surgical midday light, more vivid than it probably is. In photographs taken here, the males would appear nearly red. In the soft light of lower altitudes, the same birds would look almost drab.

They are not any one thing. The flock contains several species so similar in size and coloring that they move together as a single entity, their different calls blurring into the chittering dry note that underlies the colony roar. If you could slow the flock down and examine it — which you can't, because slowing it would require stopping it, and stopping it would require catching it, and catching it would require being faster than a bird — you would find that the apparent unity of the flock conceals a constant micro-negotiation. Each bird is making individual decisions about direction and speed and distance from neighbors, and the flock-shape emerges from the sum of these decisions in real time, continuously, without any bird serving as leader.

The flock turns away from a shadow and there is no bird that turned first. The flock rises and there is no bird that gave the signal. There is only the flock, turning, rising, flowing around a boulder, settling on a cushion plant and lifting again five seconds later, too coordinated to be random, too local to be planned.

They have learned the raptor's orbit. They have incorporated its position into their collective awareness in a way that isn't conscious in any bird but operates through all of them simultaneously. When the raptor is high and circling far to the north, the flock spreads slightly and feeds. When the raptor moves south and descends a few hundred meters, the flock contracts and rises and begins to move. No bird in the flock has looked directly at the raptor recently. No bird has communicated information about the raptor to its neighbors in any signal that has been identified. The knowledge is somehow in the flock without being in any bird.

They spend the night in the rock debris, each bird in its own crevice between stones, which retains enough heat from the day's sun to keep the thin air inside slightly above freezing. They go in at dusk individually, each one finding its own space without apparent coordination. In the morning they come out and the flock assembles itself again, gradually, each bird joining the rising cloud, until suddenly it is complete and moving, as though it had never stopped.

---

Below the surface of things, in the actual rock, in the soil pockets, in the base of the cushion plants where organic matter has slowly accumulated into something almost black and almost soil: there is a layer of life that is older than any of this, older than any individual or species or pattern that is visible from standing height.

The lichen is not one organism. It is two — a fungus and an alga, living so completely interdependently that they function as a single thing, that naming them separately is technically accurate and practically misleading. The fungus provides structure and access to the mineral surface. The alga provides energy from the sun. Together they produce an organism that can live on bare rock with no soil, no water beyond what condenses on its surface, no temperature that is reliable. They expand at a few millimeters per year in good conditions. They stop expanding and wait in bad ones. They can survive being frozen, desiccated, baked, frozen again. The rust-orange lichen on the south-facing boulder surfaces has been there for centuries. The almost-black lichen in the deep crevices has been there for longer.

They are not doing anything recognizable as living except at a timescale that dwarfs everything else on the plateau. Compared to the lichen, the cliff birds are frantic. The pika is practically feverish. The cushion plants — already understood to be slow — are practically rushing.

The lichen is patient in the way that makes patience seem like the wrong word, because patience implies that something is being waited for. The lichen isn't waiting for anything. It is simply present, metabolizing when conditions allow, not metabolizing when they don't, persisting across a span of time that it has no relationship to whatsoever, containing no experience of duration. It has covered this rock. It is covering it. It will cover it further. These three statements are, from the lichen's perspective, a single statement.

---

The small dark pools in the basin floors contain what they contain.

Not fish — the pools are too cold and too isolated and too young in geological terms to have been colonized by anything that arrived by swimming. What lives in them is microscopic: communities of single-celled organisms, algae and protists and bacteria, living in the few centimeters of water above the silt layer, invisible from the surface, making the water slightly less clear than pure water would be and giving the pool its dark quality. They are communities — not in the sense of having social organization but in the sense of having established interdependencies: this organism produces something that organism needs, this other organism consumes the waste of both. The communities in different pools are slightly different from each other, having diverged over whatever long period they've been isolated, each pool becoming a small and separate experiment in what is possible given these specific parameters.

The water is very cold and very clean except for what lives in it, which is very small and very old in its forms. Life in the pools preceded the birds on the ledges by a vast margin. It will be here after the birds have become something else. It does not know this. It does not know anything. But it persists in the way that simple things persist when the conditions are adequate: completely, without drama, by doing the only thing it knows how to do.

Around the pool margins, where the sedge grows and the tiny yellow flowers appear in brief windows of warmth: springtails. Tiny arthropods, barely visible, moving through the litter of dead plant material at the pool's edge. They eat decomposing organic matter and are eaten by almost nothing, here, because the things that eat springtails are not present at this altitude in sufficient numbers. So the springtails are, in the ecosystem of the pool margin, more abundant than they would be elsewhere. They have the pool margins largely to themselves. They move through their available world with the brisk purposefulness of things that have no concept of abundance.

---

There is one more thing, and it is the hardest to describe because it does not have clear edges.

The plateau, in the way it has settled into itself, in the way its equilibrium is maintained across all these species and timescales simultaneously, produces a kind of collective behavior that none of its inhabitants exhibit individually. The cliff birds don't know that their guano fertilizes the pools, which supports the algae, which supports the aquatic communities, which retains moisture in the basin floors, which allows the cushion plants to grow at the pool margins, which the pika harvest and the finch flocks rest on and the raptors use as a reference point when they survey for prey. No individual bird or pika or cushion plant or pool organism is managing this. There is no manager.

But it has been managed, in the sense of having arrived at a stable configuration that tends to restore itself when disturbed. The plateau has been here long enough that the contingent has been sorted from the necessary: what is here is here because it fits, because it completes a cycle that includes everything else, because its removal would create a gap that would pull toward being filled. The finch flock is not optional. The lichen is not optional. The pika's hay-gathering, which aerates and disturbs the soil pockets in small ways that turn out to matter for what grows there, is not optional.

This is what lives here: everything that remains after enough time has passed for everything else to have left. The selection is long and thorough and impersonal. What it has produced is a community so interlocked that the community itself has become a kind of organism — not metaphorically, but in the sense that its behavior is coherent at a scale that no individual constituent can perceive. The roar is its voice. The lichen-covered stone is its skin. The pools are its memory, holding in microscopic community the history of what this water has always contained.

You stand on the plateau and you are inside it. It doesn't notice. It has been doing this long enough that visitors don't register. You are not a threat and not a resource and not a disturbance significant enough to require adjustment. The pika notes you and barks twice and returns to its hay. The raptor's orbit widens slightly and then narrows again. The finch flock parts around you like water around a stone and reconstitutes on the other side.

The colony roars on, indifferent, magnificent, ancient, complete.
