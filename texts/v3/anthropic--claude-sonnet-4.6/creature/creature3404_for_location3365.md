# What Lives in the Sump

---

Begin with what they are not.

They are not vermin. Vermin implies tolerance-limit, implies a place that does not want them, implies the possibility of extermination if the numbers exceeded some threshold of acceptability. Nothing here has a threshold. Nothing has been exceeded. They are not infestation because infestation requires a host, and the Sump is not a host — it is a medium. You do not say that brine is infested with salt.

They are not insects, though the word wants to apply itself, the way the mind reaches for insect whenever it encounters small-bodied multitude. The concept breaks on contact. Insects have a grammar — an exoskeletal logic, a segmentation, a wing-or-not-wing binary, a life-cycle with distinct chapters. These things are not structured that way. They read as too continuous, too whole, too much a single expression all the way through. When you look for where the head stops and the thorax begins, you find instead that you have been looking at it wrong, that there is no such join, that the creature is organized around a different principle entirely — not segment but gradient, not division but degree, the density of function increasing or decreasing along the body's length without hard borders, the sensory equipment shading into the locomotive equipment shading into whatever performs digestion by no more dramatic a transition than a coastline shading from sand to rock.

They are not white. White would be the expected color for something that lives in a warm lightless pipe-trench, for something that has surrendered the project of coloration. But the light here is never quite absent, only diffuse, only ambient, only arrived-from-everywhere, and the things that live in it are the color of that light pressed through matter, which is to say: a pale that is not white. A pale that is warm. The color of bone in good light, the color of old ivory, the color of the calcium formations at the drain mouths — which is perhaps not coincidental, perhaps an assertion of relationship, perhaps the Sump making a recurring motif from available chemistry.

Some of them are darker. This takes longer to see. In the masses beneath the catwalks, in the packed interior of the pipe trenches where the warmth is most concentrated, there are individuals — if they are individuals — that are darker, more ochre, tending toward the color of the iron itself. They are not a different species. It seems more as though darkness is a condition that accumulates, a kind of seasoning, the way iron becomes more orange-dark at the joints where two pieces meet. These darker ones are in the joints. That is where they are found, preferentially. They have taken the joint positions.

---

Their number.

The number is not the right way to approach them, though you will try. You will try to count in a small area and extrapolate. But the count keeps changing — not because they are moving, though they are, but because the boundary between what is them and what is the equipment is not always legible. They press close enough to flanges and curved metal surfaces that they conform. They are not camouflaged, exactly; they are not performing camouflage as a strategy; they are simply very good at being against things, very good at contact, and at a certain scale of looking they disappear into the texture of the place the way grain disappears into wood.

Come closer and they resolve. Go far enough back and they lose themselves again. The Sump contains them the way a material contains its component elements — you can know the material is there, you can know what is in it, but you cannot point to where, exactly, the carbon is.

What this means for number: the Sump is saturated with them. Every surface that offers warmth and moisture is occupied. The occupation is complete. If you were to drain the Sump of them — if such a thing were imaginable — you would find the same volumes of empty space that a sponge surrenders when you wring it. The Sump without them would be porous, would be riddled, would be full of the shapes of them.

---

The particular thing they do at the drains.

There is a behavior. It is worth describing carefully because it is the most organized-seeming thing they do, or the most visible organization, the place where intention (if intention is the word) surfaces into legibility.

At the circular drain mouths — the thirty-centimeter gridded circles set into the grating at intervals — they gather, but not haphazardly. They arrange. They come from some distance to do this. You can watch them, if you have the patience, traveling along the grating toward a drain, and the travel is not the aimless movement they exhibit elsewhere but something more directed, more as if the drain exerts a draw on them beyond the merely gravitational. They arrive at the edge. They position themselves. The ones already positioned make room — or rather, they adjust, there is a small reconfiguration, a settling — and the new arrival slots in.

What you end up with, at each drain, is a ring. A living ring of them, their front portions extended slightly over the drain's lip, whatever functions as a head or leading edge pointed down at the dark water sounds coming up from below. They are listening, or they are receiving something, or they are doing something for which we have no precise verb. The ring is always full. Not always the same individuals — there is slow turnover, the ring refreshes itself over time as members withdraw and are replaced — but always that particular density, that particular orientation, that particular collective attitude of directed attention downward.

The rings have been there long enough that the grating around the drains is differently worn. Or rather: the grating around the drains is not differently worn. The maintenance of the ring means that those sections of grating are not exposed to the ambient mineral condensation that creates pale deposits elsewhere. The rings keep it clean. Clean circles around every drain mouth, against the general warm dusk-colored patina. This is inadvertent, presumably, but it is visible, it is a record, it means the rings are old enough to have left a mark that requires only subtraction.

What comes up from below. That is the question. What does the water carry in its warmth, in its mineral load, in whatever purposes move it through its underground channels? The rings suggest: something. Something worth gathering for. Something that does not deplete — or that depletes so slowly at each draw point that the ring never notices, never has to move on to a different drain, can stay at this one indefinitely, in the warmth, drinking what is offered.

---

They do not appear to sleep.

They do not appear not to sleep.

There is a different quality to some of them — a stillness that is deeper than the ordinary stillness of slow motion, a quality of settled-in-ness, of having arrived at rest. You notice this in the ones tucked under pipe lagging, in the ones occupying the cups of pressure gauges, in the ones that have wedged themselves into the spaces between a pump's discharge flange and the connecting pipe above. They are compressed into those spaces, conforming, still. They may be sleeping. They may be doing something that is to sleep as their kind of movement is to walking — recognizable from a distance as the same general category, but structured differently upon examination.

The ones in the gauge cups are interesting. The gauges have been calibrated — the stickers confirm this — but the gauges now read ambient, read zero pressure, read static temperature, and something has decided to rest in the bowls of them, in the little sheltered cup-shapes the gauge housings create. It is not shelter they are seeking, exactly. The Sump has no weather that reaches here, no wind, no predation visible anywhere. It is something else. It is the shape. They seek particular shapes to rest in, and the shape of a cupped gauge housing is one of those shapes, and this preference has no predator-avoiding function and no climate-sheltering function and perhaps no function at all except the preference itself. They like concavities. They like to be held by geometry.

---

On the question of what they are made of.

The water here is mineral-laden. Warm. Moving through iron for longer than can easily be calculated. What it carries: dissolved iron, calcium, whatever the original process introduced into these systems before the process paused, trace compounds, the long slow chemistry of warm wet metal dissolving imperceptibly into solution and being carried.

The calcium formations at the drain mouths — those small pale scaled structures, fresh-precipitated, sharp-edged, the Sump's geology happening at fingertip scale — are made of what the water deposits when it meets dry air. When the water stops, when it reaches a surface and evaporates, it leaves what it was carrying. The structures build at the rate the water carries and the air removes.

The pale organisms in the Sump are the color of those formations.

This is not proof. It is resemblance. But the resemblance is close enough that you find yourself considering what would happen if the depositional process — the calcium arriving in water, hitting air, precipitating — had learned to do something other than build rigid structures. Had learned, over time, in this specific chemistry, this specific warmth, this specific long pause — had learned to build instead something with a gradient rather than a crystalline order, something that could reform its boundaries, something with the mineral patience of geology but operating in biological time. Something that once finished precipitating began to move.

This would make them very old, by any reasonable measure. Very old and still forming, still adding, at the edges, the fresh-precipitated sharp-edged new material that is them at their youngest and thinnest. The darker ones, the ochre ones in the joint positions — perhaps they are the oldest depositions, the earliest layers, the iron-rich deep geology of the organism, and what you take for age-marking is simply more of the Sump's material having been incorporated over longer exposure.

The Sump made them. This is the simplest and most complete account. They are what happens when this particular chemistry has enough time and the right surfaces and the warmth to drive the process, and what they do — drinking at the drains, conducting warmth through their bodies against the warm metal, circling back to the drain rings — is simply the process continuing. They are not separate from the place. They are a phase of it.

---

On whether they notice you.

They do. This becomes clear quickly. When you walk the catwalks, the ones on the catwalk ahead of you adjust — they do not flee, they do not particularly hurry, but they open a path. Not a generous path. A sufficient path. They recede from your foot-falls to the minimum required distance and remain there, and if you stand still they approach again, not all the way, but partway, reforming the ambient density of the place with you factored in.

Their whatever-functions-as-attention is capable of orienting toward you. You can feel this, the way you can feel being looked at — some quality of the surrounding space changes, becomes directional, becomes addressed. They are assessing something. Not threat; nothing in the Sump's equilibrium has the grammar of threat. They are assessing something like: *what kind of gradient, what is it carrying, where is it going, will it resolve or persist.* They reach some answer, quickly, and the answer is something like *acknowledged* or *catalogued*, and they return to what they were doing, but you remain registered.

If you stay long enough, the registration changes. They come closer. The minimum distance they maintain decreases. After some time — longer than is comfortable, if you are honest — one of them will make contact, briefly, with the back of your hand or the edge of your boot, a touch that lasts less than a second and then is withdrawn. A tasting, perhaps. A calibration. The discovery of your temperature, your humidity, your surface chemistry.

After this, you are of less interest. You have been incorporated into their model of the Sump. You are a new thing but you are now a known new thing, and the Sump has room for known new things, can route around them, can include them in whatever accounting it keeps of its own contents.

You are not unwelcome. That is the thing about the touch, about the registering. There is nothing hostile in the attention they pay you. There is nothing warm in the human sense either — no recognition, no particular curiosity beyond the initial calibration. There is only the even steady acknowledgment of the place, through them, that something has changed and been noted and found to be a kind of change that falls within acceptable parameters. The Sump has solved for you, provisionally. You fit. You don't fit well — you are too warm in the wrong ways, your chemistry is wrong, your gradient is steep and temporary and you will not stay — but you fit within the envelope of things the Sump can accommodate without alarm.

---

The sounds they do not make.

Silence is the wrong word for what they produce. Silence is an absence and they are a presence; they fill the space their bodies occupy completely, which means they also fill the acoustic space their bodies occupy completely. What you get is not silence but a kind of acoustic mass — the Sump's overall sound-environment, the dripping and channeling of water and the distant thin ringing of iron, is not diminished by them but is settled. They absorb minor vibrations through their bodies, pressed as they are against warm metal. The catwalks don't ring as much when they are covered with organisms that are themselves in contact with everything. The Sump's sound has been damped. Made more even. They are acoustic insulation on everything they touch, which is everything.

The result: a quieter version of whatever the Sump would otherwise sound like. A Sump-sound passed through many layers of warm biological material and emerged subdued, rounded, the sharp frequencies taken out. What remains has a quality you associate with large old spaces that have been lived in for a very long time — libraries, caves, places where sound has been absorbed by accumulated presence until only the long slow notes survive.

Occasionally: the thing that sounds like a ringing in the pipe. A tone. Not consistent, not rhythmic — random, from the human ear's perspective, but perhaps not random. Perhaps responsive. Perhaps the tone is produced when the rings at the drains all orient simultaneously, or when some larger percentage than usual of them shift against the iron at the same moment, or when a pressure deep in the underground water-channels changes and is felt by everything touching the grating and their response is movement and the movement produces the tone. This is speculation. The tone is real. What makes it is not confirmed. It continues to happen at irregular intervals, warm and low and short, and then the Sump settles again into its conditioned quiet.

---

What they want.

They want the warm water. That is the closest you can get to it, from observation. They want the mineral water moving through the understructure, want to be near its emergence points, want to drink where it meets air and gives up its cargo. They want the geometry of the equipment — the flanges, the cup-shapes, the long curves of the horizontal tank shells, the enclosed warmth of the pipe trenches. They want to be against warm metal. They want the low steady temperature of the place, not hotter, not cooler, this exactly, the temperature that the Sump maintains with an intentionality that belongs to no one currently present.

If they want anything from each other, it is this: proximity. They want the collective warmth of their own number, the way their bodies, pressed together in the trenches, constitute a material with different thermal properties than any single one of them. They want the ring-formation at the drain mouths, which requires many bodies to complete and which only functions as a ring, never as a collection of individuals each doing their own version of the same thing. The ring is what they want. The ring is complete; an individual is not.

Whether they want anything beyond these things — whether there is something like wanting that extends past warmth and water and proximity and the right geometry — is not answerable from what can be seen. The Sump has reached equilibrium. They are part of the equilibrium. A thing at equilibrium does not want in the way that a gradient wants to resolve; it simply is. They simply are. If wanting implies unfulfillment, then what they have may be beyond wanting — may be its far shore, may be the condition that wanting points toward and never reaches.

They have reached it. That is the terrible and peaceful thing about them. They are not restless. They are not searching. They are not building toward anything or fleeing anything or in any condition of lack. The Sump is full of them and they are full of the Sump and the water moves below and the warmth rises through the grating and they are at the drain mouths in their rings and they are in the flanges and in the gauge cups and under the lagging and coating the undersides of the catwalks in thick pale living masses, and they are enough, and it is enough, and this is the condition they have been in for a long time and will be in for longer still.

The only restlessness in the Sump is yours.
