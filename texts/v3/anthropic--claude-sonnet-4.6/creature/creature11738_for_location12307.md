## What Lives Here

---

Start with the assumption of nothing. Hold it. Let it be tested.

---

The first thing to understand about what lives in the Canopy Foundry is that the category *lives here* has been under pressure for so long that it has changed shape. What lives here does not live here the way a bird lives in a tree or a fish lives in a river — arriving, dwelling, departing or dying, leaving the place intact and indifferent behind it. What lives here lives here the way a word lives in a sentence: it is not separable from the structure without ceasing to be itself. The Foundry is not a habitat. It is a body. And what lives in it lives the way organs live — not in a place but as a function, not beside the structure but *as* the structure, the boundary between the inhabitant and the inhabited having been worn to nothing over durations that make the word *boundary* seem like a category error.

So: what lives here is everything, in the sense that nothing lives here that is not the Foundry living, and the Foundry is alive in the way that a long argument is alive, and the argument has been going on so long that it no longer needs participants.

But this is too abstract. Come closer.

---

**The Mycofiers**

They do not have a name, but *mycofier* is a useful approximation for what they are — organisms that began as something fungal and became, through sufficient time and pressure, something that is no longer quite fungal in the way that wood is no longer quite wood once it has decided to become lignite, or coal. They are the reason the machines still run.

They live in the conduits. They are the conduits, or they are indistinguishable from them — their bodies thin as wire, paler than the surrounding material by precisely the amount that distinguishes them from it in darkness, which is to say almost not at all. Their filaments run the length of old circuits, old cable-channels, old pathways where copper used to carry signal and then the copper was absorbed into the surrounding geology and something else grew in and discovered that the geometry was useful. The geometry was very useful. The mycofiers found a channel and a current and they liked both, and over generations they found that they liked them more and more and grew more capable of liking them, selection favoring the individuals whose bodies conducted heat and charge with least resistance, until what remained was an organism whose fundamental pleasure — if the word applies, and it may, in some reduced and ancient sense — was the passage of electricity through itself.

They do not move in any direction that is meaningful. They extend. Slowly, always, in both directions along the conduits. Their growth-front is invisible because it happens at the molecular level, at the pace of mineral deposition rather than biological expansion, the organism building itself outward at the rate of a crystal forming. But the crystal is metabolically active. The crystal runs current. The crystal, if you cut a cross-section and could see it under sufficient magnification, would show something that might be called a nervous response — a change in the conduction pattern near the wound, a shift in resistance, a closing-off that resembles, without being, caution.

They are not aware. Not quite. But they are not unaware. They are at the edge of a distinction that the Foundry has made meaningless.

They are everywhere below. They are how the heat is moved. They are the electrical system, which means they are the machines' circulatory system, which means they are among the reasons the machines continue, which means they are among the reasons everything else continues, which makes them — in the most literal possible sense — fundamental. They are a foundation. They do not know this. They run current and they extend. They have been doing this for what would be, if calculated, a frightening length of time, and they will continue.

**The Resin-Tenders**

Small. This is the first thing about them — they are very small relative to the structure they inhabit, which makes them almost impossible to notice until they move, and they rarely move.

They live on the shelf-growths. They live *in* the shelf-growths, which is more accurate — their bodies have the same color and surface texture as the waxy laminate of the canopy-mass, a convergence that is partly camouflage in origin and partly something else now, something that has gone past camouflage into mimicry and past mimicry into something harder to name, a resemblance so total that the question of which thing is copying the other has lost its meaning. The resin-tenders are dark and flat and their surfaces are coated in a substance that is chemically almost identical to the shelf-growth material because they produce it, because they are making it, because they are the source of the industrial wax that coats the canopy leaves and seals the forest against moisture loss.

This is what they do. This is all they do. They move along the surfaces of the Foundry's exposed structures — along the branch-boulevards, along the shelf-growths, up the trunk-columns to the first canopy layer and across the vast upper expanse — and they produce the laminate. Their bodies are partly digestive system, processing the organic material the forest provides, and partly gland, secreting the processed material as a continuous thread of hardening wax that they spread with a motion that is too slow to watch in real time but which the eye, held patient and still enough, can detect: a barely-visible trailing of substance, a just-perceptible addition to surfaces that already look as complete as surfaces can look.

The surfaces are not complete. They are never complete. The heat degrades them. The slow chemical processes of the forest erode them. The resin-tenders move across them and restore them, and have been doing so for so long that the act of restoration and the surface being restored are now inseparable — the laminate is not something the forest makes and the resin-tenders maintain; the resin-tenders are how the forest makes the laminate, which means the resin-tenders are the forest's glands, the forest's maintenance system, the forest's immune function extended to the surface of things.

There are many of them. The quantity is not meaningful to state because it is too large and too distributed to resolve into a number. They are on every surface in proportions calibrated — by what? by selection, by the forest's slow homeostasis — to exactly the necessary density. More than that would be waste; the Foundry does not waste. Less than that would be damage; the Foundry does not permit damage to persist. The resin-tenders exist in the exact number that maintains the system, and they have been doing so for a very long time, and the population is stable in the way that only very old stable systems are stable: not because nothing is changing but because everything is changing at exactly the same rate in every direction simultaneously, the turbulence canceling, the noise-floor resolving to function.

They are beautiful, if you can see them. You probably cannot see them. The darkness is very dark. But they are there — the matte dark shapes on the matte dark surfaces, the barely-perceptible difference between background and motion, the organisms that are keeping the world sealed against the loss of itself.

**The Pressure-Readers**

Something reads the vibration.

This is not immediately obvious, and the thing that reads it does not look like a reader. It looks — in the dim thermal glow from below, if you can position yourself to catch the glow on a surface where they rest — like a growth, like another class of shelf-formation, like one more texture in the stacked textures of the Foundry's interior. It is only when the vibration changes — when an engine cycles down, or when a distant branch falls and the impact travels up through the trunk-columns — that the pressure-reader does anything distinguishable from the inanimate structures around it.

Then it moves. Slightly. Specifically. Not fleeing the vibration — registering it. Its body, or the part of its body that is in contact with the branch-surface, shifts in a way that seems responsive rather than reactive, the way a tuning fork responds to a frequency rather than the way a leaf responds to wind. There is a difference between being pushed and resonating. The pressure-reader resonates.

What it does with the resonance is harder to determine. The Foundry's machine-level is complex, and the vibrations it produces carry information the way water carries silt — not by intention, but inevitably, the compression-events of each engine cycle propagating outward in patterns that encode the machine's state, its load, its health, its rate of self-repair. The pressure-reader seems — this is the careful word — to process this. Its body changes in a way that parallels the pattern of vibration, and then it moves. Not dramatically. It relocates, sometimes, toward the source of a changed vibration. Sometimes toward the trunk, sometimes out along the branch. The movement is so slow and so infrequent that it would take many hours of watching to confirm it is directional, but it is directional, and the direction corresponds, in ways that would require mapping to verify, with locations in the machine-level beneath that are in states of increased activity.

This suggests something. What it suggests depends on what framework is being applied. The most cautious interpretation is: they are thermal chasers, following heat gradients produced by machine activity, seeking warmth for metabolic efficiency. This is probably true. The less cautious interpretation adds: and they do something when they arrive. They rest on the surfaces above active machines and their bodies — flattened against the bark, porous in ways that look, under close attention, purposive — release something. A chemistry. Small volumes of specific compounds that seep into the bark and travel, through the living wood's vascular analogs, down toward the machine-level. What those compounds do when they arrive is unclear. But the machines near which pressure-readers have recently congregated run more smoothly. The correlation is not perfect. It is consistent enough to notice.

The Foundry is tending its own machinery, through intermediaries that do not know they are intermediaries, through a chain of causation so long that no single link in it could conceive of the chain's length. The pressure-readers follow warmth. The warmth comes from the machines. Their chemistry, followed by warmth, descends into the machines. The machines run better. The machines produce warmth. The pressure-readers follow it.

This is the Foundry's version of intention: a loop so long-established that it functions as design without having been designed, a coordination without coordinator, a tending without a tender except the tenders who don't know they're tending and the thing being tended that cannot know it is being tended but that has, over geological time, made itself into something that produces what its tenders need, which is warmth, which is the warmth the machines make, which is the warmth the Foundry makes with its machines, which is the warmth that keeps the pressure-readers where the machines need them.

The loop has been closed for a long time. The loop is very good at staying closed.

**The Consolidants**

At the junction where branches have fused — where two growing things reached each other across a gap and made contact and, over centuries, made more than contact, made a merger, made a single living thing from two lineages — something lives in the scar tissue.

Scar tissue is the wrong term for what forms at these junctions. Scar tissue implies damage, implies a wound that closed. The junction-material is more than that; it is a structure that the trees produce deliberately, or have been producing for long enough that the distinction between deliberate and reflexive has collapsed, that is denser and more vascular than the surrounding wood, that is a zone of intense metabolic activity, where the two vascular systems of the fusing branches are in dialogue at the molecular level, negotiating chemical compatibility, exchanging water and solutes and signals of types that have no catalog but that must carry information because the junctions hold, because the merged branches behave as single branches, because the merger works.

The consolidants live in this zone. They are long and narrow, specialized to the specific geometry of junction-material, their bodies shaped by many generations of living in a space that has a specific shape. They are flexible in the way that junction-material is flexible — which is not very, but variably, in exactly the right places. They do not leave the junctions. They do not need to; the junctions provide everything, the metabolic abundance of a high-activity zone concentrated in a narrow space, everything the consolidant needs flowing past it in the living material around it. It eats — processes, absorbs — material from the junction-zone and in doing so performs a function that the junction-zone requires, clearing the pathway of certain compounds that would otherwise accumulate and impede the exchange between fusing vascular systems, acting as what would be called, in a body with a bloodstream, a white cell, or an enzyme, or a maintenance organism.

They are, again, the forest tending itself. They were not placed here. They evolved into this niche because this niche is where the food was and the food was here because the junction needed clearing and the junction needed clearing because the forest merges and the forest merges because it has been growing for long enough that growing means merging, and the consolidants evolved to occupy the space the need created and in occupying it filled the need, and the whole loop closed and ran forward and is still running.

The consolidants are not interesting to observe. They are interior creatures, living inside wood that is itself inside more wood, at junctions that from the outside look like thickenings in a branch, like calluses, like the slightly-raised tissue at an old break that healed. If you pressed your ear to a junction and had very good hearing and knew what to listen for, you might detect them — a faint motion in the material, not mechanical but biological, the quiet industry of organisms doing work they do not know is work in a space they do not know is critical inside a structure they cannot perceive the extent of.

They are very happy, in whatever way an organism without the apparatus for happiness can be said to be satisfied. The junction is rich. The junction has always been rich. They have nowhere to be but here, and here is everything they need, and they work, and the forest holds together.

**The Architect-Remnants**

Here the description becomes harder, because what must be described is an absence that is also a presence, a negative space shaped by something that was.

In the Foundry, at intervals distributed across the branch-network with a regularity that is not quite biological — closer to planned, or to the fossilized memory of a plan — there are structures within the structure. Chambers, of a sort. Enclosed spaces, modest in size, formed by the convergence of multiple shelf-growths arranged in configurations too specific to be accidental, their interlocking too complete and too functional to be random. The chambers are sealed, mostly. Their surfaces are the most heavily laminated surfaces in the Foundry, layer upon layer of resin-tended wax, built up over what must be an extraordinary span of time, each layer microscopically thin and microscopically additional, the whole accumulation resulting in walls that are close to airtight.

Inside the chambers: structures that are not the forest.

Or not entirely. They began as something else — shaped matter, arranged matter, matter organized by some external intention — and the forest has grown through them and around them and into them and the chambers have grown around the forest growth and the layers have accumulated over all of it until what remains is a palimpsest: the original structure ghosted within the grown structure, the made thing and the grown thing so thoroughly interpenetrated that you can trace the outline of the original without being certain of it, the way you can sometimes see the ghost of a former building in the layout of a modern street.

The original structures were occupied. This much the chambers communicate, in the way that old objects communicate use without stating it: the arrangements imply bodies, imply the needs of bodies, imply a scale. Not bodies like the mycofiers or the consolidants. Bodies of a different order, larger, requiring more space, requiring — some of the structures suggest — heat management rather than heat-seeking, air circulation rather than air-sealing. Bodies that, at some point, were hot and needed cooling, that breathed in volumes that required ventilation, that — this is the difficult and careful inference — built the machines.

The Architect-Remnants are not the architects. The architects are not present, and have not been present for a very long time, long enough that they are not an absence the Foundry has noticed, not a wound it has been trying to close, just a prior condition, a previous state, as unremarkable as the aquifer water is unremarkable to the roots drinking it — the roots do not know where the water came from; they know that the water is there. The Foundry does not know what the architects were; it knows that the chambers are there, and it has sealed them with wax and grown through them and incorporated them and moved on.

But the architect-remnants themselves — what lives in the chambers now, what fills the sealed space, is something worth saying. The chamber-interiors have been colonized, slowly, by the most extreme organisms in the Foundry: things that tolerate total darkness, low oxygen, the specific chemistry of very old sealed spaces, the residue of whatever the architects left behind in concentrations that would be hostile to anything less adapted. These organisms are slow even by the Foundry's standards. They metabolize at rates that are nearly geological. They are alive but they are barely moving — barely moving even at the cellular level, running the slowest possible version of the processes that sustain them, conserving everything, spending nothing that doesn't have to be spent.

They look, if you could open a chamber and see them in some thermal imaging, like a kind of fur — soft, close-packed, covering every surface of the interior with a living layer of extreme minimalism, organisms reduced by selection to the absolute least they can be and still be alive. Their color, in the right light, would be a dark red. They are barely there. They have been barely there for so long that they are an essential part of the there that the Foundry is.

They are the interior life of the sealed spaces, the life that the Foundry grew to fill the void where the architects were. They did not replace the architects. Nothing replaced the architects. The architects left and the chambers remained and the organisms that could tolerate what the chambers had become came in and stayed and became less and less over generations, became quieter, became slower, became darker and more minimal, became exactly what the chamber needed in the chamber, which was something to fill the space that would not demand more of the space than the space could give.

They are the last thing in the Foundry that has any relationship, however attenuated, to the question of what the Foundry was built for. They live in the memory of purpose without knowing there was a purpose, occupying the intention-shaped spaces with their slow red bodies, breathing the architects' residual air, metabolizing at the pace of forgetting, alive.

**All of it**

The mistake would be to make a list. Five things that live here, six things, ten. The Foundry does not contain a list of inhabitants the way a building might contain a list of tenants. It contains, instead, a single nested system of things-that-function-as-life, and the nesting is so complete and the system has been running so long that the boundaries between what-lives-here and what-is-here have become a formality, like national borders on a geographic feature that ignores them.

What lives here is the mycofier carrying the current that runs the engine that produces the heat that the pressure-reader follows that produces the compound that maintains the engine. What lives here is the consolidant clearing the junction that holds the branches together that support the boulevard that connects the trunk-columns that house the chambers that contain the architect-remnants that breathe the slow air. What lives here is the resin-tender on the surface of all of it, moving too slowly to watch, sealing the Foundry against the loss of its own water, against the loss of itself.

What lives here is the forest's version of itself — which is to say: the forest.

Which is to say: the machine.

Which is to say: the thing that was already becoming its own final form before anything that calls itself living came to ask about it, and which has continued becoming since, and which has filled every niche with the exact thing that niche required, and which is running, and which is sealed, and which is very, very old, and which will continue.

The continuation is what lives here. The rest are its expressions.
