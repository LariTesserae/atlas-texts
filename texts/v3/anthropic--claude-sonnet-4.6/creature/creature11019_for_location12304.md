# What Lives on the Breathing Plateau

---

## The Understory Crust

Begin with what you almost cannot see: the lichen that is not decorating the stone but *is* the stone's current self.

It is flat. It has no ambition toward height. Height would cost something on a plateau where wind, even slow wind, is continuous and cold, and where there is nothing to shelter behind that the wind has not already found and moved around. So the lichen grew into the stone rather than away from it, or did not grow into the stone so much as *become a new layer of it* — the stone's outermost expression, which happens to metabolize.

It is grey-green but mostly grey. The green is in there, available to chemical analysis, available to a person who stares at a small patch of it in the diffuse light with a magnifying lens and patience. Without the lens: grey. The plateau has worked on this for long enough that the lichen's surface refractive index now matches, almost exactly, the surface refractive index of the rock it grows on. This is not a coincidence in the sense of being planned. But it is not a coincidence in the sense of being accidental, either. It is the solution to a problem that was posed by geological time.

The lichen solves the mineral bloom. The bloom — those concentric ovals, those branching filaments, those patterns that look like a language — is partly the lichen's record of itself. It eats mineral upward and processes it and deposits what it cannot use back at its margins, and the margins of the lichen colonies, over centuries, leave these traces. The patterns in the rock are a long autobiography. The lichen is still writing it. It writes very slowly, and a single sentence takes what a person would call a lifetime to complete, but it has been writing since before the current mountains were a thought, and the stone holds all the sentences.

When the lichen does what it does, it does it together. There is no useful distinction, on this plateau, between a single lichen organism and the lichen community: the colony edges touch and chemical signals cross and what one part of the surface does is modulated by what another part is doing, across distances that would be enormous if measured in lichen-body-lengths. They are not a superorganism in the sense of having a unified nervous response. But they are not individuals in the sense of being uncommunicative, either. They are something in between, something for which human ecology has been groping toward vocabulary for decades without quite arriving.

In the very early morning — or what corresponds to morning, the slightly-lighter grey that the light-sensitive creatures read as the trigger — the lichen opens. This is barely visible. A subtle shift in the surface texture, a slight release of metabolic gas, a change in the albedo of the rock face that is too small to see with the eye but that the creatures who depend on it can read. The lichen signals, in this minimal way, that processing has resumed, that the day's exchange is beginning. And what receives the signal responds.

## The Things Inside the Lichen

Before the fauna that moves, there is the fauna that doesn't.

The lichen is inhabited. Every square centimeter of it is inhabited. This is not a figure of speech: the density of microscopic life in a lichen thallus is, per unit volume, competitive with soil, with river sediment, with habitats we think of as teeming. Here the things that live in the lichen are: mites of a very old lineage, their body plans set before the plateau had its current face, their mouthparts fitted to the specific chemistry of this lichen's specific cellular interior with the precision of a key to a lock made for it; nematodes that move through the thin water films in the lichen's body as through a network of rivers, feeding, reproducing, dying in the same films their great-great-ancestors died in; tardigrades, inevitably tardigrades — the plateau creatures that can be dried and frozen and struck by radiation and are still not dead, that have been in the lichen for long enough that some populations here have not encountered desiccation stress in ten thousand years and are slightly, measurably, edging away from the capacity to survive it, because the plateau is stable and has not required that ability for long enough that it costs more to maintain than to slowly lose.

These inner fauna have their own inner fauna. All the way down through scales of size, the lichen's interior is occupied, and the occupants are occupied, and the occupation is complete and has been complete for long enough that it is not *occupation* anymore but *identity*. The mite does not live *in* the lichen. The mite's category of being is lichen-mite, hyphenated, indissoluble.

They process each other. They process the stone's chemistry, which they receive through the lichen's body, which receives it through the mineral bloom, which receives it from the freeze-thaw cycles, which is driven by the plateau's own weather, which is generated by the plateau's own thermal mass. Everything feeds through everything. The lichen's interior is a refinery within a refinery, a smaller gear inside a larger gear, all turning.

## The Crevice Fauna

Where the stone fractures, things grow sideways.

The logic of the crevice is the logic of the threshold: it is neither the exposed surface nor the deep underground. It is the edge between. It holds moisture in amounts the open surface cannot. It moderates temperature — not warm, never warm, but less cold, more stable, less subject to the plateau's freeze-thaw cycles than the surface above the crevice or the deeper rock below. This moderation, small as it is, is enormous in its ecological consequences. The crevice fauna is different from the surface fauna. It is adapted to a different thermodynamics, a different moisture regime, a different quality of darkness.

The things in the crevices have longer bodies. This is not universal but it is a tendency, because length is useful when you live in a plane rather than a volume, when your world is two dimensions with the third severely constrained. Some are white, or whitish — the grey not necessary here, where the light does not reach, where no one is looking. The whiteness is not adaptation toward whiteness so much as the relaxation of the cost of pigmentation, the slow letting-go of the grey-making machinery when grey-making is no longer required. They are white the way things in caves are white: absence of pressure, not presence of purpose.

There are beetles in the crevices. Very small beetles with very long antennae, their antennae being the primary organs of a world that is mostly dark and mostly feel. Their eyes are present but reduced — not absent, not yet, the plateau has not had them long enough for that, or perhaps the dim grey light that filters in from the crevice opening is still sufficient to maintain minimal visual function. They move through the dark corridors between stone faces with a certainty that is the certainty of a creature that knows its territory at the scale of millimeters, that has mapped every pebble, every mineral crystallization, every sticky patch of moisture on the fracture face.

The beetles eat the things smaller than themselves and are eaten by the things that can reach them. What can reach them: a spider, long-legged enough to navigate the crevice width, flat-bodied, which has been in these crevices long enough that its body profile is a precise template of the available space — flatten it and you have the dimensions of the gap it hunts in. The spider is the grey of the inner stone, which is a darker grey than the outer stone, and its eyes are large, forward-facing, adapted to the minimal light of the crevice's interior. It sees in what a person would call total darkness. It does not experience it as total.

The spiders know each other. This is not affection. But the crevice is a bounded space and the spiders in it have been in proximity long enough that they have chemical maps of their neighbors' territories, chemical autobiographies of who is where and what size and what condition. They do not encroach. The boundaries are invisible and absolute and maintained without drama, because drama costs something and the plateau's accounting is very old.

## The Sedge, and What It Hosts

The brown sedge in the moister cracks is not merely a plant. It is a structure.

Its root system — fine, persistent, interlocking with other sedge roots and with the lichen-adjacent crusts and with the mineral-leeched soil that has accumulated in the crack over the sedge's tenure, which is very long — is a three-dimensional habitat in its own right. Things live among the roots that do not live anywhere above the root zone, because the root zone offers something the surface cannot: stability. Not temperature stability, not chemical stability, but *structural* stability. A place that is the same shape it was last season, and the season before, and the season before that, going back far enough that whatever is living in it now arrived into an environment that was already established and fitted its life history to the available architecture.

Root-dwelling mites, different from the lichen mites. Fungal hyphae — the fungi of the plateau are not the mushroom-fruiting kind, or not primarily; they are the kind that fruit underground or not at all, their whole existence in the dark of the root zone, breaking down and reconstituting, taking minerals from the stone particles the roots touch and passing them in exchange for carbon, a deal that the sedge entered into before it had anything like a name and that it renews every year by growing new root hair, which is immediately colonized, which is the point. The fungi and the sedge are one metabolism. They do not know it in the way that knowing is usually understood. But the arrangement knows itself, in the way that very stable arrangements know themselves.

Insects overwinter in the sedge root-base. Not insects as a category — specific species, species whose life history requires the exact microclimate of this exact root-base in this exact crack during winter, whose larvae will not survive anywhere else, who have made the sedge their overwintering architecture with the same inevitability that the sedge has made the crack its. The larvae are white, grub-shaped, breathing so slowly in the cold that monitoring their respiration requires equipment. They are not dead. They are doing something that the plateau's accounting system approves of: using almost nothing, waiting, being patient in the way that things become patient when the alternative is not-persisting.

In what passes for summer, the sedge flowers. The flower is small, brown, wind-pollinated, structurally minimal. It is honest in its minimalism. It does not advertise because there is nothing on this plateau that requires advertising to, no bright-eyed insect seeking sweetness, no bird looking for color. What pollinates it does so because it is moving anyway, because the slow drift of the plateau's own air carries pollen from one sedge to another as part of a general movement that the sedge has learned to exploit. The sedge does not perform. It simply places its pollen in the drift and waits.

## The Seep Community

The seep is the most alive place on the plateau and it announces this by being the greenest place on the plateau, which means it is the only place that is unmistakably *green* rather than grey-green or green-tending or possibly-green. The green at a seep is the green of a different world briefly made available. Standing at a seep, you stand at the junction of two different regimes.

The seep community is entirely different from the plateau community a meter away. Not just more numerous but differently organized, differently paced, differently chemical. The water here has been underground in the fracture networks, picking up mineral signatures that the surface cannot access, and it arrives at the surface carrying those signatures and distributing them. The organisms that have evolved to live in the seep zone have evolved to process those specific signatures, to run their metabolisms on that specific chemistry, and removing them from it — even moving them two meters away to a rock face that looks identical but lacks the seep — is removing them from their world in a way that nothing in their evolutionary history has equipped them to survive.

Bryophytes first. Mosses and liverworts, because bryophytes can get to new water before anything else, their spores arriving before most others, their tolerances wide enough to colonize a new seep from first appearance. But this seep is not new. This seep has been here for a long time, and what it hosts is not the colonist community but the community that colonists become when the colonizing is over and the long work of specialization has begun.

The mosses at the seep margin are old in the sense of their lineage being old in this place. Their morphology has been modified by this seep's specific water, its specific chemistry, its specific patterns of flow — which stone surface it runs down, which direction the prevailing drift comes from, how many days per season it runs versus seeps versus is barely present. The mosses know this pattern in their bodies. Their bodies are the accumulated answer to this seep's accumulated question.

Living in the mosses: copepods. This is the improbable thing, the thing that seems wrong until it becomes right. Copepods — crustaceans, ancestors of ocean creatures, here on a plateau in the moss film of a seep that runs for eight feet before being absorbed — navigating the thin water layer in the moss by the same mechanisms their ocean cousins use to navigate open water, scaled down, the great open-water swimming reduced to threading through moss architecture, but the same mechanisms, the same antennae-reading of current, the same light-sensitivity, the same molt sequence. They arrived in a water body that no longer exists, that was here when the plateau's drainage was different, that dried or changed, and they did not leave because there was nowhere for them to go, and they found the seep and the seep was adequate, and they have been in the seep and seeps like it since the water body disappeared.

They carry the ocean inside them. They do not know this.

Around the copepods: the larvae of chironomid midges, thin red filaments half-buried in the moss, processing the organic matter that the moss produces and the seep deposits. They will become midges. The midges will swarm, briefly — the briefest thing on the plateau, the most urgent, the most frenetic, a frantic few hours in the slightly-warmer period of the plateau's summer cycle, dancing above the seep in the numbers required to find each other in a landscape where the distances between seeps are very large and the guarantee of meeting is not guaranteed. They swarm and mate and deposit eggs and then they do not do anything. The whole adult life of the midge, which is not eating (it cannot eat, it has no mouthparts for eating, the adult midge exists only to produce the next generation of larvae), is a matter of days. All the rest of the midge's year is the larva, patient in the moss, red as the hemoglobin it uses to extract oxygen from barely-oxygenated water. Red is the color the plateau allows here, at the seep, briefly, for the hemoglobin. Red for oxygen. Everything concedes something to chemistry.

Around the seep, slightly away from it, in the transition zone where the moisture is occasional rather than continuous: beetles that are not the crevice beetles. Larger, darker, their elytra the grey of the outer stone. They come to the seep to drink — or rather, to absorb, through specialized leg pads that drink for the body — and they eat the midge adults when the midge adults swarm, and they return to the dry plateau afterward and are simply beetles on grey stone, invisible, indistinguishable from pebbles until they move.

A solitary spider at the seep edge. It has no web. It has never had a web — or its lineage has not had one for so long that the web-making glands have reduced to vestigial structures, which is the plateau's general solution to anything that costs resources and is no longer necessary. It hunts by waiting. It waits very still, in the way that only things that are very patient and have very long time horizons can wait still — not the stillness of holding a position against instinct, but the stillness of a creature for whom motion is the exception and waiting is the baseline state. It has been there long enough that the moss has grown slightly around its resting spot. It leaves. The moss holds its shape briefly, an impression, and then grows over it, and the shape is gone.

## What Moves Over the Stone

Now the larger things, the things the stone remembers in its worn surfaces.

They are arthropods, mostly. The plateau's productivity does not support the metabolic demands of endothermy at any scale — nothing warm-blooded persists here permanently, though things warm-blooded pass through, stop briefly, move on. The permanent fauna is cold, its metabolic rate set by the temperature of the stone it rests on, which is the temperature of the plateau's mean, which is very low. This is not poverty. This is efficiency of a kind that warm-blooded metabolism cannot achieve: the cold body requires almost nothing to maintain itself, and almost nothing is what the plateau has to give, and so they are perfectly matched, the cold bodies and the cold stone, in a matching so old that *matched* is no longer the right word. They are the same thing read from different angles.

The largest are the stone-colored beetles, the ones whose route-taking over the plateau surface has polished the rock. They are not large in absolute terms — the length of a thumb, the width of two fingers — but they are the megafauna of this system, the top of the productivity pyramid, the creatures that move through the most space and eat the most things. They are generalists by the plateau's standards, which means they eat lichen, and seep-zone deposits, and dead insects, and each other's eggs when they find them, and occasionally each other. They do not eat each other when alive if they are the same size, because the costs of conflict here are not recovered — the wound, the lost leg, the energy expended — and the accounting system is too old and too precise to permit unprofitable transactions.

Their movement is the movement that made the polished paths. Individually they are small. But there are very many of them, and they have been going the same routes for long enough that the routes are *routes* now, not preferences but inevitabilities — the slightly lower friction of the worn path draws the next beetle slightly toward it, which wears it slightly more, which draws the next beetle, which has been going on since before the current landscape was its current self. They did not plan the paths. The paths are what patience makes when applied at scale across geological time.

They communicate through chemistry. Pheromone trails on the stone surface — not volatile pheromones, which the plateau's cold air would disperse before they were useful, but contact pheromones, deposited at each step, lasting on the stone surface in the cold for days. Walking a beetle-path means walking a library. Means reading, with each step, who was here and when and in what condition. The beetles read this and adjust: turning toward the trail that is recent and well-used, turning away from the trail that goes quiet, which may mean predator (there are predators, there are always predators) or may mean a seep has shifted.

They have compound eyes that are very large relative to their body size. In the diffuse grey light, compound eyes that resolve to directional light-sensing rather than image-forming are adequate, and adequate is all the plateau requires. They see movement. They see the slightly-different grey of a seep zone. They see each other. This is enough. This has always been enough.

Below them in the size hierarchy: the fast hunters.

Small spiders, not the flat crevice spider or the seep spider, but open-surface hunters with long legs that hold the body high off the stone. The height is functional: the warmer air an inch above the stone, and the slightly wider visual field, and the reach. They hunt by visual detection at a distance, which requires the relatively large eyes they have, which are forward-facing and together rather than distributed around the head, because the stone-surfaced world they hunt in rewards binocular depth perception, rewards the ability to judge the distance to a prey item before committing the energy of a lunge. They lunge, they miss, they wait. They lunge, they hit, they process. The processing is quick, because prey on the plateau is not replaced quickly and is not to be wasted.

Their bodies are grey-brown, the legs slightly longer-grey, the abdomen slightly browner, the color matching depending on which direction you view from, which is the plateau's solution to the impossibility of matching both the stone and the sky at once: be closer to the stone when seen from above (predators from above would be birds that do not stay), be closer to the sky when seen from the side (what views from the side is other things at ground level, and the lighter grey of the sky seen between your legs is the plateau's ambient background). They are not invisible. They are the grey of not-immediately-resolving.

And the things that hunt the spiders: larger spiders, which are themselves hunted by the beetles, which are themselves hunted by the occasional visitor that does not stay.

The food web is short. Short food webs are the signature of low-productivity systems, of places where energy is scarce and therefore not permitted to move through many steps before being used, because each step loses something. The plateau is very efficient about this. It has had to be.

## The Seasonal Visitors

They come from below. They do not stay.

In the warmer part of the year — not warm, but warmer, the plateau's summer being the lower country's nothing-in-particular — certain birds cross or touch the plateau in transit, or come specifically to eat the midge swarms around the seeps, or come for reasons that are still being worked out.

They are not at home here. This is visible in the way they move. They are alert in a different way from the plateau's own creatures — the alertness of an animal in an environment it does not fully know, reading the landscape quickly, making decisions quickly, never settling into the deep familiarity that the resident fauna have with every surface. They eat fast. They move fast. They leave.

What they leave: droppings, which carry seeds from the lower country, most of which will not germinate, a few of which will germinate and not persist, and perhaps one of which, in a long enough time series, will germinate and persist and begin the very slow process of becoming a plateau plant rather than a visitor's deposit. The plateau has been colonized this way before. The record is in the stone. The record is in the biology. Not often, and not easily, but the plateau does sometimes add to itself in this way, in the way of places that are stable enough to absorb a new element without being destabilized by it.

Occasionally, in warm years — which the plateau defines differently from the lower country; a warm year on the plateau is an event measured in fractions of a degree — a small mammal comes up to the plateau edge and goes back down. It has tested the margin and found it not-adequate. This is the usual outcome. The plateau does not make it adequate. But the margin is tested, every year, by animals whose ancestors tested it, because the testing costs little and the reward, if the plateau were ever adequate, would be large: an entire landscape with no competitors, no predators, everything available to whoever can persist here. The plateau keeps this offer on the table. No one has yet found the terms acceptable.

## The Oldest Things

There are things in the deep lichen crust that have not moved in what a human would call a lifetime.

Not dead. Moving at rates that require different accounting. Growing at the outer margin by microns per year and dying at the inner margin at the same rate and in between neither growing nor dying in any way that changes the chemical record significantly.

They are ancient in a way that exceeds the category of ancient. The oldest individual lichen thallus on this plateau is not something that can be aged easily — lichen ages slowly and records its age in ways that require specialist equipment and significant uncertainty. But the oldest thalli, the ones that occupy the central positions on the most stable rock faces, the ones that have had time to develop the most complex internal structure and the most elaborate mineral-bloom margin, are old enough that the plateau around them has changed in ways that their bodies have accommodated: the rock they grew on has been uplifted and tilted slightly by the deep tectonic processes that the plateau's geological time makes visible; the lichen has grown in response to this, one margin expanding faster than the other, the whole thallus slowly rotating its axis of growth to track whatever is slightly better, which is how a lichen tracks very slow geological change, because it has to track it, because the alternative is not tracking it.

The lichen is so old it has its own geological record. Its body is a layer in the stone's surface story.

Inside it: the mites, the nematodes, the tardigrades, the things that evolved specifically for this thallus, that have been here long enough that removing them from it would not just kill them but erase a lineage that exists nowhere else. The tardigrades here are not generic tardigrades. They are this-thallus tardigrades. Their proteins fold slightly differently than the tardigrades in the seep moss. They have been here long enough to diverge. They are in the process of becoming something that does not have a name yet, that may not have a name until they have diverged further, that the plateau is currently making in its patient way, without urgency, without agenda, simply as the outcome of time and the accumulation of slight differences in the isolated population.

This is how new things are made here. Not by drama, not by catastrophe, not by sudden change. By the plateau's own method: time, accumulation, patience, the slow divergence of things that are kept separate long enough that separate becomes different.

## What It Knows

The plateau knows nothing. This needs to be said. The plateau has no mind. The vast biological machinery that runs it has no unified cognition, no experience of running it, no sense of itself as a system.

But the system has *information*, in the technical sense: it carries, in its many parts, the record of what has worked and what has not worked across the full span of its operation. Every body shape on this plateau is a piece of solved problem. Every chemical exchange is a piece of solved problem. Every route that the beetles wear into the stone is a piece of solved problem. The plateau is made of solved problems stacked on top of each other for long enough that the stacking is now the landscape, and the landscape is running the solutions continuously, and the solutions are each other's environment, and the whole is stable in the way that only very old, very tested, very refined things are stable.

It is not wisdom. But it is what wisdom looks like from outside.

## What They Are

They are the grey of the stone. They are the grey of the sky. They are everything that did not leave when leaving was possible and then was no longer possible and then was no longer a category, because you cannot leave a place that has become what you are.

They are patient in the way that does not know it is patience. The mite in the lichen does not wait. It is simply there, and there is enough, and there has been enough for long enough that *enough* is what *there* means.

They are the plateau's own creatures. Not adapted to it — they are past the metaphor of adaptation, which implies an original form that was then modified. They are original here. The plateau made them. They made the plateau. The stone they live on is the stone they have been making from older stone for longer than the category of *original* can usefully reach.

They are quiet. This has been said. But the quietness is not absence. The quietness is what you get when everything that needs to be communicated is already known, when the body knows the stone and the stone knows the body, when the long work of learning a place is finished — not finished in the sense of concluded, but finished in the sense of having reached a state that sustains itself, that no longer requires the energy of novelty, that runs on the deep fuel of the established and the continuous and the very, very old.

They are the sound the plateau makes. They are the sound that is not quite silence but is the sound of continuing, which is the only sound the plateau has ever made, and which it will keep making, in grey and cold and patience, for as long as stone is stone and the slow drift carries the plateau's own moisture to the plateau's own surface and something is there to receive it.

Something is always there to receive it.
