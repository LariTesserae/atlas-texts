# What Lives Here

---

Start with what lives here the way you started with the floor: by crouching down, by putting your palm against it and feeling it give.

---

**The Beneath**

First, the water.

It would be wrong to say the water lives here, because living implies a direction — toward something, away from something, with a preference. The water below the floor has no preference. It has been moving in its one direction since before the floor was laid over it and it will move in that direction after the last reed decays and the last cane-knot gives way. It is not slow because it is lazy. It is slow because slowness is not a quality it possesses but a quality it *is*. It has no awareness of the floor above it, no sense of being covered or hidden. It does not know it is dark. It does not relate to the word navigable. It moves. That is enough. That has always been enough.

Whatever lives in it, if anything lives in it, lives in the way things live in places that don't register their presence: without ceremony, without surface, finned perhaps, pale probably, feeling the water's temperature gradient as the only calendar available and finding it sufficient.

---

**The Woven Things**

Then, the weave itself.

This is more complicated than it looks, and it looks complicated.

The weave is not alive, but it is inhabited by a distributed organism that arrived when the first water seeped up through the first gap between reeds and was trapped for long enough to become useful. A biofilm, thin as a thought, covering the underside of every reed where moisture collects. It is green-black in color where it exists in any thickness, and elsewhere it is invisible. It doesn't do much, the biofilm, but it does one important thing: it softens the cane from beneath, very gradually, and the weave presses back against this softening with the slow mechanical argument of compression and tension, and this dialogue between the living film and the made structure is one of the reasons the floor feels like something more than floor. Something in the middle register between made and grown. The biofilm does not know it is contributing to this quality. It is simply eating what is available and releasing what it must.

The red beetles are of this floor. Not visiting. Not passing through. They have lived here for enough generations that the specific amber of the cane-fiber is the color their eyes are tuned to. They can detect a split reed at the distance of several meters. Whether they are drawn to the split reed because something edible gathers there, or because something in the beetle's behavior has become structural over time — a slow replacement of instinct with utility — is a question the beetle does not consider. The beetle finds the split place. The beetle investigates. Sometimes it lays eggs there, in the soft wet interior of the damaged cane, and the larvae eat what the crack contains, and the floor heals around them, or doesn't, and either way there are more beetles.

What the beetles know: wet, dry, the chemical signature of mold, the chemical signature of each other, the warmth of the floor's surface, the slightly greater warmth of the shallow standing pools. What the beetles do not know: that they are part of what maintains the floor, that their investigating is a form of attention, that the place would be different without them, that they are old here. The floor knows. The floor doesn't tell.

---

**The Vines**

The pale green vine, threading itself between the reeds, finding the gaps: this thing is one thing. This is not obvious. It looks like many things — a groundcover, a colony, an opportunistic scatter — but if you could trace the stems back to their origins, you would find fewer origins than expected. Perhaps one. Perhaps the whole living mat of it, several miles of pale branching, is one organism that has been expanding since it found the first foothold in the weave, pressing its pale fingers into every gap, not strangling, not competing — the vine is not aggressive — but simply going where there is space and staying there.

It grows toward the drips.

Not toward light, because the light is everywhere and therefore it is not anywhere in particular. Toward water. The vine has learned — over generations, through the slow accumulation of differential survival, through nothing that looks like learning — that the places under the broad leaves are the places where water arrives on a schedule. It clusters there. It is dense under the drip-points and thinner in between, and the thinner sections are the old sections, the sections that grew when the broad-leafed plants weren't yet here, and the thicker sections are the new sections, the growing edge of the organism's understanding of this particular floor.

The vine has, distributed across its full extent, a sensitivity to tension. Pull a section of it and something happens several meters away — a subtle reorientation, a slowing of growth in one direction, a slight acceleration in another. It cannot be said to feel. But something propagates. Something is communicated through the tissue of it that changes what the tissue does. If you sat near the vine long enough, you would notice that it is always moving, and that the movement is responsive, and that the things it responds to are the things that matter here: moisture, warmth, the subtle vibration of a drip. In a different place, in a place with urgency, the vine might be called intelligent. Here it is just very good at being a vine, very thoroughly a vine, more completely a vine than most vines manage to become.

---

**The Spiders**

The spiders who have built between the structures: these are not one kind but several, and they share only the location and a quality of patience so thoroughgoing it reads as temperamental rather than tactical.

The largest of them — gray-brown, the color of the structural cane itself, with a body the size of a thumb — sits at the center of a web strung between two upright posts. The web is large. Architecturally, it has incorporated one of the swaying woven panels as a backstop, and the geometry of it is not the perfect radial geometry of a textbook web but something more pragmatic, adapted to the angles available, to the slight irregularity of the posts' lean. It was built over several days. During the building, the spider was fully itself — the same spider the whole time, purposeful in the same way, unhurried in the same way — and now that the building is done, it has installed itself at the center and is being fully the same spider at rest. The stillness of sitting and the stillness of building are the same stillness.

It catches what the place provides. In a place like this, that is a lot. The humid air collects in the web as it collects on the leaves, and small things — the small iridescent hoverers, a gnat-like creature in clouds too fine to see individually, the occasional disoriented beetle — find themselves in it. The spider does not rush. There is no need to rush. The web will catch the next thing and the thing after that and the spider will eat at the pace the place offers and will find the pace sufficient, as everything here finds its conditions sufficient, as the conditions seem to have been arranged in advance for sufficiency.

What the spider knows is spatial and vibrational. It has a map of the web, precise as engineering, and every variation from the map's expected tension is a message. The messages are: food, or not food. Repair needed, or not yet. Almost everything here is *not yet*. The spider lives in a long succession of not-yets with occasional meals and the slow replacement of silk and the eventual production of an egg sac that she will install in a corner of the structural frame where the cane overlaps and guard with the same quality of total stillness she brings to everything else. She will not guard it urgently. Urgency does not live here.

---

**The Birds**

The birds sitting on the tops of the tall reeds: they have come and they will leave and they will come again. They are not from the floor exactly. They belong to some larger circuit that includes the floor and includes other places, and they arrive here at intervals that are not random but that are not simple enough to be called regular. They arrive and they sit and they are very still and then they move with total commitment — a repositioning, a plunge, a brief disappearance below the surface of a shallow pool — and then they are still again.

They eat small things from the shallow pools. The small things are themselves a whole community: transparent crustaceans, a thumb-length fish with silvery sides, the larvae of insects that will become the small iridescent hoverers. The pool community lives in the pool's specific conditions — warm, still, extremely shallow, lit from above with the same sourceless white that lights everything — and has organized itself around the constraints and gifts of the pool. The crustaceans eat the biofilm that grows on the pool floor. The fish eat the crustaceans. The birds eat the fish. And the birds' droppings fall onto the woven floor and are absorbed by the vine and by the weave itself and the nutrients return below where the biofilm receives them and grows.

This is not a small thing, this cycle. This is the metabolism of the place.

The birds know nothing about this. They know: still water means fish. They know the shallow pools by the shine of them, by the accumulated memory of previous visits that has become, over generations, a kind of knowledge they were born with. They know each other — there is a loose hierarchy among the birds of similar species that shares a pool, worked out in the subtle language of posture and the timing of stillness. They know the note they make — one of them makes it every few minutes, the irregular-regular note you heard — as a maintenance of presence, a low-cost announcement: *I am here, and I am not threatening you, and you are not threatening me, and we continue.* It is the minimum social gesture. It is, perhaps, the appropriate social gesture for this place, where everything is at equilibrium and the news is always the same news.

---

**The Slow Animal**

The thing you glimpsed at the edge of your visibility, low to the floor, unhurried, which seemed already at its destination by the time you fixed your attention on it.

It is here. It has always been here, in the sense that the floor has always been here, the sense in which things that outlast memory become permanent. It is large — larger than the low-to-the-floor suggested — but it keeps itself horizontal, keeps itself at the floor's level, and so appears smaller than it is. It has a broad head, gray-green, the same gray-green as the bark of the trees with the soft-looking bark, which is either coincidence or not coincidence.

It does not move often. When it does, it moves with the quality of something that has calculated that movement is acceptable. The calculation is not quick. It may have been underway for some time before the movement occurred. And when it arrives at its new location, it does not arrive panting. It arrives as if it has been there, considering.

It eats the vine, in places. Not aggressively. There are sections of vine, here and there, that end in clean cuts, and the cuts are old, and the vine has grown around them, and this is the slow animal's work from some previous occasion. The vine does not avoid the slow animal. The slow animal does not pursue the vine. They have an arrangement that is older than either of them.

What is the slow animal? It is the thing that was here before the floor was finished, that watched the floor being made from whatever counted as a shoreline then, and eventually walked out onto it when the floor was solid enough, and found it good, and stayed. It is the measure of the floor's age. It is the only thing here that could tell you, if it were capable of telling, how long the floor has been floor.

It is not capable of telling. It sits on the warm woven surface and it is warm and the light is on it from all directions and it does not comment on any of this. It has been not commenting for a long time. It is very good at not commenting. When you look at it directly, if you ever manage to look at it directly, it looks back. It does not look away. It also does not tell you what it knows. It continues looking. You continue looking. Eventually one of you looks away, and it will not be the slow animal.

---

**You**

You are sitting on the floor.

This is worth including. The place has registered you — not with alertness, not with alarm, but in the way a system registers a new variable: something has changed in the inventory. The vine, over the next several days, will grow very slightly toward where you sat, following the warmth-differential your body created in the surface of the weave. The beetle nearest you has changed its path by a small degree that will aggregate, over many beetle-lengths, into a significant displacement from where it would have been. The spider has noted the new tension in the web introduced by the sound of your breathing and has been waiting to see if it resolves into something, and it hasn't, and she has resumed not resolving it into something, which is its own kind of resolution.

The birds have noticed you and have not changed what they're doing.

The slow animal noticed you before you noticed the slow animal and has not changed what it is doing, which is: being here, in the same way it has been here, in the same way everything is here, which is to say: as if arrival were the thing that happened and staying were simply what arrival means.

---

The floor does not comment either way.

But everything on the floor comments, continuously, in the only language available: the drip-language, the still-water-language, the language of the beetle's altered path and the vine's slow turning and the single bird-note at its irregular-regular interval. The comment is not addressed to you. It was not waiting for you. It will not stop when you leave.

It says: *here, here, here, here.* It says it at the speed things are said here, which is the speed of accumulation, of pattern, of a depth that was never announced and is therefore everywhere. It has been saying it since before the floor had edges to speak of. The floor is still, perhaps, being built, at the edges no eye reaches honestly. The saying continues. The drip falls. The water, below everything, neither knows nor needs to know what it is holding up.
