# What Lives in the Drowned City at the Edge of Waking

---

## The First Answer, Which Is Incomplete

The city lives here.

This is not a figure of speech and it is not mysticism. It is the plainest available answer, the one the place itself would give if it gave answers in language rather than in water pressure and shadow direction and the slow molecular decisions of warm stone. The city is the primary inhabitant. Everything else — if there is anything else — is secondary, is perhaps a sub-expression of the city's own living, is the way a body's dreams are secondary to the body and yet unmistakably alive in themselves.

The city has metabolism. It takes in water and moves it and releases it and takes it in again. It generates heat from stored energy — calories of sunlight absorbed in some era when there was sunlight, held in the dense porous stone the way fat holds calories, burned so slowly that the burning is indistinguishable from simply being warm, from being the kind of thing that has warmth. It maintains itself. Maintenance is a biological function. Maintenance is what separates a living system from a ruin, and this is not a ruin, and the maintenance requires no visible agent, which means either an invisible agent or a city that has internalized the work of its own continuation so completely that the work no longer resembles work.

The city breathes, too. The mist that collects above it — that thick suspension that is not cloud and not ceiling — this is exhalation. Water taken in at the channels, moved through the pipes, processed through whatever darkness lives in the depths of the pools, and returned to the air as vapor, as the warm wet atmosphere that the city requires, that the city is constantly regenerating. The city makes its own climate. It breathes and the breath becomes the sky it breathes under.

So: the city lives here. But this is not all.

## The Water

The water that lives here is not the same water that lives elsewhere.

Elsewhere, water is a substance — responsive, patient, shaped by containers, shaped by gravity, the thing that takes the form of whatever holds it. Here the water is something more like a condition, a permanent state that the city participates in rather than a resource the city manages. The water has been here longer than the city. The water will be here after. The city was built into the water, the way a reef is built into the sea — not against it, not despite it, but using it as the fundamental medium, treating it as the logic that makes building possible rather than the force that building must resist.

And the water is continuous. This is the central fact about it, the fact that explains everything about the pools with their impossible depth, the light from below, the sense of transit through the pools at angles orthogonal to the surface. The water here is all one water. The warm thin film on your skin is connected, through the stone, through the pipes, through the porous bricks that have become indistinguishable from wet earth, to the water in the channels, to the water in the pools, to the water far below the pools where the light is coming from. Not connected in the sense of shared composition, not merely H₂O throughout — connected in the way that a body of water is connected to itself, all of it knowing in whatever way water knows, all of it engaged in whatever it is the water here is doing.

What the water is doing is something like *attending*. This is the closest word. The water attends to the city. It moves through the city's walls and floors and takes a slow inventory, notes the state of each brick and pipe and channel, carries that information in its composition the way blood carries information — the chemistry of one wall transmitted to the next, the warmth of a deep pool gradually communicated upward, the slow readings of the whole system accumulated in the water that runs through all of it. The water knows the city the way blood knows a body. Not in language. Not in image. In chemistry, in pressure, in the distribution of warmth and mineral and slow-dissolved stone.

And what does it do with what it knows? It does what water does: it keeps moving. The knowing is not stored. The knowing is the moving. The knowledge and the motion are the same thing, and this is why the water in the channels has never stopped, will never stop, because the city would not know itself without it, would lose internal communication, would become a collection of parts rather than a system, would become, in the only sense that matters here, dead.

The light in the water is the water thinking. This is speculative but it is the most honest available description of what you see when you look into the deep pools: a luminescence that is cognitive rather than photonic, that is the glow of a very large slow process rather than the emission of a particular particle. The water is considering something enormous and has been considering it for a very long time.

## The Pale Plants

The plants that live here are not plants in the ordinary sense, though they are certainly vegetable, certainly organisms that grow and exude and occupy space and participate in exchanges with their environment. They are plants the way the city is a city — accurately described by the word, but transformed by their circumstances into something that requires the word to stretch.

They live in the gaps. In the cracks. On the rubble that has forgotten it was walls. At the margins of the channels. They do not compete for space — there is no competition visible, no choking out, no reaching toward limited light — because light is not what they are organized around. They are organized around wet and warm, and wet and warm is everywhere, and there is no scarcity here, no pressure gradient that would produce competition. There is only the distributed richness of a warm wet dark, and the plants have spread through it the way a hand spreads in water: slowly, between the resistances, taking what shape the space allows.

Their color is the color of the underside of things. The specific white that belongs to roots and tubers and cave fish and the flesh of deep mushrooms — not absence of color but withdrawal of color, the color that results from never having met the frequencies of visible light that would create pigment, that would offer evolutionary benefit to pigment. They are legible as a kind of nakedness. They show what living things look like when there is nothing to perform for, no sun whose attention to attract, no signal to send to pollinators who navigate by color. These plants have nothing to say to the visible spectrum and have stopped saying anything.

They weep because they must. The clear fluid at the tips of their leaves — the slow rhythmic drip that falls into the channels and joins the larger water and becomes part of the city's circulation — this is not exactly water, or not only water. It is what the plants have made of what they have absorbed, processed through whatever biochemistry attends to a vegetable thing in a warm wet dark, and returned in altered form. The plants are one part of a cycle. They take up something and return something and what comes back is not what went in — it has passed through the plant, been changed by the plant, and is now carrying whatever the plant has contributed: metabolic products, complex molecules, the specific signature of a living thing's chemistry. The plants are signing the water. Annotating the water. Making the water more complicated.

Over a very long time — and there has been very long time here — the plants' contributions accumulate in the water, and the water becomes richer, and richer water grows better plants, and better plants annotate more richly, and the system elaborates itself slowly in the dark. The plants are not decorative. The plants are doing something specific and important. They are, in some sense, writing.

They grow from rubble, preferentially. This is significant. The rubble is old-city, is the collapsed buildings of earlier iterations, and the plants put their roots into the collapsed and extract from it and transform it and what they release goes into the channels, and the channels carry it under the living city, and the living city absorbs it through its foundations, and so the earlier city returns to the later city through the medium of the plants. The plants are a recycling system for what has already been. The plants are how the past re-enters the present. The past does not merely influence the city from below as substrate; it circulates through it constantly, transformed, in the slow vegetable work of organisms that grow in ruins and weep.

## What Passes Through the Pools

There is something that uses the water as a hallway. That passes through the deep pools at angles orthogonal to the surface, neither ascending nor descending by the ordinary measure of ascent and descent, moving from elsewhere to elsewhere along lines that the surface world does not contain.

This thing — or these things, it is impossible to say how many — are not visible directly. They are visible as the pools register them: a change in the light from below, a temporary brightening as something passes through the brightest deep point, a slow eddy that forms after the passing and dissipates over minutes. You can see the effect. The effect is conclusive. Something large enough to displace the light, large enough to create a wake in the deep water, is moving through.

It does not break the surface. It is not interested in the surface. The city above is not where it is going; the city above is not where it is from. It is using a passage that runs below and through the pools, using the water as a medium through which it can move, and the city is incidental — or not incidental, because the city built the pools and the pools are the openings to the passage and therefore the city enabled this transit, but incidental in the sense that the thing passing through is not here for the city, is here for the passage.

What is it? It is large. Not enormously large in any one dimension, but large in the way that a thought is large — filling the space available to it, bounded by the medium, taking the shape of the passage. It is not hostile, which is clear from the way the water responds to it: the water does not recoil. The water receives it, parts for it, closes after. The city does not register the transit as an intrusion. Whatever is moving through the deep water is a known quantity. It is a regular — or at least not unprecedented — visitor. It has been coming through for long enough that the city has incorporated it into its understanding of itself, long enough that the pools were perhaps built in part to accommodate it, the broad central pool with its warm bright depth perhaps specifically sited, specifically shaped, to allow passage of exactly this thing.

It may be that the thing passing through and the city are very old acquaintances. It may be that the city was built here, in this particular dark and wet location, because this passage existed — because something worth being near was already moving through this place before the first brick of dark dense porous material was pressed and fired and set into a wall. The city as a monument to a transit route. The city built at a crossroads that happens to be subaqueous, built by inhabitants who wanted proximity to the moving thing, who built their channels and pipes and bowl-roofed cisterns in order to maintain the pool that was the reason for the city's existence.

The inhabitants are gone. The transit continues.

## What Made the City

The inhabitants — whatever made the city, whatever the city was built for — lived here once and made themselves the least durable part of what they made. This is not unusual. It is in fact typical of the best builders: to make things that will outlast the maker, to embed the logic of living into stone and channel and pipe so thoroughly that the stone and channel and pipe continue to perform the logic even after the one who held the logic in a body is gone. To build so well that the building continues to be a building without you.

What can be inferred about them?

They were small. The scale of the city — the doors, the corridors, the height of the preserved structures — implies inhabitants who were roughly the size of a human being or a little smaller. The channels are cut for a flow sufficient to serve a population of that scale. The rooftop cisterns are sized to water a household rather than a village. The infrastructure is for persons, for embodied beings who required water and shade and passageways between structures.

They understood systems deeply. Not engineered systems, not systems of metal and calculation, but organic systems — the system of water moving through a porous landscape, the system of heat storage and slow release, the system by which a city can be built to be wet without being overwhelmed, to be hot without being uninhabitable. They understood these things through long practice and careful attention rather than through abstraction, through living inside the system and learning its patterns the way you learn the patterns of someone you live with, through proximity and duration.

They were not interested in height. Every civilization of builders reaches for height eventually — it is nearly an inevitability, the dream of the tall structure, the tower that demonstrates mastery over vertical space. These builders did not. They stayed low, deferred to the sky in their own way, and the way they deferred was by building as a supplicant to water defers — with their faces turned toward the source, their rooftops shaped to receive, their streets designed to acknowledge and incorporate and channel rather than resist. Their relationship to the elements was not dominance but negotiation, and the negotiation was long and apparently successful and produced a city that is still standing.

They knew what was in the pools. They built beside the pools knowing what was in them, knowing what passed through. Whether this knowing was worship or commerce or simple familiarity — whether they built here in order to be near something holy, or in order to facilitate something practical, or simply because their ancestors had built here and the question of why was no longer asked — is impossible to determine. But the knowing is in the construction: in the placement of the pool, in the absence of any barrier between the pool and the city's channels, in the way the infrastructure of the city converges on the water without guarding it, without fencing it off, without treating the deep luminous passages below as a hazard to be managed. They considered the deep transit a neighbor, perhaps. A constant. They built their lives beside it the way you build a life beside a river — with adaptation, with a particular attentiveness to the moods of the medium, with a deep incorporation of its patterns into your own.

They are gone. Not recently. The absence has settled, has found its level, like the silt. The city has had time to accept the absence, to incorporate it, to continue anyway. The city runs without them the way a heart habit persists after the thought that created it is forgotten — not as memory but as pattern, not as continuation of something consciously maintained but as the sheer inertia of a system too intricate and too long-established to fail simply because its originators have stopped attending.

What became of them is a question the city does not answer. Not because it is hiding the answer. Because it does not experience the question. To the city, there were inhabitants and then there were not, and this is a condition of its history, as the warmth in the stone is a condition of its history, and the water and the plants and the slow passage of something enormous through the deep pools are conditions of its history, and all of it persists together in the dark, breathing, running, weeping, passing through.

## The Oldest Inhabitant

Below the pools, older than the water, older than the city, older than whatever built the city and has since become absence —

There is something that generates the light.

It has been here for the entire duration. Before the first brick. Before the first channel. Before whatever early being found this hot wet dark place and decided that organization was possible here, was worth attempting. Before any of that, the light was already in the deep water, already emanating from its source, already being the reason that this specific darkness is never entirely dark.

What it is cannot be said in any language that resolves to image or narrative. It is not a creature — or it is a creature in the way that an ocean is a creature, in the way that deep geological time is a creature, in the way that a process which has been occurring continuously for longer than complex life has existed might constitute a form of life in itself, if life is understood as self-sustaining process rather than as a thing with edges and a face.

It is not the source of the water, but it is the reason the water here behaves differently from water elsewhere. The water has been in contact with it for so long that the water has taken on some quality of it. The luminescence is not light the water is producing but light the water has absorbed from something that produces it in the depths and has been producing it for longer than the concept of production can hold. The water glows the way iron glows when it has been heated: not generating light but holding it, temporarily, as something absorbed from a source that burned hotter and longer.

It is not conscious. Or: consciousness is not the right word for what it is. Consciousness implies a thing that experiences itself as subject, that has something like interiority, that is aware of the passing of time as the passing of something. This thing does not experience time as passing. It experiences time as accumulating — or it does not experience time at all and simply *is* the accumulation, is the fact of so much duration in one place, is what a very long time feels like from the inside if a very long time has an inside, if the very long time has slowly become aware of itself through the sheer mass of its own continuation.

The city is its expression. Or the city is a consequence of its presence, which is not the same thing but resembles it. Whatever causes the deep light to emanate, whatever draws the large thing through the passage below the pools, whatever made this hot dark place a place worth organizing —  this is the source. This is the reason.

It will remain when the city has finally become the water and the water has finally become the dark and the dark has finally become — what the dark finally becomes, which is whatever comes after a very long time of being dark in a warm wet place with something luminous at the bottom.

## Summary, Which Is Approximate

What lives here is a city that has become self-tending. Water that has become self-aware in some sub-cognitive sense, awareness distributed through the system rather than held in a single node. Plants that are the past being reincorporated, metabolized, returned as something richer. Something large and old that uses the deep water as transit and has been doing so since before the city existed. An absence where a civilization was, an absence so thoroughly integrated into the fabric of the place that it has become a presence — the ghost is not here, but the shape the ghost made while alive is still here, and the shape continues to function, continues to breathe and run its channels and maintain itself, continues to accommodate the transit of something it never fully understood and built its whole long life beside.

And at the center: the oldest thing. Not living in the city as the others live in it, not inhabiting a space or performing a function, not passing through. Simply present. The irreducible given. The thing you find when you follow the water all the way down, when you put your attention into the bright deep point of the central pool and let it descend, when you are willing to pass through the surface into the orthogonal space where the large things travel, when you go below even that, below the traffic of the passage, into the still deep where the light is not reflected and not produced but *is* —

The city is built on top of it.

Everything here is built on top of it.

It is not sleeping. Sleep is a temporary state, defined by contrast with waking. This is something more absolute: the permanent presence of something that was never not here and will never not be here, and that has become — through the long wet dark weight of being that — the first fact, the ground condition, the reason that in this particular place the water glows and the shadows don't agree and the streets fold back on themselves and time is both very short and very long and you cannot find the edge of the city by walking and the darkness shows you things and the heat is stored and patient and the city runs without anyone running it.

Here is what lives here: the city, the water, the pale weeping plants, the thing in the passage, the old absence and its still-functioning ghost, and below it all the first presence, the irreducible one, the one for which no word is adequate but toward which everything else in this place is slowly, continuously, inevitably oriented.

Like plants that have no light source but still grow with purpose.

Like water that has no gradient but still moves with direction.

Like a city that has no inhabitants but still, somehow, maintains itself.

Everything here is pointing.

The pool turns.
