# What Lives Here

---

## The Canopy Tenants

Begin with the simplest category and find that it immediately refuses to be simple.

The birds. There are more species than can be counted in a single visit, and most of them are doing the thing birds do in storms, which is not the thing birds do in calm: they are compressed into the densest vegetation, they are wedged into fork-junctions, they are facing into the wind at angles that look aerodynamically wrong and are aerodynamically correct, they are not singing. The silence of birds in the canopy is one of the things that gets lost under the roaring, one of the absences that would register only in retrospect, only when you had left and were reviewing the experience in a quiet room. The birds are present. They are not performing. They are surviving, which is its own performance, but the audience is not considered.

The herons are different. The herons are always different. They operate in storm with the same economy they bring to calm, and this is what makes them strange — not the fact of their patience but the universality of it, the way the patience does not increase or decrease with the severity of conditions, as though the heron has decided that patience is not a response to conditions but a property of the self, a decision made so long ago it is no longer recognizable as a decision. A heron standing in the shallows of the valley floor lake-that-is-becoming-a-lake does not look like an animal that is enduring. It looks like an animal that has correctly identified the essential nature of existence and has organized itself accordingly. The rain falls. The wind bends the willows in great arcs. The heron is still. This is not stubbornness. This is technique. The fish will move toward the stiller water, and the stiller water is near the heron.

Songbirds in the fern-understory: dozens of species, most of them small enough to navigate the fern-corridor as though it were a different world entirely, which it is. The fern-corridor at ground level is a calm world threaded through the violent one. The fronds intersect and overlap at a height of two meters and above that height is where the wind is, and below the fronds there are lanes, irregular green corridors, and small birds work these corridors at speed, almost never visible as more than a movement, a departure from stillness. Their feeding behaviors in storm are different from their feeding behaviors in calm. In storm they are purposeful in a way that borders on frantic, which may be a judgment colored by the observer's mammalian desire to see equanimity. They may simply be efficient. The insects they eat are less mobile in the cold and the wet, and efficiency does not require speed when the prey is also resting.

## The Things in the Water

The fish.

The fish deserve their own consideration because the fish are the valley's most complete expression of what it means to thrive in a place that most organisms would consider adversarial. The channel that has not yet decided on a single course, that braids and reunites across the valley floor — this channel is not stable in any seasonal sense. It runs high in storm, higher than the banks that haven't fully committed to being banks yet. It changes direction by increments. It carries sediment from the young hillsides in quantities that shift the substrate on timescales that should make colonization impossible. And yet there are fish, and the fish are not refugees from calmer water upstream but are here, are making here their business, are holding in the current with what appears effortless and is in fact constant.

They are silver-bodied and fast. The silver is not decorative but functional, the silver of something that must be visible as not-the-background, which is the best defense available in water that carries enough suspended sediment to be slightly opaque, where predation is a matter of profile rather than detail. In a clear stream, a silver fish is conspicuous. In a silty channel, a silver fish is a light source, and light-sources are ambiguous, and ambiguity is safety. Whether the fish arrived silver or became silver is a question that requires more time than the valley has had.

They school loosely in the slow pools that form on the inside curve of bends. This schooling is not the disciplined schooling of open-water fish but something more casual, a keeping-company, an awareness of neighbors without strong coordination. When a gust hits the water surface and disturbs it, the school disperses and regroups with the specific quality of something that has practiced a response so many times it has become reflex. The dispersal is fast. The regrouping is fast. The fish return to position in the slow pool and resume their holding, and the pool surface is already settling, and the ferns on the bank are already recovering from the gust, and the whole thing — the disturbance, the response, the recovery — lasts perhaps four seconds and affects nothing downstream.

Invertebrates in the channel substrate: stonefly larvae and mayfly larvae and things without common names that live in the sediment layer where the channel floor becomes uncertain, where the boundary between water and mud is a gradient rather than a surface. These animals are the base of the column and they do their work invisibly, which is to say they do it in the way that most of the world's actual work is done, in conditions that do not invite observation, in numbers that make the individual irrelevant. They are important in the aggregate. They convert the organic matter that comes off the hillsides — leaf fragments, bark chips, dissolved compounds from the decomposing wood that the storm continuously harvests from the canopy — into animal tissue, and the animal tissue becomes the fish, and the fish become the herons, and the herons become the stillness at the center of the storm. The invertebrates are not simple. Their nervous systems have just enough resolution to distinguish good substrate from bad substrate, oxygenated water from depleted water, and this is enough, and the fact that it is enough is one of the things the valley quietly demonstrates.

## Small Mammals in the Fern-Cover

They are numerous and seldom seen.

Voles, certainly, and their cousins — small-bodied, dense-furred, high-metabolism animals that eat constantly because they burn constantly, that tunnel through the base-layer of the fern-cover and the dead-fern accumulation below the live ferns, that maintain elaborate networks of runways barely wider than their own bodies through grass-root and soil and the matted fibrous underworld of the sedge-meadow. A vole in the open is a vole under active predation. A vole in the tunnel network is a vole going about its business, which is eating, and reproducing, and eating again, and occasionally dying of a cause that is not predation, though predation is the most common cause and they seem to have concluded that the correct response to this fact is to reproduce prolifically and die without apparent regret.

Their runways are complex in three dimensions. They go under the water table — not through standing water but through the wicking wet of the capillary zone, where the soil is saturated but not submerged — and the voles navigate by smell and by memory and by the tactile information from their whiskers in the dark, and they seem untroubled by the cold of the saturated soil, which is perhaps only because they are always in motion and motion is warmth. Their nests are in the driest spots available, which are not very dry: moss-lined chambers in the hollows under fallen branches, under the overturned root-masses of trees that fell before the valley was what it is now. The nests are warm with body heat and smell like animal and like wet plant-matter in roughly equal proportions.

The larger mammal whose movement the description suggests — the shiver in the fern that is too large to be a vole and too low to be a deer — is probably a muskrat working a margin between the sedge-meadow and the open water, or a mink in transit, or something else at the uncertain boundary between this place's present fauna and its eventual fauna. The valley is young. Species arrive. Some stay. The identities of the permanent residents have not yet been confirmed.

The deer come at the margins, at the edges where the valley floor begins to lift toward the young hillsides. They do not come far into the valley in storm — they are not adapted to the groundwater's ubiquity, to the substrate that does not hold weight cleanly — but they come to the fern-edges at dawn and at dusk when the wind briefly considers resting, and they eat the fern-tips and the willow-shoots in great quantity, with the unhurried focus of animals that have found adequate food and know it, that move from fern to fern with the deliberate rhythm of the satisfied. Their legs are wet to the knee even at the valley's margins. They seem not to notice. They shake their ears at the sound of something in the canopy and continue eating.

## What Cannot Be Categorized by Ordinary Ecological Method

There are organisms here that the preceding categories do not reach.

The fungi.

The fungi are the valley's actual nervous system, and this is not a metaphor in the way such statements usually are. The mycorrhizal networks in the soil of a forest this young and this wet and this productive are extensive beyond visualization — they form connections between root systems of different species, they move nutrients from areas of surplus to areas of deficit by mechanisms that are not well understood, they respond to damage in one part of the system by rerouting resources through other parts. Whether this constitutes something that can be called information-processing is a question the valley does not answer definitively. The valley goes about its business.

The fruiting bodies that emerge at the surface are only the smallest visible part of this system, and they emerge constantly here — the substrate is never dry, the temperature is rarely too cold, the organic matter available for decomposition is replenished continuously by the storm's harvest of the canopy. Bracket fungi on the alder trunks. Small brown caps rising from the fern-layer in clustered groups that appear after the heaviest rain and dissolve back into the substrate within days. Translucent things on fallen wood that look like they were made from the water rather than from the wood, that look wet in a way that is intrinsic rather than a consequence of rain. They are processing the valley. They are converting death to growth with a thoroughness and a patience that makes the herons look hasty.

The slime molds: transitional organisms, organisms that refuse categorization in ways that are instructive rather than merely inconvenient. In the wet and the dark at the valley floor they move — not quickly, not in any way that registers to the human eye in real time, but they move, and the movement is not random, it is the movement of an organism solving a problem, finding the efficient path between food sources, and this path-finding is so mathematically elegant that researchers have used slime mold growth patterns to model transit networks for human cities, have found that the slime mold and the engineer converge on the same solutions by entirely different methods. The slime mold in the valley is not doing this consciously. It is doing this the way the valley does everything: by being fully what it is, by following its own nature with complete commitment, and letting the results accumulate.

## The Things That Live In the Noise Itself

Not metaphorically.

There are insects whose entire adult existence is acoustically structured around sounds that the storm produces, that lay their eggs in wood softened by the specific moisture levels that storm-climate creates, that time their emergence to the barometric patterns of the approaching fronts, so that the storm is not merely context for their lives but is literally the calendar they live by. Storm-associated beetles. Storm-associated flies whose larvae develop in the temporary pools that form in the overturned bark sheets the wind flips from dead logs, pools that exist for a week and then dry and then refill and then freeze at the margin before the beetle arrives to reclaim the space and the larvae develop faster than seems possible, faster than the textbook says the species develops, as though this particular population has had occasion to practice speed.

There are spiders in the angles of the structures at the valley's edge. They do not build webs that the storm would tear. They have concluded — not in the way a deliberating creature concludes but in the way a lineage concludes over time, through the selective pressure of torn webs — that the lowland storm forest is not a web-building environment, and they hunt instead, moving over the hum-surfaces of the half-buried instruments with a precision that suggests they can feel the frequency through their legs and have incorporated it into their understanding of the landscape. The hum tells them something. It may tell them that the surface is warm relative to the surrounding air — the instruments generate heat, presumably — and warmth draws insects, and insects draw spiders. The technology and the spider have arrived at a working relationship.

## The Creatures of the Canopy Proper

The ones that live in the violence.

This is the part that requires attention.

The birds that actually inhabit the canopy during the storm — not the ones hiding from it but the ones that are at home in it — are a specialized category. There are crossbills, possibly, or their functional equivalents, birds with beaks evolved for the specific business of prying apart the cones and catkins of the alders and cottonwoods, birds that move through the canopy in the storm with the comfort of animals who have decided that wind is not a hazard but a medium. They track across the arc of a wind-thrown branch and continue feeding on the far side without apparent acknowledgment that the branch moved. Their grip is extraordinary. Their sense of where they are in space during violent movement is extraordinary. They are the size of a fist and they are flying in a storm that is tearing branches from trunks and they find this ordinary.

Certain corvids work the storm for what it shakes loose. This is the more obvious adaptation, the one that does not require a leap: a crow watching the storm tear the canopy is a crow watching a process that provides. Larvae exposed where bark is stripped. Seeds from cones that would have remained closed in dry weather. Small animals dislodged. The storm, from the crow's perspective, is not a disruption but a service. Crows do not need to think this consciously. Crows that worked the storm found food. Crows that sheltered from the storm found less food. And so the crows here work the storm, moving through the mid-canopy with the slanted sideways flight of animals compensating for crosswinds, landing, prying, departing in a direction that is only partly their own choice, landing again somewhere that is useful.

Their intelligence is present in the valley even when they are not visible. A crow that has worked a territory figures out the territory, builds a model of it that includes the locations of the instruments, includes the patterns of wind-down through the valley's specific geography, includes the particular logs and bark-sheets that yield larvae after specific rain intensities. This model is updated continuously. It is not a simple map. It is a dynamic representation, which is what every living nervous system sophisticated enough to have one actually maintains — not a picture of the world but a prediction about the world, a constantly revised bet. The crow's bet about the lowland storm forest is a very good bet. It has been refined by residence.

## On the Instruments Themselves

They have their fauna.

The lichen on the non-corroding surface is slow but present, the pioneer species that arrives before soil, before anything more complex, that breaks down substrate by chemical means and creates the condition in which something else can eventually follow. The lichen here is growing on something that is not stone but treats it like stone, grows on it like stone, is creating on its surface the thin pale crust that is the beginning of a soil-like thing, into which eventually moss will attempt to root, and after the moss something with true roots. The instruments are becoming part of the substrate. This is happening faster than their builders, who presumably built them to resist exactly this, anticipated. The valley is operating on its own schedule.

The moss that has colonized the roofs is a community in itself: springtails and mites and tiny worms in the wet interior of the moss-mat, eating the moss and each other, cycling nutrients in the tiny closed economy of the mat, oblivious to the instruments they live on, oblivious to the hum, treating the roof as they would treat any wet flat surface with adequate light. The moss does not know it is growing on something built. The moss knows: wet, light, substrate. The rest is not its concern.

A pair of small birds, the short-winged ones from the description, have explored the gap between the instrument and the overgrown wall beside it and found it adequate for a nest. They have not nested yet — the season is wrong — but they have found the gap and they return to it in a way that suggests they have filed it under future consideration, which is a behavior that birds have and are not always given credit for having. They will return. The gap will still be there. The hum will be there. Whether the hum disturbs or reassures is a question whose answer is: they will return. The answer is behavioral rather than reported.

## The Ground Itself, Considered as a Habitat

The ground is not a surface here. It is a zone.

Between the standing water and the dry-enough-to-support-rooted-plants there is a gradient that in this valley is measured in centimeters, and in that gradient there are organisms that have no analogues in drier places, organisms whose tolerances are precisely calibrated to the zone between flooded and merely saturated, between anaerobic and barely oxygenated. They are small. They are not charismatic. They are the reason the alder roots can reach into ground that should be too wet for roots, because the organisms in the root-zone are managing the chemistry of it, are creating the conditions the roots need, are in relationship with the roots in a way that is so thoroughly interdependent that calling it a relationship is barely accurate — it is more like a shared physiology, a single functional thing that has two names and two evolutionary histories.

The worms: enormous in this ground, enormous in the way that only very wet, very warm-at-the-bottom, very rich-in-organic-matter ground produces, earthworms of a size that seems cartoonish when you encounter them in the soil while pulling a fern by the base, dark red-purple animals thicker than a thumb, moving through the soil with the specific confident torpor of animals that have no reason to hurry because everything they need is within reach. They eat the soil and they improve the soil, and in this valley that is not a minor contribution. Without them the ground would compact under its own wetness. With them it remains porous, remains capable of the through-drainage that prevents even this wet ground from becoming truly anoxic, remains alive in the sense of having internal architecture rather than being a solid.

## What the Valley Collectively Is

Consider: a billion organisms, each doing its own specific thing, each following its own nature, each processing matter and energy in the direction of its own continuance — and from this following, from this accumulation of specific individual urgencies, something emerges that is not any of them, that is not reducible to any of them, but that is real in its effects.

The valley grows faster than valleys should.

The valley heals faster than valleys should. Clear a section of fern and return in a month. The fern is back, and there is something else with it, something that was not there before but that the disturbance created conditions for, and that something else is already becoming the substrate for a third thing. The succession here is not sequential in the way ecology textbooks describe succession, where one community hands off to the next in an orderly relay. It is more like multiple successions happening simultaneously at different spatial scales and different temporal rates, each creating conditions that redirect the others, so that the overall trajectory is toward complexity faster than seems reasonable.

There is a mouse — a single mouse — sitting in the exposed root-mass of a tree that fell before the valley was itself, that fell when this was hillside, and whose root-system now makes a cave above the groundwater level. The mouse is eating something it found, something small, and it is eating with the focused intensity of the very small and very metabolically demanding, and it is completely dry because the root-cave is completely dry, and the roaring outside the root-cave is complete, and the mouse does not look at the roaring, does not acknowledge it, eats what it has with an attention that is not philosophical but is the image of philosophy if you are standing in the fern-cover with cold water running down the back of your neck, trying to think in complete sentences.

The mouse finishes. Turns. Is gone into the root-system.

The root-system is gone into the ground.

The ground is gone into the water.

The water is gone into the roaring.

Everything, here, is always becoming something else. The organisms of the lowland storm forest are the agents of that becoming, each one a specific transformation, a particular shape that matter passes through on its way to being other matter, and the shapes are beautiful and precise and have names, and the names are inadequate, and the valley does not require the names, and the valley grows.
