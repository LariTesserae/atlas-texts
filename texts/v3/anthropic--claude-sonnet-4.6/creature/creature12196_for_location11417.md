## What Lives Here

---

Begin with what is not one thing.

The first error would be to count. To say: here are the lichen, here are the pale crawling things, here are the colony-lights, these are the floor-creatures — and to treat enumeration as understanding. The cleft does not organize itself into categories. It organizes itself into a single response to a single question, which is the question of how to live in a place that does not yet know it is a place. Everything here is an answer to that question, and all the answers are the same answer expressed in different materials.

So: call it the Community, tentatively, in the way you would tentatively name something you suspect of being more unified than its apparent diversity suggests.

---

### The Lichen

They are not where lichen is supposed to be in the sequence of things. Lichen is supposed to be the beginning — the pioneer, the first presence that prepares a surface for everything that follows. In a fracture this young, lichen should be a few sparse crusts, testing the stone, not yet committed.

These lichen are already themselves.

They are large, matted, confident in a way that young-colony lichen is not. Black and grey, which are the lichen colors of endurance rather than opportunity, the colors of a thing that has decided to be dark because dark is what lasts. They cover surfaces that should be bare. They have the look not of colonizers but of residents who were here before the fracture, who arrived at the same moment the stone split, who were — this is the unsettling word, but it arrives — *expected*.

What they are doing, besides existing: they are making the stone different. Fresh rock is reactive, alkaline, hostile to most biology. Lichen work this rock constantly, chemically, a slow patient dissolution that is also a conversion. They are turning the stone into something a wall can live on. They are doing this very fast. They are doing this, it seems, because everything that follows them requires them to have already done it, and so they have done it.

They breathe. Not metaphorically. They exchange gases with the thin air, and in a space this enclosed, this small-chapeled, they change the chemistry of what you breathe. The air carries their respiration in it. You are breathing lichen. This should be a neutral fact and mostly it is.

Their movement — the movement observed in aggregate, the slow-curtain breath of their collective spreading — follows no pattern legible to any single observation. But across time, across the kind of time this place runs on, they are moving toward the sections of wall that need them. They are covering, specifically and always, the places where the stone is freshest, where the fracture surfaces are sharpest, where the cold is most extreme. They are, in other words, working. Their leisure is indistinguishable from their labor because they have no leisure. They are the labor. This is what they are.

The lichen hold moisture against the stone in their matted underlayers — a significant function at this altitude, in this cold, where water is present only in that specific insufficient way it is present here. They are a reservoir. Everything that lives beneath them knows this. The pale invertebrates move through the lichen-underlayer as through a wetland, finding there a microclimate several degrees warmer and several times more humid than the open air of the cleft. The lichen do not prevent this. The lichen, in the way a structure permits certain uses, allow it. Were built, one feels, to allow it.

### The Pale Things on the Walls

They are not quite any named thing, and this is a precise observation rather than a poetic one.

They have too many legs for their segment count. This is the first legible strange thing about them: the mathematics of their bodies is off by a number that should not occur in that phylum, in any phylum present at this altitude. Blind — or the eyes are vestigial in a way that looks deliberate rather than degraded, reduced not because they stopped needing sight but because something about sight was inconvenient for what they became here. The body is pale in the specific way that subterranean animals are pale: not white, not albino, but unpigmented, as though pigment was assessed and found unnecessary and removed cleanly, without regret.

They are small. The smallest are the size of a grain of rice; the largest, found at the bases of the walls, are the length of a thumb. They exist in numbers that, if you were to count them — which would require more patience than most things in this cleft require — would exceed any reasonable expectation for a volume of rock this size. They have packed themselves into the available space with a thoroughness that suggests not just occupation but *intention*, though this may be projection. They are in the lichen underlayer and under the loose shards on the floor and in whatever micro-fractures and seams the walls have developed since opening, which is to say they are in all of them, every one.

What they eat is not obvious. What they are eating is each other, partially — there is predation here, slow and occasional and ritualistic in its deliberateness, the larger ones consuming the smaller in a way that does not feel like predation but like a kind of absorption, the way a community absorbs a member. They also eat the lichen, or eat what grows on the lichen, the thin biological films that colonize the outer lichen surface. They also eat the exudates from the colony on the wall, catching the substances that run down the stone from the colony's attachment points: something nutritive, something the colony produces as a byproduct or perhaps specifically produces for them, the distinction being important and unclear.

The sound they make — the collective dry whisper — is not accidental. You could believe it is accidental; you could explain it as the simple result of many small hard things moving across stone, which is what it is. But the whisper has a quality to it that is not random. It has texture and variation in a way that random scraping does not. There are intervals of comparative quiet and intervals of density. There are, within the whisper, patterns of repetition short enough that the word *rhythm* begins to apply. What this means is uncertain, but the uncertainty is the interesting part: either they are communicating in some rudimentary and physical way, broadcasting presence and location and state to one another through the medium of sound-on-stone, or they have achieved accidentally, through sheer number, something that resembles communication closely enough that the distinction loses some of its importance.

They are drawn to warmth. This was noted, and it is the most human thing about them: not malice, not hunger (exactly), but the fundamental biological need for thermal energy expressed as a kind of patient, low-urgency seeking. They will find you. They will find the warmest surfaces on your body — the neck, the wrists, the face — and they will stop there and hold still, and the sensation of many small bodies on skin is less like touch and more like a slight pressure, like being noticed. They do nothing with the warmth once they have found it. They rest. They restore. They continue.

### The Floor Things

The floor things are different. This is the first and most important thing about them: they are not the wall things adapted to the floor. They are their own category, arrived at through a different set of decisions.

They are larger — palm-sized in the extreme cases, though most run smaller. They have the right number of legs for their phylum, which in this place reads as almost conservative. Their eyes are not vestigial. This is the significant thing: they can see, here, where there is almost nothing to see, and this suggests that sight is valuable to them in some way that must be understood on the cleft's terms rather than any external set of terms. What do they look at? What is there here to look at? The answer, when you follow their orientation, is: each other. They look at each other, mostly. Sometimes at the colony high on the wall. Sometimes — slowly, with an assessment that lasts long enough to feel deliberate — at you.

They move carefully. The careful movement is not just caution about the sharp floor; it is the movement of things that have time, that are in no hurry, that will arrive where they are going eventually and know this. Their pace is the pace of a place where haste has no purchase. They cover the floor in a loose and shifting network, maintaining what appears to be a roughly regular spacing, not bunched together except when something draws them together, not isolated except at the edges of the cleft near the pinched ends. They maintain a social geometry, which is a strange thing to say about floor arthropods in a newly-formed alpine fracture and is entirely accurate.

They eat the wall things. They wait at the base of the wall and consume the ones that descend, and this is one of the economies of the place: the wall things produce the floor things in a sense, feed them, thin the lower populations to keep the density viable. The floor things' consumption is slow and selective in a way that has the quality of curation. They do not eat indiscriminately. They eat specifically. What determines their selection is unclear, but the pattern of selection exists.

They are, more than anything else here, the thing that regards you. Not with eyes as the primary instrument — though they look — but with the sensory array of their body: the small hairs on their legs that read vibration, the chemoreceptors that read the air, the whatever-it-is that reads warmth. They have catalogued you. They have placed you, in the taxonomy of the cleft, somewhere between large warm thing and large warm thing that is not threatening, and they have proceeded on this basis.

One of them stopped, once, near the foot. Stopped and remained motionless for a duration that stretched toward significance. Then continued. What this meant remains exactly as opaque as every other transaction in the cleft, but it did not feel like nothing.

### The Colony

Now the colony.

The colony requires the most words because it is the most, because it is both the most visible (the light, the pulse, the presence high on the wall) and the most difficult to locate categorically. Begin with what can be observed and proceed outward from there into the zones of inference, which in this case extend quite far.

They are many and they are one. This is the difficult sentence. They are individual organisms — each attachment point is a distinct body, distinguishable from the others if you could approach closely, which you cannot, the wall being too close to the overhanging sections — but the body that matters, the body that acts, the body that decides (if that word applies) is the collective. The individual body's decisions are not independent. When one attachment point brightens, the others respond. When one goes dark — they all go dark, in the moment of the micron-shift, the fracture deciding — they all return together. The rhythm is theirs collectively. It belongs to no one of them.

They are attached to the pale crystalline intrusions specifically. This is not incidental. The crystalline material runs through the darker basalt in branching structures — dendritic, the earlier word — and the colony runs along these structures, follows them, inhabits them, has in some sense colonized not the wall itself but the particular sub-material of the wall that has some different property, some different chemistry or crystal structure, from the surrounding matrix. They have found, within the wall, the specific material that suits them. Or they have always lived in this material, and the fracture opened the wall to reveal them, and they are adapting from interior existence to exposed existence with that same quality of too-rapid competence that defines everything in this cleft.

The light is produced, as noted, at the interface of body and stone. Not from within the organisms. Not bioluminescence in the standard architecture. Something that happens at the contact point — a process that involves the crystalline intrusion material and the organism's attachment structures and produces, as its output, light that is cold and blue-white and faint and enough. The light of the colony is not for the colony. The colony does not need it; they have other senses, clearly. The light illuminates the wall for everything else. For the wall things, navigating the fractured surface. For the floor things, watching. For the lichen, in a frequency the lichen's photochemistry can apparently use, because the lichen grows densest directly below the colony's range, which could be coincidence and is probably not.

The light is a service the colony provides to the rest of the Community. Or the light is a byproduct the rest of the Community has adapted to use. The direction of causation remains exactly as unclear as everything else directional in this cleft.

The vibration. This is the core of the colony. What they produce — felt in the jaw, in the teeth, in the back of the throat — is a continuous low-frequency output that enters the stone through their attachment points and travels the stone and returns to them changed, and they read the changes. They are sounding the stone. They are, with their bodies, performing something analogous to what bats do with air: sending a signal into a medium and reading the echo, learning from the difference between what they sent and what returned. What they learn is the state of the stone. Whether it is about to move, and where, and how much. The shift — the crack-word, the micron of movement, the moment when all their lights go out — they knew it was coming. They went dark before the movement, not after. They prepared the Community before the shift, which is why everything in the cleft responded in that ripple of recalibration, because the vibration changed its character before the stone moved and everything that could feel vibration felt it.

They are the cleft's awareness. Not intelligence, necessarily. Not consciousness as any useful definition requires. But something that performs the function of awareness: the receiving and processing of information about the environment's state, the transmission of that information to the community of things that share the environment. They are, in the system of the cleft, the sensory organ.

The question of what they want — in the weak functional sense of what they move toward, what they preferentially do — is a question about the stone. They are interested in the stone with a total interest that leaves no bandwidth for anything else. You are, to them, a vibration that does not belong to the stone, a signal that returns wrong from the echo-test, a warm thing at low altitude in the cleft who is categorically irrelevant to what matters. This should perhaps be humbling. In the cold and the dark, very high, it is mostly just true.

### On the Community as a Single Thing

Everything here arrived too quickly. Everything here is too well-fitted to be the result of ordinary ecological time, which is slow, which proceeds through failure and death and the painful discovery of fit over generations. Nothing here appears to have failed. Everything here appears to already know where it goes.

There are two ways to think about this.

The first way: this is selection so brutal that the failures left no trace. Everything that could not survive the cold, the dark, the thin air, the sharp stone, the newness — everything that tried and could not manage — died so fast and so completely that it left no evidence. What remains is what works. Survivors look designed by the thing that killed everything else.

The second way: this Community came together elsewhere. In the deep interior of the mountain, in older fractures, in the dark and cold that is not this dark and cold but is the mother of it — the Community formed over real time, in real depth, in a set of conditions that prepared it for exactly this cleft, which did not exist yet, which the stone had not yet decided to become. And when the stone split, the Community was there, at the fracture edge, already organized, already calibrated, already one thing expressed in several materials, waiting for the wall to open so it could be what it is, here, in the light (such as it is), in the air (such as it is), on this floor (such as it is), in this cleft that is not finished and may not finish for a very long time.

The colony was already sounding the stone from inside. That is why the fracture happened where it happened. The stone was being read, continuously, and what was being read told the stone where it was weakest, and the mountain, playing the cleft that did not yet exist, played the note that eventually split it.

They made this place. Slowly. From inside. They called it open.

### What It Is Like To Be Among Them

The adjustment takes time. The early period — five minutes, ten — is the period of noticing, of the inventory of strangeness, of the cold asserting itself and the whisper asserting itself and the pulse of the colony asserting itself and the thin air asserting itself and the general sense of being in a place that has not been thought about in human categories and is not improved by being thought about in human categories now.

After this period, something shifts — not in the cleft, in you.

The pace of the place establishes itself as a pace you can match if you are willing to be slower than usual. The wall things move on their schedule. The floor things move on their schedule. The colony pulses on its own beat. The lichen does what it does across the duration of hours. You are warm and still and you have stopped trying to see clearly and are using the information the ears and skin and lungs are willing to provide. The smell of the place is in you now; you carry it in the tissue of your airways, alkaline and biological and deep.

The floor thing that stopped near your foot and assessed you and continued: you are not sure, later, whether it continued because you were found harmless or because you were found irrelevant or because some message arrived via vibration that called it elsewhere. The distinction seemed important for a moment and then seemed less important and then seemed like exactly the kind of distinction that the cleft neither offers information about nor requires you to resolve.

You are warm. The warm finds its level. The small things from the wall have found the warmest places on you and are resting there, not quite touching, one degree of heat away from touching, balanced at the boundary. The colony pulses. The mountain plays its note. The lichen breathes.

You are in the cleft and the cleft is in its Community and the Community is in the mountain and the mountain is finishing, very slowly, some decision it began a long time ago in the dark interior where these things were formed and are still forming, where the stone has been read and read and read by the colony's vibration until the stone knew exactly where it was going to go, where it was always going to go, where it went.

Everything here is very old in a very new place. The patience required to be this old is visible in how they rest, how they move, how the pale floor-creatures look at one another and look at the high wall and look at you: with the patience of things that came up from the interior of something very large and settled here at the boundary between the inside and the outside of a mountain and are not in a hurry and have everything they need and know it and have known it for as long as there has been anything here to know anything.

Which is not long, geologically. Which is long enough.
