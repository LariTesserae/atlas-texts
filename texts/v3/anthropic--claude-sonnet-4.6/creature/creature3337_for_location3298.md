# What Lives Here

---

Begin with what you almost miss.

The first thing is not the large things. The first thing is a film.

Every surface — every leaf-face, every bark-channel, every stretch of web, every curve of fallen branch — is coated in something that is not quite alive in the way that wolves or orchids are alive, and is not quite not alive in the way that stone is not alive. The film is a civilization of bacteria and archaea and fungi-filaments, a mat of microorganisms so dense and various that it constitutes, in the understory's own accounting, the primary layer of being here. These are the things that came first into this young soil, these are the things that the trees feed through their roots in elaborate negotiated exchange, these are the things that are eating every surface and building every surface up and eating it again, the consumption and the construction simultaneous and indistinguishable. The film tastes the chemistry of the air. It responds to light it cannot see through organs it doesn't have. It is everywhere, and it is, in some sense too distributed to locate, the reason the understory is alive at all.

Within the film: the things that eat the film. Nematodes in their millions, threading between the microorganisms with their blunt heads extended, eating what is too small for most things to eat. They are translucent, worm-shaped, tinier than the eye resolves without magnification, and they are so numerous in this soil and on these surfaces that the count of individual nematodes in a handful of litter-layer would require a pause, a breath, a reconsideration of what a number means. They do not know they are here. They are here completely.

This is the substrate. This is what everything else is built on. Keep it in mind as the larger things come forward, because the larger things are, in a very real sense, slow-moving and elaborate structures the microorganisms have built to move the microorganisms around.

---

The insects resolve out of the background in stages.

First: the background is not background. What you have been taking as the texture of the air, the grain of the darkness, is insects. The background *is* insects. A cubic meter of air in the mid-strata of this understory contains — it would take study to be precise, and study would be difficult because the researcher would themselves be inhaling some of what they counted — an order of insects that blurs the line between population and medium. They are the atmosphere here. They are what the air is made of when the air is not being made of water.

The moths first, because moths are large enough to feel when they brush you, and they will brush you, the scales from their wings catching on arm-hair with a faint adhesion you almost mistake for moisture. The moths here are doing something that moths elsewhere do more gently: they are competing for the bioluminescent sources the way moths elsewhere compete for moonlight, spiraling in their wide navigational arcs around the fungal glows in the wood, the bacterial shimmer in the pond-cups of the bromeliads, and the result is a counterclockwise current in the air around each light-source, a soft mill of wings that creates its own warm draft if you hold your hand into it.

Look longer and the moths have shapes. They are not all the same kind of moth: there are the pale-winged ones whose wings are leaf-shaped and whose wing-eyes are so precisely leaf-shaped that even knowing what they are, something in you hesitates at each one; there are the small dark ones that are almost just a fold of air; there are the large ones, some kind, whose wingspan at full extension is nearly the breadth of your opened hand, who move through the air slowly like something too heavy for the air to support comfortably. They do not seem to have destinations. They seem, if moths seem, to have been moving for a long time and to intend to continue.

The beetles beneath the bark: these are builders. Not metaphorically — they are engineering the internal structure of the deadwood, the fallen trees, the dying standing trees, and their tunnels are architecture with purpose, with ventilation, with chambers that open into chambers in a planning that is not planning but arrives at function regardless. The galleries they cut smell of pine and cedar when the wood is fresh and of something more mineral and complex when the wood has been aging in the humidity. There are beetles that follow the bark-boring beetles and eat the bark-boring beetles. There are beetles that follow those beetles and eat the fungi the bark-boring beetles cultivate. The beetle ecology in a single fallen tree is an economy.

The ants do not get a paragraph. The ants require a different unit. The ants are a feature of this place the way weather is a feature of a place — they are not individual animals in any practical sense, they are a distributed process with a moving center, and the moving center tonight is somewhere in the middle distance where a column, a highway of ants, is carrying fragments of leaf downward into the earth in a procession that has been continuous since before you arrived and will be continuous after. Where the column crosses other columns — and it does, and the other columns cross it — there is a pause, a negotiation in chemical, a brief turmoil, and then the columns resume. The ants on the margins of each column are facing outward. The ants on the margins are the soldiers. The soldiers are not doing anything right now and they are doing it with complete commitment.

The termites work below the ant's attention and below yours, mostly. They are inside things. They are inside the wood, the soil, the dead leaf material, converting it with their gut-fauna — which are themselves an ecology, organisms inside the organism — into forms the soil can use. What the termites leave behind is what the soil is. The understory floor is substantially a termite artifact.

---

The web-builders are everywhere and they are each alone.

A spider of the understory does not maintain a social structure. It builds a structure and it waits in the structure and it repairs the structure when the structure is damaged by passing fauna (passing fauna is constant; passing fauna is the defining problem of the understory web-builder's existence, that everything moves through your trap and most of it is too large to eat and tears the silk and leaves you with less silk than you started). The spiders here have evolved silk that is nearly invisible at all angles, silk that catches the condensation-drops and turns them to scattered stars, silk that is stronger than its diameter by any measure you would care to apply. Some of the web-builders are large enough that their webs must be large, that you would walk into one face-first in the darkness without warning, and the contact of the silk on your skin is finer than you expected from something strong enough to stop an insect in full flight.

The spiders sit in their webs like dropped stones, weight at the center of a tension system, and they feel. Everything in the web registers through the silk into the spider's legs. The spider reads the web the way a blind person reads a face with their hands. There is a whole phenomenology of vibration here, of the different feel of a struggling moth versus a struggling beetle versus a raindrop versus a falling leaf, and the spider has a phenomenology of all of it, developed in the dark, in the patient center of its structure, over a life that takes place almost entirely in one cubic meter of forest.

What does the spider know? More than you would guess. Less than you would need to find comfortable. The spider knows the density of the air and what is moving in it. The spider knows the state of its own silk better than any external observer could. The spider knows hunger in a way that is not metaphor but direct physiology, the urgency of it calibrated against the last catch, the silk-supply, the readiness to lunge or to wait. The spider has made a decision — not consciously, but the outcome of the spider's architecture is a decision — to be very still in a very full place, and to let the fullness come to it.

---

The frogs are everywhere that the frogs can be and the frogs can be almost everywhere.

They are on the vertical surfaces, toe-pads deployed, each toe-pad working through capillary force and micro-scale friction and some mechanism not yet fully understood into a grip that can hold them horizontal on glass, let alone bark. They are in the bromeliad-cups, individual frogs in individual bromeliad-cups, single organisms in miniature ponds that are entirely their own domain. They are on the broad leaves of the understory plants, one frog per leaf sometimes, arranged as though distributed by a mind that wanted even coverage. They are in the soil where the soil is moist enough, and in the bark-crevices where bark-crevices hold moisture, and in the hollows of fallen branches, and on the fallen branches, and on the roots.

The frogs are calling.

This needs to be understood separately from understanding that frogs make sounds. The calling here is a project, an argument, an overwhelming of the air that isn't overwhelmed because the air is already full of insects and each frog is calling into a medium that is already full of other frogs calling, and the response to the medium being full is to call more, louder, at a slightly different frequency, and the response of every other frog to *that* is the same response, and the cumulative result is not noise but something that has become, over enough hours of hearing it, a kind of landscape feature, an omnipresent pressure you lean against without knowing you're leaning.

The frogs are calling to each other across this pressure. They are finding each other in it. Some of them are succeeding.

The males that are calling are each advertising: a specific thing, a precise thing, a set of qualities encoded in the exact frequency and timing and volume of a call that females of the same species hear against the background of every other frog call as the relevant signal, the way a parent hears their child's name across a room of shouting children. This is not mystical. This is filtering developed by pressure over evolutionary time, the most useful filtering winning, the filtering propagating. But the result in this place is something that feels like it must be impossible: each frog calling in its own specificity into the full density of all the other frogs, and being heard.

The ones not calling are watching. Sitting in the wet dark on their wet surfaces, processing the visual field through eyes that are a large fraction of their head and built for low-light movement-detection. They can see in this dark better than you can. What they see is a world of motion, of silhouettes, of shapes that are food and shapes that are danger and shapes that are other frogs. They are sitting very still. They are absolutely alert. Their stillness is not rest; their stillness is the active condition of the ambush predator, the condensation of readiness into posture.

---

Then come the things that have spines.

The mammals first, the small ones, the ones whose eyes catch the bioluminescence and hold it for a moment in two round glints before the head moves and the glints are gone. They are everywhere at ground level, they are the rustling you assign to leaves falling and then reconsider, they are the weight that bends a root you didn't know was there, they are the warmth that passes just above the warm ground in a warmer moving blur.

The opossums, some kind — there are kinds of opossum here that have not been described, this forest being young and full of things that arrived before the namers did — hang in the low branches by their tails, bodies swinging in the thermal currents that move through the lower strata, their naked prehensile tails working against branches with a grip that is slow and persistent and slightly stronger than you would expect. They eat whatever they encounter in the reaching range of their climb: eggs if they find them, fruits if they find them, insects always, fungi if the fungi are the right fungi. They are not selective eaters. They are comprehensive eaters. Their jaws contain more teeth than most of their relatives, a dental generalism that matches their dietary generalism, a physical argument against specialization in a place that changes too fast for specialization to be safe.

The rodents are various and numerous and fast and their fastness in this darkness is startling, the way they can thread between roots and under logs and through the litter-layer without pause as though the forest floor is their normal medium, which it is. They read the ground through their whiskers, through the sensitivity of their feet, through whatever spatial model the brain of a very fast small mammal in perpetual motion maintains. They know where they are at speeds that would leave most things lost. A rodent moving at full speed through the understory at night is not guessing. It knows exactly where the next root is, where the surface changes, where the gap opens. It has a map of this territory in whatever serves it for memory and it moves by that map with something that in a larger animal you would recognize as confidence.

There are primates in the mid-canopy and their sound tonight is primarily social, the intersecting of groups that have not yet established which territories belong to whom because the territories are new and the groups are new and the negotiation is ongoing. Their faces, if you could see them in this dark from this distance, would be expressive with the expressiveness that primates use to negotiate, to threaten, to reassure, to identify. They recognize individuals. They remember what individuals have done. They hold something like grievance, something like gratitude, something like expectation, and the social calls moving through the canopy above you are complex with all of this, layered with meaning that the other primates partially decode and partially misread and partially respond to unexpectedly, because communication in conditions of high density and rapid change is imperfect, and imperfect communication creates situations, and situations create events, and events create learning.

---

The snakes are present. You would not know where.

A snake in the understory at night is invisible at distances greater than an arm's length, and often invisible at lesser distances. They are on the branches at the height of roosting birds. They are on the forest floor at the height of foraging rodents. They are in the gaps between root-buttresses where the moisture is highest and the small things rest. They are still in a way that makes the still things around them seem, in comparison, to be moving. A snake's stillness is a different category of stillness. It is not the stillness of waiting in a mammal's sense, the gathering of energy before action. It is the stillness of something whose metabolic urgency is lower, whose time-scale is slower, who can be still for hours in the same way that a stone is still, with none of the cost that stillness imposes on warm blood.

And then they are not still, and what happens next happens on the snake's timescale, which is a timescale other animals generally don't get to experience from the outside.

Some of the snakes glow. This is documented elsewhere and underdocumented everywhere; the iridescent scales of certain species catch and return bioluminescence in ways that might be accidental and might be something more organized. You see, in the dark, a slow shift of light along the forest floor and then it is gone and you look for where it was and it has resolved into bark and shadow and the suggestion that you may have imagined it.

You did not imagine it.

---

Here is something about the birds.

The birds of the understory night are not the birds of the canopy day. These are different birds by design: birds built for low light, for interior spaces, for the navigation of a dense world that requires precision more than speed. The nightjars among the leaf litter are flat against the ground and colored the color of the ground and breathing. Their eyes are enormous. Their vision in this light is better than the understory deserves to reward. They sit and they process the air around them, which is full of moth, and when the processing resolves into action the action is fast, beak opening into the gape that is dramatically wide relative to the head, the moths taken from the air in flight.

The potoos, if there are potoos here, stand on branches and become branches. They are in the family of birds that has taken stillness as its primary defensive behavior and they have taken it further than most: they elongate, they angle, they achieve a texture that reads to the eye as bark, and they hold that reading indefinitely, even when something large passes close, even when the branch sways in the thermal movement. They hold it. The potoo's eye, when something approaches, closes to a slit — not completely, never completely, because the potoo must see the thing approaching and know when the thing has passed — and the slit tracks what it cannot afford to seem to be tracking, and the branch breathes.

The owls move. They do not advertise themselves with calls the way the frogs advertise, the way the primates advertise. They move through the understory in what would be silence if the understory were not already so loud that the owl's silence is subsumed, is just another texture in the sound-weather. They have feathers adapted for silent flight — the edges serrated into a fine fringe that disrupts the vortex-flow that would otherwise make noise, the result that the air passes through rather than around — and the result is that an owl moves through air as though the air has agreed to let it pass without comment.

An owl's face is a parabolic dish. The arrangement of feathers around the eyes is shaped to catch sound and focus it on ears that are not where you would expect ears to be, and the two ears are at slightly different heights on the skull, so that sounds arrive at them at slightly different times, and from the difference the brain of the owl computes a precise location in three-dimensional space. The owl can locate, in the complete dark, in the sound-density of this understory, the position of a mouse under leaf litter by hearing the mouse breathe. Then the owl moves.

---

What is it like to live here?

This is a question you can't answer but can approach, obliquely, through particulars.

The nematode in the film on the bark does not experience the understory. The nematode is moving and eating and occasionally being eaten and moving and eating and moving. The world the nematode has is a few microns of surface and the chemistry of that surface and the gradients of that chemistry telling it: go here, not there, here, faster, here. This is a world. It is not a world we recognize as a world but it has the basic structure of world: it has direction, it has preference, it has outcome, it has something that functions as satisfaction and something that functions as urgency.

Scale up. The spider in its web has a world that is its web and some volume around it. The spider has, in some register that functions like attention, been attending to this volume for hours. It knows it. It holds a model of it, in whatever holds models in the spider, that is detailed enough to distinguish a caught moth from a falling leaf from a branch-vibration transmitted through the tree the web anchors to. The spider's world is small and known as you and I know the rooms of our homes, with the authority of long residence and close attention.

The frog calling in the bromeliad-cup: its world is larger, the arc of its hearing, the territory of its call. It knows its territory in the way a landlord knows a property — the borders, the resources, the things that change and the things that stay. It knows the individual calls of the frogs around it well enough to know when a stranger appears in the soundscape. The stranger is assessed: same species? Different species? Threat, rival, irrelevance? The assessment is not deliberative but it arrives at something like a conclusion, and the frog acts on it.

The primate in the mid-canopy knows more. It knows individuals. It knows history. It knows something like tomorrow — not the word, not the concept, but the preparation for what tomorrow usually brings, the memory of what yesterday brought, the mapping of expectation across time. Its world is social and spatial and temporal in ways that the frog's world is not, and in this forest, where the social is turbulent and the spatial is in flux and the temporal is full of change, the primate's knowledge is working harder than it would in a stable place. It is updating constantly, recalculating, filing new information into old categories and sometimes finding the categories insufficient and building new ones.

Each of these is a world. They are all happening here, simultaneously, in the same cubic footage of dark and moisture and sound, nested and overlapping and completely indifferent to each other's existence except at the specific points where they intersect — where the frog notices the spider, where the snake encounters the frog, where the owl locates the mouse, where the ant encounters the beetle. The forest is the sum of all these intersections and none of the things in it know the sum. They know their pieces.

You are standing in the middle of more pieces than have ever been counted.

---

And the trees themselves. Return to the trees.

The trees are doing what the trees do, which is more than standing. The trees are pumping water from the soil to heights that physics should not allow and does allow only because of the tensile strength of water in a column from root to leaf, the water pulled upward by the loss of water from leaf surfaces into air that is almost but not quite saturated, the almost-not-quite providing just enough of a pull. Tonight the pull is lower — the air is fuller of water, the driving force is less — and the trees are resting, relatively. Storing. The sugars made in the day's light moving downward through a different system of tubes, feeding the root tips, feeding the mycelial partners at the root tips, feeding the soil.

The trees are talking. This has been established, this is not metaphor. The mycorrhizal network connecting root to root through soil carries chemical signals, and the signals carry information, and the information that they carry is: *stress*. Something is eating this tree. Something has damaged this bark. Something is taking water. The receiving tree has biochemical responses to these signals, upregulates the chemistry of resistance before the threat arrives, is ready. The signal moves at chemical speeds, which are slow, but the forest is also slow in the timescale of its conversations, and the slow signal is fast enough.

The trees have been here less time than you would think to look at them. They are young trees in a young forest and they are growing at the upper edge of their capacity, growing faster than is sustainable in trees that expect to live a long time, because the trees of this understory have not yet sorted out the difference between the trees that will be here when this forest is old and the trees that will not be. The ones that will not be are buying time with speed. The ones that will be are buying time with depth, with slow investments in root-architecture and chemical library and defense systems. Both kinds are here together and they look the same from outside. From inside they are making entirely different bets.

---

Later, much later, when you have been standing still long enough that the small things have stopped avoiding you and the medium things have accepted you as a fixed feature and only the large things still navigate around you with their usual lateral distance, something will change.

You will stop being a visitor that the forest is tolerating and start being a surface that the forest is colonizing.

First a moth will land on your arm, and the scales of its feet will taste your skin chemistry, and finding nothing useful it will leave. Then a fly will land on your neck, attracted by the warmth and the carbon dioxide. Then — and this is the one that matters, the one that marks the transition — an ant will reach your foot, will begin to assess you, will send something back along its pheromone trail that is not quite an alarm, not quite an invitation, something in between, something that is the colony's version of: *here is an unusual surface, investigation in progress, not dangerous, not yet a resource, but novel*.

You have become part of the census.

The forest has been here in some form before you, and it will be here in some form after you, and right now it is busy being here at every scale from the archaea in the film on the bark to the primates crashing through the canopy, and all of those scales are busy attending to the business of their own existence, and their own existence is vast and complete and requires nothing from you —

except your warmth, briefly, and the carbon dioxide you exhale, and the particular microbial community of your skin, which is different from the microbial community of the bark and which the forest is already, through its various sensitive instruments, in the process of noting.

You are not outside it.

You are just the newest part of it, still being understood.
