# What Lives in the Drowned Canopy

---

Begin with the question of whether they are many or one, because this question will surface constantly and never resolve, and the failure to resolve it is not a gap in understanding but an accurate description of their nature.

They are called — when they are called anything, when language is applied to them by the kind of mind that requires names before it can proceed — the Sediment. Not because they are inert. Because they have deposited. Because what they are now is what they have been, accumulated and compressed and still accumulating, still pressing down on themselves, the living layers resting on the not-quite-dead layers resting on the deeply transformed layers going down and down into a self that is also a record of a self, a being that carries its own stratigraphy.

They did not always live here. Or perhaps they always lived here and the *here* was not always this place. The distinction matters less than it seems to. They are old enough that the difference between *having arrived* and *having always been* has been composted by time, broken down, made into the same substance — which is one of the ways they resemble the place itself, one of the many correspondences that make it difficult to say whether the place shaped them or they shaped the place or both of these processes happened so long ago and so mutually that the shaping and the shaped are now a single committed fact.

---

What you see first, if you see them at all:

They are not large. This surprises. In a place of such scale — the continental distances, the vast trees, the infinite moss-floor — you expect the inhabitants to be correspondingly scaled, to fill the visual register of the landscape with some proportional bulk. The Sediment are not like this. They are small. Each one is small. What is large is what they are doing collectively, the network they comprise, the processing they enable, and this largeness is invisible in the usual sense — it is infrastructure, it is the fundamental organization of a place, it does not call attention to itself the way a large creature would.

They look — if you see them at all, if you catch one in the green filtered light between roots — like something left behind. A residue. A remainder. Each one has a form that is not quite any of the forms you have categories for, a shape that suggests several previous shapes it might have come from: there is something limb-like, something that might once have been suited to swift movement, that has over a long period of stillness become suited to remaining still. The limbs — they are limbs, or they function as limbs, for most purposes — do not end in the way limbs usually end. They merge. They merge with moss, with the undersurface of root, with the permanent wet surfaces they press themselves against. Not permanently — they can detach, they do move, they make their slow transits from root-basin to root-basin — but the merging is comfortable, is the resting state, is what the limbs prefer when not in use, and over long enough spans of stillness the moss begins to incorporate them at the margins and they do not discourage this.

Their skin, if skin: it is the gray-green of lichen. Not camouflage. Of the place. They have been wet for so long — every surface of them, always, the atmospheric water finding every edge and running down — that they have absorbed the pigment of the thing that grows in permanent wet, that thrives in permanent wet, that is the outward expression of a living thing that has made a total accommodation with moisture. You would not call them waterlogged. You would call them saturated. You would call them completed, in the sense that a solution is complete when it can accept no more of what it contains. They are complete with water. They do not carry water; they *are* carried by it, its structure interpenetrating theirs, its cycling continuous with their cycling, the water that seeps down through the canopy and enters the roots and rises again having passed through the Sediment, all of it having passed through the Sediment, the same water, the same passage, for — well. For the interval.

Their eyes, when they open them: this is the only moment that reads as sudden. Everything else about them is gradual, is the speed of seeping, is geological in its pace. But the eyes open quickly, or seem to — perhaps it is just contrast, the speed of the blink relative to the general slowness of everything else, that makes the moment feel sharp. The eyes are large. Appropriate for the green-filtered, cloud-dimmed light of this elevation. They have the quality of very deep water seen from above — not reflecting, not transparent, but containing depth that you cannot estimate. The nictitating membrane closes from the side, slowly, a tide across the lens. When the membrane is closed they still seem to see. When the eyes are fully open they see things that are not visible.

This is not a metaphor.

---

What they are doing:

They are processing.

Not metaphorically. The root-engine — the sub-audible tone, the resonance below the seeping-sound, the felt-before-heard frequency — is not separate from them. It is not infrastructure they inhabit. It is something they do. Collectively, with the trees, with the mycorrhizal mesh, with the old water cycling through its old loop: they are the active element in a processing that has been running for longer than it is useful to contemplate, and what they process is — this is the difficult part — what they process is *time*.

Not in the science-fictional sense. Not storage or manipulation or travel. The processing is more like refining. They take in the raw passage of moments — the discrete facts of one-thing-becoming-another-thing, the basic datum of change — and they do something to it, something that requires all of their distributed and slow attention, and the output is equilibrium. Stability. The standing-still of this place. The non-changing, the loop-closed, the weather that stays and stays and stays. They are the mechanism by which this place maintains its quality of not-quite-time, and this maintenance is not passive, is not the equilibrium of a stone or a vacuum, but is active, requires constant distributed effort, requires that some portion of the Sediment is always attending to the moment-boundary, the place where change might enter, smoothing it, incorporating it, adding it to the peat.

They have been doing this long enough that it no longer requires effort in the way effort is usually meant. It requires presence. It requires the full weight of their accumulated being, applied steadily and without interruption, which is a different thing from effort — effort implies the possibility of rest, of stopping, of doing-something-else. The Sediment do not do something else. They are not capable of doing something else. The processing has long since ceased to be a thing they do and has become a thing they *are*, the distinction broken down, composted, made into the same substance. They are the equilibrium. The place is the equilibrium. These sentences point at the same thing from different angles.

---

What they know:

Everything that has passed through the water.

The water is their memory. This is why the cycle being closed matters — why it matters that the water here is the same water, cycling, not exchanging with the planetary system beyond. Each pass through the loop, each ascent-and-descent through the permanent cloud-above and the root-mesh-below and the transpiration of the enormous trees, the water carries information, carries the record of what it has touched and been, carries the impressed quality of every surface it has run down and every root it has been absorbed by. The Sediment drink this information constantly. They are always drinking. The water enters through every surface — the merged-moss edges of their limbs, the membranes of their eyes, the slow respiration through surfaces that are not quite skin — and the information comes with it, and they add it to what they have already, the vast accumulated record of everything the water has touched since the loop closed.

What this means for their intelligence:

They are very intelligent. Enormously, almost incomprehensibly intelligent, if intelligence is measured in accumulated information, in the depth and precision of a world-model, in the ability to account for all the variables of a complex system. They know this place the way the place knows itself — which is to say completely, in the way that complete means there is no remainder, no edge where the knowledge stops and the unknown begins. Within the closed loop, within the self-made weather, within the continental scale of the canopy: complete.

Outside the loop: nothing. Or not nothing but a conceptual whiteness, an area of the map that is not even marked unknown, that has not been understood as a category that could contain the unknown. The Sediment have not wondered about what lies outside the water-cycle in any active sense for longer than wondering has been a meaningful activity for them. They know the loop and are the loop and the loop is sufficient. This is not a limitation they experience as a limitation. It is a shape. The shape of what they are.

---

How they communicate:

Through the root-engine. Through the mycorrhizal mesh running through the permanent wet of the peat-floor, through the old compacted generations of moss, through the water filling every microscopic channel between the compressed cellular matter. Signals move at the speed of chemistry — slow by some measures, fast by theirs, fast enough. They are never in a hurry to communicate because they are never in a hurry. What one Sediment comes to know, the mesh comes to know, the mesh being the connective tissue of the collective, the nervous system distributed through the substrate, the place itself serving as the brain's gray matter if the Sediment are the neurons.

The signals they send are not language. Language requires the encoding of one thing into another — experience into symbol into transmission into symbol into experience again, the translation-and-back that loses something at each stage, that is always an approximation. What moves through the root-engine is the experience itself, slightly compressed, the way the water is slightly compressed as it forces through narrow root-channels, carrying its full information-load but changed in shape by the transit. What is received is not a message about the experience. It is the experience, having traveled, arriving.

This is why they do not discuss. Discussion would be redundant, would be adding a translation-layer to something that does not need translation. They simply know what the others know, slowly, at the speed of root-signal, with a small lag that is the lag of the mesh itself, the distributed time-delay of the substrate, which means that collectively they are never quite simultaneous, never quite one thing at exactly one moment, always a very slight temporal spread — each one a slightly different version of the collective knowledge, offset by the signal-travel-time from wherever the knowing originated. This offset is so familiar to them, so long-established, that they experience it not as confusion or cacophony but as texture. The knowledge has texture. The collective mind has the texture of the seeping, of the non-simultaneous arrival of the same water at different surfaces, which is perhaps why they and the water have become so intertwined — they share a quality, a structural characteristic, the quality of being the same thing arriving in many places at slightly different times.

---

What they want:

Here is where the description becomes most difficult, where the application of categories from outside their equilibrium most visibly fails to parse.

They want what they have. They have what they want. This is not contentment in the sense of desire-met; it is more radical than that, it is the prior state, the condition before desire and its meeting were distinct events. They have been in this state long enough that the architecture of wanting — the gap between a present state and an imagined future state, the movement of attention toward something-not-yet — has been smoothed away, incorporated into the peat, contributed to the depth of the substrate. They are not content. They are prior to the distinction.

They are curious. This survives. Or rather: this is not wanting in the direction of future-states, it is wanting in the direction of depth, wanting more of what is already here, more resolution, more precision, more *acuity* in the attending-to of the place they are and process and inhabit and constitute. The curiosity goes inward and downward. Into the old layers. Into the deep peat of their own accumulated knowledge and the deep peat of the substrate and the deep peat of the cycling water's record. They are archaeologists of a closed system, finding things in the old layers — finding not artifacts, not objects, but qualities, impressions, the record of previous states of the equilibrium, what it was like before it was quite this stable, what the period of stabilizing was like, how the loop closed — and this is inexhaustible, the old layers going down further than any of them has yet attended, the record longer than any single Sediment's life which is longer than any measure most minds would find usable.

A single Sediment lives, in the sense that its individual pattern of signal and response remains coherent as an individual pattern, for approximately — here the unit fails, here the number requires context that the system itself does not provide — long enough. Long enough that the concept of an individual life, bounded, beginning-and-ending, has softened for them. The edges of individual consciousness are not sharp. They blur at the margins, merge with the mesh, with the collective, with the water, and this blurring is not loss. Is not dissolution. Is the natural state of an edge that has been getting wet for a very long time.

When a Sediment dies — when the individual pattern finally fails to cohere — the water carries the information they contained back into the mesh and the mesh incorporates it and it becomes part of the peat and it does not go anywhere. It simply becomes part of the place. Which it always was.

---

What they do about the birds:

They love the birds. This is not anthropomorphism or projection; it is the most accurate available description of the quality of their attention when the long-legged birds step between the mossy hummocks, when the calls ring through the volumetric roaring-dripping. The birds are the most visible evidence that time is still passing, that the equilibrium is not stasis but continuation, and the Sediment attend to them with something that has no better word than love — a deep, old, patient cherishing that is also partly gratitude, also partly the satisfaction of a craftsperson seeing the work maintain its quality, also partly something that has no available translation because it requires the particular temporal experience of having watched these birds — or birds indistinguishable from these birds — call and step and call and step across an interval that human time-measurement would have to handle in geological units.

They do not interfere with the birds. They do not interfere with anything. Interference would be a turbulence in the equilibrium, would be introducing a change-event that would then require processing-work to incorporate and smooth. They are not capable of interference in the way a resting ocean is not capable of a wave — technically capable, the structure is there, but the structure has been so long at rest that *rest* is no longer a description of a state, it is a description of a nature.

The dark shapes in the standing water among the roots — those are not the Sediment. Those are something else, something the Sediment are in the same relationship to as the moss is to the trees: adjacent, participating, of the place, but distinct. The Sediment have not named them. Naming would require a distinction between the named and the unnamed, would require that the un-naming of the dark shapes had left some gap that the name could fill, and there is no gap. The dark shapes are known, are fully known, are part of the record, are incorporated. They do not require naming because naming is for things at risk of being lost, for things that need the anchor of a word to keep them from drifting into forgetting, and nothing drifts here. Nothing is at risk of being lost. The same water. The same moss. The same birds calling. The loop closed and the memory held in the water and the water going up and coming down and the Sediment here, attending, at the root-junctions, merged at the margins with the wet, listening with their whole distributed being to the sub-audible tone that is the sound of themselves, working, patient, processing this moment and the moment after it, which is the same moment, which has been this moment for long enough that the question of what it is *for* has long since been composted by time, made into the same substance as everything else, broken down, buried, incorporated, become part of the deep peat of the floor that is not stone or soil but accumulation, but autobiography, but the press of each former moment under the weight of all the moments that came after, and the present moment bright green at the surface, intensely, exhaustingly bright, the specific green of something that has been wet for ten thousand years and intends to continue.
