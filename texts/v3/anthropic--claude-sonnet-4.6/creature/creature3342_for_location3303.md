# Those Who Live in the Hollow of Woven Houses

---

Begin with what is hardest to say: they are not aware of being remarkable. This is not humility. Humility requires the concept of an alternative self, a self that might have been more, against which the present self is measured and found appropriately modest. They have no such alternative. They are what living in this place, for this long, makes of a people — and that is simply what they are, the way the moss is what it is, the way the cold between four and seven degrees is what it is. The weirdness of sufficiency extends inward. They have completed their becoming.

So it is almost impossible to describe them without importing strangeness that is yours, not theirs — the strangeness of encountering a thing that does not know it is strange.

Try anyway.

---

They are small in the way the buildings are small: not miniaturized, not stunted, but *edited*. The architecture of a body shaped by generations of low-ceilinged channels and knee-height doorways and the specific demand that you carry what you carry in both hands rather than dragging it. They fold well. The hinge points — wrists, ankles, hips, the back of the neck — have the quality of something worn smooth by frequent use, easy in their motion, unhurried. When they crouch, they crouch the whole way down without negotiation, without the visible calculation of a body testing whether it will be able to rise again. They rise without ceremony. This is not performance. The performance, if you could call it that, happened generations back and has since been absorbed into structure, into the geometry of the skeleton, the proportion of limb to torso, the particular depth of the chest that a life of cold air requires.

The hands are large relative to the rest. This draws attention immediately — how the hands exceed expectation, how they arrive first in your perception and then you work backward to understand the body behind them. These are hands evolved for fine work, which is not the right word because no single life evolved them, but shaped: by weaving, by the manipulation of small tools against clay, by the harvest-comb through spider-silk webs, by root-work and wall-work and the patient chemical negotiation that passes a fern from one careful hold to another. The fingers are very slightly wider at the tip than most fingers are, as though each finger has been pressing against something for a very long time and has taken the shape of pressing. There is no soil under the nails because the work is too practiced to be careless, and carelessness with the fingers means losing sensation, and losing sensation in the fingers here is — not crisis, exactly, but serious, the way losing pitch is serious for a musician, which is to say it changes what you are able to know.

Their faces are — interior. This is the best word. They have the faces of people who have spent a long time not looking outward for information, not scanning horizons, not reading the weather in the distance. Faces tuned to the close, the interior, the particular quality of condensation on a wall that tells you something the wall needs. The eyes are slightly deeper-set, which may be the result of spending the bright overhead hours in a space that floods with directionless light requiring no squinting, no shelter of brow. Or it may be older than that, structural, the whole face having slowly concentrated itself inward over time. They are not an inexpressive people — expression moves through them constantly, but it is small in range and high in resolution, the way a small instrument played by a skilled hand produces a narrower range of notes with more information in each one.

They are mostly silent, which has already been said, but what has not been said is why, which is not from stoicism or ceremony but from a specific relationship to sound that comes from living in spaces where sound does not dissipate normally. The woven walls and the compressed moss of the roofs and the packed earth and clay absorb some frequencies and return others changed; the channels do not carry a voice but carry its pressure, its warmth, not the words. They learned a long time ago — they cannot remember when — that speaking into the settlement is speaking into something that listens and changes what it hears, and this makes casual speech feel imprecise, feel like throwing something soft into water and watching it blur. They prefer to wait until they are close enough that speaking does not travel. What they say then is exactly what they mean. The hollow has been an excellent editor.

---

What do they know?

They know the hollow absolutely. They know it the way you know a dream you have had more than once, more than a hundred times — not consciously, not as a set of propositions, but as a deep residue, a sedimentation of accumulated experience into something like instinct. They know which sections of the flagstone channels become unstable in the wetter months and must be stepped around. They know the sound difference between a woven wall in good repair and a woven wall that needs attention, because the frequency at which it breathes shifts slightly, like a voice hoarsened. They know every root-bed intimately, the name and character of every plant in it not as information held in the mind but as a kind of long familiarity, the way you know the shape of a person's shoulders before you see their face.

They know the sky above the declivity the way you know a window — framed, finite, informative within limits. They read weather with great precision in a space thirty meters across, which is a different skill than reading open weather, requiring attention to the specific way pressure changes behave when they push against the bowl of the hollow, how the moss-temperature on the northern wall tells you something forty minutes before the sky does. Meteorology practiced at the scale of a room, refined over centuries. They are very accurate within their area of knowledge.

Beyond the hollow they know very little, and this does not feel like ignorance to them because ignorance requires a known category of unknown, a sense of the shape of what you lack. They do not have this. The world beyond the lip of the declivity is not unknown in the way a foreign country is unknown — it is simply not within the set of things that concern them. Now and then someone goes up. They return with materials, occasionally — a particular stone for clay-working, a certain grass for cordage, once the dormice who have now been tenants so long they are no longer visitors. They go up without ceremony and return without ceremony. What they bring back is evaluated for use. What happens up there, in the wind and the directional light and the long distances, is noted and set aside. Not frightening. Not fascinating. Simply not theirs.

---

They have stories. This needs to be said because you might assume, from the silence and the compression and the editedness, that narrative has been compressed out of them — that they have lived too long in equilibrium to have anything that needs telling. This is wrong. They have stories the way the ground here has layers: not spread across a surface but pressed down into depth, accessible only through excavation, through the specific technique of sitting with someone for a long time in the low light until they begin.

The stories are almost always small in the way the hollow is small. They are not myth in the usual sense — they do not concern origins or gods or the establishment of moral law. They concern particular situations, particular moments of problem and solution or problem and failure, passed down as refinements of practice rather than as narrative drama. *The season when the water in the south basin turned,* they say, *and what was learned from that, and what was changed.* The stories are not satisfying in the literary sense — they do not have crisis and resolution so much as *condition and response*, and the response is always technical, always specific, always something you could do. This is what they think stories are for. They are manuals written in the first person. They are extremely interesting if you understand what they are. They are extremely boring if you expect them to be something else.

There are longer stories, older ones, whose technical content has been eroded by time until only the shape remains. These ones are beautiful. They are told very quietly, by the oldest, and they are about the hollow itself — about what it wanted, about what it asked for and what was given, about the conversation between the people and the place that has been happening since before any living mind and will continue after. In these stories the hollow is not personified. The hollow is not a character. It is a condition with preferences that can be learned, and the whole of their life together is the learning of it, the endless empirical accumulation of data points toward an understanding that keeps deepening but does not arrive, will not arrive, is not supposed to arrive. The learning is the point. The listening is the point.

They tell these stories about once a year.

---

Children here are — the word that comes is *incorporated*. They are present everywhere, mixed into the flow of ordinary work the way the dead are mixed into the living moss — not segregated, not separate, not in a zone set aside for smallness and learning. Small hands appear alongside large hands in the clay-beds, doing smaller parts of the same work, doing it wrongly at first and then less wrongly and then in a way that will be theirs specifically, a particular pressure or angle that will eventually be their contribution to the practice. No one teaches in blocks of time. Teaching is what happens when a child's hand is close enough to yours to see, and then the child sees, and then the child tries, and you are patient, and the hollow is patient, and eventually understanding accumulates.

The children are quiet in the way the adults are quiet — not because they have been told to be quiet but because silence here is communicative, because the hollow talks back to sound in a way that makes silence feel responsive rather than empty, because a child growing up in this place learns very early that you can know things by listening that you cannot know by speaking. They listen with an attention that you would call uncanny if you did not understand its source. They have been trained by the hollow itself.

---

What do they want?

This is the question that is hardest to approach from outside, because want implies lack, and lack implies an awareness of what is not present, and they have very little of that. But they are not without desire — desire is the wrong frame. They have what might be called *orientation*. They are oriented toward the continuation and deepening of what they have, the way a groove worn in stone is oriented toward the water that made it — not wanting the water, exactly, not waiting for it, but shaped by its passage into a form that will receive it well when it comes.

They want the walls to be sound. They want the water in the basins to stay clear. They want the compost in the sealed clay chambers to keep working through the cold, because this is always uncertain and always matters. They want the knowledge they have to be accurately transmitted to the hands that will need it after them. They want the moss on the roofs to remain extraordinary green, which they understand has less to do with wanting than with maintaining the conditions under which the moss can be itself, which is to say: they want to maintain the conditions under which everything here, including themselves, can be itself.

This is not a small want. It is, in its way, the most ambitious thing imaginable: to maintain perfectly, indefinitely, the conditions for a way of being — to resist all the forces that push toward entropy, toward change that is not deepening, toward the different rather than the more. They are conservatives in the original sense, the radical sense: they are conserving something. They do not think about this as a project or an identity. They think about it as the clay basin and whether it needs re-sealing, the wall and whether its breathing has changed, the root-bed and what it seems to want this season.

They are enormously skilled at what they are skilled at and uninterested in what they are not. They are neither happy nor unhappy in ways that translate cleanly; they exist, much of the time, in a state that is further back than either, a state before the evaluation occurs, the raw datum of being-in-this-place-doing-this-work, which has no valence.

Occasionally something surfaces. A kind of satisfaction so deep it takes a moment to identify — watching a wall that was wrong become, over a season's work, right. The particular quality of the overhead light when it floods the settlement in the clear-sky hours, a brightness that has no angle and no shadow, that makes everything equally visible. The sound of the old stories told by the oldest, in the low-ceilinged chamber that has been used for this for as long as anyone knows, where the walls have absorbed so many years of those words that sometimes, they say — they say this without mysticism, as simple reported fact — you can hear something in the silence before speaking that is almost the shape of what will be said.

---

They will not be here forever. This is true and they know it in some sense, in the sense that the long stories contain something like this knowledge, that the hollow is older than they are and has held other arrangements before this one, that the moss is not waiting for anything, that equilibrium is long but not permanent. They do not fear this. Fear requires a strong purchase on the self, a sense of the self as something that would be lost, and they hold themselves more lightly than that. They hold themselves the way the hollow holds them — as an arrangement that currently exists and is worth maintaining, deeply worth maintaining, worth every careful hand and every deliberate silence and every year of patient work, and not immortal, and not required to be.

When they go, the moss will remain. The flagstone channels will remain for a long time. The basins will slowly fill with whatever comes. Something will live here, as something always has lived here, drawn by the cold and the stillness and the particular quality of light that floods a bowl-shaped place from directly overhead and makes everything visible, without shadow, without direction, everything the same distance from the source.

But now they are here.

Now they are moving through the channels with their large-tipped hands and their deep-set eyes and their silence that is not empty. Now the walls are breathing at the right frequency. Now the compost works in the sealed chambers and the moss is green past the point of being a color and the bronze beetles cross the flagstone in the early bright hours.

Now is what they have been working on for a very long time.

Now is what they are.
