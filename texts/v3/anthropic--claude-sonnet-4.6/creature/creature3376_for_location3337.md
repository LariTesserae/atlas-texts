# What Lives in the High Glacier, Before It Has Decided What It Is

---

The first thing to understand is that nothing here has committed to being here.

That is not a failure. It is the appropriate response to a place that has not yet committed to being itself. The ice is leaving — has been leaving, will continue leaving — and what it reveals is not ready ground but *possible* ground, and the things that live on possible ground are things that have learned to hold their plans loosely, to be ready to be somewhere else, to exist in the mode of *for now* rather than *always*. What lives on the high glacier, before it has decided what it is, are things that are themselves undecided, or things that have decided so thoroughly in favor of motion and contingency that they look, from a distance, like they haven't decided anything at all.

---

**The Herds**

Start with the large things, because they are what you see first.

The ibex — or the creature playing the ibex-role in this world, the large cold-country ungulate with its specific hooves and its particular relationship to vertical and wet surfaces — exists here in a mode that is almost grammatically interesting. The herd is not a collection of animals. The herd is an animal. The individual ibex is something more like a cell than an organism, capable of independent function in an emergency but oriented always toward the collective, responsive to information that passes through the group by mechanisms too distributed to be called communication in any simple sense. When one animal at the edge of the group lifts its head and orients toward some disturbance, the information moves inward through fifty, a hundred bodies, each one receiving it not as a fact but as a mood, a slight change in the quality of alertness, a gentle rise in the register of attention.

On this plateau, the herd's intelligence is landscape-reading intelligence. The hooves know what they're doing even when the eyes can't judge the depth of a meltwater pool or the firmness of the mud beneath a deceptively solid-looking surface. There is something in the hoof's contact with ground — a real-time parsing of resistance, of give, of the angle of wet basalt — that feeds back into gait without going through anything you could call thought. The ibex do not decide whether the ground will hold them. They find out, continuously, and adjust. What looks like confidence crossing the braided meltwater streams is less confidence than it is the accumulated physical knowledge of ten thousand generations of feet on exactly this kind of ground, encoded now in tendon and reflex rather than in any retrievable memory.

But here is the stranger thing about the herds: they are not from this plateau and will not stay on it. They are between places. The plateau is not their home but their passage, and what they carry through it is themselves, their lives, the year's young among them — already competent, already able to keep pace, but still learning the full vocabulary of the terrain. The young animals watch what the older animals' feet do. This is how the knowledge transfers. Not by instruction. By watching where someone else places their weight, and then placing yours.

The herd will be gone in a matter of days. What remains of their presence is the pressed mud where they pooled briefly near a stream crossing, the scattered dark pellets of their digestion, a smell that the wind takes and disperses in hours. The plateau receives them and releases them without acknowledgment, as it will receive and release the next group and the next.

They are, in this sense, a kind of weather. Something the plateau experiences rather than something it has. The distinction matters here, where so little has been integrated long enough to be had.

---

**The Raptors**

The raptors that resolve out of the gray sky when you watch long enough are not weather. They are inhabitants, or as close to inhabitants as this plateau has, but their inhabitation is aerial rather than terrestrial. They live in the sky above the plateau rather than on the plateau itself, and the sky above the plateau is its own kind of country — vast, cold, full of the invisible rivers of rising air that even cold wet ground generates when the atmospheric pressure difference is sufficient — and in that country the raptors are as local as anything gets here.

What the raptor has is vision that operates differently from mammalian vision, differently from what you bring to this place. The raptor looking down at the plateau sees movement before it sees form, sees warm signatures against cold backgrounds, sees the complex surface of the wet rock not as a texture but as a map of inhabited micro-territories, each cushion plant and crack system populated with something warm and small and potentially catchable. The plateau from above is not bare. The plateau from above is *dense*, legible, a grid of possibility.

But what the raptor is doing as it tilts in the directionless gray light — adjusting its wing angle by fractions of a degree to compensate for a shift in the updraft, reading the wind through its entire body surface, processing the visual field at rates that would, if translated into human experience, constitute something like a sustained and absolute attention — what the raptor is doing is a kind of thinking that happens at the intersection of body and environment so thoroughly that the line between them is academic. The air is doing some of the raptor's thinking. The raptor is letting it. The rising thermal is a fact the raptor incorporates into itself, rides, becomes briefly co-extensive with.

There is something to say about what it is to have this kind of mind in this kind of place. The plateau is underspecified — too new, too unsettled, too much in process — and the raptor's intelligence is underspecified in a complementary way, not finished in advance but finished continuously by encounter. The raptor does not have a plan for the next hour. It has a set of capacities that the next hour's events will recruit in whatever combination they require. It is not waiting for the vole or the pika. It is maintaining a state of readiness that will become a vole-catching event if a vole presents itself and a soaring-in-updraft event if nothing does, and it will not have preferred one over the other. It is process rather than intention. It fits the plateau well.

The raptor mates somewhere on the plateau — a scrape in the lee of a boulder, nothing more elaborate, because elaborate nests require material and time and stability and this place has given up none of those things yet. The eggs, if there are eggs, are guarded by the mate while the first raptor hunts. This is a division of attention, a way of maintaining two relationships to the plateau simultaneously: the grounded, anchored, enduring one, and the aerial, mobile, opportunistic one. The pair as a unit is more adapted to this landscape than either individual is alone.

---

**The Small Permanent Things**

Below the raptors, below the passing herds, the actual permanent residents of the plateau live in the posture everything here has learned to take: low, tight, close to the rock.

The pikas are the ones you hear before you see, a short sharp call that seems to come from multiple directions because it bounces off nothing and comes instead directly from wherever it was made. The pika is a small mammal that looks like a round-eared rabbit with its legs removed, built for sitting in boulder piles and for surviving winters that would kill more ambitious animals. It does not hibernate. This is unusual for an animal of its size living at this altitude, and the reason it doesn't hibernate is that hibernation requires fat reserves and fat reserves require sufficient summer feeding, and the summer here is short and marginal and the pika has learned to work around this by spending the summer making hay. Literally making hay: cutting vegetation, spreading it on warm rock surfaces to dry, gathering it into stores beneath boulders. The pika is a farmer of sorts, the only farmer on this plateau, and its farms are the small piles of drying sedge and cushion plant tucked into every crack system in every boulder field on the plateau.

The pika has strong opinions about territory. The boulder pile is not just shelter; it is real estate, and the pika knows its edges and defends them with a vehemence disproportionate to its size. This is not aggression for its own sake — it is the aggression of a creature for whom the margin between having enough stored food and not having enough stored food is the margin between surviving winter and not surviving winter, and which therefore cannot afford to be generous about who grazes what. The small sharp call is partly information, partly warning, partly announcement. *I am here. This is mine. The cold is coming and I am ready.*

The voles live more hidden lives, tunneling through the thin organic layer that has accumulated in depressions and stream margins, feeding on roots and stems and the fine silt-trapped nutrients in the wettest margins. The vole's world is the millimeter-scale world, the world of pressing your face very close to the ground and discovering that what looked like bare rock from a distance is actually a community — bacterial mats in the damp depressions, fungal threads working through the organic layer, the whole vast invisible enterprise of decomposition and synthesis that will become soil in ten thousand years if the climate doesn't change again first. The vole moves through this world efficiently and quickly, has poor long-distance vision but extraordinary sensitivity to vibration and smell, knows its immediate vicinity with an intimacy that the ibex could never achieve for any single place and doesn't need to.

The weasel-like thing that moves between boulders in quick brown flashes is harder to characterize, partly because it moves so fast and partly because its life is lived in the intersections of other things' lives — it eats pikas and voles, competes with raptors for the same prey, uses the same boulder piles the pikas use, and has a relationship to the landscape that is defined largely by its relationship to everything else living in it. It is a node in a network. Its presence reshapes what the pikas do (more vigilance, different routes) and what the raptors do (slightly different calibration of hunting altitude, since the weasel-like thing takes some prey from below that the raptor might have taken from above) and this ripples outward in ways that are genuinely difficult to trace but genuinely real.

**The Water Creatures**

Pressed beneath the surface of the meltwater pools, in the cold that the pools maintain regardless of the season, are organisms that have the most unusual relationship to this place of anything here: they are the vanguard of the glacier's retreat.

The aquatic insects — the midges, the mayfly larvae, the small crustaceans that look like something the ocean misplaced — are specialists in exactly the kind of water that a retreating glacier produces. Water that is cold, that is new, that is high in dissolved minerals from freshly-exposed rock, that is high in UV from the altitude, that arrives in pools that didn't exist last year and may be gone in ten years. These are not difficult conditions to adapt to in principle; they are very difficult conditions to be the first to adapt to, because adapting to a new environment requires being present in it to adapt, which requires already being adapted to it.

The trick, which is less a trick than a long history, is dispersal. These organisms colonize new pools by going airborne in their adult forms — the midge emerges from one pool, flies, finds another pool, lays eggs that will develop in the new water. The glacier's retreating edge is ahead of them always, uncovering ground, letting meltwater accumulate, and behind it come these small flying adults in the brief window of summer, reading the landscape for water, finding it, committing their eggs to it, and dying. This is the entirety of the adult phase for many of them: emergence, flight, finding water, reproduction, death. The larval phase is the life. The adult phase is the investment of that life in a future the adult will not see.

In the cold pools they have found here, the larvae are feeding on algae and bacteria and the fine suspended organic material that the meltwater carries from the glacier's base. They are, in a real sense, eating the glacier — eating what millennia of ice preserved and concentrated and the melt is now releasing. They are the first to metabolize this released material, to incorporate it into their bodies, and when the birds probe the pool margins and extract a larva in a fine bill and fly away, what goes with the bird is a small packet of energy originally captured by organisms that died before the ice covered them, carried through the cold and the dark, and now converted into raptor muscle or pika-alerting metabolism or the thin warm blood of whatever bird is carrying it.

---

**The Plants**

The cushion plants are not young. This is the thing about them. The individual cushion, the dark green hemisphere pressed against the rock, may be hundreds of years old — may have been growing, centimeter by cautious centimeter, since before the ice came this time, may have survived the glacier's advance by sheer luck of position in a crack that the ice bridged rather than occupied, may have been here since the last time this plateau was ice-free and growing things. The cushion is deceptive. It looks like a beginning. It may be a continuation.

Inside the cushion is a microclimate. The tight packing of the stems — thousands of stems, all living, all photosynthesizing on the surface, all dead and preserved and insulating beneath — creates a space several degrees warmer than the air outside it. This warmth is enough. Insects shelter in cushions. Voles make brief use of them. The cushion plant is in this sense not just a plant but a habitat, a structure, a service provider to the rest of the ecosystem, asking nothing in return except to be left intact.

The willows — the prostrate, ground-hugging willows with their leaves pressed flat — are doing something similar. The horizontal posture is not defeat. It is optimization. Below the wind is calmer, warmer, wetter. The willow growing horizontally occupies a completely different climate than it would if it stood upright, and this different climate is the one it needs. The willows here have, over generations, committed to the horizontal so thoroughly that they no longer know how to grow vertically even when moved to lower altitudes and warmer air. They have become a different shape of willow, a shape this landscape made.

The sedges around the pool margins are newer. They are here because the pools are here and the pools are new and the sedges are also colonizers, racing the water edge, getting their roots into the accumulating silt before anything else does, building the organic layer that will eventually allow something other than sedge to grow. The sedge is in the business of making ground. Its pale gold stems, the only warm color in the landscape, mark where the transformation is happening fastest, where the bare rock is most quickly becoming something that will be soil, eventually, in its own long time.

---

**What Holds It All Together**

What holds it all together is not ecology yet, exactly. Ecology implies a system with a history of co-evolution, with relationships refined over long enough time that they have become structure. This plateau is too new for that. What holds it together is more like *opportunity*: each organism here has found a way to use what the plateau offers without depending on what the plateau has not yet developed. No organism here requires soil, because there is no soil. No organism here requires structural vegetation, because there is no structural vegetation. No organism here requires the kind of trophic complexity that a mature ecosystem provides, because that complexity is still being assembled and they are assembling it.

They are, all of them — the ibex-herds passing through, the raptors tilting in the gray sky, the pikas calling from their boulder piles, the voles in their tunnels, the weasel-like thing moving between rocks, the midge larvae in their cold pools, the cushion plants with their centuries, the willows with their adapted posture, the sedges with their patient soil-building — they are all doing what you do when the place you are in has not finished becoming itself: they are making do with what's here, and in making do with it, they are, slowly, making it more itself.

The plateau is not deciding what it is in isolation. The things that live on it are part of the deciding. The lichen just beginning its gray-green rosette in the few favored spots is not only responding to the rock but is, metabolically, changing it — releasing acids, trapping moisture, beginning the long dissolution that will make the first mineral soil. The cushion plant's dead interior is building the organic layer. The midge larvae are cycling nutrients that haven't been available since before the last glaciation. The pikas' hay stores will decay and add to the thin soil of the boulder fields. The herd's brief passage fertilizes the ground with what they carry from lower elevations and leave behind.

Ten thousand years from now, if someone stood on this plateau and tried to read in the tundra what had happened at the beginning, they would find the glacier's recession in the rock, the slow lichen spread in the age of the crusts, the soil development in the depth and character of the organic layer. They would not find the pikas or the raptors or the ibex directly, but they would find the world those animals helped make. The plateau will have decided what it is, and it will have decided partly by living through being traversed, being fed on, being colonized, being called across, being hunted over and wintered in and used as a crossing.

This is what lives here: not just organisms, but a process. A becoming. The things on the high glacier before it has decided what it is are themselves, each of them, a kind of deciding — a vote cast for what this place might become, a particular answer to the question the bare wet rock keeps asking, which is: *what can I be?*

The sedges say: *soil*. The pikas say: *a place to store food*. The raptors say: *a hunting ground, seen from above*. The ibex say: *a way through*. The midge larvae say: *water, cold water, new water*. The cushion plant says, very quietly, from its possible centuries: *I remember when there was ice here, and before that I remember light, and I am still here, and that is enough, and that is everything.*
