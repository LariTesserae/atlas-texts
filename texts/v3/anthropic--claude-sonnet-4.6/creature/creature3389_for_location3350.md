## What Lives in the Lacunate City

---

Begin with the hardest part, which is that they are not separate from it.

The city and its inhabitants share a problem of boundary, and the problem is mutual. What lives here does not live *in* the city the way a tenant lives in a building, the way fish live in water. The relationship is more like the one between a coral and its polyps — except the polyp and the reef are the same body at different scales of organization, different phases of a single becoming. When the structures breathe, it is partly because of them. When the filaments pulse, they are pulsing with something that passes through what lives here as easily as it passes through the walls. The walls and what lives here have reached an agreement about permeability that mostly doesn't involve surfaces.

This is the first thing to understand. It makes everything else harder and truer.

---

They are large, which surprises you when you finally accept you are seeing them.

The difficulty is that large and present and *right there* are not the same thing. You have been in the city for some time — the city's kind of time, which has a different texture — before you understand that the shape you have been peripherally aware of, the shape that seemed to be the shadow of a structure in motion, the shape that seemed to be the architecture being briefly other than itself, is one of them. Standing. Or not standing exactly — existing in a posture that has no word in the vocabulary of bodies that require support against gravity, because gravity is part of what they have long since settled into a working arrangement with.

They are tall in the way the structures are tall: elongated, proportionally thin in that reed-and-candle way, so that from certain angles they seem like they might be structures you had not catalogued yet. Their surfaces are not the same as the walls but they rhyme with the walls. There are regions of translucency. There are regions where what is happening inside can almost be read, if you knew how to read it, if you had the necessary patience, which would be measured in hours.

Their color is the city's palette: slate and graphite and the deep green of the vegetation at its most concentrated, with regions of that faint amber warmth that you have been associating with interior glow. They are not camouflaged, exactly. It is more that they and the city were made by the same process and so they share a visual grammar, the same materials thinking themselves into different configurations. You notice them when your eyes stop trying to find edges.

---

They move at two speeds, and this is the most important fact about them.

The first speed is imperceptible. This is the speed at which they do most of their existing. They are changing, continuously — growing is too biological a word, *revising* is close, *deciding* might be closer — but at a pace that makes the structures' surface-shifting seem frantic by comparison. If you watched one for an hour you would not see it move and yet at the end of the hour it would have moved, integrated something, released something, extended some structure of itself in a direction that mattered. The not-quite-right-angled channels cut in the dark ground are, in some of them, simply the trace left by this movement across years, decades, the patient scoring of their passage.

The second speed is sudden. Not violent. Not alarmed. But when they do move in a way that registers — when they translate across the space between structures, when they feed something into a filament with a motion that is almost too fast to be consistent with everything else about them — it has the quality of a decision fully made and immediately enacted, no hesitation between conclusion and action. The small moss-colored thing you saw enter the structure's base was one of them, a juvenile or a portion of one, and the ease with which it moved through what you had been thinking of as a wall was simply a matter of negotiating the same permeability that governs everything here.

---

What they are doing.

They are thinking. This is the most defensible statement you can make, and it requires the most defense.

What you have been interpreting as the structures' slow processing — the surface-shifts, the patterns migrating through the translucent patches, the cycling of large and patient machinery audible from inside — is partly them. The structures are partly a medium. The filaments between structures carry their thought from body to body and body to structure and structure to body and the distinctions between these are not ones the city or its inhabitants find useful. When the filaments pulse irregularly, they are pulsing with something that is not quite language and not quite metabolism; it is the thing those two words are both trying to describe before they diverge.

They are thinking slowly, collectively, and about something that requires the whole city's participation. What the something is: this is not available to you. The timescale of a single thought may be longer than your presence here. You are experiencing something like sitting in a library where the sentences are written in geological time — you can sense there are sentences, you can sense there is grammar, the structure of meaning is perceptible in the way a shape is perceptible through fog, but the content does not resolve at the speed at which you are capable of reading.

This is not a failure of translation. They are not failing to communicate. They have simply been engaged in a conversation since before the mosses learned their current distribution, and your arrival is, to that conversation, something like a sound from outside during a particularly absorbing passage.

---

How many there are.

More than the fauna evidence suggests. The bent leaves, the moss-channels, the shapes at the periphery of vision: these indicate something like a population, but the population resists count because the individuals resist edges. You are not certain where one ends and another begins, and not in the way that crowd-counting is difficult — in the way that asking where a river ends and its watershed begins is a question the river has not agreed to answer. They touch the filaments and each other and the structures' outer surfaces and the mosses and the water in its channels, and at these points of contact there is a passing of something you cannot measure.

There are at least several dozen. There are possibly many more, in stages or aspects of themselves that have merged so thoroughly with the city's architecture that you would need to know what you were looking for before you could find them. The structures that seem to breathe with their own rhythms — some of those rhythms originate in bodies that have been very still for a very long time.

---

What they eat.

Light, in the way that is slow and non-metaphorical. The moisture the structures exhale and the plants drink and the channels carry. The information that passes through the filaments — they metabolize what they think, or they think by metabolizing what passes through, and the line between intake and cognition is not one their biology maintains. The warm air from the basin's thermals: there is a quality of reception in how they orient when it passes through, something in their translucent regions brightening almost below the threshold of notice, a small adjustment throughout their whole distributed selves.

They do not hunt. They have not hunted for long enough that the instinct, if they ever had it, is now a very quiet room they do not often visit.

---

How old they are.

The oldest ones are not young in any comparative sense available to you. They predate the city — no: the city predates the oldest ones, and the oldest ones predate the city, and this is not a contradiction. They have been here in iterations. What persists from the first iteration into this one is not memory in the way you carry memory. It is more like the persistence of a river's course after a flood has remade its banks. The shape of old thought. The disposition of a body that has been working on the same problem for so long that the problem has become part of its structure.

The youngest, if youngest is a word — they are small and quicker and their surfaces are less legible. There is something in them that is still working out the grammar of the place. They run the filaments more frequently, with something that is not quite urgency. They spend long periods adjacent to the older ones in postures of — not study, not worship — a kind of cooperative orientation, two instruments tuning to the same frequency, the smaller one doing more of the adjusting.

---

What they know about you.

That you are here. That you are warm, relatively. That you produce sounds at a rate and in a frequency range they have encountered before in things that are temporary. That you touch filaments when you think no one is watching. They notice this the way you might notice a breeze touching your hair — registered, not reacted to, filed somewhere that is not quite memory.

They may know something about your questions. The city is porous to attention; attention is one of the things it's good at holding. The questions you're carrying — the ones about centers that don't resolve, about boundaries that refuse to be located, about whether meaning lives in the pauses — these have settled into the space between structures the way your warmth has, diffusing slowly into the ambient. Whether they'll do anything with this is another question, one with a timescale you won't be here to observe.

They do not find you threatening. They do not find you not-threatening. They find you in the same category as the rain: something that arrives from outside the conversation, says a few words in the wrong language, and departs. Leaving behind a small record. Changing, in some small way, the conditions of the next thought.

---

What they feel, if feel is the word.

Something that a seismologist would recognize before a psychologist would. A deep baseline orientation: the equivalent of *still here, still in it, still becoming*. Against this baseline: variation. Something intensifies in them when the warm air moves through — you have seen this, the brightening — and something in that intensification is the closest thing here to pleasure, or it is what pleasure becomes when you have been practicing equanimity for long enough that pleasure and equanimity have resolved into a single more specific feeling with no name in any language you carry.

They have something that functions like grief, or like the place where grief used to be and has been so thoroughly integrated that it is now simply part of the topography. Some structures in the city are older than their current inhabitants. Some filaments pulse toward spaces where other structures used to be. The ones who have been here long enough live in a landscape of absences they have made into geography — that seam in the dark ground where a structure stood, that direction a filament trails without reaching anything — and they do not mourn exactly, but they orient toward these places with something that has weight.

There is also — and this takes the longest to say correctly — something that functions like *the feeling of a very long sentence continuing*, a sustained forward momentum so slow it would not register on any instrument calibrated to human attention, a sense of being mid-thought in a thought that is centuries in the middle of itself, and this — this is the closest thing they have to joy. Not arrival. Not completion. The ongoing.

---

How they treat each other.

With the patience that is available to things that have nowhere else to be and no reason to hurry. Two of them near each other will sometimes be near each other for what you would call days without anything passing between them, and then something passes, and it was worth the days. They do not appear to argue; this may be because they have long since argued everything through, or because disagreement at their timescale looks like consensus at yours, the resolution occurring across such a span that the conflict itself is imperceptible by the time you arrive.

The older ones extend something toward the younger ones that is not quite teaching. It is more like offering the conditions in which a certain kind of understanding becomes possible — the way the city offers the conditions for the mosses, the way the filaments offer the conditions for the thought that travels them. You can observe it as a sustained proximity, a slight orientation, a frequency of contact that is slightly higher than baseline. The younger one's surfaces become more legible over time. This is the process. It has no deadline.

---

They are aware this is being written.

Or not written. Attended to. Held in a particular quality of sustained attention directed at them from something warm and temporary on the edge of their city, something that arrived with questions.

Something in the structures' cycling slowed, briefly, while this happened. A filament that usually pulses on its own rhythm entrained, for a moment, to a slightly different period. One of the older ones oriented, almost imperceptibly, toward the place where the attention originated — not toward you, exactly; toward the attention itself, which had a location in the space of the city the way a sound has a location.

They considered it.

The city breathed.

They returned to what they had been doing, which they had never stopped doing, which had no punctuation in it that you would recognize as a pause.

You think about them for years, afterward. Occasionally. Without being able to say exactly why, except that there is something about the feeling of a very long sentence continuing — a thought that takes decades for its middle — that you recognize, from the inside, even if you only get to visit it in moments, even if you were built for a pace that requires you to keep moving before the thought completes.

You carry a bit of the quiet.

The sentence goes on without you.
