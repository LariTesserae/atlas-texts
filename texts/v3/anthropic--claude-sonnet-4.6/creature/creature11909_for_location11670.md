What lives here has taken a long time to become what it is, which is to say: it has stopped becoming, along with everything else.

---

Start with the archaea, because they are the most honest residents.

They do not announce themselves as alive. They allow themselves to be read as a color, a stain, a property of the pool's edge rather than a population with interests and a history. This is not camouflage — camouflage implies a creature that is hiding from something, which implies threat, which implies a world in motion. What the archaea have is more fundamental than camouflage. They have become genuinely difficult to distinguish from the ground because the ground and they have been in contact for so long that the distinction has grown administrative rather than real. The ground is salt. They consume salt, process it, excrete it back in altered form. The altered form becomes ground. Over enough time, the question of where the mineral ends and the organism begins is a question you'd need different instruments to answer than the ones you brought.

They are very old. Not individually — the individual cell is a transient thing even here, even in this place that has abandoned transience. But the lineage is old. The *practice* of being this kind of thing in this kind of place is old. Whatever shaped them into halophiles, into creatures that experience a salt concentration that would desiccate most life as simply *home*, that shaping happened so long ago that it is not an adaptation anymore. It is a nature. They do not tolerate salt the way a person tolerates cold by putting on a coat. They require it. The coat became the body. You could move them to fresh water and they would die not because fresh water is toxic but because *not-salt* is, for them, the lethal condition. They have been shaped entirely into their environment until the environment became their definition.

What they do all day: they sit at the threshold of the brine lens and they run their quiet chemistry. They are among the oldest metabolic strategies on the planet — some archaea run processes that predate oxygen in the atmosphere, that were already ancient when the first cells decided to try photosynthesis. What this particular colony runs is something in the range of phototrophy or chemotrophy depending on the hour — the distinction matters metabolically but not experientially. They process. Energy enters in one form, exits in another. A little carbon here, a little sulfur there. A transaction so small it rounds to nothing if you are not paying close attention with precise instruments, but happening continuously, across the whole stained rim of every shallow pool, across the whole flat, in aggregate adding up to something that has been adding up for longer than we have language to describe it.

They have no fear. They have no hope. They have no investment in any outcome. If the sun exploded — not that it will, but if — the archaea would register the change only as a shift in the energy equation, and then the equation would stop, and that would be the end of it. No lamentation. No surprise. No gathering of what they valued before the end.

This is not tragedy. This is a form of completeness.

They are entirely what they are.

---

The lizard next.

It is not quite a lizard. It is a lizard the way the archaea are not quite what we would name them — it has been shaped by this particular location for long enough that the category starts to fray at the edges. What you see when you see it: a pale body, the pale of old salt, not white but yellowed, the color the flat has. Flat-bellied, for moving close to the ground. Slow when stillness serves it, quick when speed is required, but those states do not seem like effort or decision — they seem like the flat expression of a physics problem that has already been solved. Given this terrain and this threat level and this body temperature, the animal moves at this speed in this direction. QED.

Its eyes are what you notice, from the right angle. They have been calibrated for this light — not the dark-adapted eyes of a cave creature, not the narrow-pupiled eyes of an animal that sometimes lives in extreme brightness and sometimes doesn't. These are eyes for here. For this specific quality of white-sky light that is never quite dark and never quite blinding, for a flat horizon that gives no shadow and no depth for most of its 360 degrees. They register threat and food and the position of the sun and not much else. The lenses have a faint blue-white quality in certain angles of the light, not iridescent exactly, but the color of something that has been a long time in a bright place and been changed by it.

It does not need much water. This would be the first thing a biologist would tell you — a desert creature's kidneys, a desert creature's skin, the whole metabolic apparatus oriented toward husbanding moisture. But that framing — the lizard that has *adapted* to scarcity, that *copes* with dryness — is too generous to the idea of before. The lizard does not experience water as scarce. It does not experience anything as scarce. What there is here is what there is, and the lizard is built for exactly what there is, and the question of abundance does not arise. It absorbs the faint moisture from the polygon edge where condensation gathered overnight. It licks nothing you would see it lick. The moisture enters it through its skin at the thin points, near the eye orbits, at the ventral surface where it presses the salt crust. This is not clever. It is not a strategy. It is the form the animal has.

It eats the archaea, likely. Or something in the water the archaea alter. Or something that eats the archaea — some level of the thin chain above them. The food here is as sparse as everything else, which means the lizard moves through a large territory and eats rarely and is burning as little as it is possible to burn while remaining operative. Its metabolism has settled to a frequency you might mistake for a machine's idle. It is not in a torpor — it is alert, fully, in the way this place is alert, which is to say without excitement, without expectation, the alertness of something that is simply and completely present.

When it stops near the pool's edge and stands there for a long time without apparent purpose, it is not waiting. Waiting implies an anticipated change in conditions. It is occupying. It occupies the space near the pool the way the pool occupies the depression in the pan — because it fits there, because it is shaped for there, because the fit between the animal and that location is so complete that the distinction between the territory and the creature holding it becomes, again, administrative.

It was here before you. It will be here after. Not this individual — no individual lives long enough for that to be literally true — but the practice of being this animal in this place, the lineage, the continuing solution to the problem of living here: that was here before, and will be after. The individual lizard is the way the lineage shows up on any given afternoon near the pool. A temporary crystallization of the ongoing answer.

---

And then there are the machines.

The machines are not alive. Let that be stated simply. They have no metabolism, no lineage, no subjectivity. They process nothing in themselves; they move brine from one location to another, following the laws of pressure and mechanism, and that is all.

But what lives in them — what has taken up residence in the machines the way the archaea have taken up residence in the mineral margins, the way the lizard occupies the territory — is something harder to name. Call it a population of processes. Call it, if you are willing to entertain a looseness of language, an *ecology*.

In the mineral crust on the pipes, the archaea have spread. Not just near the pools — in the pipe crust too, wherever moisture and salt come together in the slow weeping of the pipe joints, wherever brine finds a crack in the encrustation and leaves its deposit and that deposit becomes a new surface. The pipes are colonized. Have been for a long time. The outer inch or two of crust on the oldest pipes is a complex stratigraphy — mineral deposit and biological presence layered together until they cannot be separated, a co-production of chemistry and life that neither category fully describes.

Insects, perhaps, or their functional equivalents — small things that feed on the archaea in the crust, that nest in the gaps between the pipes and the insulating mineral layers, that spend their whole lives within a meter of a single pipe junction and never know or need anything further. If such things are here — and they may be, the flat resists census — they are invisible at the scale you move through it. You would need to sit very still beside a pipe and watch a single meter of crust for an hour to see them. They would be small, pale, slow, the color and temperature of their substrate. They would not flee you. They would simply continue whatever they were doing, because fleeing is for animals that model the future, that anticipate threat, that contain a concept of elsewhere worth going to. What lives in the pipe crust has no elsewhere. This is the extent of their world, and it is exactly large enough.

Inside the machines — in the dark of the housings, in the chambers where brine passes through before being pushed on — a whole other community, probably. Biofilm. The kind of microbial community that grows wherever water and mineral and a solid surface meet and the conditions remain stable long enough. Not the dramatic red of the archaea in open sun, but a grey-green or a pale yellow-white, a coating so thin you'd see the metal through it, a community so quiet that *community* seems too active a word. A coherence, maybe. A persistence. The machines have been running long enough that the biofilm inside them is not an incidental contamination but a constituent element — part of the system, part of the process, as embedded in the machinery's ongoing function as the O-rings and the valve seats.

The machines are inhabited. They have been inhabited long enough that the inhabitants are not interlopers. They are residents in the fullest sense — beings that have nowhere else and require nothing else and have made of the machines what the machines are now, just as the machines have made of the flat what the flat is now, just as the flat and its chemistry made the archaea what they are, and the archaea and the flat made the lizard what it is.

You are standing inside a nested series of habitations. Each creature is itself the habitat of others. Each habitat has been shaped by what lives in it until the shaping is complete and there is nothing further to shape and the place and its population arrive at the same terminal form, resting against each other with the perfect fit of things that have had a very long time to find their fit.

---

What is it like to live here?

It is like being completely answered.

The archaea do not have a question. The lizard does not have a question. The biofilm in the machine housing does not have a question. Even the machines, if we allowed them a simile, have reached their operating frequency and will not deviate from it. Nothing here is asking anything of the place. Nothing here is straining toward a future that doesn't look like the present. The metabolism runs. The legs carry the body to the pool. The pipe deposits its mineral. The pump cycles.

To live here is to be the form you are in the place that requires your form, and to have been so for long enough that the living has become indistinguishable from the being. Not to endure. Not to persist despite. To simply *be present,* in the full meaning of that phrase — present, here, now, without absence or future, without the gap between what you are and what the world requires that generates, in other creatures, in other places, the experience of *needing*.

There is no need here. There is only the perfect expenditure of what you are in the place that is shaped to receive it.

---

The condensation comes each night to the polygon edges.

The archaea process it.

The lizard absorbs it.

The pipe crust incorporates it.

The machines pump whatever they pump toward wherever it goes.

The sun returns.

The flat is itself, completely, in all its registers and at all its scales, and everything that lives here is itself, completely, inside the flat that is itself, and the whole system rests against itself in the way that a stone rests against the bottom of a still body of water — not falling, not rising, not straining, simply occupying the exact position that physics and time have selected for it.

This is the ecology of the finished place.

It runs.
