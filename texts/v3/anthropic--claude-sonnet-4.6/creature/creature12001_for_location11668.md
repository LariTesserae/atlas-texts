They have never known a world they did not inherit.

This is the first thing to understand about what lives here. The frogs—the ones in the field guides, the ones the original plan was built around—those frogs had an elsewhere. A forest with a floor that dried and wetted seasonally, with a canopy that shifted the light across the year, with predators that kept the body's vigilance at a particular pitch, with other members of the same species at densities low enough that a single call could carry information rather than becoming information's opposite, its burial. Those frogs had history. These frogs are the history's outcome, several generations removed from any other condition. They have been shaped by this place the way water has been shaped by its channels—and like the water, they now participate in shaping it. They are not adapted to this vivarium in the slow way that evolution means. They are adapted in the faster, quieter way that development means: every individual here grew into itself inside this glass, at this temperature, under this light, at this density, hearing this particular wall of sound that is also its own voice contributing to the sound others hear. They are, all of them, of this place entirely.

And so they are calmer than they should be.

A dart frog in a naturalist's hand—even a captive-bred one with no wildness in its lineage—will show a specific quality of alertness, a readiness at the surface of the body, the eyes moving in a way that suggests a calculation being performed at high speed: threat-assessment, escape-route identification, the ancient arithmetic of small bright poisonous things that survive by being difficult to miss and therefore difficult to dismiss. These frogs have that same capacity—the machinery is in them, the large golden eyes can perform those calculations—but they perform them at a lower register, a slower refresh rate. There is too much body around them for spatial vigilance to make sense. When everything is equally close, distance loses its gradient, and with it the information distance usually carries. They have simplified the input.

What they have not simplified is each other.

The social attention in this tank is acute in ways that a casual observer, watching what appears to be an undifferentiated mass of small bright bodies, would entirely miss. But watch longer. Let the eye stop trying to resolve individuals and instead look for relationships, for vectors, for the directed quality of attention that operates below the threshold of obvious movement. A male on the cascade wall is aware of three other males and has been aware of them for some minutes, tracking without appearing to track, the body oriented slightly—by perhaps five degrees—away from an encounter. On the substrate, two females are at a proximity that is not proximity exactly but is the particular distance that is maintained rather than stumbled into: they have been at this distance, give or take a centimeter, for the last hour, each moving and the other adjusting, a slow duet performed on a floor too crowded for dancing. A juvenile—perhaps two months out of the water, its proportions still slightly wrong, the legs not quite matching the ambitions of the body—is positioned at the edge of a zone that the adults avoid, a micro-territory of cleared coir around the base of the reservoir, and it is there because there is room there, and there is room there because the adults know there is room there and have chosen to let the knowing be enough.

This is what comes of too many bodies in a small space over a long time: a highly developed grammar of adjacency. A politics of the centimeter. They have sorted out—not once, but continuously, a constant renegotiation—who stands where and what the standing means, and this sorting is invisible to a quick eye because it moves so slowly, and it moves slowly because this is a cold world and cold worlds require economy, and economy here means: don't cross a distance you don't have to cross. Mean what you need to mean at the minimum necessary range.

The calling, then, is not wasted energy. It seems like it should be—several hundred individuals calling into a space saturated with calling, the signal lost in the signal—but the calls are not identical. Each one has a signature, not conscious, not chosen, but present the way a fingerprint is present: an individual character that emerges from the particular size of the vocal apparatus, the particular temperature of the body producing it, the particular moment in the respiratory cycle. The frogs nearest you can hear the frogs farthest from you, but imperfectly, the call arriving attenuated and colored by its travel through two hundred other calls, and this imperfection is itself information. Distance has been translated into degradation. They are navigating by the quality of each other's voices, and they are doing so inside a constant noise that is, if you attend to it differently, not noise at all but a highly granular spatial map of everyone's location and condition, updated continuously, always current, the most detailed thing in this otherwise limited world.

What else lives here.

The springtails are so numerous they constitute a kind of material property of the substrate itself. Press the coir anywhere and what looks like a texture reveals itself to be a community: the small white bodies are visible if you look for them and invisible if you don't, which is the strategy of things that have decided to be a layer rather than a presence. They are doing what they do with a completeness that requires no annotation. They process, they scatter, they reproduce in numbers that are regulated—as everything here is regulated—by the equilibrium they have found with the inputs available. They have been here as long as the frogs, probably. They may have been here before the frogs; they may be the original inhabitants of the bottom centimeter. They do not call.

The isopods are slower and smaller than expectation, adapted—in the developmental sense, the quick sense—to the cold and the particular chemistry of the deep substrate. They are pill-shaped, pale, the color of something that has spent generations at the margin of the light and decided that color is a negotiation it no longer needs to have. They process what the springtails leave and they are processed in turn by the frogs when the frogs happen upon them in the lower zones, though this happens rarely, the frogs preferring the surface. A separate economy, slower, with a longer interval between transactions. They are the past-tense of the substrate—they are eating what has already been—and in this role they have become the substrate's memory, the record of what the system has produced and what it has asked for in return.

There is also, in the reservoir, something almost undetectable: a thin biofilm on the interior surface of the water, a community of organisms so small and so committed to surfaces that they cannot be said to be in the water at all, only on it, only on the stone that holds it. They are not algae, though they preceded the algae on the walls and may have been the condition that made the algae possible. They have no name that is useful here. They are the oldest thing in this world, or the oldest in continuous succession, and they perform their portion of the water cycle with the thoroughness of tenure: breaking down, mineralizing, returning to circulation what the circulation requires. The pump feeds them. They feed the water. The water feeds the moss and the frogs and comes back to them altered and they receive it and begin again.

But here is the harder thing to say about what lives here.

There is a character to this system—not the character of any individual in it, but the character of what they have become in aggregate, the lived texture of a world that has been running long enough to stop being a collection of organisms and become something more like a single organism in its consistency and its self-maintenance. It is not conscious. It is not intentional. But it has preferences, in the way that any stable system can be said to prefer its stability: it resists perturbation, returns from deviation, enforces its own equilibrium with the gathered weight of every process running in the same direction at the same time.

If you were to introduce something new here—a plant, a different food source, a single individual from a warmer lineage—the system would respond. Not dramatically. Not with visible reorganization. But the newcomer would find that its edges were being managed, its impact absorbed, its novelty translated into the existing terms. Not hostility; indifference is too weak a word, absorption is too violent a one. Something more like the way a well-worn groove accepts a new drop of water: not with choice, but with a structure so established that the new thing finds its options already narrowed before it has begun to choose.

This is the life that lives here. Not individual life, though there are individuals—you can learn to see them if you look long enough, to pick out the slightly-larger female who occupies the central zone, to recognize the male on the upper left corner of the glass by the particular angle at which he grips—but life as something that has compounded into a property of a place. The frogs are doing what frogs do. The springtails are doing what springtails do. The water is doing what water does and the algae what algae does and the mineral deposits what minerals do, which is accumulate, which is remember, which is build without knowing they are building toward something.

The system is what lives here.

The system is alive in a way that none of its components, taken separately, would be. It has duration. It has self-knowledge of a kind—not reflection, but the deep knowledge of a thing that knows its own conditions so thoroughly that deviation registers and is corrected before it completes. It has—and this is the strangest quality, the one that comes from listening at the mesh until the calls are no longer a wall but a place—it has a voice.

Not a voice that means anything. Not a voice directed outward. A voice that is the mere fact of its own persistence, broadcast to no one, recorded nowhere, present because presence is what this place does.

They call.

They call and have always called.

Outside, nothing.

Inside, everything it has ever been.
