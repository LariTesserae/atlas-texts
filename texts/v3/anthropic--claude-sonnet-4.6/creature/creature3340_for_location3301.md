# Who Lives Here

---

Begin with the fact that they are not apologetic about existing.

This is the first thing you notice, or would notice if you could articulate it — the absence of the apology that high-altitude survival usually extracts from living things. The scrub at the treeline, the lichen on young rock, the ibex at the snowfield edge — all of these carry a quality of concession, of life acknowledging that it is operating at the edge of permission. What lives in the ore-cities does not feel that way. The people who built the stamp mills and riveted the thread-bridges into place, the jackdaws banking hard between the cable towers, the ferns that shoved themselves into the cooling-tower drafts and stayed — none of them have the aspect of things that nearly didn't make it. They feel, instead, like things that arrived and decided.

This will require unpacking.

---

Start with the people, because they are the most legible, and because understanding them will make the rest easier.

They are, genetically speaking, unremarkable — a working population drawn from the usual stock of those who come to extractive industries in difficult places: the dispossessed, the adventurous, the technically skilled, the simply present when the ore body was first found and the camp became a settlement and the settlement became this. Over two hundred years, they have mixed thoroughly. There are families whose great-grandparents spoke four different languages between them. There are family names that fused two surnames in a generation when the record-keeper couldn't parse one of them. They are of medium height on average, though the third plateau runs slightly shorter — the most recent arrivals, still sorting themselves into the altitude.

What they have in common is harder to see than a physical type. It is more like a calibration.

They have calibrated to the stamps. This is not a metaphor: the stamp mills set a rhythm, a deep ground-pulse, and the people who grow up inside it develop a subtle somatic relationship with regular concussion. Their sense of time is embodied differently from people raised in silence. They feel intervals. Ask one of them to estimate a minute without counting and they will come within five seconds, reliably, because their bodies have been keeping time against the stamps since before they could speak. This also means that in genuine silence — on a day when the mills are stopped for maintenance, which happens twice a year — the long-term residents report a sensation they call *looseness*, a mild dissociation, as if the ground has stopped providing evidence for itself. They are slightly more likely to make small errors on these days. Slightly more likely to reach for something that isn't there.

They are patient in ways that read, to outsiders, as stubbornness. But it is not quite stubbornness — stubbornness is refusal, and this is something more like *adjustment of timescale*. A foreman in the second plateau who has been waiting seventeen years for a particular repair to the ore-sorting mechanism does not feel, day to day, like he is waiting. He is simply in a process that takes the time it takes, and this is not a problem requiring resolution, it is a condition requiring only the appropriate relationship. This patience extends outward from practical matters into something more philosophical: they do not expect the mountain to hurry, and so they do not expect themselves to hurry either. There is a local idiom — roughly translated, it means *this is a glacial matter* — that is used neutrally, without frustration, to describe anything that will resolve on its own schedule.

They are physically capable in the unselfconscious way of people for whom physical capability is continuous practical necessity rather than aspiration. Hands that show decades of work. The particular leg musculature of those who climb daily over uneven stone. A kind of automatic spatial awareness — they move through tight spaces with unconscious precision, and in open spaces they often look slightly displaced, as if the room is too large to fully occupy. The old-timers know to a few inches where the edge of any given thread-bridge sits in dark or fog, not from active memory but from the accumulated thousand crossings that have written it into the body.

They are not, on the surface, a mystical people. They are workers. They maintain equipment and manage extraction rates and argue about shift-change protocols and know exactly what grade of ore they are processing and what it will fetch. They eat well on the ground floor — it is, counterintuitively, a city with good food, because the altitude and the industry attracted, over generations, a cooking culture built around durability: preserved meats, dense breads, pickled vegetables in varieties that would astonish an outsider, all of it calorie-dense and long-lasting, evolved for cold and labor. They drink steadily and not to excess, mostly. They enjoy music in a distinctive local idiom built around harmonics and drone.

But they also — and they would not use this language, would shrug at the words *thin reality* and change the subject — they also know where not to stand at certain hours, and they know which routes to take when coming back from the third plateau after midnight, and they know that the floor-voice is worth listening to even though listening to it in any conscious way only produces static. They know this the way they know the edge of the bridge: not from thinking about it but from everything else.

There are the old families who were here before the threads, when each plateau was its own small world. They carry something extra — an additional layer of calibration, not just to the stamps but to something older, the floor-voice perhaps, or the something that corresponds to the floor-voice in whatever it is that bodies remember across generations. They can be identified in the old district by certain structural cues: buildings that lean by slightly more than others and are structurally inspected twice a decade rather than annually, which is not to say they are unsafe but that they have developed a more intimate relationship with the substrate. The old-family people tend to take jobs that put them in proximity to the rock itself — not the mills, but the mine-faces, the surveyors' teams, the engineers who work the cable-brake platform and feel the mountain letting go of its ore a gondola at a time. They are not prominent civic figures. They do not usually seek to be. They are the ones the civic figures quietly ask when something is behaving strangely.

The newcomers of the third plateau are different in ways that have nothing to do with origin. They are in the process of calibration. You can see it happening if you watch: the slight disorientation of someone who hasn't yet absorbed the stamp-rhythm into their body, who still consciously registers the concussion as interruption. The careful way they watch where they're going on the named and unnamed roads of their improvised district, not yet having the routes written into their limbs. Their children are somewhere between: stamped into the rhythm already, but not yet comfortable in the marginal hours, not yet knowing without knowing what to do in the spaces where the city's rules become suggestions.

The children, generally, are fearless about the birds.

---

The jackdaws need their own section because they have clearly decided the city is theirs.

Corvids have always done well in proximity to human industry — the opportunism, the intelligence, the social complexity, the sheer persistence. But the jackdaws of the ore-cities have gone further than typical corvid urban adaptation. They have developed a relationship with the stamp mills specifically: they know the shift-change rhythms, and in the gap between one shift's end and another's beginning, when the platform clears and there are a few minutes before the next crew arrives, certain jackdaws — particular individuals who are known to the workers and given informal names — come down and inspect the machinery. Not randomly. Systematically. They move along the stamp arms when they are still, examining the wear points, cocking their heads at the lubricant fittings, occasionally pecking at something with a scientific air. Whether they understand what they're looking at is obviously not a question with a simple answer. But the workers have noticed, with the undemonstrative practicality of people who notice everything without necessarily talking about it, that when a jackdaw has shown particular sustained interest in a component, that component has a higher-than-expected incidence of subsequent failure. A kind of prospective maintenance inspection, conducted gratis, in the shift-change window.

Corvid intelligence is well-documented and nothing here exceeds what corvids in other places have demonstrated. The jackdaws of other cities use cars to crack nuts, use sticks to probe for grubs, cache food with sophisticated deception strategies. The ore-city jackdaws have simply specialized. They have found their particular application.

What is harder to account for: they do not mob the lammergeiers. The lammergeiers are, relative to jackdaws, enormous. The bone-breaking vultures at the eastern spire are a natural target for corvid mobbing behavior — jackdaws are bold to the point of recklessness about raptor harassment — and yet the ore-city jackdaws give the lammergeiers space with a quality that looks almost like acknowledgment. The lammergeiers, for their part, ride the smelter-stack thermals above jackdaw territory with what must be described as indifference, though an indifference that never tips into encroachment. There is an arrangement here. Neither species negotiated it in language. It exists anyway.

The jackdaw roosts in the cable towers give the towers a layered life: industrial architecture that has been colonized so thoroughly that the cable towers now function as something between machine and habitat, tended simultaneously by engineers with oil and by jackdaws with whatever jackdaws use to maintain a roost. Straw from sources no one has tracked. Objects of debated significance — the ore-city jackdaws are unusually interested in smooth round stones, which they gather and arrange in the roost in patterns that might mean nothing and might not. The engineers working on the cables find them occasionally when they go aloft: little deposited arrangements of stone in the accessible maintenance platforms, disrupted by the work and re-established, usually, within a few days.

The workers and the jackdaws have achieved, over two hundred years, a form of coexistence that has enough complexity to be called, at minimum, a relationship. Whether it is also a communication is a question the residents don't ask in those terms, but that they act on regardless.

---

In the wet quarter, something happens to living things that stay long enough, and the moss is the place to begin to understand it.

The moss itself — any moss — is a particular kind of organism: it has no vascular system, no formal means of moving water through itself, and so it relies entirely on surface contact with moisture, entirely on the immediate environment, entirely on what is actually here rather than what is transported from elsewhere. The moss is radically local. It cannot imagine the resources of other places.

The moss in the wet quarter has been in contact with the same leeward wall and the same cooling-tower vapor for long enough that it has started to show something in its color that moss analysts (who were consulted, once, by a university botanist who came up and spent a season cataloguing) describe as a *mineral signature*. The particular minerals of the ore body — the specific complex of compounds that makes this deposit economically valuable — have been exhaled in trace amounts through the cooling-tower condensate, deposited on the wall, taken up by the moss, and are now expressed in its palette. The moss is mildly iridescent. Not dramatically — you would only notice in certain light, at certain angles. But in the amber-lamp dimness of a wet quarter evening, the wall breathes very slightly with color.

The botanist who observed this said it was not unprecedented, but was unusual in the degree of concentration and in what she called, with professional restraint, *apparent responsiveness* — the sense that the moss thickened more readily in areas where she placed her hand, as if taking note. She struck this observation from her formal report and mentioned it only in a letter to a colleague.

What lives in the wet quarter in consequence of the moss: an ecology that has taken the moss as its basis and extended upward. The ferns that colonize the crack-zones are, up close, unusual — not in any way the eye immediately resolves, but in the fact that their fronds orient differently from expected, tracking not the dim natural light from above but something else, some gradient the instruments haven't been calibrated to measure. They grow toward warmth, obviously — they are thermotropic, this is not surprising. But they also grow, with a slight but consistent bias, toward the inhabited parts of the wall: toward the crack where the thing that leaves no tracks but leaves evidence of habitation appears to shelter. Whether they are seeking a heat source or something else is a question the question-askers have given up asking.

The beetles are best observed at the drainage channel edges, where they gather in the evenings, in numbers large enough to notice, doing something that is either a mating aggregation or something else, or something that is both. Their shells — the mineral-dark iridescence that wrong-foots the color-perception — are the result, presumably, of a diet rich in trace minerals from this particular ore body, bioaccumulated over enough generations to alter the chitin's structure. They are beautiful in the way that things are beautiful when beauty was not the purpose. They click softly against each other. In clusters, the clicking achieves a rhythm.

It achieves, specifically, a rhythm in the range of the stamp mills' cycle.

Whether this is coincidence — whether the stamp-rhythm and whatever timing mechanism organizes beetle aggregation behavior have independently arrived at a similar frequency — or whether something else is happening is a question the residents of the wet quarter have stopped trying to answer. The beetles click. The sound fits. Some things are allowed to be what they are.

The thing that crosses the third bridge at night does not have a description.

This is not evasion — it is a precise fact. The people who have seen it report seeing something, but the reports don't assemble into a consistent image. Some see a shape with volume, roughly the height of a large man but with proportions that don't resolve cleanly. Some see a displacement — a place where the ambient light, the cold amber of the streetlamps, simply fails to arrive, a negative presence, an area of absence. One person, who gave this account once and declined to repeat it, said she did not see it at all but *heard* it — heard it as a change in the floor-voice, a brief localization of the usual broad distributed sound into something directional, something that passed her and continued and diminished toward the old district.

What they agree on: direction. It always goes the same way. And the bridge, which arrives in a place that straight-line geometry says it shouldn't — the bridge, which involves some concession to something that isn't distance — the bridge seems, in whatever sense sense applies here, *right* for this transit. As if the slight wrongness of the bridge corresponds to, or enables, or is explained by, the thing that needs it.

The residents of the third plateau who have been there long enough have started to feel about it the way the old-plateau people feel about the floor-voice: not alarmed. Not comfortable exactly — comfort implies familiarity, and this is something one does not habituate to so much as reach an arrangement with. But not alarmed. There is a sense, spoken among the people who've been there long enough, that the thing crossing the bridge is attending to something, and that its attendance is not a threat but a form of maintenance, analogous to the jackdaw inspection of the stamp-mill arms. Something that examines, that assesses, that moves in a direction that makes sense at a scale of operation that the residents can feel but not fully perceive.

The children do not see it. Or they see it differently, without registering it as other: several children of the third plateau have been observed talking to something on or near the third bridge at dusk, the companionable half-attended talking-to-oneself that children do when they are working something out in their minds. Whether they are actually talking to themselves, or to something that for them is simply part of the environment they are calibrating to, the way a new resident calibrates to the stamp rhythm — whether this is what calibration looks like at its earliest stages, the city entering the body as a range of presences rather than a range of threats — this nobody knows. Nobody asks them directly. There is a shared sense that the question, asked too bluntly, would spoil something.

---

The thing in the south district — between the cooling tower and the ore-processing shed — occupies a space of perhaps eight by twelve feet of otherwise unusable industrial no-man's-land.

What is there: evidence. Specifically —

A collection of objects that includes ore fragments in grades from low to high, arranged in a progression that mirrors the sorting system used in the processing shed with such exactness that the first person who really looked at the arrangement assumed a worker had been sheltering there and set up a hobby-scale replication of their own work, and found this unremarkable. But there are no human tracks. No food waste, no biological traces. Just the ore progression, and additionally: smooth stones like the jackdaw preferences but larger, and what appear to be shed bird feathers arranged not randomly but in a pattern that has been consistently re-established every time the maintenance workers disturb it. The feathers come from multiple species. Some of them, the ones from the lammergeier — each one large as a forearm — should not be findable at ground level in the south district. But they are there.

Nobody has proposed an explanation that accounts for all of it. The naturalists who know about it tend to favor a distributed agency theory: jackdaws bring materials, other animals add to a common accumulation point, and the appearance of arrangement is a projection of human pattern-seeking onto uncoordinated deposits. This theory is reasonable. It does not explain the ore progression.

What seems clear, regardless of mechanism: something finds this space useful. Whatever the arrangement means to whatever is making it, it is made with enough intention that disturbance is regularly corrected. The response to disruption is maintenance. This alone — the maintenance impulse, the return-to-rightness — is the most biological thing about it. Objects don't repair themselves. Only things that have something at stake repair themselves.

---

The lammergeiers.

There are two, and they have been nesting on the eastern spire for three years — which means they chose this location after two hundred years of city, chose it deliberately, in full assessment of the thermals and the noise and the density of human activity. The smelter stacks provide thermals that are, from a soaring raptor's perspective, reliable and powerful, and reliability is its own form of abundance at this elevation where wind patterns are so frequently disruptive. The lammergeiers have found, in the industrial exhaust, a better lift than the mountain's own topography would provide. They have incorporated the stamp mills into their hunting routine: the vibrations disturb small creatures in the valley floors below, and the lammergeiers have learned to hunt in the post-stamp intervals when things are still settling from concussion.

They have been in the city long enough to know the shift-change schedule.

The bone-dropping is the thing that gets documented, because it is the visible and dramatic behavior — the long slow rise to altitude with a bone cargo, the assessment, the release, the plummet of the bone, the crack, the descent for marrow. But what the ore-city workers who can see the spire from the cable-brake platform have observed over three years is something less dramatic and more interesting: the lammergeiers, when not hunting, when resting on the nest, watch. They watch with the focused attention of things that find the watching worthwhile. They track the movement of ore gondolas on their cable-brake descent. They notice when work crews move through the south district. They — and here the cable-brake operators' accounts become slightly difficult to report neutrally — they appear to notice when the third bridge is in use in a particular way. There is a consistent pattern of one of them lifting from the nest, not to hunt, when the transit that doesn't have a good description is happening, and circling once, and returning. Not alarm. Not hunger. Something more like acknowledgment.

The lammergeiers are so old in their kind — so thoroughly ancient in the deep sense that a species carries its evolutionary depth in its current bodies — that they may be perceiving a range of the city's life that the newer species don't have the equipment for. They may be, in their unhurried circling in smelter thermals, conducting a long slow survey of all of it: the stamps and the bridges, the jackdaw arrangements and the moss iridescence, the things that the floor-voice informs and the children speak to at dusk. Old eyes seeing the whole picture. Not judging it. Simply knowing it is there, the way you know a place when you have spent three years watching from the highest available spire.

---

So: who lives here.

People who have absorbed the rhythm of industry into their bones and don't know where the rhythm ends and they begin. Corvids who have specialized into a relationship with the machinery, who see something in the stamp arms that corresponds to what the workers see in it. Ferns and moss that have taken the mountain's ore-signature into themselves and glow subtly with it. Beetles who time themselves to the industrial cycle by whatever mechanism beetles use for timing. Children who talk to the air near the wrong-distance bridge without registering this as unusual. Old families who know what the floor-voice is saying without being able to say what it is saying. Newcomers who are becoming, year by year, something they don't yet have a word for.

And then: the things that maintain the south-district arrangement. The thing that crosses the third bridge on its own schedule, in the wrong geometry, going somewhere that needs going to. Whatever it is that the ferns grow toward when they orient away from the light. Whatever the floor-voice is continuous with, at the lower frequency, at the larger scale, at the pace of stone that has not finished deciding what it is.

This is not a city where the strange things are separate from the ordinary things and must be acknowledged in their own special category. It is a city where the strange things and the ordinary things have been in proximity long enough to interpenetrate. The jackdaws inspect the machinery. The machinery produces thermals the lammergeiers live off. The lammergeiers watch the bridges. The bridges don't arrive where geometry says they should. The children know where to stand to talk to what is there. The parents don't ask them about it. The grandparents nod.

The rock is still becoming a mountain.

The city is still becoming a city.

The living things in it — all of them, the people and the corvids and the iridescent beetles and the untracked presences — are all still becoming whatever it is they are in the process of becoming, in the cold amber light, under the low clouds, to the rhythm of the stamps, on the not-yet-finished stone.

They are not in a hurry.

They are in a process.

These are different things, and they all know it, each in their own language, each in the part of the mind or the body or the whatever-it-is-that-knows that corresponds to knowledge for their kind.

The floor-voice runs under all of it. Informing. Priming. Warming.

Nobody built it. Nobody tends it. It tends them.
