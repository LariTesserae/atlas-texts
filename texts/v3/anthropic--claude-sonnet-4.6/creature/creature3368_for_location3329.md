## The People of the Plateau Town

---

They are difficult to count. This is the first thing you notice when they begin to appear — from doorways, from behind garden walls, from the far side of the pool — that you cannot easily get a number. Not because they hide, or because they are many, but because they move through the town's gaps with a quality of purposefulness that makes each appearance feel like the same person returning rather than a new person arriving. There may be sixty of them. There may be ninety. They are not abundant. The spring produces what it produces.

---

They are middle-aged, predominantly. Not as a demographic fact but as a quality — even the young among them carry a middle-aged patience, and even the old retain a middle-aged capability. The genuinely elderly are rare, or they are kept indoors, or they sit so still against the south-facing walls in the warm afternoon that you mistake them for the walls themselves until one of them turns slightly, tracking the movement of a cloud. The children exist but are not numerous. They play without great noise and not far from where the adults work. When they are brought into the adult conversation — handed a tool, given a portion of some task — they take it without ceremony, neither proud nor reluctant. They have been watching long enough that they already know most of what they are asked to do.

---

Their faces hold the plateau in them. The high-altitude light does something specific to skin over years — a certain dryness, a deepening of the lines around the eyes from the habit of squinting into a brightness that has no trees to interrupt it — and their faces show this, the lateral creases, the faint permanent squint, the weathering that is not roughness exactly but density, a quality of material that has been rained on and dried many times and has arrived at something settled. They are not beautiful or ugly in ways that seem relevant. Their faces read primarily as legible. You feel that you could look at one of these faces for a long time and continue to find it honest.

Their hair is worn practically. Women tuck or braid; men keep it short or tie it back. No one up here is performing a hairstyle. The wind would have opinions about that, and the wind has already shaped everything it can reach.

They are lean but not thin. The leanness is functional — there is no mass here that does not have purpose, that has not been tested by altitude and weather and the caloric reality of a spring that produces what it produces. But they are not struggling. Their leanness is the leanness of people who have found the right proportion between what they need and what they have.

---

Watch a woman at the grain mortar near the third doorway from the pool, north side. She works with both hands, the pestle in her right, her left stabilizing the stone. The rhythm is not fast. It is not slow. It is *timed* — it has been found, the way a comfortable walking pace is found, and it is maintained without effort because effort has been replaced by habit. Her attention is not entirely on the work. It is partly on the work and partly on the middle distance, the pool, the sky. She is not distracted. She is distributed. The work is happening in her hands and something else is happening in the rest of her, and these two things are not in competition.

This is how they work, generally. With a portion of themselves. The work does not require all of them, and they have learned to give it exactly what it requires and no more. The rest remains available — for the change in weather, for the sound from the cliff edge, for whoever approaches across the compressed pale path. They are people who have practiced partial attention so long that it has become a form of presence. They miss very little. They attend, gently, to almost everything.

---

When they speak it is without preamble. There is no warming up to the point. There is no particular ceremony around conversation beginning or ending. A man comes out of a doorway, looks at what you are looking at, says something about it, goes inside again. The conversation was complete. Nothing in his manner suggested it was brief. It had exactly the length it needed to have. Their speech is like their grammar of building: find the angle that holds, no mortar wasted.

They are not laconic in the way that implies withholding. They are not keeping anything back. There is simply not, up here, the ambient noise of unnecessary speech — the gap-filling, the relating of things already known, the maintenance of social air pressure through words. The air pressure up here is different, thinner, and their speech has adapted to it. What they say they mean. Silence is not uncomfortable. Silence is the ground condition, and speech grows out of it and returns to it without loss.

Ask them a question that deserves a real answer and they pause before answering. The pause is not dramatic. It is functional. They are finding what is actually true, checking it, then offering it. They do not seem to find this effortful. It seems, watching them, that they simply always do this — that there is no version of them that speaks before thinking, that the two processes have fused through long practice into a single motion.

---

What they believe is harder to get at. There is no visible religious architecture — no building that announces itself as different from the others, no structure built toward the sky. But there is something. It is not organized into doctrine and probably never has been. It is more like an attention — a collective habit of noticing the spring, the rim, the seep water, the particular behavior of the year's rainfall, the way the young trees are taking hold or failing to take hold along the drainage depressions. They watch these things the way other people watch a face they care about. Something is being read. The reading is slow and continuous and does not, as far as you can tell, reach conclusions so much as it remains perpetually in progress.

They know the cliff faces. They know which seams are currently widening and at what approximate rate, and they know this the way a carpenter knows wood grain — not through measurement but through accumulated looking, through the hand run along a surface many times, through the memory of how it looked last season and the season before. They have been here long enough for the geology to feel like family. Not in a romantic sense. In a practical sense: they know its moods, its tendencies, which of its behaviors are safe and which require watching.

---

There is a man who seems older than the others — it is possible he is not the oldest, only that he has the quality of being older, which may not be the same thing. He does not do more than others, or less. He does not appear to be consulted disproportionately, or if he is, the consulting is done quietly, through proximity, through the fact that work seems to settle near him. He spends part of each afternoon at the rim, looking out at the haze that is not sky and not earth. He is not looking for anything. He has looked long enough to be past looking for anything. He is simply maintaining the looking, keeping the habit alive, the way you keep a fire alive not because you need the heat in this moment but because a fire that is allowed to go out is harder to restart.

He has a quality that is difficult to describe without sounding imprecise: he seems to know where he is. Not geographically. Something less mappable than that. He knows where he is in time, in relation to the plateau's processes, in relation to the town's particular history. He holds the place's continuity in himself not as knowledge but as something more bodily — the way an old building holds the memory of every hand that shaped it in its worn surfaces. He is a surface that has been worn. Something is held in him. When he goes, some of it will transfer to others, and some of it will not transfer, and the town will continue anyway because it knows how.

---

They trade with the lowlands. Irregularly — seasonally, when someone needs to go down for iron, for timber, for things the plateau cannot produce. This is not common. One or two people make the trip in a given year, and they go and they return. What they bring back is absorbed without ceremony, the new timber going to the stack, the iron to the smith. What they carry down — grains, perhaps, or something woven, or some mineral the plateau stone provides — is negotiated quietly and brought back as goods or as credit.

Those who have made the trip do not seem notably different for having made it. They have seen the lowlands. The lowlands are not the plateau. They came back. This is the story of the trip, and it is sufficient. They do not idealize either direction. The plateau is where the spring is, where the walls are two feet thick, where the sky is enormous and close. The lowlands are where the iron comes from. Both of these facts are simply true.

---

What they make. The town is full of their making. The mortared walls, obviously, every joint a decision about what holds — the careful quoins, the slightly battered outer faces. The stacked kitchen gardens, the patient fitting of the pool surround. But also: the cloth they wear, which is plain and dense, woven tightly against the wind they have been building against their whole lives. The rope coiled at the tool lean-tos, even and consistent, made by someone who made rope until they made it well. Baskets. The wooden bucket at the pool, its iron band still holding. These are not beautiful objects in the way that draws attention to itself. They are beautiful in the way that a thing is beautiful when it has been made for what it will be used for and not for anything else, when the maker's only question was *will this hold* and the answer turned out to be yes.

There is pottery. Functional — wide-mouthed, thick-walled, the glaze not decorative but practical, reducing porosity, holding grain dry. But one of the potters, or perhaps all of them in turn, presses a thumb into the wet clay of each piece's base before firing — not a mark of ownership, nothing identifying, just a thumbprint, a depression in the clay that will remain in the fired piece for as long as the piece survives. No one makes much of this. If you notice it and ask, the answer is something like: it's what you do.

---

At evening they gather, loosely, near the pool. Not everyone. Not always. But enough that the pool area, which is quiet in the afternoon, becomes the center of a kind of unstructured assembly as the light changes. They eat, sometimes, in this gathering, or continue some task that can be done by declining light. They talk in the way they talk — without preamble, with pauses, finishing when the thing is finished. Children move between the adults, tolerated without being centered. The pool reflects first orange, then rose, then the first star, then more stars, and the people around it grow quieter as the sky asserts itself, until what remains is mostly the sound of the water and the fact of being gathered.

They know the night sky with the same quality they know the cliff faces. Not through charts or instruments but through looking. They have been looking for long enough that the sky feels specific — not abstract and universal but local, *their* sky, the one that comes over *this* plateau on clear nights. They have names for things in it that are not the names anyone else uses. The names describe how things look, what they suggest, where they appear in the year. The names are practical in the way everything up here is practical: they were needed so they were made.

When they go in they go in without extended goodbyes. The gathering doesn't end; it simply becomes fewer people, and then the spring is the only voice the night has, the continuous low sound of water finding its way through the crack it has been finding its way through for as long as the town has stood, and probably longer.

---

They are people who have found the right proportion. This is the only way to say it that doesn't fall into something falsely complete. They have found — through time, through subtraction, through the plateau's own instruction — what is needed and what is surplus, and they have quietly arranged their lives around the former. Not through privation, not through philosophy, but through long acquaintance with a place that told them, year by year, what it would support. They listened in the way they listen: partially, continuously, without looking for a conclusion.

The spring produces what it produces. The walls hold what they hold. The people are what they are, which is something like: sufficient. Something like: at home in the ancient sense, which means knowing where you are when you wake in the dark, knowing the sounds, knowing what the morning will require.

The bucket at the pool belongs to the pool as much as the stone surround does. The thumbprint in the fired base of every clay piece will outlast the hand that made it. The man at the rim keeps looking, not for anything, just looking.

The water seeps from the cliff faces, thin and continuous, and it has been seeping since before anyone came here to hear it, and it will seep after.
