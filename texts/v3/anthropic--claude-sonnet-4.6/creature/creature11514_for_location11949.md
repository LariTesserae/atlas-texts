# Those Who Live on the High Plateau

---

## The People First, Before Their Particulars

The first thing to understand about the people who have come to live on this plateau is that they arrived—or stayed, or were born into staying—without the place having asked for them. The plateau did not arrange itself for habitation. It did not moderate anything. What it offered were fractures and energy and the cold ongoing fact of stone becoming more stone, and someone calculated that these could be made into something livable, and the calculation was correct, in the narrowest sense of the word.

The people who remained after the first calculations, and the decade of construction that followed, and the quieter decade after that, are people who were correct in a narrower sense as well. They calculated that they could live here and they were right. Whether this has made them happy is a different question, and one they tend not to ask in quite those terms.

What they are, before they are anything more specific: people at calibrated pace. This phrase, in the description above, is almost thrown away—*the purposeful unhurry of people who have calibrated their pace to distances*—but it contains almost everything. They have not slowed down. They have not become philosophical about time in any way that would show up in conversation. They have simply, over seasons and years, learned that the distance from one part of the settlement to another is a real number, and that real numbers have implications, and that fighting implications is a particular kind of stupidity they have come to identify with the lowlands. So they walk at a pace that is not leisurely but is not hurried either. It is the pace of someone who knows where they are going and knows approximately when they will arrive and has made an internal adjustment so that the gap between those two facts does not produce anxiety. This is not peace. It is arithmetic. But it functions the way peace functions, most days.

## Their Relationship to Scale

The settlement spreads across four hundred square kilometers of stone. This means that neighbors are often not visible. It means that running into someone you know is not a common accident but a planned event or a genuine surprise—one or the other, rarely a middle ground. It means that solitude is not an achievement here the way it is in denser places; you do not have to work for it, cultivate it, protect it from encroachment. It is the default. What you have to work for is encounter, density, the feeling of being embedded in something larger than yourself.

This changes people over time in ways that are visible if you know what to look for and invisible if you don't.

People from the plateau are often described by lowlanders as difficult to read. What the lowlanders are detecting is a recalibration of social signal that happened because the social field here is sparse. In a place where you rarely encounter another person by accident, every encounter has weight. The casual social filler that lowlanders deploy—the phatic exchanges, the noise that says *I acknowledge you and wish you well and will not remember this in an hour*—doesn't perform the same function here, where every exchange is between people who have traveled to be in the same place or have been coincidentally routed to the same fracture-line path on some particular morning, which itself feels, in the plateau's quiet, like something mildly improbable. So the casual filler has, over years, been stripped away, or compressed, or replaced with something denser. Not coldness. Not unfriendliness. A kind of directness that lowlanders experience as omitting something they expected, and that plateau people experience as ordinary.

What they have in its place: a particular quality of attention when they're with each other. The sparseness of company seems to have increased the signal-to-noise of it. When you have been alone for a significant portion of your day—as most of them have, most days—the presence of another person is registered as a real event. They look at each other when they're talking. They listen in a way that has weight. They do not check their peripheral awareness for other stimuli while engaged in conversation; there are not, in most parts of the plateau, enough other stimuli to divide attention. The conversation is what's happening. They are present for it.

This can be unsettling. Lowland visitors report feeling somewhat over-attended to.

## Their Relationship to the Stone

There are people who work directly with the geological processes of the plateau—engineers and monitors and specialists in the self-managing infrastructure network, people who have made careers out of watching the plateau become itself and adjusting the city's tolerances accordingly. And then there are people who simply live here, who do other work, who are not professionally concerned with the tectonic, but who have absorbed it anyway through the soles of their feet and the calibrations of their buildings and the annual news of some new seam opening two kilometers north of the northern district.

Both populations have developed a particular relationship to the idea of *not finished.*

Lowland settlements are built on ground that is, functionally, done. The stone under most cities is ancient, compressed, weathered into something that behaves like permanence. The bedrock of most cities is so old that it operates as a synonym for fixed things. You build on it because it will not change. The plateau builds on rock that is changing continuously, that is growing into the foundations of buildings, that opens new cracks on a seasonal cycle. The engineers account for this. The tolerances are routine. But the psychological experience of it—the knowledge that the ground is not a given, that it is mid-process—does something to the people who live there long enough to internalize it.

They are not anxious about it. This is the surprising thing. A visitor might expect a population living on actively rising ground to be characterized by some ambient geological nervousness, some structural worry carried around in the body. But that is not what happens. What happens instead is something more like: they become comfortable with provisionality at a level that is not usually touched in people who live on stable ground. The buildings are not permanent. The ground is not permanent. The seams that opened last season were not there five years ago. The infrastructure routes itself differently as the fractures shift. Everything is working, everything is functional, and everything is provisional.

This is not nihilism. The plateau people care about their buildings and their infrastructure and their city the way anyone cares about things they have made and maintain and live in. But they hold it differently. The permanence that lowlanders experience as a property of built things—*this is here, this will be here*—has been replaced by something more like trust. This is here. The systems are managing. The engineers are adjusting. The stone continues to do what stone does. This has been the case for decades and there is no particular reason to expect it to stop. You proceed on that basis, and it is enough, and that is what trust is, at the geological scale or any other.

The oldest residents, the ones who have been here since the early construction phases, have a habit of touching walls when they pass them. Not deliberately. Not with any performed meaning. Just: a hand trailing across the bonded stone for a moment before pulling away. When asked about it—and it is, occasionally, asked about—they tend to say they're not sure. Something about the warmth of the material. Something about checking. Something they can't quite articulate, which is itself unusual for people who tend to be articulate.

## The Children

The children born on the plateau are the first generation that will never have to learn it, because they will have started there, and starting somewhere is different from arriving.

They play in the fracture patterns the way children anywhere play in the specific geography of their home: with an intimacy born of a thousand small experiences and a kind of ownership that is not legal or conceptual but physical, the ownership of a body that has learned a place. They know which new seams have opened and approach them with the appropriate mixture of genuine curiosity and genuine caution that is the only sensible response to a new crack in the earth, and which is, in this, indistinguishable from the adult response. They are not frightened by the tectonic because the tectonic has always been normal. The ground rising is as unremarkable as weather. New stone is just new stone.

What they find remarkable, when they encounter it, is stability. Lowland rock—encountered on trips downward, in the few places where the plateau people go to conduct business or attend events or visit the lowland relatives who didn't make the calculation they made—strikes the plateau-born children as somehow wrong. Not threatening. Not unpleasant. Just: settled in a way that doesn't feel right. Like a room with no air movement. Like a conversation where nobody says anything new. Some of them describe this later, as adults: the strange oppressive quality of bedrock that has been old for a million years, the sense that something should be happening that isn't.

The plateau-born adults listen to this and recognize themselves.

They also recognize that this perception is particular to their formation, and that it has limits, and that most things in the world are settled in ways they are not, and that this is fine. They do not evangelize geological dynamism. They have been raised in the plateau's horizontal quiet and have absorbed its indifference to drama. The rock is rising. The rock has no opinion. Neither should they.

## Their Shared Practices

There are things they do together, though *together* on a settlement of four hundred square kilometers takes some work.

They share a technology for coordination that works through the same infrastructure network that carries everything else. Signaling moves through the fracture system faster than anyone walks, and the system's capacity for something adjacent to computation means that coordinating the logistics of getting forty people to the same location involves a kind of organic routing that plateau residents experience as low-friction in a way they can't fully explain to visitors. The system helps. Not in a way anyone can point to; the engineers could point to it, technically, but the residents experience it as the slightly magical ease with which plans seem to cohere. As if the stone itself is, mildly, accommodating.

Gatherings are not large. The distribution of the settlement makes scale difficult, and the culture has evolved to treat this as appropriate rather than limiting. Things happen in groups of eight, twelve, twenty. When there is something that involves more people than that, it tends to be distributed: the same event, the same time, spread across multiple locations in the settlement, each location connected to the others through the network. Whether they are one gathering or many is a question that plateau residents do not particularly care about resolving.

Their food is imported more than grown. The plateau is not agricultural territory—the cold, the stone, the irregular precipitation—but they have developed cultivation techniques that work in the fracture network, certain root structures that thrive in the channeled underground, certain organisms that do well where the tectonic warmth seeps up. A fraction of their nutrition comes from directly below them, grown in the narrow passages through which their heat and water and power also run, tended by people who work in the same underground geography as the invertebrates they work alongside. These foods taste of mineral and depth. People who have eaten them and then tried to describe them use the word *clean* more often than any other, and then often qualify it: *clean but not simple. Clean like something that knows where it came from.*

## Their Silences

In a place where sound does not carry—where the wind takes voice and the stone absorbs footfall and the buildings do not reverberate—silence is not the absence of something. It is the texture of the place. The people who live here have incorporated it the way people incorporate any ambient condition of their environment: by stopping to notice it less, and thereby letting it become part of their structure.

They are comfortable with silence in conversation in a way that lowlanders often interpret as discomfort or displeasure. A plateau resident who has nothing to add to what has been said will add nothing, and will let the silence sit, and will wait for whoever is speaking to continue or not continue, and will regard this pause as a sign of appropriate functioning. The lowland habit of filling silence—with sound, with gesture, with the verbal equivalents of nervous movement—strikes them as effortful in a way they have trouble understanding. Silence is simply what happens when there's nothing to say. Why would you fight that?

The children learn this before they learn almost anything else, because the plateau teaches it without trying. The wind takes the sound. The stone takes the rest. You speak when you have something to say and you are quiet when you don't, and between people who understand this, a silence can carry an enormous amount of content—comfort, or thought, or shared attention to something third, like the way the light has shifted or the low frequency of the machinery rising through the soles of your feet from whatever the infrastructure is doing this morning in the rock below.

They are not, for all this, a cold people. The warmth is real; it is simply distributed differently. It comes out in the quality of attention already described, and in the way they treat guests—with a deliberateness that a visitor might initially read as formality before understanding it as care—and in the particular gentleness they extend to the fauna with which they share the place. The birds. The transiting ungulates. The invertebrates in the network. These things are not them, and the distinction is clear, but the distinction is not a hierarchy of dismissal. The birds have been here longer than the city. The plateau tolerated life before it tolerated construction. There is, among the people, a mild but genuine acknowledgment that they arrived into an ongoing situation and that they are, whatever else they are, participants in it rather than authors of it.

## The Engineers, Specifically

The engineers who work with the tectonic infrastructure deserve their own moment, because they are a specific kind of person who becomes more specific through their work.

They start, most of them, as lowlanders. The plateau's infrastructure engineering requires a background that can only be gotten elsewhere, at institutions that have not yet emerged on the plateau itself. They come up with training and then they encounter the actual system—the network that manages itself, the computation happening in the rock that no one talks about officially, the tolerances that account for growth—and they change.

Not quickly. Not dramatically. But over years, the engineers who stay on the plateau begin to operate from a different model of what an engineered system is. Lowland engineering is premised on control: you specify a system's behavior in advance, you build it to spec, you maintain it against drift. The plateau's infrastructure is premised on something closer to relationship. You understand the tendencies of the system. You adjust at the margins. You watch for signals that something is shifting and you respond to them. You do not control the fractures; you have been working with the fractures for decades and you have a relationship with them, which is different. You do not control the tectonic energy; you receive what the plateau offers and route it. The distinction seems philosophical until it becomes practical, which it does, usually in some moment when a lowland-trained instinct is about to produce an error and the accumulated years of plateau work intervene, and the engineer knows something differently than they would have known it otherwise.

These are the people most likely to trail their hands on walls when passing. These are the people most likely, when asked how the system is doing, to pause in a way that seems like they're listening before answering. Their models of the infrastructure and the geology have, after long enough, become hard to separate from each other. They think of the plateau as a system they are part of, not a system they operate. Whether this is a cognitive distortion or a more accurate model than the alternative is a question that the plateau's ongoing functionality does not help resolve. Both positions remain defensible. The engineers seem, mostly, not to need it resolved.

## What They Know That Others Don't

They know what it is to live with ground that is becoming.

This is not obvious knowledge and it is not easy to transmit. People who have not lived it can be told about it, can understand it conceptually, can appreciate that the plateau is rising and the stone is new and the infrastructure routes itself through cracks that were not there before. They can know all of this and still not know what the plateau-resident knows, which is the felt sense of provisionality-as-normal, the body's understanding that the ground is mid-process and this is alright, this has always been alright, the systems are managing, the stone continues.

What this knowledge produces, when it has had long enough to settle into a person, is something that looks from the outside like equanimity and feels from the inside like something more structural than that. Not peace—that word implies an achievement, a resolution. More like: a baseline. An adjustment to a reality that many people never encounter, which is that the stable things are not as stable as they seem, and this is not catastrophic, and the appropriate response is neither denial nor despair but the purposeful unhurry of someone who has calibrated their pace to the distances involved.

This is what they have, living where they live. It is not a philosophy they have developed. It is something the plateau taught them by being what it is—by rising, quietly, without drama, and taking them up with it, and continuing to do so, as far as anyone can tell, for the foreseeable geological future.

The city continues. The plateau continues. The fractures open on their seasonal cycle and the new stone catches the light at angles that older stone can't. In the mornings, the temperature swings bead water on the flat stone and the birds are the loudest thing. People move between structures at the pace of people who know what they're doing and know approximately when they'll arrive, and the sound of their footsteps is taken by the wind before it can accumulate into anything, and the low frequency of the infrastructure rises through the stone, and the place is quiet, and the place is working, and below it all, constantly, something deep and tectonic continues its unfinished work, indifferent and generative, not knowing and not needing to know what has been built above.
