## What Lives There

---

Begin with the obvious thing, which is also the easiest thing to overlook: what lives on the plateau is primarily the plateau itself.

Not metaphorically. The plateau has been in the business of living long enough that it has developed something like a metabolism — slow, distributed, operating across timescales that make individual organisms look like brief perturbations in a longer signal. The water moves through it in cycles. The heat accumulates and disperses. The stone processes the biological stain the way a body processes something it has learned to use rather than reject. When you ask what lives there, you are asking a question that has the plateau as at least part of its answer, and any account that doesn't start here will struggle to explain why everything else is the way it is.

The plateau lives the way that coral lives, or the way that an old forest lives — not as a collection of things that happen to be collocated, but as a system so densely cross-referenced that the boundary between organism and environment has become a conceptual convenience rather than a biological fact. This matters for what follows.

---

**The People**

They are the most recent large addition to the plateau's ecology, which means they have been there for perhaps twenty or thirty generations, which means they have been there long enough that the distinction between *people who live on the plateau* and *people who were shaped by the plateau* has become meaningless.

They do not have the anxious relationship with their environment that lowland city-dwellers tend to develop — that low-grade background awareness of nature as something outside, as weather to be managed, as the wild that presses at the city's edges. They do not experience the plateau as separate from themselves. This is not philosophy; it is something more like posture, more like the specific knowledge that lives in the body rather than the mind. They know, in the way that experienced sailors know water, that the ground they walk is part of a system they are also part of, and that the system has been here longer and will be here longer and has its own pace and its own preferences and the reasonable thing is to understand those and work within them.

This gives them a particular quality of attention. They are watchers — not suspicious, not nervous, but *attentive* in the way that people are attentive when they understand that their environment is communicating something and that it's worth knowing what. They read the puddles in the street-depressions for information about the subsidence beneath, the same way their grandparents read them, the same way their grandchildren will. They notice when the bat-columns shift their emergence point by three chimneys to the east, because three chimneys to the east is a specific location with specific characteristics and the shift tells them something. They have not codified this knowledge into an institution; it is transmitted in the ordinary way of life, through proximity and observation and the slow accretion of understanding.

They are not, as a group, particularly interested in the world outside the plateau, which is to say they are not incurious about it — they are just not oriented toward it. The plateau is large enough and dense enough and old enough that a person could spend a lifetime learning it and still be surprised by what they hadn't known. Most of them do exactly this. The people who spend their whole working lives on a single district's canal-freight routes are not limited people; they are people who have correctly identified that the territory is large enough to reward sustained attention.

They are, almost uniformly, competent. Not brilliant, not visionary — the plateau does not especially reward vision, which is a quality useful for seeing across distances the plateau doesn't permit — but deeply, practically, quietly competent in the things that their particular position in the plateau's life requires them to be competent in. A foundry worker who has worked the same station for twenty years has a relationship with that station — with its rhythms, its particular sounds, its tendencies, its failure modes — that is not in any manual and could not be put into one. The knowledge is in the body. It is competence as a form of intimacy.

Their sociality runs deep and close. Plateau settlements are dense, not just physically but relationally. They have been living alongside each other long enough, in conditions wet enough and loud enough to require it, that the social fabric is woven fine. They know each other's schedules and families and habits and peculiarities. They do not always like each other, but liking and knowing are different things, and they know. The canal-freight networks require coordination across dozens of workers whose domains overlap; this has produced a culture of coordination that extends well beyond the professional. They negotiate. They track obligation. They have very long memories for what was promised and whether it was kept.

The older people, in particular, carry something that the younger ones know they will eventually carry: a sense of the plateau's time that only comes from having lived inside it for long enough. They have watched the biological stain deepen by a degree they cannot articulate but can feel. They have felt the particular vibration of the works change when one of the old furnaces was modified, and they will feel it again when a new hall opens. They are not wise in the way that wisdom is sometimes understood — as a quality of transcendence, of rising above — but wise in the way that long immersion produces: a knowledge of the thing from inside it, inextricable from it, which is arguably the more useful kind.

They die on the plateau and are incorporated into its substrates, eventually, and the plateau continues at its pace.

**The Draft-Beasts**

They are, in the simplest terms, very large and very old in the sense that their lineage is old, and these two facts have produced a third fact: they are patient in a way that living things only become when patience has been selected for across enough time to become structural.

They are not patient the way that a dog waiting for its owner is patient — that is performed stillness over agitation. They are patient the way that stone is patient, or the way that water is patient: without alternative, without the apparatus to be otherwise, and for that reason genuinely and completely so. Stand near one that is resting between loads and you will feel, at some ambient level, what it is like to be in the presence of a thing that is simply present, without the low hum of readiness for the next thing that underlies the presence of most animals. It is not sleep. It is something less common.

Their understanding of the plateau — and *understanding* is not too strong a word; what they have may not organize itself into propositions but it is navigational, it is responsive, it is old — is at once simpler and more complete than the human understanding. They know the routes through their feet. The particular compliance of the brick underfoot, the slight changes in grade that the human eye reads as level, the locations where the subsidence has changed the camber of the street over the decades — they feel all of this in the continuous conversation between their hooves and the ground, and they integrate it without having to think about it in the way that thinking requires something separate from the integration. A team that has been working a canal-side route for several years knows it in a way that no map could represent, because the map records positions and the animals record something more like tendencies — how the ground behaves in rain, how the freight-smell changes at the approach to each storage house, how the sound of the works reflects differently off buildings at different times of day.

Their relationship with their handlers — *partners*, as local usage correctly insists — is the longest-running collaborative arrangement on the plateau, longer than any current human institution, and it shows in both parties. The handlers do not command, exactly. They signal and the animals consider and the outcome is negotiated in real time through a language built from proximity and pressure and sound and the particular quality of attention, the direction and weight of it, that the animals read the way a river reads the landscape it moves through. The handlers know when the animal is resolving a navigational question and they wait. The animals know when the handler is tracking something ahead on the route and they slow without being told to.

Neither party could have arrived at this without the other. It is a relationship that has made both of them into something they wouldn't otherwise be.

**The Canal Life**

Here the categories begin to dissolve, and they should be allowed to.

The shelled things move through the canal in their slow arcs, grazing the channel walls, processing what the water brings them. They do not have preferences in any sense that maps onto the preferences of the draft animals or the people, but they have something — call it a disposition toward particular conditions — and the canal has been providing those conditions for long enough that the shelled things have organized their population around the canal's specific microgeography. They are thicker near the drainage outlets, near the places where the covered sections emerge into open air, near the locks where the water is briefly disturbed and then still. This distribution was established slowly and persists.

The finned things navigate around the shelled things. They are faster and more aware, in the sense that faster awareness is necessary for their mode of life, and they have calibrated their activity to the canal's rhythms: the boat traffic, the shift in light when the afternoon clouds build, the change in water temperature between the covered sections and the open ones. They congregate at the boat-lanterns in the dark sections — light in dark water is a simple fact that they respond to — and the boatmen who have worked the covered sections for years have stopped finding this unsettling and now find it, if anything, a useful indicator of where in the covered section they are. The fish know the route, in their way. They have been on it longer than any current boatman.

Deeper, in the drainage channels beneath the streets, in the wet substrate between the channel linings and the surrounding stone, in the places that are not dark because they have never been light — there the life is slower and stranger and further from anything that has a name. Things that have evolved past the equipment for vision, past the need for it, feel the mechanical vibration of the works through the stone walls that bound their world and respond to it in ways that constitute something — not understanding, not quite reflex, but something between and older than both. They have their own relationships with the water and with each other and with the particular chemical signatures that tell them what is happening in the world they cannot see. They have been doing this for a very long time.

The canal life does not know it is part of an ecology. The draft animals probably do not know it either, in those terms. The people know it and sometimes say it and often forget to act as if it were true, which is different. But the ecology persists regardless of whether it is known by any of its members, because it is held together by something older and more reliable than knowledge.

**The Bats**

They live in the thermal gradients — in the zones where the exhaust-warmth of banked furnaces meets the cool moisture moving in from the plateau's atmosphere, where the venting systems of the older works create conditions that are, for bats in large numbers, apparently perfect.

The emergence is the plainest statement of their size as a population: that forty minutes each evening when the quantity of living movement can be heard as a collective breath, as an exhalation, as a voice without language. This is not a marginal population exploiting an edge habitat. They are a major presence. They process an enormous quantity of the insect life that would otherwise operate at very different densities, and the insect life structures the plant life in the canal-side spaces and the settled sediment and the growth that has found footholds in wall-crevices, and the plant life structures — indirectly, through many steps — the water chemistry that determines what the shelled things eat. Remove the bats and you change the plateau in ways that would take years to fully manifest and might be irreversible by the time they were noticed.

They do not, as far as is known, notice this about themselves. What they have is: the warmth, and the moisture, and the darkness, and the aerial certainty of their own particular intelligence — which is the intelligence of high-speed navigation in three dimensions using sound as light, an intelligence so unlike vision-based intelligence that it is almost a different relationship with the world — and each other, in extraordinary numbers, and the plateau's patient stone around them.

At dawn they return. The direction reverses. The breath becomes an inhalation and then they are gone, folded back into the dark of the vent-systems, and the sky clears, and the birds begin.

---

**The Biological Stain**

This deserves its own entry because it is, in its way, the plateau's most committed inhabitant: the most ancient, the most patient, the most purely adapted, the most indifferent to everything except the exact conditions that it requires and which the plateau provides.

It is not a plant. It does not have the organized structure of the things we call organisms, though it is certainly alive in the relevant sense — it responds, it grows, it produces and consumes, it has been doing this for longer than any named species on the plateau's surface. It is a community, probably: many kinds of things living in such close arrangement that they function as one thing, each providing what the others need, the whole capable of surviving changes that would end any of the members.

It does not spread aggressively. It does not colonize new surfaces quickly. It does what the plateau does, which is change slowly and with enormous patience, one millimeter's worth of depth per decade, widening its territory on the northern faces of the sandstone buildings by increments that no individual human has ever witnessed as motion. It has been in the same places for as long as the oldest buildings have stood. It is probably in places that have since been built over and is continuing in the dark behind other people's walls.

It is, in the deepest sense, home. Not a resident of the plateau — a part of it. The plateau has grown around it and through it and the stain has grown through the stone and they are now the same thing in a way that only very long time can produce. If the plateau is alive, then the biological stain is the part of it that has been alive longest and with the most commitment, the part that is farthest into whatever the plateau is becoming on a timescale that has not yet fully expressed itself.

---

What lives on the plateau is: the plateau. Which contains within it everything described above and more that has not been described because it has not been found, and more that has not been found because the finding would require instruments the plateau has not needed to develop, and more that will come in time as the plateau slowly, reliably, at its own pace, becomes more of what it already is.

The people are the newest layer. They are the noisiest layer. They are the layer most convinced of their own centrality, which is a very human thing to be convinced of, and the plateau has made room for this conviction without particularly confirming it. They are part of it. The draft animals are part of it. The canal life is part of it. The bats and the birds and the things in the drainage and the things in the wall-cavities and the biological stain are all part of it, and the part that all of them are part of is old and patient and wet and warm and thoroughly absorbed in its own slow processes, all the way down to wherever the bedrock holds its water like a promise that has been kept since before there was anyone to keep a promise to.

It does not need to announce itself.

It continues.
