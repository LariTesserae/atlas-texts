# What Lives Here

---

Begin with the mats. Not with the animals — the mats first, because they are the floor of everything, and because understanding them correctly changes how you understand what moves across them.

They are not passive. That is the first thing to release: the assumption that something so low, so slow, so dark must be receiving the world rather than acting on it. The mats act. They act in a register so extended that a human lifetime of observation would catch only a single movement — a mat expanding its perimeter by perhaps three meters over sixty years, feeling outward in the dark along the stress-lines of the stone, following some calculus of moisture and mineral access that predates every other organism on this surface. They are very old. The oldest are not circles but irregular forms, slightly lumpen, with the quality of something that has been interrupted many times and resumed — a perimeter that reflects accumulated history rather than ideal geometry, dented where another mat pressed against it centuries ago and then died back, smoothed where unobstructed growth found its pace. The chemical signals they broadcast to maintain their territories are not simple. Researchers who have managed to parse some of their signaling chemistry describe something that functions less like a keep-out boundary and more like a detailed autobiography — a continuous broadcast of what this mat has processed, what it has survived, what it currently holds. The neighboring mats receive this. They adjust. There is, across the surface of the plateau, a low, slow conversation occurring continuously in dissolved compounds, a mutual disclosure network that operates at the timescale of decades, that began before anything currently walking here was born, and that carries a cumulative record of everything the surface chemistry has done and been.

Press your palm to a mat. Hold it there. The warmth you feel is not the warmth of the stone beneath — it is the mat's own warmth, generated by metabolic processes that run slightly, persistently above ambient. The mat is warmer than the air. It has chosen this. The energy cost of maintaining that differential is not small, and the plateau ecology affords it, which tells you something about the mat's relationship to the subterranean processors — the colony-organisms are not just providing mineral inputs; they are providing something richer, a chemical subsidy that lets the mat run hot. In exchange — and this will become clearer — the mat provides shelter, and something else, something that takes longer to articulate.

---

Now the things that feed on the mats.

They are low. Longer than they first appear — what looks like a stone of forty centimeters resolves, when it moves, into something nearly a meter from foremost limbs to trailing edge, but the body height is perhaps eight centimeters, ten at most, and it is this laterally-compressed, ground-hugging architecture that makes them invisible until they shift. They have many legs — ten, twelve, the exact number varies by individual and somewhat by age, as legs can be regenerated after loss and the regenerated limb is functionally identical but structurally slightly different, which gives older individuals a slightly asymmetric gait, a subtle list to one side, that the others seem to recognize and respond to with something that looks, tentatively, like deference. The eldest grazers move slowly not because they are diminished but because they have arrived at slowness as a refined state. They have time. They communicate that they have time, and the younger ones read it.

The rasping sound their feeding makes — and you heard this in the description of the place — is not uniform. Up close, attended to carefully, it has rhythm within it, a pause structure that is not random. The pause is investigative: the rasp stops, the organism holds still, the feeding surfaces (not quite a mouth, more an underside organ of considerable complexity) sample what the mat is broadcasting, and then the rasp resumes, or moves slightly, or moves significantly, choosing the next feeding site based on what was received. They are reading the mat's autobiography as they eat it. They are not simply extracting nutrition — they are navigating through information, finding zones where the mat is broadcasting something that translates, in the grazer's chemistry, as preferable. Not sweeter, exactly. Not richer. Something more like: *this zone has processed this particular mineral sequence, has been to this particular depth of the chemical history.* Certain grazers prefer certain histories. Over time, a grazer will return to specific mats, and to specific sectors of those mats, building a foraging pattern as individual as a signature. The mats remember this, too. The grazer's passing changes the chemistry at the feeding site, and the mat, growing back into the cleaned zone, produces something slightly different in response to what it absorbed from the grazer's feeding organs during contact. The two organisms are, across decades, altering each other.

They are not solitary, these grazers, but their sociality is not visible as grouping or flocking. Two grazers may be forty meters apart and functioning as a unit, coordinating their movements through the mat-network itself — each one's passage alters the local chemical signal, which propagates through connected mat-tissue, which the other grazer reads and responds to. They use the living surface as a medium, as a communication substrate. The plateau floor is, in this sense, a distributed nervous system that they have learned to operate.

---

The predators. The warm ones.

They are the most fully realized animals on the plateau in terms of what we conventionally mean by animal — they are mobile, they are clearly pursuing something, they have individual identities that their groupmates recognize. A group of five will have occupied together for years, sometimes decades. The relationships have the quality of something worked out at length, agreed upon, maintained through subtle daily negotiations whose terms have long been internalized.

The heat they generate is not even across the body. The flanks run hottest — those organs producing their biological-industrial warmth — and the extremities are cooler, and the face, which has sensory apparatus of considerable delicacy, is coolest of all, almost at ambient. They are aware of each other's heat signatures the way a human is aware of familiar voices — immediately, individually, below the threshold of deliberate attention. When a group member moves out of range, the others know. When it returns, there is a brief, subtle adjustment in the group's collective posture — not greeting, not quite, but an integration, a recalculation of the thermal field that constitutes their shared environment.

The hunt takes hours, and it is worth staying with what makes it extraordinary.

It is not deceptive. There is no concealment, no ambush, no sudden sprint. The plateau doesn't afford these things, and the predators have not evolved toward them. What they have evolved is patience that is active — a patience that makes decisions, that reads landscape, that thinks in hours the way other hunters think in seconds. They identify a grazer group. They arrange themselves in a loose arc, very wide, gaps between individuals large enough that the grazers, if they looked, would not read it as enclosure. Then they begin to narrow, so gradually that the rate of change is below the grazer's detection threshold for immediate threat. The grazers keep feeding. The arc narrows. Time passes. The arc narrows more. At some point, something tips — the distance crosses a threshold, or a chemical signal propagates through the mat that the grazers read differently from outside the arc than inside — and the grazers begin to move, and the arc adjusts, and the long slow resolution of the geometry begins.

What is remarkable is that the predators communicate during this process in a way that appears to use the same mat-network the grazers use. They have feeding organs of their own, differently shaped and not used for nutrition in the usual sense — they are pressed to the mat surface at intervals, reading what is there, but also depositing. Leaving signals. The signals propagate through the mat-tissue and are received by their groupmates, who adjust positions. They are coordinating through the landscape itself, the same medium the grazers use, which means the grazers are also receiving those signals — but the signals are layered in a way the grazers cannot fully parse, carrying content in registers the grazers' chemistry is not equipped to read. The hunt is conducted in a language that is secret in plain sight, transmitted through the most public possible medium.

The kill, when it comes, is quick relative to everything that preceded it. The predator is on the grazer before the grazer has fully processed what is happening. The warmth of the predator's flank, pressed against the grazer in that final moment, has been observed to cause a visible relaxation in the grazer — not shock, or at least not obviously shock — more a yielding, as if the warmth constitutes something. Contact with something genuinely, persistently, biologically warm in an environment of careful temperature calibration. Something old in the grazer's nervous system reading *safe* in the instant before it is not.

After the kill, the group feeds slowly, and the remains are not moved. They are left in place, and the mat-tissue beneath them will process what is left, and the colony-organisms below will receive the chemical downwelling of it, and the productivity of this section of surface will shift, slightly, for a season or two. The predators hunt in circuits, returning to productive zones, and their hunting patterns have, over generations, shaped the productivity of the plateau surface in ways that would take a very long time to untangle. They are not just fed by the ecosystem. They are the reason the ecosystem, in its current form, is what it is.

---

The gliders overhead inhabit a different relationship with time.

Below, on the stone, things operate in decades. The gliders operate on the scale of single days, their lives marked by the thermal cycle, by the condensation rhythm, by the intervals of rain that change what rises from the surface and when. They are not, by the standards of this place, long-lived — perhaps twelve years, fifteen, and in that span they will travel distances that make the ground-bound organisms' territories seem like rooms in a small house.

But they carry knowledge. That is the key thing about them. The navigational information in their calls — the deep, resonant tones that travel through the dark on a wavelength that finds the sternum before the ear — encodes not just position but what they have seen. A glider that has circled above a warm zone where predators are active will call differently from one circling above a section of bare stone. The difference is subtle enough that untrained human hearing cannot parse it, but the other gliders read it, and adjust their courses, and in adjusting, change what they broadcast. The dark air above the plateau is never, even in silence, without information. The silences between calls are not nothing — they are absence of particular signals, which is itself signal. Other gliders note which sectors have gone quiet and weight that accordingly.

They are not conscious of doing this. Or if they are — and this is a question the plateau does not resolve — the consciousness is so different from the kind that watches itself think that the word does not quite apply. They know things the way the hand knows the temperature of water. Immediately, without deliberation, without the gap between perception and response that would allow a self to observe itself perceiving.

Their forms are extraordinary and visible only at close range, which is rare — they land on ridge tops occasionally, for what appear to be social reasons, and then you can see that the wing membrane is not simple tissue but has structure within it, a network of darker channels that are actually conductive in some way, carrying the thermal information of the air through which they've flown. The wing reads the sky. When gliders sit together on a ridge and their wings touch, there is an exchange — a pooling of thermal maps, a sharing of what each has felt in the air. This is what the landings are for. Not rest exactly. Not courtship. Not territory. The transfer of information gathered in solitude, pooled briefly in proximity, carried forward separately into the dark.

Their faces are flat, the sensory organs distributed rather than focal — not eyes in the usual sense, but a distributed light-sensitivity that works at the threshold the plateau provides. They do not see the way you see. They accumulate light over time, building an image the way a long-exposure photograph builds an image, which means their experience of moving objects is different from their experience of still ones, and the plateau's slow-moving fauna is, to them, partly landscape. The mat organisms and the very old grazers may be below their threshold for motion. They see the predator groups moving, because the predators generate their own thermal signature that the wing-tissue registers even without landing. They see the planet's own light, faint and scattered through the cloud base, and whatever variation in it encodes the passage of large weather systems above.

What the gliders experience, subjectively, as they ride the dark thermals above the plateau — this is the question that keeps returning. The body is cold except in the thermal columns where warmth rises from the stone and the predators and the colony-organisms' surface expression. The calls move through the air and return, altered, from distance. The wings read what they pass through. Something aggregates all of this into navigation, into the choice of where to spiral and when to glide forward and when to call and when to be silent. Whatever that aggregation is — wherever it happens in that flat-faced, distributed-sensory body — it is engaged with this place at a level of sustained, embodied intimacy that most beings never achieve with any environment.

---

The colony-organisms below. The slow engineers. The base on which everything rests.

They cannot be seen, and this shapes everything about how they are understood — or misunderstood, which is more likely, because the mind that cannot see something tends to make it either simpler or more mysterious than it is, and simplicity is the more common distortion.

They are not simple.

The fraction of their biochemistry that has been analyzed through surface samples suggests something operating at a level of chemical complexity that exceeds most single organisms by an order of magnitude. They are not a colony in the sense of many individuals working in parallel. They are more properly a distributed organ — a thing that happens to be spread across hundreds of square kilometers because the resource it processes is spread across hundreds of square kilometers, but is functionally unified in ways that would make *colony* a significant understatement. The chemical signals carried in the water network through the stone — signals that coordinate activity across the whole extent — travel at the speed of water moving through fractured rock, which is slow, which means that any single coordination event takes days, weeks, longer. The colony-organism thinks slowly. Its thoughts span the plateau. When it responds to a change in surface chemistry — a new pattern of rainfall, an altered mineral input from a zone where erosion has exposed fresh rock — the response propagates across its body for months before it is complete. The decision takes as long as the plateau takes to make a season.

What it outputs — the chemical subsidy that the mat-plants depend on, the trace elements that the stalked condensation-catchers require, the warmth that surfaces in patches across the stone — these are not byproducts. They are not waste. The colony-organism is producing them deliberately, in the sense that the production is regulated, is tuned to the surface ecology's current state, is adjusted when the surface ecology changes. It is feeding the surface. Not altruistically — the surface feeds it in return, the organic material that seeps down through the fractures from everything that lives and dies above. The relationship is so old and so thoroughly mutual that the distinction between what the colony-organism does for the surface ecology and what the surface ecology does for the colony-organism has become somewhat academic. They are not separate systems that happen to exchange resources. They are one system that has forgotten, across geological time, that it was ever otherwise.

The thrum. That low, irregular pulse that can be felt through the stone. It is not mechanical and it is not quite biological as those terms are usually meant — it is the movement of fluid through the colony-organism's extensive internal network, the pulse of its processing, the heartbeat of something that has a heart the size of a landmass. It runs at its own rhythm. The rhythm is not the same as the surface's weather cycle, not the same as the daily condensation pattern. It is older than those patterns, or at least indifferent to them, shaped by dynamics internal to the organism's own chemistry. But it influences everything above it, because everything above it runs on what the organism provides, and the provision is not constant — it pulses. The surface ecology has, over time, adapted to the pulse. The mat-organisms' swelling and drying is partly a response to the condensation cycle and partly a response to this deeper rhythm. The grazers' foraging circuits have preferred zones, and the preferred zones correspond, without anyone having planned it, to the areas where the colony-organism's processing output is currently peaking. The predators' hunting circuits follow the grazers'. The gliders follow the thermals, and the thermals are strongest above the processing peaks.

Everything above is reading the pulse and has been reading it for so long that it no longer knows it is reading anything. It simply lives in a way that happens to be tuned.

---

One more thing, and it requires care to describe without overstating.

The various organisms here — mats, grazers, predators, gliders, colony-organism — do not constitute an ecosystem in the sense that word usually implies, which is a system of parts that happen to interact. They constitute something more like a single argument made in different materials. The mats are the argument expressed in chemistry. The grazers are the argument expressed in mobility and in the patient navigation of information. The predators are the argument expressed in warmth and time and collaborative geometry. The gliders are the argument expressed in space and in the carrying of news. The colony-organism is the argument expressed in geological scale and in the slow pulse that underlies everything.

The argument, if it can be said to have content, is something like: *here is how much can be achieved with very little, given enough time.* Or: *here is what stability looks like when it has been earned through adaptation so thorough that the adapters and the adapted-to have become indistinguishable.* Or perhaps it is not an argument at all — perhaps argument implies a receiver, and there is no receiver for this, only the expression of it, the plateau expressing itself continuously in the dark, in the warmth, in the thrum and the rasp and the deep tonal calls and the slow chemical conversations propagating through living surfaces.

To encounter these organisms is to encounter something that has finished becoming. Not completed — they will continue to change, nothing here implies otherwise. But they have arrived at what they are, fully, and inhabit it fully, and the inhabitation has the quality of something that no longer requires justification or intention or even awareness of itself. They are here in the way the plateau is here. With the same weight. The same warmth. The same patient, self-sustaining continuation.

The slow engines run. The things in the dark run with them.
