# What Lives in the Hollow Mill-Green

---

## I. The Oldest Resident

It does not have a body in the way that would let you ask *where is it*. The oldest thing here is the hollow itself — not the depression in the earth, not the geomorphology, but the *agreement* between the hollow and the chemical process that has been maintaining it. Call this a living thing if you'll allow that living things can be patient enough to look like landscape.

It arrived before anything else currently present. It arrived as an event: a moment, maybe four thousand years ago, maybe more, when the water-table crept high enough and the shade fell long enough and a mat of *Sphagnum* achieved sufficient mass to begin its feedback loop — began, that is, to manufacture the conditions for its own continuation, became self-maintaining, became a *project* rather than a patch of moss. 

From that moment it has been doing one thing. Slowly lowering the pH. Slowly acidifying the groundwater. Slowly building the dead-but-preserved substrate that becomes its own floor. Slowly sealing itself against change.

Its metabolism is geological in tempo. You could not watch it work. You could only be absent for a century and return to find it slightly more itself.

What it eats: sunlight, dissolved minerals, time. What it produces: acid, preservation, more of itself. What it wants, to the extent that wanting is a category that applies: continuance. Exactly this. Not growth, not expansion — just the perpetuation of this specific equilibrium, this particular chemical mood, this dampness held at precisely the margin between wet and wetter.

In winter it does not sleep. It barely slows. The *Sphagnum* continues its chemistry under ice if ice comes, and ice rarely holds here, because the hollow breathes a warmth up from the insulating depth of itself, a thermal blankness that keeps the air two degrees above freezing when the surrounding land has gone cold and stiff. It overwinters its own climate.

It is not conscious. It is not, by any reasonable definition, an organism. It is a process that has persisted long enough to become indistinguishable from a place, a community, a fact.

If it has anything like an intention, it is the intention of a machine that has been running so long it has forgotten the possibility of stopping.

## II. The Sedge-Clumps, Understood as Individuals

They are older than they look, which is already old. What stands above the moss — the bladed leaves, the reddish-brown flowering spikes in season — is the most recent thinking of a root system that has been deepening and braiding for decades underneath. The visible part of a sedge clump is a fraction of the sedge clump. What is mostly sedge is dark, compressed, tightly interlocked, invisible, and has not moved in twenty years.

But the individual clumps should be taken seriously as individuals. They have achieved *spacing* — the gaps between them are not accidents of competition or failed establishment, they are the result of a long negotiation about territory. Each clump has explored its surroundings with rhizomes and encountered the rhizomes of its neighbors and both have stopped. The gaps are borders. They are settled, mutual, old.

Stand at the center of the hollow and look at the sedge clumps from above — metaphorically above, since the hollow offers no elevation — and you see: scattered presences in open moss-ground, each one self-contained, each one at ease. The word that keeps offering itself is *composure*. They have composure. They are not leaning toward anything. They are not straining. They are simply occupying their particular piece of old ground with the complete attention of things that have never been anywhere else and expect to be nowhere else.

The leaves have that edge. That thin violence of the central rib that will cut you if you move wrong. This is not incidental; this is a long-maintained investment. The hollow is grazed, sometimes, by deer that come down the deer path and step into the subsidence without noticing and then stand, damp-hoofed, browsing. The deer avoid the sedge clumps after one or two reminders. The sedge does not pursue its advantages. It is not aggressive. It is merely *precise* about its defense, and it has had a long time to become precise.

In high summer the clumps release seeds from their flowering spikes — small, three-sided nutlets that fall into the moss and are generally swallowed by it and generally fail to germinate because the chemistry is too acid and the *Sphagnum* outcompetes the seedlings and the hollow is not in the business of expanding. The sedges continue anyway. They seed into the moss the way a person might speak a sentence into an empty room: not for effect, not expectantly, but because it is what they do at this time.

## III. The Sundew, Patient Violence Made Manifest

If the hollow practices a philosophy, the sundew is its clearest text.

It sits with its leaves flat against the moss, a rosette of round-tipped pads each fringed with reddish hairs that each end in a glistening drop. The drop is not dew. It does not evaporate in sunlight; it replenishes. It is mucilage, adhesive, produced by a gland at the hair's tip at metabolic cost, maintained precisely because the hollow's nutrient-poor acid water gives the plant almost no nitrogen from the substrate and it must therefore catch its nitrogen from the air.

The sundew is, at its core, a nitrogen problem that evolved a solution.

The solution is beautiful in the way that efficient things are beautiful. The glistening drops catch light and, incidentally, catch midges. The midge lands seeking — what? moisture? something — and the hairs curl inward, slowly, over hours, wrapping the midge in a chamber of digestive fluid, and the fluid dissolves what the hollow's water cannot provide, and the sundew absorbs it and grows another leaf and produces another drop.

There are several rosettes visible in the hollow. They will be in more or less the same positions next year. They are among the smallest living things in the hollow that can be said to have *strategy* — everything else just grows, just persists — but the sundew has architecture and behavior and patience. The patience is the most remarkable thing. A single leaf may wait a week before anything lands on it, and this is fine. It keeps the drop ready. It does not retract the investment.

It flowers in summer on a thin white spike that rises above the moss and opens one or two small white blooms above the level of the insect traffic — high enough that the pollinators are not caught in the trap, because the sundew also needs the pollinators, and it has figured this out over a long time, the separation of catching and pollinating, the careful vertical distinction between whom to trap and whom to welcome, and this too is philosophy, this is the hollow thinking about what it needs and how to hold onto it.

## IV. The Alders at the Rim

They are not old but they have done old work.

They came after the hollow was established. Seeds rode in — bird-carried, water-carried — and germinated in the slightly-higher, slightly-less-acid ground at the hollow's rim, and grew, and grew their roots down toward the moisture, and did the thing alders do everywhere which is to offer their roots as substrate for nitrogen-fixing bacteria, little Frankia nodules clustered on the rootlets, industrious, invisible, running their own chemistry parallel to the *Sphagnum*'s below.

The alders are, in a sense, a second factory layer. The moss acidifies and preserves below; the alders fix nitrogen and build root-architecture above. They are not competing. They have separated their work cleanly by depth.

The bark is the thing. Not the interior of the trunk, which is red and gives red to water cut through it — but the surface of the younger branches, which is silver-green with algae living in the bark's texture, a third organism in a symbiosis that includes the bark and the ambient moisture and the particular light quality of the rim's edge. The alders are never not damp. Their leaves are slightly sticky in a way that is not the sundew's strategy but the incidental product of their surface chemistry, slightly waxy, slightly resistant. Rain beads on them and runs and falls and the hollow drinks it.

Their faces — the faces of the trees, if a tree can be said to have a face — are turned outward, toward the surrounding forest and fields. Their roots are in the hollow but their leaves are of the ordinary world, working ordinary photosynthesis, and there is something about this that feels like mediation. They stand at the threshold with their feet in the ancient chemistry and their crowns in common light.

In autumn their leaves fall into the hollow and begin to decompose and are slowed by the acid water and partially preserved and add their partial decomposition to the substrate and the substrate grows the *Sphagnum* that acidifies the water. Absorbed into the system. Nothing escapes here. Not easily.

## V. The Frogs

They are here all the time but rarely visible as individuals. They are more a condition than a population.

What you perceive of them: the low churring from inside the moss, which is not exactly a mating call and not exactly a warning call and may simply be the sound of small cold-blooded bodies idling in warm water. The occasional surface disturbance that closes behind itself before you've resolved what opened it. The fact that the water in the moss, when you press your palm down, sometimes gives way slightly too organically, slightly too much — because under your palm a frog has made itself flat and still and is waiting with frog patience for you to lift your hand.

There are more frogs here than the hollow looks like it can support. This is because you are not seeing the hollow right. You are seeing the moss surface, the water table at three inches, the sedge clumps in their spacing. You are not seeing the vertical dimension of the moss itself — the four, five, six inches of living and semi-living and compressed substrate — which is riddled with frog-sized chambers and frog-sized waterways, which is structurally complex in a way that sponge is structurally complex, which contains entire neighborhoods that have never been visited by anything with eyes that work in air.

The frogs live in both dimensions. They surface and call and go under and navigate the interior of the moss and winter in the substrate below frost and emerge in spring before the hollow has finished considering it. They eat the midges and occasionally the smaller dragonfly nymphs in the standing water at the moss margins and they are eaten by the herons that appear at irregular intervals — grey shocks of verticality, still as the alders are still but a different kind of still, the still of attention — and the herons eat and leave and the frogs rebalance without apparent trauma.

The hollow has been doing this longer than herons have looked like herons. The frogs are old before they are born.

## VI. The Dragonflies

They are the hollow's emissaries to the ordinary world.

They spend their longest life underground — or rather, underwater, in the still shallow pools at the moss margins — as nymphs, hunting in the manner of ambush: motionless until not motionless, extending a hinged jaw faster than the eye resolves, taking midge larvae and small crustaceans and occasionally each other. They are patient as anything else here and then violently, briefly not.

The nymph phase lasts two years in the species that uses this hollow. Two years of that slow time, that geological metabolic pace, submerged in acid water. And then: something changes in the biochemistry, and they climb a sedge stem, and they split open, and they are different.

The adult dragonfly is a different argument entirely from the hollow's philosophy. It is speed. It is the warm air above the moss, the still air of the greenhouse warmth, the mechanical dry ticking of wings working at thirty, forty wingbeats a second. The adult dragonfly is all fast time in a place of slow time. It has perhaps four to eight weeks. It hunts midges — eating above the hollow, eating efficiently, a body redesigned for aerial interception — and mates in the air over the moss, and the female dips and deposits eggs into the marginal water, and the eggs sink into the acid and develop slowly and hatch slowly and the nymphs settle into the substrate and begin their two years.

The adult dragonfly does not belong to the hollow. It visits. It is the hollow's contribution to the air above, a burst of fast life manufactured in slow water, an investment in two years of patience that pays out in eight weeks of flight.

Watch one hover at head height over the hollow — helicopter-still, which means working furiously to appear still — and you see the disconnect between the two time signatures this place maintains. The wings are in one time. The moss is in another. The wings are the faster event by a factor that is not metaphorical.

## VII. The Cicadas

The sound is the fact you must accept before understanding the rest.

The cicadas are not beautiful in the way the dragonflies are beautiful or the sundew is beautiful. They are numerous. They are *numerical*. Individual beauty is not the point. The collective output — industrial, steady, parametric in its fluctuation — is the point.

What they are doing: finding mates. Competing with each other's calls. The male tymbals buckle and release and buckle and release and the calls constructively interfere across the hollow and what you hear is not any individual cicada but the collective interference pattern of all of them at once, the aggregate, the sum. This is why there is no direction to look toward. You are inside the output, not outside it.

They live in the sedge stems as nymphs, some of them. In the alder roots, some of them. In the upper layers of the moss, a few. They feed on plant fluids — the sedge's xylem, the alder's root secretions — and they do not damage what they feed on because they are too numerous to afford damaging their substrate. A hundred cicadas that killed their host plant would have no host plant. The hollow enforces this negotiation: nothing here exhausts anything here. The system is too closed for that.

The adults emerge in summer and the calls begin and the warmth trapped in the hollow amplifies the calls and the calls fill the hollow's acoustics the way water fills moss: completely, intimately, as an inherent property rather than an addition.

After the calling they die. Their bodies fall into the moss and are preserved by the acid and become part of the substrate and the substrate feeds the *Sphagnum* and the *Sphagnum* acidifies the water. This again. Always this. The cycle takes the bodies and holds them and the preserved dead become the structure the living stand on and the living call into the warm still air and the cycle does not end.

## VIII. The Deer

They come down the path and step in without noticing.

They are not residents. They are visitors who don't know they're visitors. The moss gives under their hooves and water rises around the edges of their prints and they lower their heads and graze along the rim — the sedge clumps from a safe distance, the alder leaves, whatever has come loose and lies on the surface — and they stand there for a while and then they leave.

After they leave their prints remain. The moss is compressed but not dead. It will rise in a day, two days, reinflating as water migrates back under capillary pressure. The prints become shallower and shallower and in a week they are gone, and in two weeks the hollow has not registered the passage, and in a month there is no record anywhere in the hollow that the deer were here.

The hollow does not hold the deer the way it holds everything else. They pass through and leave nothing.

But their path leads to the hollow. Has always led here, or near here. The path is worn by generations of deer, maintained by generations, part of the deer-landscape that predates the current deer's grandmothers. So in a sense the deer do leave something — not in the hollow but toward it. A route. An orientation. A way that remains navigable because they keep walking it, not because anyone decided it should stay open.

The hollow has a door, which is the deer path. The deer carry the door in their collective movement without knowing there is a door.

## IX. The Thing With No Common Name

Between the sedge clumps, in the moss, at knee height if you were crouching — which you should be, which this hollow encourages by being the kind of place where kneeling is appropriate — there is something that requires time to see.

It is a fruiting body. Fungal, probably. It has emerged from the *Sphagnum* substrate where the mycelium has been living since before the current moss surface was the surface, where the mycelium may have been living since the hollow was young. Mycelia don't leave clear records.

The fruiting body is small — a centimeter across, two — and the color is the color of old ivory deepening toward amber at the edges, and its surface is not smooth but tessellated, divided into small geometric sections that have no clear adaptive purpose you could argue for, that are simply what they are: a structural decision the organism made somewhere in its evolutionary history and has continued making because nothing selected against it.

It will last three days. Four, if the weather stays damp. Then it will collapse inward and the moss will absorb it and the mycelium will continue below.

No one will see it. You might, if you came on exactly the right day, if you were already crouching, if the angle of the light was right and you were the kind of person who stops for small pale things in moss.

The hollow did not produce it for you. It did not produce it as display or information. It produced it because the mycelium reached a moment in its cycle where this was the next step, and the next step was taken, and now the fruiting body is here for three days and then it will not be here and the hollow will not miss it because the hollow does not accumulate losses.

## X. The Hollow Itself, Reconsidered

After everything, what lives here is one thing that has several names.

Call it the process. Call it the equilibrium. Call it the wet, acid, insulating, preserving, self-reinforcing chemistry that has been running since the hollow first agreed to be a hollow, and recruited the *Sphagnum* that made the acid that slowed the decomposition that built the substrate that grew the *Sphagnum*.

Everything else — the sedges, the sundew, the frogs, the dragonfly nymphs in their two years, the cicadas in their sedge stems, the alders with their roots and their bark and their nitrogen — is inside the process. Is permitted by the process. Is, in some sense, *produced* by the process, because none of them would be here if the process had not first prepared a specific kind of water, a specific kind of ground, a specific kind of quiet that has room in it for the lives that need it.

The deer do not live here. The herons do not live here. You do not live here.

What lives here lives slowly enough to look like it is not living. It lives in a time that makes your heartbeat seem like an emergency, your life seem like a brief warm disturbance, your understanding seem like a thing that passes through and leaves prints that close up in a week.

There is no enmity in this. The hollow is not hostile to faster things. It simply does not notice them at the level of its own operations, which are running on a clock set to a different interval, a different patience, a different understanding of what *now* means.

What lives in the Hollow Mill-Green is now. A very long, very specific, very wet now, four thousand years deep, still breathing, still manufacturing itself, still exactly what it is.

You stepped into it.

It will not remember you.

It will not need to.
