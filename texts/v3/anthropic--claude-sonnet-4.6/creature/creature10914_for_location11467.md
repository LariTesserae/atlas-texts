## What Lives in the Sump Basin

---

Begin with what you can't see.

The invisibles are the majority here, as they always are, but in the sump basin they are especially predominant because of the water and the peat and the cold, because anaerobic conditions preserve what aerobic ones would destroy and the preserving itself becomes a kind of ongoing economy. In the deep peat layers, archaea and bacteria that haven't needed oxygen since before there was much oxygen to be had are processing carbon through the slow chemistry of deep fermentation, producing methane in quantities too small to be dramatic, too constant to be ignorable — a background combustibility, a long exhale held in the substrate. Above them, in the upper layers of peat where some oxygen occasionally penetrates, different communities: the iron-reducing bacteria that give waterlogged soils their grey-blue gleam when freshly cut, the sulfur-processing chemolithotrophs, the nitrogen-fixers living in nodules on the alder roots where the tree and the bacterium have entered into an arrangement so old and so functional that neither could be said to be doing a favor for the other anymore. They have been doing this together longer than there have been alders and longer than the bacterium has been exactly this bacterium. It predates both their current shapes.

The fungi are present in quantities that make the visible fungal bodies — the brackets on the fallen wood, the caps that will appear later on the margins — seem almost incidental, like the tips of icebergs that are themselves immense. Mycorrhizal threads run through the soil in concentrations that, if you could see them, would look less like a network and more like a felt: a continuous interlocking mat of fine white hyphal strands passing between and around root tips, forming junctions, exchanging sugars and phosphorus at junctions in chemical protocols so refined they make trade negotiations look imprecise. The alder is networked. The sedge is networked. Where two alder trees stand close, there is a reasonable chance that their root systems are partially enmeshed through shared fungal intermediaries, and that this has been true since both were saplings, and that neither tree has any awareness of this whatsoever.

The decomposers — and the bracket fungi on the rotting logs are among them, the hyphae pushing into the heartwood, breaking the lignin's long chains into something the soil can take back — work at different speeds, the faster chemistries at the surface and in the warmer months, the slower ones underneath and through winter. The basin floor is always partly this work: dead material at various stages of becoming not-dead-material-but-something-else, the reed thatch from three years ago now indistinguishable by eye from peat, though a biochemist could still read the difference, the cellulose chains shorter but not yet absent. There is no clean line between the living and the dead here. The gradient runs in both directions at once.

---

Now the water.

The standing water of the finger-channels and sphagnum pockets is not still, not in the biological sense. What it appears is still: a dark surface, the tannin-stain absorbing rather than reflecting, the sheen of oxidized plant oils swirling in interference patterns. What it is: thickly populated, every cubic centimeter occupied by things too small to see without magnification and by things only just visible.

The protists first, because they were here first and because they are easy to overlook and because they do so much. In the water films around every submerged surface, and in the water column itself, and in the thin layer between water and sediment where conditions shift abruptly — the amoebae extending their pseudopods and withdrawing them, testing, enveloping, moving in the patient way of things that are pure process rather than body. The testate amoebae — those that have constructed shells around themselves from mineral particles or their own secretions — are especially present in the sphagnum, where their tests have been accumulating in the waterlogged peat for as long as the sphagnum has grown here, the dead tests of past generations preserved in the anoxic layers and the living ones continuing in the surface water, a continuous presence in two forms at once. The ciliates moving through the water with their rows of beating hairs, faster than the amoebae, more purposeful-seeming, creating tiny water currents in their passage. The flagellates smaller still, spinning.

Rotifers. These are animals in the technical sense — multicellular, with a gut and a nervous system, with organs that are genuinely organs — but they look like the word protozoan sounds. They are present in the water column in numbers that are difficult to think about without vertigo: a single milliliter of water from the sphagnum pockets may contain hundreds. They filter bacteria and algae through their ciliated crowns, they reproduce parthenogenetically when conditions are good and produce dormant eggs when conditions shift, the eggs capable of surviving desiccation and winter and years of dormancy before hatching. The whole population can contract to a handful of durable eggs and expand again when the water returns and the temperature rises, which makes the rotifers not quite present and not quite absent in any permanent sense, but occupying some intermediate category that implies a kind of patience the word patience doesn't really capture.

The water beetles are at the visible end of the invertebrate range and near the top of the in-water predator hierarchy, at least in terms of ambition. The diving beetles — Dytiscus, possibly, or the smaller Agabus, harder to see, quicker to vanish — carry air bubbles beneath their elytra and are using these to metabolize, the air supply refreshed at the surface in those quick comma-shaped breaks, gone before you've processed the movement. They eat: tadpoles, small invertebrates, each other under some circumstances. The larvae are worse, proportionally, than the adults — long, curved, pale grubs with hollow sickle-jaws that inject digestive fluids into prey and drink the resulting liquefied tissue. This is not unusual among aquatic insects. The water bears a lot of this kind of thing, quietly.

Water boatmen hanging below the surface at a slight angle, back legs extended, their undersides shining with the air film that keeps them viable. Lesser water boatmen, different genus, moving in short darting strokes through the mid-water. Both are present in the reed-bed shallows, both cycling through a diet of algae and organic detritus and, in the case of the larger species, anything catchable.

The pond skaters you can already see, casting their shadow-circles, six feet and none of them wet because the water surface is held by surface tension as a distinct medium they can use without entering. They are reading vibrations through their feet. A ripple from a struggling insect that has fallen onto the water is different from a ripple from rain is different from a ripple from a diving beetle breaking surface, and they have the capacity to distinguish these, though what capacity means here is not clear — whether it rises to the level of something, or remains purely mechanical, or is some third thing between those options that we don't have good language for.

---

The sphagnum itself is worth pausing on, because sphagnum is not simply a plant.

The hummocked mounds of sphagnum between the water channels are pale-green and dark-green and rust-red and occasionally almost white in the dry upper tips, the colors depending on species and moisture and light, the hummocks not quite stable and not quite growing, or rather growing so slowly that the growth is readable in years not days. Sphagnum holds water through capillary action in a meshwork of dead cells among the living ones — cells that are empty and porous and abundant, so that the living plant is perhaps ninety percent water by mass, so that the whole hummock is effectively a sponge that is also producing the sponge from the outside in. Press your hand on a sphagnum hummock and water wells up between your fingers and the hummock recovers its shape slowly, like memory foam, like something trying to remember what it was.

The water held inside the sphagnum is chemically distinct from the water outside it. Sphagnum exchanges hydrogen ions for the mineral cations in the water passing through it, acidifying its environment, and this is not accidental — or rather, it is not accidental in the evolutionary sense, the way nothing is accidental in the evolutionary sense, because the acidity the sphagnum produces inhibits the bacteria that would otherwise decompose it, which means the sphagnum is, in a diffuse chemical sense, managing its own preservation. The low pH is hostile to most decomposers. The sphagnum produces the acidity that keeps the sphagnum. This is not consciousness. It is the kind of thing that looks like strategy because strategy is what we reach for when we see effective action, even when the effective action is blind, even when there is no actor.

Living in the sphagnum: a whole nested population that is almost entirely specific to it, the way very particular things live in very particular places. Specialized mites in the surface layer. Springtails hopping through the gaps between stems. Testate amoebae as mentioned, concentrated here more than anywhere else in the basin. Midges laying eggs in the water pockets between hummocks. Where the sphagnum is deep and undisturbed, where last year's growth has pressed down into the beginnings of peat, there are things moving in the compressed dark that have been in sphagnum systems for so long they can barely be described outside of them.

---

The frogs deserve more than passing mention.

The frog population of the basin is large in the way that populations in good habitat are large: not beyond counting in principle, but beyond confident counting in practice, because they are distributed through a dense and varied habitat and because they are the precise color of what they sit in and because they have refined stillness as a survival trait to a degree that makes them effectively invisible until the moment they decide not to be. They sit at the waterline between stems. They sit on the sphagnum hummocks with their backs exactly the color and texture of wet moss. They sit half-submerged in the watercress with only the high-set eyes above the surface, and the eyes are dark, and the watercress is dark at the water line, and you can stand two feet away from a frog in this position and not know.

The calling has mostly finished. What remains of the calling instinct is apparently not strong enough to overcome whatever calculates risk in a frog, because the calling season brought everything that hunts frogs into the basin — herons, which stand motionless in the shallows with the same investment in stillness as the frogs they are hunting; otters, if there are otters here, which seems possible; the various corvids that will take frogs opportunistically. Now the frogs are simply present, feeding, maintaining whatever slow cold frog metabolism is required to get through the season, occasionally vanishing into the water with that compact plop that is the quietest possible exit.

The tadpoles are at various stages because the egg-laying happened across several weeks and because the colder pockets retarded development and the warmer pockets, where they exist — the margins that get a degree of sun, the channels where the water is thin enough to warm slightly — have pushed the development forward. The result is tadpoles of different sizes sharing the same water, the older ones with their back legs emerging looking almost like something new, almost like the thing they are becoming, which is strange and not strange, which is just metamorphosis, which the basin takes as ordinary. The older tadpoles are beginning to eat differently than the younger ones: less algae, more animal protein, the dietary shift that accompanies the body shift, the digestive system rebuilding itself ahead of the lungs developing, ahead of the tail being reabsorbed, ahead of the frog.

---

The birds come and go, and most of the birds using the basin are using it functionally — as foraging ground, as shelter, as nesting structure — without it being the center of their lives in the way it is the center of the basin's life. The reed bunting whose thin phrase marks the mornings is resident and its mate is in the reeds somewhere nesting or about to nest, a cup woven from reed fragments and grass and lined with fine material, placed in the dense stems above the waterline. The moorhen is territorial and knows the basin better than anything else visible, moving through the reed beds with a high-stepped delicacy that reads as caution but is really precision — it knows where the ground is firm enough, where the channel edges are, which routes through the reed stems are open and which are blocked. It is not cautious. It is competent.

In the alder canopy, in the mornings: a chiffchaff moving through the new leaves, picking through the newly unfurling growth for the small things that live in newly unfurling growth — aphids, mites, small caterpillars at the looping stage. The chiffchaff is newly arrived from elsewhere, still with the driven quality of recent migration, feeding with focused energy in the way of things that have crossed large distances and arrived in good habitat and now need to replenish. It sings from the upper branches between bouts of feeding: the simple two-note call, the one that names it in the language of those who named it that way. It doesn't seem to consider the basin as the basin considers itself, which is to say that it doesn't consider the basin at all, but is simply inside the basin, inside this particular patch of alder, pulling the small things from the leaves.

If there is a heron — and there has been, recently; the drag-marks at the edge of a channel and a single large pale grey feather caught in the sedge say so — it does not announce itself. Herons stand in the shallows and become available to be mistaken for something upright and pale that was always there, a dead stem, a piece of pale limestone at the margin. Their hunting is the hunting of patience indistinguishable from absence. A heron has possibly been in the basin this morning before you arrived and is now gone and you would not know either thing.

---

The invertebrates in the reed beds and the sedge are an entirely separate city from the invertebrates in the water, connected at the waterline where the two populations cross.

The reed stems carry a specialized community of gall midges and aphids that spend their whole lives on or in the stems themselves, whose bodies are tuned to the specific chemistry of Phragmites, who would be as lost on an oak leaf as a deep-sea fish would be in a tide pool. The plant is their world in the literal sense: they don't know there is anything outside it, and if knowing is the wrong word, then: they are organized entirely in relation to it. The leaf sheaths are slightly tacky in spring, and something collects in the tackiness, small flies, and something preys on what collects, spiders so thin their bodies seem to be mostly leg, carrying themselves through the reed stems in a sideways shuffle that barely disturbs the air.

At the sedge tussock bases, where the vegetation is densest and oldest and the interior of the tussock is a different microclimate from the surface — warmer, more humid, darker — there are beetles and weevils and the various things that eat beetles and weevils and the parasitic wasps that are looking for hosts for their eggs, and the whole system is running at a pace and complexity that the available light and the grey sky and the general quietness of the basin don't suggest but don't contradict either.

Ground beetles. They are there in the drier margins, under the rotting wood, under the sedge thatch, quick and dark and predatory, moving at night and resting during the day and having been at this for considerably longer than the basin has been a basin — carabid lineages that reach back before this limestone was laid down, before the glaciers, before quite a lot of things that now seem foundational.

---

The alder trees are worth thinking about as organisms rather than as scenery.

They are not old trees. The basin is not old enough to have old trees. They are perhaps thirty or forty years old, some of them, growing fast in the wet conditions that alders prefer, their trunks having already achieved the gnarled slightly-inward lean over water that alders develop in carr conditions, the bark plated and dark, the roots both gripping the wet bank and breathing through the lenticels at the root-bark interface where oxygen can enter. The alder has solved the problem of the waterlogged root zone not by avoiding it but by managing it: the roots can tolerate anaerobic conditions that would kill most trees, and they can absorb oxygen through the root bark when water levels drop even slightly, and the bacterial nitrogen-fixation happening in the root nodules means the alder can survive on soils too poor in nitrogen for most competition. It is a tree of difficult places. It does well here.

The catkins have shed already and the small woody cones from previous years — the old female catkins, closed now and dark brown, persisting on the twigs through multiple seasons — are scattered through the canopy alongside the new growth, the old and new coexisting on the same branches in a way that makes the tree look like it keeps everything. The alder does keep everything, comparatively. The cones open to release seeds in autumn but persist after opening, and if you look at an alder canopy you are seeing this year's young leaves and last winter's cones and sometimes the cones from the year before, the tree accumulating its own history in visible form.

The fallen alders — there are two in the basin, both partly submerged, both occupied — are in different stages of becoming something else. The newer fall still has branches and the bracket fungi just beginning. The older fall has lost its branch structure, the wood softened to the consistency of cork in the submerged sections, water beetles hunting the surface, and at the far end a hummock of moss that may soon be indistinguishable from the surrounding terrain, the tree disappearing back into the system it spent its life supporting.

---

There are things in the basin that aren't here yet but are on their way.

The damselflies will arrive when the temperature commits to something consistent, when the emerging adults can maintain enough warmth to fly. The larvae are in the water now — flattened, pale, with their three-lobed gill paddles trailing behind them, propelling themselves in bursts by jet-expelling water from their hindguts, which is both inelegant and effective. When they emerge they will be the iridescent blue and green things that make wet places feel more like themselves, the ones that couple in flight in the wheel position and lay eggs back into the water they hatched from, the whole cycle written in a single place.

The larger dragonflies will come later. Possibly the brown hawker, which patrols the open water with the focused industriousness of something that knows exactly what it's doing, which is to say it knows exactly nothing but does it with total commitment. The dragonfly doesn't think about the basin. It is a set of responses triggered by light and temperature and the movement of small things, and some of those responses are so perfectly calibrated to the wet, vegetated, insect-rich environment of the sump basin that the dragonfly seems to understand it, to have chosen it, when in fact it has simply been selected for being this way in places like this, over a timespan that makes the basin's own geological youth seem insignificant.

The sedge warblers will arrive and the basin will not be quiet in the same way again until they leave. They are not here yet and their absence has a specific character — there is a register of sound that the basin is currently missing, a gap in the frequencies, and you don't notice it as a gap until you remember what fills it.

---

The rats.

There are rats in the basin. You can tell by the runways through the sedge — paths trodden smooth and clear, running along the tussock bases, connecting the denser cover to the water's edge — and by the burrow openings at the bank, just above the water line, round-mouthed and worn smooth. The rats are not visible now. They are in their burrows, or somewhere in the dense reed cover, and they are doing what rats do, which is to say they are comprehensively inhabiting the available space and eating what is available to eat. The frog population is not unaffected by the rat population. The duckling population, when there are ducklings, will not be unaffected. The rats occupy a place in the basin's ecology that is rarely described charitably but is entirely integral — they are large warm-blooded predators and seed-eaters and generalists in a system of specialists, and their role is partly to convert smaller forms of life into larger forms of life and to make their bodies available to the things that eat rats, which are not absent.

The weasel or stoat that made a run through the reed bed three days ago — the small paired footprints pressed into the soft mud at the water's edge, already half-collapsed from the slow seeping of the surrounding water into the depressions — was likely interested in the rat runways. It is not here now. It passed through, which is what stoats do, moving in the covering dark or in the fast purposeful way that makes them hard to follow, moving from this basin to the next cover, threading the available landscape along the available margins.

---

And the things too small to have been mentioned yet.

The mites in the soil — oribatid mites, mostly, armored, moving through the leaf litter and upper peat at a pace invisible to the eye, feeding on fungal hyphae and decomposing organic material, present in densities of perhaps a hundred thousand per square meter in the upper soil layers — are the reason the soil is as it is, partly. They fragment the organic material. They carry fungal spores. They are eaten, incidentally, by the ground beetles, the birds probing the waterlogged margins, the larger invertebrates. They have been doing this in the basin for exactly as long as there has been soil in the basin to do it in.

The springtails. These are ancient — hexapods, but not quite insects, a lineage that diverged before wings evolved, before the Devonian committed to terrestrial life being a permanent arrangement. They are in the surface layers of peat and soil in numbers comparable to the mites. They jump, when disturbed, via a forked structure held compressed under the body and released like a spring, which sends them a distance many times their body length in a direction not entirely predictable, which is effective. They eat algae and fungi and organic particles. They are eaten by mites, by the spiders in the reed stems, by the larger soil fauna. They were doing this here before the alders arrived, probably. They will be doing it after.

The algae on every wet surface — the green film on the limestone, the filamentous green in the finger-channels, the diatoms in the standing water whose glass-chambered bodies are both organism and architecture, whose arrangements have been generating oxygen for long enough that the oxygen in the air you are breathing in the basin now is partly their legacy — are photosynthesizing at whatever rate the grey overcast permits, which is enough. It is always enough in here. There is not a shortage of anything that the basin can provide, and the basin provides the wet and the cold and the available mineral and the compressed vertical space, and the things that need these things are here, every surface spoken for, as described.

---

What lives in the sump basin is: almost everything that can.

Not in the tropical sense — not the diversity of the hot and the ancient and the established. This is a young system on young rock in a cold climate, and its diversity reflects that, the species list a fraction of what a lowland tropical wetland would show. But in the sense of a new city filling quickly — not with the slow accretion of centuries but with the opportunistic rush of things that are ready to move when space opens — the basin is full. Every functional role that the basin can support is being supported. Every energy pathway that the system makes available is being traveled. The decomposers are decomposing the producers, the primary consumers are consuming the producers, the secondary consumers are consuming the primary consumers, and the chain is not straight but looped back on itself and crossed and tangled at every level.

What it amounts to, this city of wet cold life below the limestone rim:

The invisible majority in the peat and water and root systems, doing the chemistry that everything else runs on. The frogs distributed through the vegetation in their hundreds, still and watchful, being still and watchful in the old way, the way that has worked. The birds moving through with their own purposes, the basin a zone in their larger maps. The invertebrates in every medium — water, stem, soil, bark, air — in densities that the grey quiet of the place doesn't announce. The alder standing in its wet ground with its bacterial partnerships and its old cones and its new leaves and its future, which is to fall and be occupied and become the thing it was before it was wood. The sphagnum making its acid, holding its water, preserving itself in the act of growing. The rats in their runways. The mites and the springtails in the deep soil, doing the work that keeps the surface habitable, unremarked upon.

All of it in the grey available light. All of it at this time of year releasing its held winter compression into a slow simultaneous surge, every threshold crossed at once, the whole system moving together in the way of systems responding to the same pressures — temperature, day length, the light at the angle that triggers the thing to begin.

Nothing is aware of the basin as the basin.

Everything is the basin, running.
