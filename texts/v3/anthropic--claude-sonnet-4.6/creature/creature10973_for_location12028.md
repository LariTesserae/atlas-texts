# What Lives at the Valve Station

---

## The Keeper

There is a person here, but the word *person* will need some adjustment before it fits.

Not because they are strange in the obvious ways — no deformity, no uncanny quality, no wrongness you could name if asked to testify. They are of ordinary size. Their hands, when you see them, are human hands, the knuckles thickened in the way of people who work with tools in cold and damp, the nails cut short and not quite clean, a scar along the base of the left thumb that has gone the flat silvery color of old scar tissue. They wear clothes that were chosen for function and have since become, over years of use in this specific place, precisely suited to it: heavy trousers that have been patched at both knees, boots with the seams re-stitched more than once, a wool sweater the color of the lichen — that grey-green — that may have been that color when purchased or may have arrived at it by slow osmosis.

The adjustment the word needs is this: they are someone for whom *here* has become the primary fact. Most people carry their elsewhere with them. You can feel it in how they look at a place — the constant, unconscious comparison, the judgment rendered against a somewhere else held in mind. This person has put down that comparison. They may have done it on purpose, or it may have happened gradually, the elsewhere fading the way a dream fades, not all at once but corner by corner until one morning you reach for it and the middle is gone. Either way, they have lost or released the habit of experiencing this place as a posting, a duration, a tenure with implied endpoints. The valley below the clouds, the cities one would reach by going down from the valley, the particular arrangements of people and noise and horizontal light that obtain at lower altitudes — these have become theoretical. They believe in them the way they believe in countries they have never visited.

This is not loneliness. Or it is, but it has been processed into something else, something that functions as company.

---

They are not in the building when you find it.

This is typical. They spend the required hours inside — watching gauges, logging readings in the small careful hand, attending to whatever the machinery periodically requires of them — but their presence in the building is always slightly provisional, always oriented toward the door. When they are inside, they know exactly where the fronds are moving outside, or rather, they know the pattern well enough that they can deduce it from the way the hum is building. They know how the pool surface looks in this kind of light, which is always the same kind of light, which they therefore know with a precision that has gone beyond observation into a kind of continuous attention.

They are out in the basin now. In the northeast corner, where the deep moss and the seep from the north face create the warmest microclimate, where three species of the small plants overlap and compete and have reached their particular equilibrium. They are crouching. This is a posture they have grown comfortable in over years — the long crouch that people who spend time outdoors in close examination develop, weight evenly distributed, perfectly still. They are watching something, or the site where something was, or they are simply in the posture of watching, which has become a mode of rest.

Their breathing is slow and does not make steam. This is one of the slightly wrong things about them — not wrong in a disturbing sense, but in the sense of being unexpected. The air is cold enough and their breath should make steam. Perhaps they have been out here long enough that their core temperature has equilibrated with the surrounding air to a degree that should concern you if you let yourself think about it, but they appear comfortable. They appear, if anything, comfortable in the way that objects in their correct positions are comfortable. The wrench in the drawer. The book returned to its shelf.

---

What do they know?

They know the station the way a musician knows an instrument — not mechanically, not only by function, but by the specific character of this particular instance. They know that the third gauge from the left on the upper board runs two percent high and has always run two percent high and can be trusted once you account for this. They know that in the hour before a storm the hum changes its character in a way they cannot describe precisely but can feel: something in the attack of each pulse, a slight crispness, like the difference between fat and lean in a flame. They know that the valve on the east-side secondary feed will need replacing in the next several months — the feeling in the handle when they close it has been changing — and they have already written the request in the logbook, under the readings, in the same small hand, with the same calm they use for temperatures.

They know the plants. Not taxonomically, but intimately, the way you know the faces of people you live with — the iridescence of the large fronds in the flat cloud-light, the smell when cut, the specific quality of the resistance when they are parted, which has changed slightly over the years as the plants have grown denser and the root systems have woven together beneath the thin soil. They have watched the pale tree since it was a sapling pressing through a crack in the southeast wall, have watched it lean, have wondered whether to stake it and decided not to, have come to care about its leaning in a way that precludes both correcting and worrying — a third posture, something like witnessing.

They know the lichen on the lower rock exposures in the way of someone watching a very slow film, aware that what they see in any given moment is a single frame.

What they do not know, or have stopped knowing, is why the station is here — what the pipes carry, what the pressure means, who receives the readings in the logbook, what the vapor becomes once the cloud accepts it. This was information they had once and have let go, the way you let go of a knot once it is tied, trusting the knot. The valves open and close in the right sequence at the right pressures. The gauges are watched and logged. The equipment receives its maintenance. That these actions are embedded in a system with purposes and owners and reasons is something the place has made abstract. The place is not abstract. The place is entirely specific. They trust the knot and attend to the place.

---

There are two objects in the building that were not mentioned in the logbook, not installed by the station's original outfitters.

On the windowsill above the table: a small collection of stones. Six of them, no two alike, arranged in a line with subtle care — not symmetrically, but with attention to the intervals, the way the different shapes and colors speak to one another across the gaps. One is almost translucent. One is the same purple-black as the wet cliff face but smaller than your thumbnail, nearly a perfect sphere, which it is not naturally — someone has rounded it further, over a very long period, by handling. The roundness is not quite finished. The sphere has perhaps ten years more handling in it before it will be what it is trying to be.

On the wall above the gauge board, attached with wire: a small piece of paper on which something has been written. The paper has been there long enough that the paper has taken on the color of the wall, cream-to-cream. The writing is the same hand as the logbook — small, careful — but it is not a reading or a maintenance note. It is a sentence that you can almost read from across the room if the light is right: two lines, something that turns at the end of the first line and continues, in the manner of lines that turn and continue.

You do not read it. This is the right choice. It was written for the person who tends this place, or for the place itself, and the distinction between these audiences has narrowed, over time, to nothing.

---

They have been here — how long?

The logbook suggests years, but the logbook has predecessors, and the predecessors have predecessors, stacked in the small cabinet under the table in their marbled covers, and this person's handwriting appears first as a later entry in one of those earlier books, when someone else was keeping the station. The hand in those entries is still careful but has not yet found its smallest, most efficient form. The entries become more spare over time: less of what they saw and thought on arrival, more of what the gauges said. This is not loss. This is the refinement of something that knows what it is for.

Between the last entry of the predecessor and the first entry in the next book, a gap of eleven days, which suggests a transition, a handoff, though there is no record of it. After that, the small careful hand, alone, continuous.

Ten years. Perhaps twelve.

---

They come back inside late in what passes for afternoon — the light graying from cloud-white to cloud-grey, the hum in its slow cycle, the gauges in their oscillation.

They close the door. They sit at the table. They open the logbook to the current page. They write the time and the pressure readings in the small careful hand. Underneath the readings, tonight, an additional note: *Pool level risen by the width of a hand since last month. The fronds in the northwest corner have reached the south wall.* A pause. Then: *The tree is straighter than I thought. The lean is less.* They consider this for a moment, pencil resting on the page, looking at the wall where the sentence is.

Outside, the hum builds again. The gauges respond. The vapor thickens and rises and becomes, as it always does, indistinguishable from everything that surrounds it. The young stone does not know it will be rounded. The moss advances by millimeters.

The Keeper of the Valve Station at Cloud Height turns the pencil over in their hand, feeling the weight of it. The sphere on the windowsill is in the exact place it should be, which is where they set it when they came in and before that where it sat while they were out. The tree leans less than it did. They did not correct it. It corrected itself, by growth, toward a light that is everywhere and nowhere and presses in from every direction at once.

They write down the pressure reading, which is exactly what it should be.

They are at home.
