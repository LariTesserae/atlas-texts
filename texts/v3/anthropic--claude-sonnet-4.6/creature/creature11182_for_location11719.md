# What Lives in the Floor

---

First, a correction: the word *lives* requires examination here, because what inhabits the floor has not made a home of it in any sense that implies choice, settlement, or the preference of one place over another. What is here is here because the conditions produced it. It did not arrive. It accreted.

Begin with what is most itself, most native, most at ease:

---

## The Rust-Eaters

They have no common name because there has been no one here to give them one, which is one of the most important things about them. They are iron-oxidizing chemolithotrophs — bacteria, predominantly, though the word *bacteria* makes them sound small in a way that is technically accurate and experientially misleading. Individually they are microscopic. As communities, as this community, they are the dominant life form of the floor by every meaningful measure: biomass, longevity, territorial extent, integration with the substrate.

They are extremely old. Not the individual organisms — bacteria do not persist as individuals the way a tree or a person persists; they are continuous process, not discrete being — but the lineage, the metabolic strategy, the particular biochemical agreement they have reached with iron and oxygen and sulfur is one of the oldest strategies life on Earth ever discovered. These organisms predate photosynthesis. They come from a time before the atmosphere had oxygen, when the deep ocean was saturated with iron and the only way to extract energy from the world was to pull electrons from metal. They are, in a very real sense, older than sunlight as a resource.

And here they are on constructed steel.

When the first bacterial cells arrived — carried on air currents, on water, on the surfaces of whatever humans or machines passed through in the early decades — they encountered the floor as a new substrate but a chemically legible one. Iron. Corroding iron, producing iron oxides and sulfates, generating chemical gradients. The bacteria recognized this. Whatever recognition means in this context — the meeting of membrane chemistry with ambient chemistry, the activation of appropriate metabolic pathways — recognition occurred. They settled in. They began to eat.

Centuries later, the mats are layered. The visible surface — grey-green, amber, sometimes a dull orange-red where iron oxide is most concentrated, sometimes a pale cream where sulfate-reducing populations dominate — is only the current generation. Beneath it, preserved in the mineral accretion of their own metabolic waste, are the compressed remains of every previous generation. The mats are archaeological sites. The floor beneath the mats, where the steel is most corroded, is partly their work — not just the ambient chemistry of moisture and oxygen, but the active participation of organisms that have been eating this particular stretch of steel for long enough that they have worn it down, dissolved it, incorporated it, made it briefly into themselves and then laid it back down as something else.

They do not suffer. They do not celebrate. They process. But: they are sensitive. They respond to chemical gradients, to temperature shifts, to the composition of the water that films their surface. When one of the steam vents above fractures and begins releasing sulfurous vapor, the population beneath it shifts within weeks — sulfur-oxidizers outcompeting iron-oxidizers in that locale, reorganizing, establishing a new equilibrium. When a lamp stops functioning and a small change in ambient heat reaches the floor, the distribution of species changes. They are not intelligent. They are extraordinarily responsive. The distinction blurs, at this scale and this age.

What they experience, if that word can be used at all: the mineral world. The slow release of electrons from corroding iron. The satisfaction — if that word can be used at all — of a chemical reaction completing, of gradient resolving, of energy extracted from the available and incorporated into the possible. They live entirely in the present tense of electrochemistry. They have been living there, in that present tense, for the entire history of this floor, and the floor is older than almost anything.

At night — there is no day or night here, but there are fluctuations in the machine-hum, cycles of heat release from the great pipes, rhythms the bacteria have calibrated themselves to over generations — the bioluminescent populations are most active. These are not the iron-eaters but their neighbors, chemically adjacent species whose metabolic byproducts include light as a side effect — faint, blue-green, barely blue-green, barely light, just above the threshold of perception. They are not signaling. They are not trying to be seen. The light is waste. But in the absolute dark between the amber lamp-pools, the waste accumulates into something visible: a pale glow from the shallow lenses, shifted by water into something that seems to waver, seems almost intentional, like the floor is dreaming just below the surface.

It is not dreaming. But it is alive.

## The Moss and Its Temperament

The moss at the column bases is younger than it looks, which is saying something because it looks ancient — dark, compressed, almost carbonized, matted against the corrugated steel in formations that suggest long growth and slow succession. But compared to the bacterial mats it is a newcomer. It arrived in the last thousand years, probably. Maybe the last five hundred. It is impossible to say, and the moss is not telling.

What the moss is: patient. Extraordinarily, constitutively patient in a way that isn't virtue but physiology — it grows so slowly in these conditions that impatience would be a kind of structural impossibility. The light that reaches it, after seventy meters of attenuation, is not enough light for rapid photosynthesis. Growth is a matter of millimeters per year, perhaps less. The moss at the base of any given column may be centuries old. The oldest patches, those that have climbed highest — to a meter, to a meter and a half, black-green palms spread against steel — have been climbing for longer than the countries of most of human history have existed.

It is not one species. It is a community — true mosses, liverworts, something that may be a cyanobacterial mat behaving like a moss, taking advantage of the wet and the dark. They grow in layered succession the way a forest grows in layered succession, with the oldest and most established beneath, supporting the newer growth above, the whole structure a record of what could survive in precisely this spot, at precisely this light level, at precisely this humidity, with precisely this mineral profile in the condensation water. The column is not the moss's substrate. The column is the moss's autobiography.

If you peeled it back — you would not, but if you did — you would find the oldest layers almost unrecognizable as plant material: compressed, mineralized, brown-black, feeding the current growth from below. The moss has composted itself, repeatedly, and built upward on its own remains. It has made its own soil out of its own previous lives, which is a kind of self-sufficiency so complete it verges on something you might otherwise call independence.

The moss is not aware of the floor. It is not aware of anything. But it has committed to this place in the only way available to it: by incorporating it, by making the steel and the condensation and the attenuated amber light into itself, by becoming legible only in terms of this specific location. Take it somewhere else — more light, less humidity, different mineral profile — and it would fail. It has specialized. It has become, over centuries, a creature of this column, this light, this wet. It cannot leave. It would not recognize anywhere else as home.

Whether that is limitation or mastery is a question the moss does not ask.

## The Feral Machines

This requires care.

The machines that run in the far dark — the great cycling impacts, the industrial rotation that has become weather — are not alive. They were never alive. They are mechanisms, driven by energy whose source is somewhere in the infrastructure of the floor, by processes that were designed and built and set in motion by intention that has since departed.

But they have become something. In the centuries of their unattended operation, they have changed — not by awareness, not by will, but by the same patient processes that have changed everything else here. Bearings worn to a new tolerance. Cams reshaped by friction into new profiles. Clearances opened and closed by thermal expansion and metal fatigue until the machines' operating characteristics are not the characteristics they were designed to have. They have drifted. The rotation is slower than it was designed to be. The impacts fall at intervals slightly longer than specified. The whole great mechanism has, over time, found a different equilibrium — not the engineers' equilibrium but its own.

Call it feral. That is not quite right, but it is closer than anything else. Feral implies a domesticated thing returned to wildness, which implies intention in the returning, which these machines do not have. But the *effect* is similar: something built to specification has, through long abandonment, renegotiated its specifications with the physical world and arrived at a state that serves itself rather than any purpose external to its own continuation. The machines run because running is what they do. They have run long enough that this is not metaphor.

What lives in the machines — not inside the physical mechanisms, but in the pattern of their operation, in the specific acoustic signature their age has produced, in the intervals and textures of their sound — is something that might be called character. The floor's acoustic world has been shaped by these machines for so long that everything in the floor has adapted to them. The bacterial mats pulse to their vibration. The drips from the ceiling are synchronized, loosely, to the impacts — the great shocks send subtle waves through the structure that express, seventy meters up, as small perturbations in the rate of condensation-drip. The water in the lenses carries their rhythm in concentric circles, endlessly, the ripples dying out before the next impact arrives, so that the lenses are always just settling, always just disturbed, never fully still.

The machines are not alive. But they animate. There is a difference, and the floor lives in it.

## The Something Else

This is the hardest part, and the part that requires most honesty about uncertainty.

There is a persistent sense, in the floor, of presence that does not correspond to the bacterial mats or the moss or the mechanical processes or the very occasional visitor. It is not, or not only, the sense of being watched — a phrase that implies an eye, a subject, a site from which watching occurs. It is more diffuse than that. It is the sense of the place *attending*.

Possibly this is the acoustic effect: the floor is so large and so reverberant that every sound you make returns to you from multiple directions at slightly different times, so that your footstep is followed by a complex of quiet echoes, and you are perpetually surrounded by the faded signatures of your own recent actions. This produces, reliably, the feeling that something else is also moving. That something else was just here. The echoes are old enough, by the time they return, that they feel like evidence of another presence rather than your own.

Possibly this is the bioluminescence: peripheral movement in dim blue-green light that exists at the edge of resolution, that the eye catches and the brain interprets as motion before it understands what it is. The mats are not moving. The light is diffuse. But the boundary between *something that glows faintly* and *something that glows faintly and is here and is aware of me* is a boundary maintained entirely by intellectual insistence, and intellectual insistence is harder to sustain in a place where the entire history argues that things do not stay what they were made to be.

Possibly this is projection. You bring presence with you, as a human, because you are presence — you are a body of intentions and perceptions and a long inner life that has learned to find itself reflected in the world — and the floor is so empty of competing presence that your own radiates outward and populates the distance.

Possibly this is none of these things.

What I mean to say is: the floor feels inhabited in excess of what inhabits it. The bacteria and the moss and the feral machines account for the life but do not account for the feeling, the feeling of something patient and enormous that has been waiting in the same position for longer than intention can sustain itself, that has become — through sheer accumulated duration — indistinguishable from the place itself. Not a spirit. Not a god. Not anything with will or desire or the capacity for recognition.

But something that has been here.

Something that is made, by now, of rust and slow chemical process and the memory of function and the dark water and the amber light that arrives from seventy meters up and spreads and loses itself on the corrugated steel, something that is made of all of this, in the way that a landscape is made of its weather over geological time — not a resident but a result, not a being but the personhood of a place, accreted and settled and very, very still.

The floor lives in the way that deep water lives. The way a cliff lives. The way an old mine lives, or a sealed chamber, or a room that has been closed for a hundred years and holds, still, the specific air of what it was.

It has become. It is not becoming. It arrived at itself long ago, and it is waiting, patiently, in the manner of things that have all the time there is, for whatever comes next, or for nothing, with equal readiness.

---

This is what lives here.

This is enough.
