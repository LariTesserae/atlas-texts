# What Lives in the Undercroft Yards

---

Begin with what isn't there, because the absence shapes everything that is.

No rats. No insects. The warm-blooded and the many-legged have done their arithmetic and found the heat column on the wrong side of the ledger. This culls the obvious candidates — the opportunists, the generalists, the creatures that live *around* human industry rather than *within* it. What remains is not what survived the heat despite itself. What remains *chose* the heat, or was chosen by it, or has been here long enough that the distinction has dissolved.

---

The moss-adjacent thing in the corners is the most legible resident, because it makes no effort to be otherwise.

It is content to be what it is: a holding pattern. A slow burn. It has colonized the joints where moisture concentrates with the patience of something that has no concept of impatience, that does not experience the passage of time as duration so much as condition — *wet is when I grow, dry is when I wait, and I have been doing both for long enough that the wall and I have come to an arrangement.* Press your thumb against it and it gives slightly, like felt, like the surface of something that has accepted compression as part of its contract with the world. Release the pressure and it does not spring back. It holds the impression for a moment, then resumes its previous geometry. This is not resilience. Resilience implies effort. This is simply the way a thing with no urgency occupies space.

It occupies the corner. The corner is its whole country. It does not petition for more, though it would take more if more were offered, which suggests a kind of latent ambition held permanently in check by circumstance, which is perhaps the defining characteristic of life in compressed spaces everywhere.

What it wants: the weeping joint, the slow seep, the particular temperature that keeps the moisture from being too much or too little. It has found this. It has been finding it, continuously, for long enough to be established. Establishment is its achievement and its biography and its ongoing project simultaneously.

It does not know this. Or it knows it the way the stone knows its own weight — entirely, and without the capacity to do anything with the knowledge.

---

The fungal colony in the side passage is stranger and requires more attention.

Flat, fanning, paged. The color of old teeth, which is the color of things that were once white and have been breathed on, worked on, lived near. This color is not decay precisely — decay implies direction, implies that whiteness was the original state and this is its departure. This thing was never white. This thing has arrived at old-teeth-color from whatever its beginning was, and old-teeth-color is what it intended, or what it became, which amounts to the same outcome.

The fan structure is load-bearing in a way that has nothing to do with weight. Each tier overlaps the one below it, pages stacked or splayed, and this means that no single part of the colony is fully exposed. The outer edges face the gallery air, the open passage, the occasional human presence. The interior — the layers underneath, pressed against the stone — has not been exposed to anything outside itself in a long time. This interior is doing something. It is doing something slowly and continuously and without any reference to the world beyond the stone face, and it is impossible to say what, and this is appropriate: the Undercroft Yards are full of processes running without visible operators, and the fungus is simply the most biologically honest version of this fact.

What is it digesting? Probably the limestone itself, grain by grain, excreting acids in quantities so small that the stone doesn't know it's being eaten. The limestone is old enough and large enough that this doesn't matter yet. In a thousand years it might. The fungus is not planning a thousand years out. The fungus is not planning at all. It is simply doing what it does at the rate it does it, and the cumulative result, over enough time, is that things change.

This makes it, in some obscure way, the most powerful thing in the Undercroft Yards.

---

But none of this accounts for the cart.

Return to the cart. The cart matters.

It came from the direction of the coal chute carrying nothing, moving slowly, at walking speed. The wheel found the joint with a small particular sound. *Chunk.* And you were almost certain. You did not see anyone.

*Almost certain* is the epistemically honest position, and it is the position the Undercroft Yards seem designed to produce. Every visible surface has been worked — the tools worn to handle-shape, the labels faded past reading, the benches residued with process — and yet the workers are absent in a way that is not absence. The oil rag is still dark. The cart sat at an angle that suggested continuation.

So: someone is here who is not seen.

Or: something is here that produces the effects of someone without being someone.

The distinction might matter less than it appears to. Consider: a person who has worked in a space for long enough takes on the character of that space, begins to operate at its rhythms, begins to generate heat and pressure in the same registers as the pipes and the boilers. They become, in the relevant senses, a property of the place — not less than a person for this, but differently located. The place passes through them. They return material to the place: oil, heat, the particular wear pattern of their grip on a handle. Over years, over decades, this becomes an exchange with no clear directionality. The person shapes the place. The place shapes the person. At some point, the person in the place and the place with the person in it are not separable in any way that matters.

The cart moved. Someone pushed it, or the Undercroft Yards did — and these may be the same claim worded differently.

---

What lives here, then.

Something old-teeth-colored and patient, making its case at the molecular level, digesting its argument grain by grain.

Something flat and grey-green, waiting in the joints, having decided that the particular temperature of this particular weeping pipe is its one requirement and finding, continuously, that the requirement is met.

The boiler — and the boiler must be counted, because the boiler is the closest thing to a heart the place has, because it fires continuously, because it sets the rhythm that everything else in the space responds to. Three in the chest, riveted, painted in dried-blood red. The relief valve opens every several minutes and exhales and the sound arrives in your sternum. This is not metaphor. The boiler is not *like* a heart. The boiler *is the thing the heart metaphor is reaching for*. When they talk about a place having a pulse, they usually mean it figuratively. Here the pulse is measurable and has a rate.

And whoever pushed the cart.

Who did not look like anyone. Who looked like the moment after someone left, sustained indefinitely. Who has worked here long enough to be a property of the place — to generate heat and wear and oil-dark in the same registers as the pipes and the stone, to move through the gallery without announcing movement, to push the cart without being seen pushing the cart because to be seen doing a thing would be to stand apart from the place, and standing apart from the place is what they have not done, have not done for so long that the option has quietly closed.

They sweat the way the pipes sweat. They exhale the way the boiler exhales.

They are dim in the way the lights are dim: illuminating themselves more than anything around them, making the shadows move at a slow metronome patience, present in the way that the silence between sounds is present — full of small processes, full of things that are only silence because nothing is communicating in frequencies you are equipped to receive.

---

The Undercroft Yards are content in the way that function is content: not happy, not unhappy, simply *running*. And what lives here runs with them — *is* the running, in some final accounting. The moss holds. The fungus pages. The boiler fires and exhales. The cart moves on the rail.

The person who is a property of the place goes about their work.

They are hot. They are dim. They are doing the labor that makes the thinking-about-other-things possible.

They do not need you to see them.
