# What Lives in the Cistern District

---

Start with what is easiest to name and work toward what is harder.

---

**The crickets** are the dominant fact of animal life here, more than the rats, more than anything else, because they have achieved something the rats have not: they have become environmental. They are weather. You do not observe them so much as exist within them. They are *Acheta domesticus* probably, the common house cricket, though in this light and at this density the taxonomy feels beside the point — what they are is *plural*, what they are is *sustained*, what they are is a collective that has lost track of its individual members the way a river loses track of its molecules. 

They live in every crack wide enough to admit them, which is most cracks. The brick courses are riddled with their occupancy. They have colonized the gaps where concrete failed to meet old pipe flange, the spaces behind loose conduit, the dark interior angles where I-beam meets ceiling slab. In the secondary chamber they are denser, if that is possible. You cannot find them by looking — they are behind looking, beneath it — but if you stood very still with a good light and patient eyes you would eventually see one in the open: brown, improbable, antennae longer than its body, making minute adjustments to its position with a kind of jerky precision, like something operated by a mechanism learning to simulate organic movement.

They are warm. This is the thing you would not expect about being surrounded by hundreds of crickets: the aggregate of them generates warmth, adds fractionally but measurably to the ambient temperature, which is already high. They are metabolizing. They are converting the algae and the mold and the smaller insects and whatever else the place provides into cricket, into song, into eggs tucked into the wet mortar gaps, into more crickets, into more song. The cycle is old. It predates whoever poured the last section of ceiling. It will outlast the PVC clips and probably the PVC.

What are they like, individually? Patient. Entirely committed to being crickets. Possessed of a seriousness about their project — the project of filling this space with the evidence of their existence — that would be almost admirable if it were chosen rather than simply what they are.

---

**The rats** are a different proposition. Where the crickets have dissolved into the place, the rats remain distinct from it. They have personalities, or something close enough to personalities that the distinction doesn't matter much. There are three of them, currently, though this changes — three is approximately the equilibrium the space supports, and when a fourth arrives one leaves or dies and when one dies a new one finds the entrance (what entrance? they know) within weeks.

The current three:

One is old, or seems old — slower, less reactive to sound, moving with the careful deliberateness of something that has negotiated this terrain for a long time and has stopped needing to be alert about it. It has a territory that includes the secondary chamber and the run of east wall from the corrugated door to the corner where the rags once were and where the nest-start is. It sleeps more than the others. When it moves, it moves with the gait of something that has already been everywhere it's going many times and is making the trip out of necessity rather than curiosity. Its fur is rough, not sleek. It has been in some dispute — one ear is torn, healed badly, the notch permanent. It regards the space with a familiarity so complete it has curdled into indifference.

One is young enough to still be curious, which in rat terms means it is dangerous to itself, running along the upper edges of the pipe runs in a way that has no clear practical purpose, investigating the PVC fixtures, testing the loose clip at the junction where the pipe angles down, pulling at a length of exposed wire not because it wants the wire but because it wants to know if the wire can be pulled. It has not yet learned to distinguish between useful and useless investigations. It will learn, or it won't. It smells of wet fur and the specific musk of a young rat, which is sharper, more urgent, than the smell of the old one.

The third is a female, and she is pregnant, which changes the character of her occupancy of the space. She is not nesting yet, not quite, but she is evaluating everything with a different kind of attention — not curiosity, not habit, but something practical and focused. She stops in places and holds still in a way that looks like she is testing whether the stillness holds, whether the spot is defensible, whether it is dry enough, warm enough, isolated enough. She has decided against four locations so far this week. She will decide again, soon. When she decides, the space will become something different: a nursery, briefly, and the three-rat equilibrium will break, and there will be for a period a density of new life in the secondary chamber that would be astonishing to see — naked, blind, impossibly small and impossibly insistent on existing — before the count stabilizes again, as it always does.

The rats are not afraid of the place. They are not even particularly comfortable in the place. They are simply *in it*, continuous with it, part of the same slow economy as the seeping seams and the drip-shaped depression and the algae moving its millimeter toward shadow.

---

**The flies** are a population without individuals, like the crickets, but where the crickets are a wall of sound the flies are a distributed presence, a kind of animated punctuation scattered through the space. They move between the drain grates, which are their primary resource — the drains give access to the deeper organic material the drainage system carries — and the wet walls, which give moisture, and the algae, which gives whatever flies take from algae. They are not numerous enough to be disturbing. They are numerous enough to always be present, always in motion somewhere in the peripheral vision, never quite the focus of attention but never absent from it either.

One drain in particular supports a higher density of them: the central drain, which drains slightly more slowly than the others and thus collects a thin organic film around its edges, dark and rich with whatever the city sheds. Around this drain the flies conduct something that has the appearance of community, though it isn't — they have no community, no organization, no concern for one another. They share a resource. The sharing looks like belonging.

Some of them are large and slow, and land on walls and stay for long seconds with their forelegs working at their faces in a motion that looks meditative. Some are small and fast and barely visible except as motion. There is at least one species that has colonized the algae patch near the third light fixture specifically — smaller, paler, with a delicacy of movement that seems inappropriate to the space, as though they arrived from somewhere else and have not yet understood where they are.

---

**The moths** that circle the sodium fixtures are the most visually striking occupants, not because they are beautiful — they are small, drab, their wings the color of old paper, marked with patterns so subtle they are more texture than image — but because of what the light does to them. When they pass close to a fixture their wings catch the sodium glow and become briefly luminous, warm-toned, almost amber, and their shadows are thrown against the ceiling in enormous momentary shapes — huge fluttering projections, a wing-beat that would be a meter wide if the shadow were the thing — before they angle away and return to their actual scale and their actual drabness and continue their circuit.

They do not seem to eat much, or anything, in their adult form. They are brief. Their larvae are somewhere in the space, feeding on something — the mold probably, the stored organic matter in the dry corners, the accumulated debris of years. The adults live only to mate and to circle lights, and they do both without visible urgency, on the slow elliptical schedule of things that cannot be in a hurry because they are already doing everything they are capable of doing.

---

**The algae** is life, and it is the kind of life that challenges the definition most directly, that makes you reconsider whether the definition needs challenging. It is green — startlingly, insistently, almost aggressively green in the sodium light that turns everything else amber and brown and black — in the way that only living things that are entirely committed to photosynthesis can be green. Where there is algae there is a brightness that doesn't belong, a vegetable fact in an entirely mineral environment. 

It grows in five discrete patches, each anchored to a light source, each a slightly different shade of the same green, each extending in the direction light reaches and stopping where the light fails. The growth is slow but it is genuine growth: the patches are larger than they were a year ago by amounts you could measure if you had measured then. One of them has begun to send a thin green stain along a crack in the concrete, following the moisture that runs through it, extending its range by the crack's geography. Where this will end is not clear. The crack goes a long way. The moisture goes further.

The algae does not move. It grows. This is enough, apparently. This is what the algae requires of itself.

---

**The calcium formations** are not alive, and yet they are not entirely unlike life, and this is the cistern's deepest fact: the line here is soft, negotiable. The mineral deposits extend themselves by the same logic as the algae — they go where conditions permit, they take what is available, they build structure from accumulation. They do not metabolize. They do not reproduce. And yet they grow, and they have been growing for long enough that the longest of the stalactite-nubs has achieved a quarter of an inch, a half-inch, the beginning of a real shape, a taper, a direction. Given centuries — and the cistern will likely have them — they will be remarkable. They are already remarkable, if you look at them as what they are: the place making more of itself, the chemistry of the city's water expressing itself in structure, the calcium that was once somewhere else now permanently here, arranged in a form that records the patient drip of years the way a tree ring records seasons.

---

**What lives here, taken together**, is an ecology in the proper sense: not just a collection of organisms but a set of relationships, a flow of energy and matter through a system that has found its own equilibrium. The algae feeds the flies. The flies feed the rats. The crickets feed the rats and the moths and the smaller flies and perhaps the spiders — there are spiders, you would find them if you looked, in the high corners and behind the conduit runs, small ones, their webs beaded with moisture, the webs themselves going silver where the calcium has begun to deposit on the silk, the webs slowly mineralizing, becoming something new, becoming part of the ceiling. The spiders wait in these slowly petrifying webs for what comes to them. Things come.

The calcium feeds the formations. The formations redirect the drips. The drips feed the algae and the moss — there is a moss too, in two places, dark green and dense and soft and entirely silent, absorbing the water and releasing it slowly, keeping its patch of concrete perpetually saturated. The moss feeds the flies and the crickets. The crickets feed the rats. The old rat dies eventually and the decay of it feeds something, and that something feeds something else, and the chain continues.

And through all of this the city drips. The city vibrates in the low register felt in the sternum. The city sends its heat down through the slab and its water down through the seams and its discarded organic matter down through the drains, and the cistern takes all of it and incorporates it, and the animals take it and incorporate it, and the minerals take it and incorporate it, and the whole thing turns, slowly, on its own axis, warm and wet and singing.

---

What lives here, finally, is **the place itself** — the cistern is not a container for life but a participant in it, the concrete and the brick and the old clay pipes all engaged in the same slow becoming as the algae and the rats and the moths and the calcium. The iron stairs are oxidizing into something that is not stairs. The ceiling is becoming cave, very slowly. The drip-depression in the floor is becoming a small bowl that will hold more water, which will deposit more mineral, which will raise the edges of the bowl, which will hold more water still.

In enough time — not human time, but time — this place will have made itself into something different from what it was designed to be. Not ruins, exactly. Not nature, exactly. Something the language doesn't quite have a word for: the ongoing collaboration between what was built and what builds itself through and over and around what was built, neither one winning, both of them continuing, the rats and the crickets and the calcium and the concrete all making the same patient claim on the future.

Nothing urgent. Everything happening. The drip finds its groove. The city breathes above, unaware. The cricket-sound rises and reconstitutes and rises again, the sound of the place thinking, if it thinks, or simply the sound of the place being, which is perhaps the same thing.
