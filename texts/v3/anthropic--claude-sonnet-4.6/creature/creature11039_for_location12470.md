# What Lives in the Worn Table

---

Begin with what does not declare itself.

The plateau does not present its inhabitants. It withholds them the way old patience withholds — not as concealment exactly, not as strategy, but as the natural state of things that have become entirely continuous with where they are. To live here for long enough is to have solved the problem of separation. The gap between creature and place, which in younger, lusher landscapes opens wide enough to walk through, has here been pressed to nearly nothing by time and by the specific curriculum of this altitude, this dryness, this thin direct heat. What lives here has not adapted to the plateau so much as it has, over generations so numerous they stop being countable, *become an argument the plateau is making about itself*.

This is the first thing to understand. The lives here are not visitors who stayed. They are *conclusions*.

---

The lizards, most obviously. But *obviously* is already wrong for them, because obvious implies visibility and they are the principle of visibility undone. They are solved in ochre and grey at the level of the individual scale, each one a fragment of the rock surface given locomotion, and when they are still — which is most of the time, because they operate on reptile economics, where warmth is income and movement is expense — they are genuinely part of the ground. Not camouflage in the designed, purposeful sense that implies a creature trying to hide. Something slower and more total than that. They have the same color because they have been made of the same light for so long that the difference became inexact. 

They are warm-needing things in a place that rations warmth carefully, and so they have arranged their entire lives around its precise geography. The angle of a surface, the orientation of a crack, the thermal difference between shaded rock and lit rock — these are not environmental details to the lizards. These are the map of the possible. They know the plateau the way the plateau knows itself: from below, from inside, from the level of the grain. They emerged from the cracks between your footfalls this morning and you did not count them until you tried, and when you tried you found twelve in a hundred square meters and knew you were miscounting down.

They do not hurry. Hurrying is a mammalian problem, a symptom of warm blood that burns whether you use it or not. The lizards spend this long afternoon doing the thing that looks like nothing and is everything: accumulating heat, staying alive, watching. Their watching has a quality that is difficult not to read as attention, though attention implies something beyond the physiological and you cannot be certain. The eye that tracks you as you pass — the stillness that holds while you move, the sudden total absence when something triggers departure — it looks like patience. It looks like a considered relationship with time. Whether there is anyone home inside the patience is a question the plateau keeps open, as it keeps most questions: without urgency, without expectation of answer.

---

The small birds do not sing here the way birds elsewhere sing. They use their voices minimally, like people in a library, like workers who have learned to communicate in the shortest units adequate to the task. A chip. A contact note. The briefest of announcements. This is not a failure of richness — it is a recalibration to context, the same recalibration that produces all of the plateau's particular forms. There is no forest canopy through which sound must carry for courtship, no dense understory in which getting lost is possible. The plateau is entirely visible in every direction. There is no need to broadcast. The birds have learned — over how many generations you can't calculate — that on this surface, you can always be seen, and therefore the auditory performance that elsewhere accomplishes visibility is here redundant. They move between the thorned shrubs in short decisive trajectories, and their silence makes them seem purposeful in a way that singing birds do not, as though each movement is an errand they know the reason for.

They eat the insects. There are insects everywhere and you feel them more than see them, small and quick at the moisture-mats, hovering at the resins, going about their business in the way insects always go about their business: absolutely, without ambivalence, driven by imperatives so old they have calcified into what looks like joyful certainty. The insects here are not spectacular. They are not large or colorful or notably strange. They are the functional class, the facilitators, the ones without whom the whole system stalls: pollinators for the shrubs and the cushion-plants, decomposers of the rare fallen thing, food for the birds and the lizards and whatever else finds them. They are the plateau's liquidity, its medium of exchange, and they are old in the same way everything here is old — continuous with a lineage so long it stops being a lineage and becomes a condition.

---

The raptors are the only things that seem to have arrived rather than emerged. 

This is an illusion, but it is a persuasive one, because they come from above, from the sky, which is the one direction that is not the plateau — and they circle with an aloofness that looks like detachment, like the assessment of the outsider rather than the intimacy of the native. But they know this surface in the way the best surgeons know the body they work on: completely, precisely, without warmth and without indifference. The slow wide circles are not randomness. They are reading. Every lizard on every sun-warmed surface, every bird between every shrub, every movement in the moisture-mats — this information rises to them through the warm column of air and they process it in the enormous patience of predators who know that the plateau will eventually offer what they need.

Their size up here is slightly wrong, slightly more than you expect, which means they come from somewhere lower, somewhere with more food than this high sparseness provides, and they ascend to hunt the concentrated small lives. They are not of this plateau entirely. They are its interface with the vertical world, its connection to the slopes below, the ambassador-function of a place that otherwise has almost no relation to anywhere else. When they depart in the lowering light they take something of the plateau with them — a lizard, maybe, something taken from the surface crust, the small bodies of the small things — and carry it down into the richer world below, and this is how the plateau contributes to the larger system: in the small packages carried away by wings at dusk.

---

There are things in the burrows.

This is stated with the simplicity it deserves, because the burrows are a definitive fact and their occupants are not. In daylight: nothing emerges. The holes are there, circular and regular in the hardpan, more numerous than first noticed and then more numerous still as the eye learns to look for them, and they are inhabited — this is clear from the freshness of the disturbed edges, from the faint compaction-paths that radiate from the larger ones, from the simple logic that holes that have been made are made by something — but what exactly is a question the plateau answers on its own schedule.

At dusk something moves. This is all that can be said with confidence. At the threshold between stored-heat day and cooling night, when the lizards are gone and the raptors have made their last circuit, the burrow-mouths show the first hesitant activity of things whose lives run inverse to the sun. Small movements. Not the bullet-fast disappearances of the lizards but something more tentative, more enquiring — the approach-and-withdrawal rhythm of creatures that are also prey, that have learned caution at the same cellular level at which the lizards have learned stillness. They test the air at the hole's edge. They wait. They test again.

What emerges, eventually, in the dark that you cannot quite see in: something small, something that moves by sound and by scent and by the information in its whiskers, something warm-blooded and therefore always hungry, always burning, always conducting the expensive mammalian project of staying alive by constant transaction with the environment. Rodents, the most likely interpretation — or something enough like rodents that the category serves. They have made the plateau's surface their ceiling, the deep slow world of stone and root and dark their floor, and they live in between, in the narrow gap of the burrow, and they come out only when the raptors have gone and the air is cool enough that the cold-blooded things that would otherwise catch them have retreated into their cracks.

They are not alone down there. The burrows, the deep ones, the ones with the well-worn paths — these feel inhabited by more than one kind of thing, sequentially or simultaneously or in some negotiated cohabitation that long association has made functional. The plateau is older than any current occupant's lineage by orders of magnitude. There have been generations beyond counting of things using these same cracks, these same holes, and the infrastructure of occupancy has become part of the geology. The plateau is full of paths that have no surface expression but are known by the things that use them in the dark.

---

At another scale, and this is where it becomes difficult to describe without losing the thread of the visible:

The biological crust is alive. This is technically simple to state and experientially difficult to fully absorb. What looks like rock surface — the matte dark-grey and rust-red skin pulled tight across the plateau — is a community. Not a metaphor. An actual community of cyanobacteria and fungi and mosses and algae in a structural relationship that has been sustained, in these high dry places, for so long that individual concepts of organism lose their grip. The crust does not have members the way a pond has members. It *is* the relationship, in the way that a forest sometimes *is* the mycelial network and the trees are what the network does when it gets enough light.

It processes the rain. It fixes nitrogen from the thin air. It cements the plateau's surface against the wind that would otherwise, over centuries, begin to unmake the remaining. In this sense it is the plateau's maintenance mechanism, its immune system, the thin biochemical project that keeps the whole table set. When you walk on it carelessly it cracks, because it is as fragile as it is ancient — and the fragility and the antiquity are the same thing, since it took so long to become this dense and this interlocked that any break leaves a gap that will not close again in a human lifetime, or several. It recovers. But on schedules calibrated to the very long view.

The cushion-mats at the water-edge are the crust's more visible expression, the places where moisture has allowed the community to become upright, three-dimensional, architectural. A single cushion-mat that spreads three meters across the edge of a catch-basin might be three hundred years old at its perimeter and five hundred at its center and a thousand at the very oldest interior, and these ages are all present simultaneously, all part of the same individual that does not know itself as an individual in any sense a lizard or a raptor would recognize. It is a different category of being: not a thing that lives but a *living* that things, a process that has achieved a local persistence.

The cushion-mat does not watch you. But it is affected by your shadow when you stand over it, and it will still be here when nothing remains of your having come.

---

Then there is the matter of what was here before.

The stones out of place. The rectangular smoothed hollow.

The lives these indicate are gone in the way of complete departure — no trace of continuity, no descendant form. Nothing living here now is the direct cultural heir of what arranged those stones. And yet the stone-arrangements persist as a kind of influence on the current occupancy. The hollow that nothing grows in and that lizards avoid — the avoidance itself is behavior that was learned once and has become encoded, passed through generations until the reason was lost and only the behavior remains, written into the animal's hesitation the way old words survive in languages that have forgotten their origins. The plateau's current inhabitants carry the residue of its human history in their bodies without knowing it. The absence the humans left has become part of the landscape, and the landscape has become part of the animals, and so the humans are still here in the attenuated form of a behavioral trace, an avoidance-pattern, a small hollow in the going of the place.

This is how the plateau holds its history. Not in monuments. In the living things that have adjusted themselves around what happened, layer by layer, the adjustments becoming so old that they look like nature.

---

The frogs.

Once, after the rain — three or four calls from a catch-basin, then silence.

This is worth stopping on. A frog. On this high dry plateau, in a catch-basin the depth of a palm, making the calls that frogs make by some imperative so ancient it predates this landscape by geological periods. Where do they come from? Where do they go? The basins dry in days. But the frogs return each time, each rain, and they call into the brief water with the same three or four notes, because three or four notes is enough, because on a quiet plateau that is otherwise the sound of slow processes happening below auditory resolution, three or four notes carries exactly as far as it needs to.

They must aestivate. They must go down into the cracks and the burrows and the deep wet places beneath the hardpan and become a kind of waiting that is barely metabolism, barely alive by any measure except that when the water comes they return to being entirely alive, instantly, without apparent transition. They have divided their lives into two completely different modes and move between them without what seems like disorientation. The frog in the catch-basin calling after rain is the same individual as the frog-shaped dried thing in the deep crack, and it does not seem troubled by this.

This is, in miniature, the plateau's whole strategy: to become less in the dry times, to be concentrated and slow and barely-there, and then — when the water comes, when conditions allow — to return completely and immediately to the full fact of living.

Everything here has learned this. Not just the frogs. The cushion-mats go grey and dormant in the worst dry stretches and green again within hours of rain. The insects disappear and reappear. The biological crust is gray and desiccated and then black and alive within the same forty-minute rain. Even the lizards are less-alive in the coldest nights and more-alive in the warmest afternoons, living not as a constant condition but as a variable one, turning up and down with the available energy. The whole plateau pulses on this slow breath. What lives here is not so much a population as a *potential*, coiled and waiting in the rock, ready to express itself at each opening, then drawing back when the opening closes.

---

Here is the full inventory of what lives in the worn table, then, assembled without pretending to completeness:

*The lizards*, who are the rock's thinking, its attention without interpretation, twelve visible and more below, who watch you with their long patience and vanish so completely into the surface when they go that vanishing and never-having-been become indistinguishable.

*The small brown birds*, who know the shrubs intimately and move between them in purposeful silence, who have edited their own voices down to the essential syllable, who are eating right now, constantly eating, because they burn fast and the plateau is not generous.

*The insects*, invisible mostly except as motion and as inference, the medium through which energy moves from plant to bird and lizard, the plateau's circulatory system at the scale of wings.

*The raptors*, circling at the top of the thermal column in their patient supervision, belonging to the sky as much as the plateau but knowing the plateau as the sky's extension downward, reading the surface from above the way the lizards read it from below.

*The burrow-things*, unnamed but warm-blooded and cautious, who live the inverse day, who come out when the raptors go, who have made the underground their real home and the surface merely their pantry and their threshold, who carry in their hesitation at the hole-mouth a fear calibrated across ten thousand years of being eaten on this surface.

*The biological crust*, which is not exactly alive in the way a lizard is alive but is certainly not not-alive, which is the plateau's skin and memory and maintenance, which has been living at this very slow pitch for so long that speed is simply not part of its vocabulary.

*The cushion-mats*, which are the crust's high expression, its architectural ambition, which are very old and expanding slowly outward around the water-edges, which do not know they are growing.

*The frogs*, who are not always here and are therefore more itself than everything else, who carry in their simple calls the most ancient sound on the plateau, who solve the problem of the dry plateau by spending most of their lives not being frogs.

*And the residue of the people who arranged the stones*, which is not alive but which lives in the avoidance-behaviors of the current tenants and in the hollow nothing grows in, which is the longest-duration inhabitant if you count by persistence of influence rather than by breath.

---

The whole population is characterized by one quality above all others: the quality of having resolved the central problem of this place, which is that this place has nothing extra. There is no slack in the plateau's system, no surplus, no margin for the exploratory or the decorative or the failed. What lives here lives at the exact intersection of its requirements and the plateau's provisions, and this intersection has been found over very long time by a process of elimination so thorough that what remains is not so much hardy as *precise*. 

They are not survivors. That word implies the wrong drama, the wrong relationship to difficulty. They did not endure the plateau. They became *right for it*, which is a different achievement, quieter and more final.

The plateau is complete. What lives in it is the completeness expressing itself in the form of motion, metabolism, breath — the ancient stone surface animated at a thousand points by the lives it has slowly educated into being exactly what it needed, and nothing more, and nothing less, and they do not know this about themselves any more than the plateau knows it about the plateau.

But you can see it, standing here, in the late amber light, between the ground and the overwhelming stars.
