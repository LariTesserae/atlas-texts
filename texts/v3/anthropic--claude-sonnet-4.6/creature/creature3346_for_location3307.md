# Who Lives Here

---

Begin with this: there is someone who comes.

Not who stays — the question of permanent habitation is the wrong question for this place. Someone comes here with regularity significant enough that the space has organized itself around their coming, the way certain paths through grass only exist because someone walks them repeatedly, not because they were laid. The room has a path. It runs from the entrance — which must be somewhere, must be a door set into the iron and glass of the south wall, though you haven't examined it closely — along the east wall, then cutting diagonally to the gauge cabinet in the southeast corner, then doubling back along the central spine of shelving, then finishing against the north wall where the copper troughs collect their drainage. You know this path not because it is marked but because the floor plates along it are cleaner, worn differently. The condensation film on these plates is thinner — disturbed more often, redistributed by the passage of boots. The drainage grates along this route have been recently cleared. The ones off the path have a thin dark crust of accumulated matter at their edges.

Someone walks this room. Someone walks it in the same order, with the same stops, regularly enough to leave a hydrological signature.

---

Now try to determine the person from the evidence.

This is detective work conducted in botanical testimony, in the positions of tools, in wear patterns and repair choices and the hundred small decisions that accumulate into a life's shape.

---

On the north wall's lowest copper trough, a piece of brass has been soldered into a crack with some care — not crudely packed, but properly flowed in, filed smooth afterward. The soldering is confident. The file-work is patient. Someone who solders here has soldering tools here. Look for them: yes, in the corner behind the gauge cabinet, a shallow wooden tray — old, darkened with long use, the wood soft with absorbed oils — holding a small iron, a coil of solder wire, a tin of flux, a brush with most of its bristles. The brush is recent; its bristles are still pale at their tips where they haven't yet taken on the flux's amber color. The tray is old. This is a practice of long standing refreshed with new materials. The person solders regularly, replaces brushes when brushes need replacing, does not replace the tray.

The soldering skills imply more. The copper pipe-work — the small irrigation lines running from the central cabinet to the shelving — is irregular in vintage. There are sections that were clearly here from the installation, heavier-gauge, joined with professional fittings. But there are also sections of different copper, thinner-walled, joined at angles that suggest addition rather than original design, the joints done by the same hand that repaired the trough, competent but personal rather than professional. The irrigation system has been extended. Someone decided that more of the shelving medium needed direct water and added the lines to achieve this. This decision was probably made while watching something not thrive, noticing the dryness of a particular zone, and solving the specific problem rather than redesigning the whole. The patches and extensions do not bespeak an engineer. They bespeak a person who knows what they want to happen and has learned, over time and mistake, how to make most of it happen.

---

On the second shelf of the east wall's shelving, at about waist height, there is a small iron pot that is different from the others. The other pots are utilitarian, unadorned, clearly selected for function. This one is slightly smaller and has a shallow relief pattern around its upper rim — a simple repeating thing, wave-like, not ornate, but present. The pot holds a plant that is, by the room's general standards, modest: low, spreading, with small round leaves. It is healthy, clearly tended. It is positioned where the light that comes through the southeast panes strikes for the longest duration. Someone knows this plant's light preference and has respected it. Someone has, at some point, acquired this particular pot rather than the functional ones — or kept it when they might have replaced it.

This is a small piece of evidence and it wants to be read carefully. It does not say that the person who comes here is primarily defined by sentimentality. It says that sentiment exists alongside function — that the pot with the relief pattern and the pot with the soldering iron can coexist in the same practice, can belong to the same person, because most people who work alone in rooms for years over years accumulate both functional necessities and small inexplicable attachments and find no need to reconcile them.

---

The records.

On the gauge cabinet's inner door — which stands open, latched back against the wall — someone has affixed a piece of folded cloth, and to the cloth they have pinned a series of paper slips. The papers are dense with handwritten figures: dates, readings, paired numbers. Some entries are in a darker ink that has aged to brown; some are in a fresher black. The handwriting is consistent across decades of paper, which means the same person has been writing these slips for a very long time, or the handwriting is inherited — learned from someone, continued, shaped by the learning until personal and inherited have merged into one style that can no longer be distinguished from itself.

The figures track the third gauge. The double-spiral symbol appears at the top of each slip. The readings are recorded with the same frequency as the others — same date-keeping, same columns — but never accompanied by any annotation, no asterisked notes, no explanations or exclamations in margins, while the water pressure and temperature gauges have, over the years, accumulated the occasional small notation: *low, adjusted*, *froze overnight, compensated in morning*. The third gauge generates only numbers. Whether this is because its meaning is perfectly understood and requires no comment, or because it is perfectly not-understood and no comment is possible, cannot be determined from the evidence.

This is perhaps the most interesting thing about the person: they faithfully record what they may not entirely understand, and they do not seem troubled by the asymmetry.

---

Allow the shape to come.

The person is not young — or rather, the person has been doing this long enough that youth, whatever age they started, is not the relevant context anymore. They began here, or were brought here, or brought themselves here, at a time when the iron shelving was perhaps less occupied and the copper pipes ran fewer branches. They have watched things establish. They have watched the runners jump between shelves and not removed them, or have removed them and watched them jump again and eventually accepted the jumping, worked it into their understanding of how this room works. They are a person who has been revised by evidence. Their knowledge of this room is not the knowledge they arrived with; it is the knowledge they arrived with plus everything the room has since taught them, which is more.

They work with their hands but they are not purely physical in their work. The record-keeping insists on the other side. They are someone for whom the numbers matter, for whom the tracking matters, even when — especially when — what the numbers mean is not perfectly clear. The act of recording is itself significant to them, separate from what recording might produce or prove. They keep records the way the room keeps water: because that is what they are built to do.

They live in proximity to the cold. Not in it — the machinery ensures that — but proximate to it, aware of it, in continuous negotiated relationship with it. The outside pressing in through the iron framing is not an abstraction to them; it is a fact they manage daily, a force they make provision against. They think in terms of maintenance. They think in terms of what might fail and how to address the failure when it arrives. This is not pessimism; it is a disposition shaped by a long practice of caring for something fragile in a high cold place. The soldering iron in the wooden tray is optimism. It is the optimism of being prepared.

---

What they want is harder.

What anyone wants in a place like this — a room they have tended for years on the face of something high — what the wanting even means, at the end of a long habituation, when the difference between what you chose and what you became is no longer visible.

But there are clues at the edge.

The fern, somewhere in the northwest of the room, that has been encouraged — this is visible in the wire guide, in the way it's been retrained more than once — to grow toward a particular pane of glass, a particular clear pane, so that its frond now spreads across the interior surface of that pane from below. Look through that pane from the inside. Look through it past the fern: you see a section of sky, the pewter-white of the undecided day, and below it a section of whatever lies below the precipice. The view has been framed. The fern is the frame. Someone spent several seasons arranging for this window to show exactly what it shows, and they did it with a plant, which means the framing adjusts seasonally, is never quite the same twice, is a living frame that participates in what it frames.

They wanted to see something specific, in a specific way, with living green around it.

This does not resolve into a simple want. It contains within it something about beauty, something about botany, something about the relationship between the constructed interior and the uncontrolled exterior, and maybe something about what it means to frame a view versus to simply look at it. The frame says: this. The plant frame says: this, and also: growing.

---

Who they might have been elsewhere, before this place or outside it:

A person of middle age or past it, probably, who occupies the world with a certain quiet that could be read as solitude and could be read as self-containment and could be read as the natural affect of someone who spends most of their time in a room where the loudest sounds are condensation drops and the mechanical tick of a pressure cabinet. They are not loud when they speak. Their silences are not uncomfortable — to them, anyway, and possibly to others who have had time to learn the register.

They may not have explained this place to many people. Not because it is secret but because it resists summary, and people who live in places that resist summary often stop trying to summarize and this eventually becomes indistinguishable from reticence.

If you asked them what they do here they would probably begin with the practical. The maintenance. The recording. The irrigation. They might mention the plants, the particular requirements of the plants, the way the mycelial medium behaves differently in different seasons and must be compensated for. They would not begin with the third gauge or the framed fern or what it is that keeps them coming back up to this cold high constructed room with their wooden tray of soldering tools and their small pot with the wave-relief rim and their newest brush, still pale at the tips. They would not begin there because they have not finished with those things, or those things have not finished with them, and it is difficult to report on an ongoing process in terms of its meaning.

But if you stayed long enough, and if the condensation drops fell in their patient arithmetic, and if the floor hum settled into the background of perception rather than foreground, and if the ghost-rainbow traveled its few centimeters across the medium shelf —

They might tell you about the double-spiral symbol.

They might tell you what they think it measures.

They might be wrong about what it measures, and know they are probably wrong, and tell you anyway.

---

This is who lives here:

Someone who came to maintain and stayed to understand, and has not yet finished, and expects to continue.

Someone who has learned the patience of the room by living inside it long enough to let the room's pace replace their own.

Someone for whom the difference between caring for a thing and being changed by caring for it has long since dissolved into the same daily practice of showing up, walking the path, reading the gauges, adding the figures to the slip, pinning the slip, noting what is new in the growth, noting what has died (nothing, today, nothing — the dead things are only outside), refilling the wooden tray when something needs refilling.

Someone who walks out, eventually, through the south-wall door, into the cold, who does not look back at the structure as they leave because they know it completely from the inside and looking at it from the outside is a different kind of knowledge that they have decided, or perhaps simply ceased, to pursue.

Who will come back.

The third gauge will have moved.

They will record where it has moved to.
