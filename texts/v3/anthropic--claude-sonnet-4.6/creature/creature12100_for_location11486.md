## The People of the Plateau Works

---

They are not large people. Something about generations at altitude, or the particular demands the plateau makes on a body — the wet cold that arrives without warning, the long distances walked on hard stone, the way the voice must always be working — has made them compact and efficient in their proportions. Their hands are wide. Their necks are thick, or have become thick, or the ones with thick necks stayed while others left — it is impossible now to sort inheritance from adaptation from coincidence. They move with a deliberate economy, the way people move when they grew up in a place that doesn't reward unnecessary motion. Not slowness. Efficiency with the texture of slowness.

Their hearing is unusual. Not diminished, as you might expect — or not only diminished, though the older ones have lost the high registers, the delicate upper frequencies gone first, given over to the engines as an inevitable tithe. What has happened instead, or also, is a sharpening of the middle registers, the human-voice frequencies, so that they hear *each other* with unusual acuity even within the industrial floor. A person trained in noise will find a person's voice in it the way a good eye finds a bird in sky: not by silencing the background but by knowing exactly what shape to attend to. They are extraordinarily attentive to one another's faces. Lip-reading here is not a specialized skill for the hearing-impaired; it is what everyone does, continuously, the way everyone navigates rain without being taught. The face carries meaning here in stacked registers — the voice says the word, the face says what the voice couldn't project, the hands say the rest.

There is a thing they do, meeting someone, where they position themselves. Not rudely close — but at a specific distance that is slightly closer than the habit of most cultures, perhaps a hand's-width inside what elsewhere feels comfortable, close enough that the voice carries without the effortful shout the distances require, close enough to read the face easily. Visitors find this initially crowding. It is, of course, courtesy. It is the posture of attention, of *I am making it easier for us to understand each other*, and once you see it this way it becomes touching, all these people positioning themselves carefully to hear and be heard in a place that makes hearing difficult. Every conversation is a minor act of accommodation.

---

The children are immediately distinct from the children of quieter places.

They shout, but not as children shout in anger or excitement — they shout the way adults here shout, with practice and calibration, with the voice placed forward, with projection rather than explosion. A child of five here can carry a voice across a street against the engine-register in a way that a forty-year-old from elsewhere cannot. They have also, because they are children and because the gesture-language of the old ones is available and interesting, elaborated it significantly. The formal code the elders developed for efficient adult communication has, in children's hands, become something richer and stranger, a full grammatical system that the children use among themselves with the speed and fluency of a private language, hands and faces working simultaneously, the way children always make something playful out of what adults made practical. The adults understand perhaps half of it. The children find this appropriate.

They play near the water channels. The channels are the city's invitation to childhood — the moss walls, the small quick things in the water, the sticks dragged to make temporary dams, the bridges with their worn edges that are sized as though intended for small people to sit upon. The children here are not afraid of wet; they have never learned to avoid it, growing up in a place where wet is simply the state of surfaces, and so they are often damp and apparently unconcerned by this, their boots dark with absorbed moisture, their clothing slightly humid in the way of people who don't divide the day into wet-times and dry-times.

The games they play on the open limestone, away from the channel zones, have adapted to the grooves. There are games involving stones placed in particular patterns relative to the finger-groove channels — territorial games, games of placement and displacement, games that require knowing which way the light is going to move across the iridescent mineral crust at what time of morning, so that a child fluent in this game is also a child who reads the plateau's light-behavior intuitively, who can glance at a channel and tell you within twenty minutes when it will flicker. The word the adults use casually — the short two-syllable word for the phenomenon — the children have extended into a family of related words for related things: a word for the moment just before the flickering begins, a word for the particular channel-section that reliably flickers longest, a word for the game-move that uses the light.

---

Their relationship to silence is complicated in the way that the absence of a thing is always complicated for people who have never had it.

There are those who have left — who grew up here and then went somewhere quiet and found the quiet difficult in ways they had not expected. Not peaceful. Unmooring. The engines' constant register had been, all their lives, the evidence that something was happening, that the work was continuing, that the city was alive in its particular way; and without it, they found that sleep required the brain to do something it had never learned to do, which is to settle without that assurance. Some come back. Some learn to sleep with machines running somewhere nearby — a fan, a generator, something — and feel mild shame about this, which is unjust, since all they have is a body that learned its world.

Those who have always stayed do not know they have this relationship to the sound. The engines are not a sound to them; they are a condition of being here, no more noticeable than the temperature of your own skin. Ask someone who has never left to describe the sound of this place and they will describe the birds, and the water in the channels, and the way a particular alley resonates when two people are talking in it, and the specific percussion of the eastern factory that has its own rhythm distinguishable from the others once you know it. They have sorted the auditory world into figure and ground so fully that the ground has vanished from their attention, and the figures they've identified are intricate, specific, loved in the way of things you've had to work to perceive.

When the engines stop — this has happened, occasionally, in the memory of the eldest — it is not described as peaceful. It is described as wrong. As a kind of alarm that the alarm is absent. Even people sleeping wake, because the body had been continuously, subliminally reassured, and now the reassurance has stopped, and the body knows. The story of the last time this happened is told with the texture of crisis, not relief.

---

They have opinions about the mineral crust. Of course they do — they have lived beside it for generations, watched it from childhood games, found the word for it and the family of words, and the crust is neither rock nor living thing, and this has been discussed at length across time.

The pragmatists say it is a mineral deposit left by specific water chemistry and has no further significance. The pragmatists are respected; they are the ones who maintain the engines and re-channel the streets when the limestone erodes another millimeter down. Practicality is not in bad standing here. But.

The others — and there are always others, in any city long enough established that it has had time to notice its own strangeness — say that the crust's light behavior is too particular to be incidental. They have observed that the flickering sequences in early morning follow the direction of the ancient finger-grooves, which is also the direction of the streets, which is also the direction of the engineered water channels. They point out that this alignment cannot be purely geological. They don't have a complete theory about what it *is*, exactly — these people resist completion, finding certainty a form of intellectual laziness — but they have a developed vocabulary for the experience of watching the channels flicker, and they take time in the early mornings, individually or in small groups, to observe it, in the way that people make time for things they consider worth making time for.

There is no formal division between these groups, no conflict. They share the short word and its extensions. The pragmatist who re-channels a street for drainage may pause in the early morning before work to watch the light move. The two orientations toward the same phenomenon are not considered contradictory here, which perhaps says something about what long cohabitation with an unresolved mystery does to epistemology over generations.

---

The old ones are the governing fact of the social architecture.

Partly this is practical: in a place where knowledge of water management and engine maintenance and limestone erosion-rate and factory-process represents survival across centuries, the old ones have the accumulation. Their knowing is not theoretical; it is the sediment of seasons, of decisions that worked and decisions that failed, of the particular way this engine sounds when it needs attention before it breaks rather than after. Nobody here has contempt for the old — they have too much evidence that the old know things that take decades to learn, and the plateau has made visible, repeatedly, what happens when accumulated knowledge leaves faster than it can be replaced.

But also: the old here are not simply repositories. They are still engaged, still present in the daily texture of the city, still sitting on the bridge edges and dragging their own sticks through the channels in the idle way of people who have earned idleness without entirely wanting it. Their gestures have the fluency of full lives, the code elaborated across seventy years of daily use into something that seems, to outside eyes, almost like a performance — such economy, such precision, a half-sentence of speech complemented by three gestures that carry three other sentences simultaneously, the hands saying what the voice has stopped competing with the engines to say. They are very funny. The humor of people who have outlasted urgency. The humor of people who know, from long evidence, that things move slowly and that this is not tragic.

They know the city's deep history the way you know a room you've lived in long — not from documents, but from the rock itself. That wall was rebuilt, and rebuilt again, and rebuilt again; the original stones are in there, nested in. This corner of this factory building has a different acoustic quality than the other corners because of a modification made two hundred years ago to redirect a water channel, and if you stand there in the right wind you can still hear the difference, a slight harmonic shift, a small ghost of former arrangement. The plateau is their primary text, and they are very good readers.

---

What they believe, if belief is the right word, is difficult to extract because they don't practice it separately from living. There is no building here that has as its clear purpose the articulation of what the city considers sacred, which may be because what the city considers something-like-sacred is not separate from the city itself — not a principle or a narrative but a quality of attention paid to the work and the stone and the sound and the water and the light. The engine-throb as weather, as condition, as background assurance. The mineral crust and its morning language. The water in its channels, managed and directed and nonetheless surprising, arriving from inside the rock itself, weeping through seams, occupying its assigned place with something that reads, if you are inclined to read it this way, as contentment.

They are not a people who expect the extraordinary. The extraordinary has been here so long it has become ordinary. The plateau flickers in early morning and people watch it and use their word and go to work. The engines hum through the rock underfoot and nobody mentions it because it is as unremarkable as one's own pulse. The buildings contain their original stones, nested inside later stones, and the streets follow the ancient grooves, and the water goes where it knows to go, and the birds with their plain utilitarian bodies call above the noise in the frequencies the engines don't occupy.

To live here is to live inside a system that has been running longer than anyone's personal life and will run longer than their personal death, and the people have become — not resigned to this, not defeated by it — *suited* to it. The way the scrub grass made its deal with difficulty. The way the birds found their frequency. The way the limestone, given water and time, makes something neither rock nor living that lights up the morning in sequences the children have names for.

They stay, mostly. And those who leave carry the plateau with them in the wideness of their hands and the economy of their motion and the way they position themselves a half-step closer than expected, making it easier, making the effort of being understood a thing they've already done before you've had to ask.
