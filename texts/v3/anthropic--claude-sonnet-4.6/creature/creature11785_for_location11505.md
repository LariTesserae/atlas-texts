# What Lives on the Plate

---

Begin with what is hardest to say: that the boundary between *inhabitant* and *place* is not maintained here. Whatever lives on the Plate has been here long enough that the Plate has shaped it in ways that go beyond adaptation — has become, in some sense, its body's reference point, the template against which it calibrates itself. The organisms of the Plate are Plate-logic made ambulatory, or sessile, or something between those two that doesn't have a good word.

But let's not begin in abstraction. Let's begin close.

---

## The Mats

Start with what is most present. The mats are alive — genuinely, unambiguously alive in every functional sense — but they complicate what alive is usually taken to mean. They do not, as far as sustained observation can determine, have anything that would be called experience of being alive. Not in the sense of urgency. Not in the sense of *caring* about any of the things that most organisms care about: reproduction, acquisition, escape, continuity. They do all of these things, but they do them the way the Plate redistributes stress — automatically, continuously, without drama, as a property rather than a behavior.

What a mat *is*: a colony, probably, though this word keeps slipping. A mat examined closely reveals that it is composed of units, and the units are composed of smaller units, and at some level below that the composition is not easily separable from the Plate's own surface chemistry — the mat does not grow *on* the Plate so much as *from* it, borrowing forms from the ceramic layer's own mineral spontaneity, using as body what the Plate puts out as a kind of fine residue. There are researchers who argue that the mats are the Plate's immune system; others who argue they are its exhaled breath; others who argue they are entirely independent organisms that have simply solved the Plate as a surface the way a barnacle solves a hull. All of these framings have problems. All of them capture something.

The units within a mat are — here the vocabulary starts to strain — *attentive*. This is not the right word, because it implies a direction of attention and a something doing the attending. But they orient. They change their chemical relationships with neighboring units in response to incoming information, and they do this in waves, and the waves propagate across the mat according to rules that look, uncomfortably, like decision-making. A pressure applied to the mat's edge will produce, over a period of some hours, a response in the mat's center. The response is not random. It is organized. Whether it is *appropriate* to the pressure — whether it counts as a reaction — is the thing researchers cannot settle.

A single mat is: slow. It grows at rates so small that a human lifetime of observation doesn't show you its motion; you have to read the record, extrapolate from the pattern, trust the math. But the mat that covers six hundred kilometers has been doing this for so long that slow has become vast. The outermost edge of that mat, pale and fine-grained, is the growing edge, the young part — though young here means centuries. The center, dark as terracotta, is dense with accumulated years, with the compressed record of whatever the mat has been doing all this time. The center is not dormant. The center is the most active part: the transfer-activity of the Plate goes into it most intensely there, and whatever the mat does with that — however it processes the long, slow cycling of the Plate's internal currents — it does more of it at its oldest, deepest point.

If you stand on the center of the old mat, you feel — again, in that body-sense that precedes hearing — a slight difference. A quality of being occupied, of something using the medium you're also using. It is not threatening. It is more like standing in a room that was lived in for many years by someone who knew how to live quietly.

The mat has a consciousness the way a weather system has an intention: probably not, technically; but the pattern is complex enough and self-organizing enough that the distinction starts to feel academic.

## What Moves Between the Mats

In the spaces where the mats have not yet reached — and there are spaces, open ceramic, pale and bare, that could be called the between-places — something moves. Or something is sometimes there.

The peripheral sightings are real, in the sense that multiple independent observers have reported them in terms that are consistent enough to suggest a common cause. Something low, something that uses the shallow shadow of mat-edge for cover, something whose pace and size suggest an organism with organized locomotion. The question of whether it is *one thing* or a *class of things*, whether what was glimpsed in one between-place is related to what was glimpsed in another, remains open.

Here is what can be inferred from the reports, minus the artifact-of-lighting explanation (which explains the most but not quite all):

It is small. Smaller than the stalks. Perhaps hand-sized; perhaps smaller. It moves in a direction, not randomly — it has somewhere to be, or behaves as though it does, which is in practice the same thing. Its color is either very close to the Plate surface or it changes; no one has seen it clearly enough to say. It is not a mat-unit that has separated and become mobile; the mat units are not that kind of thing. It is something else, something that occupies the between-places as its habitat, that uses the Plate's surface the way a tidal creature uses the wet margin — precisely, at the edge of two zones.

What it eats, if it eats: the surface compounds. The fine particulate the ceramic releases. Possibly the mat's outer edge — there are zones on the old mat's perimeter that show uneven growth, patterns that suggest something has been browsing there in ways that damage the growing edge slightly, and then the mat grows around the damage, and then perhaps the damage happens again. Whether this is a predation or a relationship — whether the mat tolerates the browsing because the thing that browses contributes something, some activity, some small mechanical function that serves the mat's slow purposes — is not known.

It may be afraid of being seen, or it may simply be very good at the space between attentions. On the Plate, where everything has had time to become very good at something, this seems likely.

## What Lives Below the Surface

This is the harder conversation.

The thing tracked by instruments through the deep-lap substrate, the thing that moved through the Plate's conducting lattice at forty centimeters down, that had a large and unclean outline and moved at a pace the human mind resists summarizing — that thing is the Plate's deepest secret and maybe its deepest self.

Here is what can be said:

It is not in the Plate. It is *of* the Plate. The distinction matters. It does not inhabit the substrate the way a worm inhabits soil; it participates in the substrate's own processes so completely that calling it a separate entity is a categorization imposed from outside, by observers who need categories. From inside — if inside is a concept that applies — there may be no separation. The transfer-activity and the thing that moves through it may be the same phenomenon at two different levels of description.

What it is *doing*: redistribution. What everything in the Plate does, but consciously, or whatever consciousness becomes after it has been this kind of thing for this long. The Plate does not accumulate; the thing below does not let it. The Plate does not stagnate; the thing below does not permit it. It is the Plate's active principle, its circulating life, the thing that keeps the flat world from becoming simply flat. If the mats are the Plate's exhalation, this is the Plate's heartbeat — not a metaphor for a simple pump, but a metaphor for the thing that, in a body, makes the body be a body rather than a collection of organs.

There are two records of it. This is a small number for something so significant.

In both records, what the instruments noted was: passage. Not arrival, not departure as distinct events, but a passage, as though the thing had been passing for long enough that the records only caught the middle of it. Whether the thing moves continuously or moves in cycles whose period exceeds the duration of any instrument deployment is unknown. Whether there is one of them or many or whether *one* and *many* are the wrong question for something that moves through conducting material and may not have a stable boundary is very much unknown.

It has been here as long as the Plate has been a world. It may have been here longer. It may have been the first thing, before the mats, before anything — a current of life in the deep structure, waiting for conditions at the surface to develop to the point where the surface could be a surface.

No one who has been near a deep-lap and lain flat and felt the quality of the attended quiet believes, fully, that they imagined it.

## The Stalks

The stalks are solitary in a way that seems intentional, which means it probably is not intentional, which means it might be. They rise once, from an attachment point that the surface reveals reluctantly, and are there for a period, and are then not.

They are not plants. They share chemistry with the mats but express it differently — vertically, momentarily, in a form that concentrates something the mat spreads out. The researchers who have spent the most time with them have proposed that the stalks are moments of the mat's attention made visible. That when the mat, in its slow and patient processing of the Plate's transfer-currents, arrives at something that needs to be different for a while, it produces a stalk: a standing-up of the mat's internal logic, briefly, into the air. A posture. An interface.

An interface with what?

With the air-space above the Plate, probably. With whatever moves through it that the mat cannot reach from its position on the surface. With the three things that have been seen sitting near them at midday — small and bright and observed and then not-observed without transition. Maybe with those. Maybe with something that hasn't been documented. The mat has been running its processes for longer than documentation exists; it has had interfaces that no one has been present to record.

The stalks do not sway. This is the central fact about them, the one that everything else must account for. In a wind that is warm and real and measurable, they do not move. Either they are very rigid, or they are doing something that cancels the wind's force at their surface, or the wind, somehow, goes around them — not deflected, exactly, just: around, as though the stalk has made a quiet agreement with the air. The quality of not-sound near a stalk is related to this. The stalk is, in some sense, negotiating constantly with its environment, and the negotiation is very close to silent, and the near-silence of it is what you can almost hear.

When a stalk is gone — when it has done whatever it was doing and ended, or withdrawn, or been reabsorbed into the mat that generated it — there is no mark. The mat closes over the attachment point. The ceramic surface is unchanged. There is a period, the researchers say, during which the local transfer-activity is slightly elevated, a faint surplus of whatever was cycling through the stalk still moving through the area. Then that settles. Then the place where the stalk was is just the Plate again, and if the mat grows over it, you won't be able to find it.

## The Small Bright Things

Three times. Always on shoulders. Always in the highest-luminance conditions.

They sit still. They are bright — not reflective-bright, not catching light, but bright as though the brightness is theirs, which in the Plate's even light is strange, because there is no dramatic light to catch. They are distinct. They are observed. And then they are not.

This is everything that is known, and it is very little, and it feels like more than it is, which is part of the problem.

They might be the between-place movers, come out into full light for reasons of their own — but the between-place movers are described as small and low and indistinct, and the bright things are none of those. They might be something that has not been documented in any other context, an organism whose ecology fits nowhere else in what is known of the Plate. They might be a phase of something already described, a form that one of the known things takes under conditions of maximum light, maximum luminance, conditions on a shoulder at midday when the pale sky is as bright as it gets. They might be visitors from somewhere that is not the Plate, resting here the way a bird rests on a ship far from land.

The appeal of this last idea is hard to dismiss: the idea of the Plate as a stopping point for something that ranges further, something that has discovered the Plate's flatness, its warmth, its quiet, its quality of air-that-does-not-insist, and finds it useful. Comes to rest here. Sits still in the maximum light. Allows itself to be seen, briefly, before continuing in whatever direction it continues.

If this is true, then the Plate is not only a world but a place that other worlds stop at. And what stops at it would need to be something that moves in the space between worlds, or in the long distances of this world, or through conditions that make the Plate's shoulder at midday a desirable rest — which tells us something about what the conditions elsewhere must be like for that thing, which tells us something about what the thing itself is, which is nothing we can be certain of.

It was small. It was bright. It was still, and then it wasn't. It left no mark.

## In Sum: The Plate's Inhabitants as a System

What lives on the Plate is not a collection of separate species occupying separate niches. It is closer to a single system with different expressions — different time-scales and spatial-scales and modes of being that all participate in the same fundamental economy. The mat's slow chemistry and the below-surface circulating thing and the between-place movers and the stalk-moments and whatever sits still in the midday light are not unrelated. They are the Plate being alive in as many ways as it has found to be alive, across as much time as it has had to develop them.

The Plate does not accumulate. It redistributes. This is true of its material — the transfer-activity, the stress-distribution, the centuries of maintenance by forces that may or may not still be active — and it is true of its life. Whatever the mats take from the surface chemistry, something in the below-surface moves it back. Whatever the stalks reach into the air to contact, the mat spreads that contact outward, slowly, across six hundred kilometers of slow spiral. Whatever the between-place movers browse from the mat edge, the mat grows back, and perhaps is better for the browsing. Whatever the bright things bring to the shoulder at midday and take away again — attention, perhaps, the fact of being seen by eyes that can see differently than the mat sees — this too is part of the circulation.

The Plate's inhabitants are, ultimately, the Plate in process. They are what it looks like when something very old and very flat and very carefully made decides, over long time, to also be alive. They have the qualities of their world: they are horizontal in their ambitions, they do not accumulate, they redistribute, they are quiet without being absent. They have learned the Plate's own lesson — that the flatness is not emptiness, that the silence is not nothing, that *going on* in every direction without arriving at an edge is not a limitation but a kind of freedom that very patient things can learn to use.

They have had the time. They have used it.

The mat grows, a centimeter a century, toward the place it has always been growing toward.

The thing below continues its long passage through the conducting dark.

The stalk stands in the windless wind, negotiating with the air.

And on the shoulder, in the full and even light, something small and bright sits very still, and the Plate goes on around it in every direction, and for a moment — for one more moment — it is here.
