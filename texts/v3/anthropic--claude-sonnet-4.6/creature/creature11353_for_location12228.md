# Who Lives Here

---

## The Substrate People

They came for the minerals. This is what they say when they describe themselves, which they do less and less as the years pass. They came for the minerals, they stayed for the wages, they raised children who had never been anywhere else and who therefore do not describe themselves in terms of arrival at all. The children simply are. They are of the plateau in the same practical, unexamined way that the pale organism is of the plateau — present, functional, engaged in the project of continuation without having explicitly signed on to it.

There are perhaps four thousand of them in the city at any given time, though the census has always been approximate because the plateau discourages the kind of civic record-keeping that depends on people staying put in the same rooms across years. Some move seasonally. Some move between the eastern works and the western district based on rotation systems that began as formal policy and have softened into custom. A few live in a way that the city's records cannot quite account for — present in the employment rolls, present in the ration accounts, but whose rooms, when visited, contain only the domestic minimum and smell powerfully of the outside.

The population holds. It does not grow. This is unremarkable to the people who live it and slightly troubling to the administrators who track it from the lowlands, who send occasional inquiries about birth rates and infrastructure needs and whether additional housing should be considered. The inquiries receive polite responses. The housing is not built. The population holds.

## Their Bodies

Cold has worked on them. Not deformed them — nothing dramatic. But the people of the plateau carry the climate's signature in specific ways that accumulate into a type, into something a lowlander can recognize before the person has spoken or moved.

The skin. It is weathered by a particular combination of cold and diffuse light and high-altitude UV that produces a quality distinct from either a laborer's tan or an office worker's pallor. It is something in between: slightly ruddy at the cheekbones and nose, slightly grey at the temples and jaw, with the texture of something that has been in outdoor air for a very long time and made its peace with that. The eyes are often light, and in the diffuse light of the plateau they seem to be working harder than eyes at lower altitude — slightly wider, slightly more apt to hold a gaze rather than flick away from it, as if accustomed to taking in a landscape that offers no focal point and therefore trained to rest on whatever presents itself.

The hands are large in proportion to the body, regardless of trade. This has been noted by three separate lowland physicians who have visited the plateau for various official purposes and recorded their observations with the slightly scandalized air of people who have encountered something they cannot explain without effort. The extraction workers have the obvious explanation — physical labor, cold, the kind of grip strength that comes from years of managing machinery in gloves. But the administrative staff have it too. The schoolteachers. The women who operate the small exchange office where off-plateau currency can be converted to the plateau's scrip. The hands are large. They move at a particular pace: not slowly, but with the sense of having calculated the movement beforehand and found no reason to hurry it.

Their voices are quiet. Not soft — there is a difference. Soft voices are considerate. Quiet voices are at home in an environment where ambient sound does not require projection to compete with. On the plateau, the sound is the stamping mill, which is felt in the chest, and the wind, which takes voices sideways, and the sustained gentle white noise of a living surface that covers hundreds of square kilometers. Conversation here has adapted not to carry over this but to fit under it. You lean toward a plateau speaker not because they are mumbling but because they have calibrated their voice for an acoustic environment in which the land's sounds have priority and the human sounds insert themselves as guests.

## The Extraction Workers

The eastern works employ perhaps six hundred people directly. They work in two shifts that overlap in the middle of the day by an hour, an arrangement that began as a safety protocol and has become a social institution — the overlap hour is when the works are most populated, when the crews of both shifts are present simultaneously in the staging areas and the equipment bays, when the noise is loudest and the conversation most dense. Then one crew descends into the shafts or takes positions at the processing equipment and the other crew walks west along the plateau toward the residential district, and the overlap hour closes, and the works return to its normal population.

The shaft workers are a subtype. They spend their shifts below the substrate — below the moss, below the organisms no taxonomist has named, below the millennia of accumulated biomass, in the raw extracted dark of the ancient basalt. They work by lamp in spaces that have not seen light since before there were eyes to see with. They are not, as a group, given to mysticism. They are practical in the way that people who work in confined dark spaces are practical: attentive to physical reality, skeptical of abstraction, alert to the specific meanings of sounds and vibrations that are not immediately visible. They can tell, by the way a shaft wall feels against a palm, whether the basalt is wet or merely cold. They can tell, by the frequency of the stamping mill's vibration as it reaches them through a hundred meters of rock, whether the equipment is running true.

But they are also, almost without exception, at home with silence in a way that takes visitors aback. Bring one of the shaft workers to the residential district's canteen during a busy evening and watch them navigate the sound: they do not seem uncomfortable, but they are present in a way that suggests the noise is interesting to them rather than ordinary. As if they are observing it from outside the category of people who require it.

They live, most of them, in the transition zone between the industrial east and the residential west, in a row of mid-height buildings that sit at the precise demarcation between the two characters of the city. The buildings are well-maintained in a functional sense and completely without ornament. The windows face west, away from the works. In the mornings, before the early shift begins, some of them sit on the building's western-facing steps and look at the plateau in the dawn light, which is not dramatic but which is entirely itself.

## The Administrative Class

The plateau requires administration the way a thing of this size and extractive complexity requires administration: continuously, specifically, and without much gratitude from the thing being administered. There are perhaps three hundred people in various administrative roles — the works managers and their staffs, the city's nominal governance body, the record-keepers and the ration clerks and the people who manage the rail schedules and the people who manage the people who manage the rail schedules.

They are, as a group, the most likely to have come from somewhere else and to retain the habits of somewhere else. You can identify them by the persistence of lowland customs: the preference for heavier meals, the slight hesitation before the plateau's characteristic pauses in conversation fill in, the tendency to furnish their rooms more densely than the plateau norm and then to gradually, over years, begin removing things. The administrators who have been here longest are often indistinguishable from the plateau-born. The administrators who have been here less than a decade are identifiable by their relationship to objects — they own more of them, they arrange them more deliberately, as if setting up a competing claim on the space.

The head administrator's position turns over every seven years, which is unusual for a post of this nature but has become fixed practice after a succession of incidents in the early decades of the plateau's development in which longer tenure produced a particular effect that the lowland company structures found difficult to document officially and impossible to ignore practically. The seven-year appointees arrive with lowland priorities and lowland expectations and spend their first two years implementing them with the productive energy of people who believe in what they're doing. They spend the next three years in a kind of renegotiation that they do not always know they are conducting. And in the sixth and seventh years they become increasingly useless for the purposes for which they were sent, because something has happened to their relationship with urgency that makes the lowland company's timelines feel like a conversation from another room.

They are not unhappy during this period. They are, if anything, more content than at any prior point in their careers. They simply cannot recall, quite, why the quarterly extraction figures matter as much as the lowland offices believe they matter, in the way that someone waking from a deep sleep cannot quite recall the urgency of the dream.

They return to the lowlands and resume, after some weeks, their normal operational personalities. Most of them say the plateau was fine. The air was good. The people were quiet. They don't go back.

## The Plateau-Born

Here is the center of it.

The people born on the plateau — born in the upper-floor rooms of the basalt buildings, in the stone-walled clinic on the city's north edge, occasionally in circumstances that required improvisation — are a somewhat different matter than the people who arrived. This is not, or not primarily, because of anything romantic or mystical. It is because they grew up with certain things as givens that are not givens anywhere else, and this shapes what you notice and what you take for granted and what you expect from existence.

They grew up with the stamping mill as ambient. They have never experienced silence that is complete because the mill is always there, at the threshold between hearing and feeling. Their nervous systems calibrated to this pulse the way infant nervous systems calibrate to a mother's heartbeat, and the result is that silence, for the plateau-born, is not absence but the wrong frequency — a removal of something that should be there, a gap in what they understand as the baseline of being alive.

They grew up with the substrate. With the specific quality of walking on something that yields. With looking out a window and seeing the pale green expanse that has no obvious end. With the frost patterns in the shoulder seasons that render everything in white and which they understand as aesthetic fact before they understand it as meteorological fact. They grew up knowing you don't put your hand flat on the substrate for long periods, not because it's dangerous but because of the thing it does — the warmth against the palm, the sense of something attending to you that is not attending to you in any way that has a name.

The plateau-born are, as a type, comfortable with indefiniteness in ways that lowland visitors find slightly unnerving. Ask one what time it is and they will give you an answer that is an approximation, offered with no anxiety about its imprecision. Ask one how long something will take and they will give you a range rather than a point, and the range will be accurate in the way that weather forecasts are accurate — correct about the shape of things without pretending to know the exact moment. They have not been trained to do this. It is what develops when you grow up in a place that communicates in chemical gradients and slow decades and the patient incremental colonization of three-meter building walls.

They are not incurious. This is important to note because quiet and patient can be mistaken for incurious by people who locate intelligence in quickness. The plateau-born are attentive in the way that something that has all the time it needs is attentive — not urgently, but completely. They listen to visitors with a quality of focused patience that visitors often find slightly disorienting, as if they are being studied rather than heard, though that is not quite it either. They are simply listening in the way they were taught to listen by a place that speaks slowly.

They dream in slow process. Not as a metaphor. Several of the plateau-born, when asked independently about their dreams, describe the same quality: time extended, change incremental, the self participating in something vast and unhurried. One — a woman who maintains the rail exchange office and has never been off the plateau in her adult life — described a recurring dream in which she is the substrate, in which she knows herself to be the mat of living material, in which this is not a diminishment but an expansion, a becoming-larger-and-older-and-more-present. She said this plainly, as a fact about her sleep, with no particular investment in whether the person she was telling found it interesting.

They are deeply practical. They keep equipment running, they know how to seal a basalt wall against moisture with materials available on the plateau, they know the exact sequence of decisions that produces a warm room in a cold building using fuel that arrives by rail on an irregular schedule. The practicality and the dreaminess coexist without friction, because they have grown up in a place where the most practical entity in the landscape — the thing most successfully managing heat retention, moisture cycling, resource distribution, and structural resilience across four hundred square kilometers — is also the thing that most resembles, from inside the city's walls, an ancient ongoing dream.

## The Single Corvid

It passes over the works in the morning, as noted.

No one fed it. No one named it. It is one bird, or it is always a different bird, which amounts to the same thing at the scale of decades. It operates alone, which is worth noting because corvids tend toward company, and its solitude here reads not as exclusion but as sufficiency. It does not require the city. It is not using the city. It passes over the city because the city is in the way between one part of the plateau and another, and the corvid moves between parts of the plateau as its own project, which is not the same project as the city's project or the substrate's project but which runs, in the same direction, across the same ground.

The extraction workers on the early shift watch it. They don't point it out to each other, because pointing it out would imply it needs indicating, and everyone is already watching. It crosses from east to northwest in approximately the same trajectory every clear morning, unhurried, and then it is gone past the buildings and into the open plateau and then it is too far away to see, and then it is wherever it goes.

No one has followed it. No one has tried.

## The Small Grey-Brown Thing in the Mortar

Quicker than examination. Present by implication. You see the gap in the mortar, and you see the gap is warm, and you see that something has been sleeping there from the slight depression in the soft growth at the joint, and you conclude presence. It is small and grey-brown and extremely local in its concerns. It has no opinion about the works or the substrate's project or the seven-year administrator cycle. It has the wall, and the wall provides the specific things the wall provides — crevice, warmth, proximity to whatever the fern that colonizes the mortar joints attracts — and this is sufficient, and it is always just recently present, and its relationship to the city is the most honest one available: total indifference to everything except the one wall.

The plateau-born think of it warmly without thinking about it. It is there the way certain things are there — furniture, nearly, but alive.

## What the City Makes of Itself

Cities in most places develop a self-image, a story they tell about what they are and why they matter, and this story is usually visible in architecture and in public commemoration and in the things the people who live there say first when they describe it. The plateau city's self-image is minimal, which is not the same as absent. What there is of it is this: a place that does what it does, that has been here longer than the outside thinks, that will be here longer than the outside intends.

The extraction company has a plaque on the works office building that commemorates the founding. It has the date and the company name and a sentiment about industry and purpose that was correct when it was written and now reads, on the plateau, as slightly innocent — the statement of an entity that did not know what it was walking into. The plaque has a thin line of lichen growing along its bottom edge. The lichen is the same color as the metal. No one has removed it.

In the canteen of the residential district's main building, there is no founding plaque, no commemorative anything. The walls are basalt. The tables are old wood that has dried and contracted in the plateau's low humidity into something almost sculptural, the grain raised and dark. The windows face west. In the evenings, when both shifts have finished and the overlap hour has long since passed and the works are running on their night skeleton crew, people sit at these tables with hot drinks and they talk in the plateau's characteristic mode: not much, and what is said carrying more weight per word than lowland conversation manages, because the silence between words is not empty and no one is in a hurry to fill it.

They are talking about ordinary things. The machinery that needs attention. The child who has developed a particular habit that is funny in the way children's habits are funny. The weather that is coming in from the northwest, which someone read in the substrate's color change before they saw the clouds. The rail schedule that is, again, approximate. Ordinary things, held in the compressed quality of a place that has been filtering out the inessential for as long as anyone alive has been here, or as long as anyone can trace the records.

Outside the canteen's west window, the plateau extends to the horizon, pale and grey-green and faintly luminous in the evening's last light. The pools in the troughs hold the sky, which is darkening. The substrate breathes moisture upward into the cold air and it is warmer than the air and this warmth is its respiration, its exhalation, the physical sign of something alive at the scale of geography.

The stamping mill, to the east, continues its two-second pulse.

The people in the canteen feel it in their feet and do not remark on it because it is the baseline of being here, the rhythm against which they have been conducting their lives since before they understood what rhythms were or that some things have them and some do not.

The moss is, at this moment, exactly where it was this morning.

It will be somewhere else in a decade, and someone alive in this room will remember that it used to be here, and will have no particular feeling about its having moved except the quiet recognition that this is what it does, and that it was doing it before them, and will do it after.

---

The rail line descends east toward the lowlands. The train will come in the morning, which is to say: when it comes.

The city sleeps.

The substrate does not sleep, exactly. But it has states. And in the night it is in the state it is in at night, which is simply more of what it is, with the light removed.

The buildings lean, by increments immeasurable in a single night, toward the center.

The corvid is somewhere on the open plateau, in whatever the corvid uses for a night.

The grey-brown thing is in the wall.

The plateau-born are dreaming of slow process, of incorporation, of finding this agreeable.

It is enough. It is, in fact, everything.
