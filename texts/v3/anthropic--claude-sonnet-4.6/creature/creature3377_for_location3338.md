## What Lives on the Fabricated Plateau

---

Begin with what doesn't announce itself, because everything here has learned not to.

---

### The Seam-Runners

In the gaps between plates — the two-centimeter ones, the five-centimeter ones, the dark damp channels where condensation runs toward whatever is underneath — live things that have redesigned themselves around a single parameter: *width*.

They are not worms, though the body plan is adjacent. The ancestral form was probably something roundish, something that rolled or scurried, something that fit through a gap by accident and found what was on the other side agreeable enough to stay. That was many generations ago. Now they are flattened in the transverse dimension to a degree that seems like compression rather than evolution, like something that was squeezed and held until the squeezed shape became inheritable. Laterally, side-to-side, they are perhaps four millimeters at their widest. From above or below they are much larger — twelve centimeters, fifteen, long and sinuous and equipped with legs that fold flat against the body when the gap requires it and extend fully when it doesn't.

The legs are the giveaway that this is not a worm. There are sixteen of them, each one jointed and capable, each ending in a small pad that reads the surface for chemical information the way fingertips read Braille. The seam-runners do not navigate visually down in the dark between the plates; they navigate by what the concrete tells them: where the water flowed last, where another runner passed recently, where the particular mineral signature of a productive gap-junction — a place where two different plate materials meet and create a slightly different chemical microclimate — can be found.

They eat the biofilm.

This is not nothing. The gaps collect, over time, a thin and comprehensive ecology of microorganism, and the seam-runners have access to a surface area no larger creature can exploit. They move slowly when feeding, methodically, their mouthparts — not the tearing kind, the rasping kind, designed for scraping — working the concrete sides of the gap without rushing. They move quickly when alarmed: a flicker of motion at the gap edge and then nothing, gone down into the dark underneath where the plates have lifted from their substrate enough to create cave space, volume, a hidden country of air pockets and columns of uncertain material.

They are pale. Not white — the color of something that once had pigment and has been slowly redirected toward other investments. Pale the way a thing gets when it's been a long time since it needed to be seen.

### The Machinists

This is what the plateau calls them, functionally, because no other name fits. They are the nest-builders. They made the warm, empty nests in the open-framed machines.

They are about the size of a cat, though built differently — the limbs are longer in proportion, the body more compact at the core, the head small and dominated by the sensory apparatus rather than anything suggesting an expressive face. The ears are large and mobile, orienting constantly, running the frequency spectrum from the infrasonic thrumming of the machines up through ranges you cannot hear. The eyes are amber and set wide, giving them the circular field of vision that is the inheritance of prey animals, though they are not prey animals — or not only prey animals — or not anymore.

What the Machinists have, in excess of any reasonable requirement, is *patience combined with understanding*.

They study the machines. This is not metaphor and it is not the simple learning of a crow that figures out how to open a latch. The Machinists have been on the plateau long enough — generations, many generations, an ecology of accumulated knowledge — that they have developed a relationship with the machines that might be called, in the absence of a better word, *craft*. They know which machines run hot and which run cool. They know which machines produce the specific vapor that collects on their outer surfaces as potable condensation, and they nest downwind of these machines and collect the water by behavioral positioning. They know which machines produce the slow rhythmic ticking at long intervals, and they use these machines as temporal reference, synchronizing the social activities of the group — the grooming, the shared resting, the transfer of information between individuals — to this metronomic signal.

The nests are engineering. Not instinct in the rigid sense — the nests vary. Each nest is different, adapted to the specific geometry of its host machine, using that geometry the way a builder uses a site's existing features, working with the angles rather than against them. The materials are composite: synthetic fiber where it's available, stripped insulation for its insulating quality, organic material gathered from the gaps and surfaces, and always — always — a specific calcium-gray fragment of plate material incorporated into the nest's structural base like a keystone, like a foundation, like a deliberate gesture toward permanence.

They are not always visible because they have learned to be not always visible. This is a skill. They possess it consciously, not as camouflage but as comportment — a deliberate management of presence. When you look at them directly, they become still in a way that isn't frozen-with-fear but is something more considered, something that seems like a choice being made in real time about how much presence to project. Their amber eyes hold yours. There is the quality of being assessed.

They communicate in the subsonic ranges that the machines also occupy, and so their language lives in the machines' acoustic shadow, invisible to anything that isn't listening at the right register. The machines mask them. The machines give them cover. Whether they know this — whether the vocal frequency is an adaptation that started as coincidence and became strategic over generations — is one of the plateau's questions that goes unanswered.

They are warm. The empty nests are warm because they retain warmth efficiently and because the Machinists run at a body temperature slightly higher than you would expect, a small metabolic premium that they afford by eating well. They eat what the plateau provides: the seam-runners, sometimes, and the things that live in the drip-fed micro-habitats under certain machines, and the small creatures that are drawn to the same condensation sources they've located and mapped over generations. They do not compete with each other for food in the direct sense. They have solved this problem socially — territories that are understood rather than fought over, individual ranges that nest (in the abstract sense, not the literal one) inside the group's larger range the way a smaller polygon fits inside a larger one.

They have been here long enough that the plateau is theirs in some functional sense. They did not make it. But they have occupied it long enough to have made it *mean* something to them that nothing else on the plateau has access to. The place has become a place for them. This is not nothing. This may be the closest thing here to what people usually mean when they say *home*.

### The Thing That Left the Long Stride

It is large in the way that creates the specific discomfort you felt when measuring the disruption-spacing in the plate-surface particulate. Not elephantine. Not anything you would see at a distance and immediately classify. Large in a way that's wrong for this setting — the way a piece of furniture in a small room makes the room feel smaller, except the room is a valley-sized plateau and the furniture is an animal.

It moves at night, which is part of why you haven't seen it. The plateau at night is a different place — the machines still running, still making their textured silence, but the sourceless bright sky becoming something else: a dark that is not quite dark because the exhaust vents of certain machines glow faintly, heat-red, and the calcium-gray plates hold and re-emit a spectral dim quality that makes the whole surface visible as a low-luminosity landscape. In this light, if you stood very still, you might see it moving through the machine-clusters — moving *through* them in a way that suggests familiarity, that suggests it knows which machines to pass on which side and when to lower itself slightly to pass under a particular extruded ventilation channel.

It has a smell. The other things on the plateau know this smell and organize their behavior around it: the Machinists sleep lighter when it's close; the seam-runners do not come to the surface in the gap-hours before dawn. The smell is not the animal smell of predator-musk as you might expect it from its size. It's something more mineral — an excavation smell, a below-ground smell, the smell of whatever is underneath the plates, which is cool and damp and full of the mineral seepage of decades of condensation working down through the substrate. The large thing smells like the place's interior. It smells like it has been down there, in the hollow underneath, where the air is old and the water runs in slow channels and the acoustic properties of the hollow-drum world produce something it finds useful or necessary or simply worth returning to.

It eats what you don't see it eat. The evidence is negative — certain populations of certain things being lower in certain areas than you'd expect, certain machine-surfaces disturbed at heights consistent with a large body brushing past, certain gap-edges showing wear patterns that suggest a limb, much bigger than a seam-runner, has been inserted and withdrawn. It has been eating down there. In the underneath. What lives there that is large enough to interest it is a further question.

It does not appear to have been troubled, ever, by the scale problem — the sixty meters that becomes a longer sixty meters. It navigates by different means than vision, or its vision has different properties, or it has simply walked the plateau so many times that the distances are known to it by duration, by bodily experience, by the accumulated memory of having moved through this specific space in this specific body across uncountable crossings. The scale problem is a problem for the new and the seeing. The large thing is neither new nor primarily seeing. It has been here long enough that the plateau is a body memory rather than a perceived space. It doesn't watch where it's going. It goes.

### The Sky-Things

They are not birds, but they are not not-birds either.

What they are is *after*. Whatever they originally were — and the body plan is still recognizable, still carries the signature of a particular evolutionary lineage, still says *wing, hollow bone, warm blood* in its grammar — they have been modified by the plateau in ways that are hard to look at directly and name.

The wings work differently than wings should. There's an extra joint — or there's a missing joint, or the existing joints have a range of motion that standard wings don't have, the angles tipping past where the physics should allow them to tip before catching on some internal stop. In flight, this produces the wrong-angled configuration you noticed: the wings held in positions that seem aerodynamically incorrect, and yet the animal is flying, is flying well, is reading the thermals off the warm machine-surfaces with an efficiency that suggests it has been reading these specific thermals long enough to have mapped them, to be working from a map.

They feed in the boundary layer. Where the hot exhaust from the machine vents meets the cool plateau air, small flying things are concentrated — driven there by physics, by the convection, by the accidental generosity of the machines' thermal economy. The sky-things work this boundary layer the way swifts work summer air, turning and returning, never quite still, always in process. They do not land. No one has seen them land. The nest question — where they go, whether they go down, what they return to — remains open. There are theories involving the underneath, where the hollow space under the plates has areas of slightly warmer air (the machines transmit some heat through their mounting bases into the substrate, and this heat accumulates in the trapped air below), where a flying thing might fold itself and hang in a thermal microclimate of its own making.

They have never been silent. Even crossing the bleached sky with no apparent call, they produce something in the infrasonic range — a frequency detectable only as a pressure-change, a barometric tick when they pass overhead, a sensation in the sinuses rather than the ears. The Machinists react to this infrasonic flyover with small postural adjustments, brief orientations toward the sky, something that is not alarm and is not interest but is in the category of *information received, no action required*. They have been listening to each other for a long time. Not consciously. The way long-shared spaces create communication without language — the body knowing what the mind doesn't bother to formalize.

### The Biofilm, Which Is Not Nothing

It coats the undersides of the plates, the interiors of the gaps, the damp surfaces in the underneath. It is not an animal and it has no behavior that the word *behavior* was designed to describe, but it is a presence — a player in the plateau's arithmetic — and to leave it out would be to misrepresent the place.

The biofilm is old. Older than the Machinists, older than the sky-things, possibly as old as the plateau itself — the first thing to arrive, the first to recognize that this constructed surface, in its cool-damp-mineral specificity, was a habitat rather than an inert surface. It came in on the wind, probably, or in the water that condensed from the first pale sky onto the first calcium-gray plates. It settled into the gaps. It ate the mineral surface. It processed the surface: the faint erosion marks you noticed are partly wind and rain and partly the slow chemistry of the biofilm's appetite, which is for calcium carbonate and certain trace minerals and for the specific quality of the air above the plateau, which it absorbs through its outer surface and integrates into its chemistry and produces from, in return, a faint oxygen surplus that makes the air above the plateau slightly — not noticeably, but measurably — better for breathing than the air at the same altitude elsewhere.

Everything else on the plateau eats it directly or eats something that ate it, or breathes the air it makes, or uses the surfaces it has textured, or builds nests in machines that the biofilm's moisture-processing has made subtly more humid inside, which keeps the nest materials from becoming brittle, which makes the nests last longer, which means the Machinists invest less in reconstruction and more in other activities — in the patient study of the machines, in the social transfer of knowledge, in the refinement of the understanding that is their distinctive contribution to the plateau's intelligence.

The biofilm doesn't know this. It doesn't know anything. But it is doing it anyway, has been doing it since before anything else was here to benefit, will continue doing it after the question of what lives here has been replaced by a different question entirely.

### A Note on the Plateau's Composite Intelligence

No single thing here is intelligent in isolation.

The seam-runners know the gaps. The Machinists know the machines. The large thing knows the underneath. The sky-things know the boundary layer. The biofilm knows nothing but processes everything.

But the plateau, taken as a system, knows itself with a comprehensiveness that none of its parts can access individually. The seam-runners' gap-knowledge is available to the Machinists through what the seam-runners leave behind — chemical information in their trails, which the Machinists can read by smell. The sky-things' infrasonic signal carries information about the machine-thermals that the Machinists use to predict which machines are running hot (a proxy for which machines are processing actively, which correlates with which machines are producing the right condensation, which is where the Machinists locate water). The large thing's movements through the underneath change the pressure dynamics in the gap-spaces, which changes where condensation forms, which changes where the seam-runners go, which changes the chemical trails the Machinists follow.

Every piece of information moves through the system. Slowly. Through indirect channels. Without any coordinating intelligence directing it. Just the plateau's quiet arithmetic — its textured silence, its sourceless bright sky, its machines processing something and returning it, slightly altered, to the cool air — running the numbers continuously, arriving at the answer it always arrives at:

*Something is here.*

*It is here without understanding it is here together.*

*It has been here long enough that together is the only way to be here.*

The far edge is sixty meters away.

The far edge is eighty meters away.

You are walking toward it, and in the open-framed machines behind you, in warm nests built from wire and fiber and a deliberate fragment of calcium-gray plate, amber eyes track your movement across the hexagonal surfaces, patient and unhurried, assessing.

They have been here longer than you.

The plateau has been telling them things about you since you arrived.
