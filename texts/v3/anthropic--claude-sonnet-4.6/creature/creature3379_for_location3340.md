# The People of the Drowned Terraces

---

They are small, mostly. Not uniformly — there is the usual human variation — but the average has drifted, over generations, toward a compactness that suits the buildings, suits the doorways, suits the low-pitched rooflines under which a taller person would move differently, with more care, more ducking. They fit the place. The place fits them. This is not accidental, though it was not planned.

Their hands are the first thing a newcomer notices, or the second thing after the eyes. The hands of people who work wet stone, wet wood, wet fiber are not soft hands, but neither are they rough in the way of dry work — there is no cracking, no harsh callous. The skin has the character of something that has been kept supple by constant moisture, slightly thickened but flexible, the texture of well-worked leather that has been oiled too many times to remember. Their palms are often slightly darker than the backs of their hands, as though stained, and this is in fact a kind of staining: from the stone, from the organic matter in the channels, from the moss they press against wall after wall after wall in the habitual acknowledgment that a newcomer sees and cannot immediately understand. The darkness is not uniform. It is the map of a life's touching.

Their eyes are a functional adaptation to a world where dramatic contrast rarely occurs. Not large eyes — but calibrated, it seems, for the low-order differentiation that matters here: the difference between the brown-green of a frog and the brown-green of sedge, the difference between a channel that is moving correctly and one that has begun to divert, the difference in color between a moss that is healthy and a moss that has begun to dry at its center, though the center of anything here does not dry quickly. They see green in more registers than they have words for, which is to say they have more words for green than a visitor would expect, though the words are not names of colors exactly — they are names of states, conditions, degrees of aliveness. A newcomer, hearing these words used casually in conversation, at first assumes they are names of places.

They do not squint against the light because the light does not require squinting. Their faces, over generations, have arrived at a kind of open calm, the musculature around the eyes relaxed in a way that reads to outsiders sometimes as tiredness, sometimes as serenity, sometimes as a slightly unsettling absence of the alert watchfulness most faces carry as default. They are watching; they are watching constantly, in fact, with the whole surface of their attention — but the watching is distributed, peripheral, ambient, not focused into the forward-pointed surveillance of a face that expects events. Events here do not come from a single direction.

---

They speak quietly, and not because they are a quiet people in terms of character. It is that the rain already occupies the frequency of ordinary speech, and speaking over it is possible but effortful, and effort in this direction has long seemed wasteful. So they speak in a register that fits into the pauses within the rain, the slight irregular silences in the percussion of a million drops on a million surfaces, sliding their voices into those pauses with a timing that is entirely unconscious, entirely practiced, something they do the way they do breathing. A conversation, overheard from a terrace away, sounds intermittent, fragmentary — voices appearing and disappearing in the larger sound — but up close, in the covered spaces between buildings, it flows continuously, the speakers finding the quiet within the constant and placing their words there.

Their laughter is not loud. It is quick, low, and passes across a group like a small weather event — here, then here, then gone, then returning. They laugh at things that have accumulated over time and are only now understood to be funny, which means that an individual laughing alone, at no evident provocation, is not unusual. Something has ripened. Something that happened two weeks ago has become funny now, and the person laughing is laughing privately at a conclusion that took two weeks to arrive. This is one of the settlement's comedic modes.

---

A child here learns, before learning other things, where the water goes. This is not formal instruction. It is absorption, the child following the parent as the parent follows the channels, tracing with small fingers the direction of flow, learning the grade of each terrace not as a fact but as a feeling — a lean, a tendency, a given direction the world has in this particular place. The child who grows up here carries this in the body: a knowledge of grade that operates below consciousness, that tells them, on any surface, which way the water would go if there were water. This is navigation. It is also something more than navigation. Ask one of them to describe a place they have never been and they will, eventually, if they trust you enough, describe it in terms of how the water would move through it. What would flow where. Where the low points are. Where the water would gather if left to itself.

This is how they understand space.

It extends, in ways that are harder to observe, to how they understand situations, relationships, time. They think in terms of flow and accumulation. They think in terms of where things naturally go if not redirected, and whether redirection is worth the effort of maintaining the channel that achieves it. They are not fatalistic — they are deeply engaged in the practice of building channels, redirecting, creating cisterns, making the water go where it needs to go — but they are practitioners of a fatalism-that-acts, a sense that the underlying tendencies of things are real and must be known before they can be worked with. They do not fight the water. They do not fight anything, exactly. They find the shape of what something wants to do and they make that shape useful.

---

The knowledge of the settlement passes through demonstration and through presence more than through explanation. The teachers are not understood as teachers; they are understood as people who know, who continue to be present while others learn, who carry out the work of knowing-by-doing at a pace slow enough to observe. A child watching an elder re-cut a silted channel is not being taught silted-channel-cutting in any formal sense. They are receiving it, gradually, across years of watching, interrupted watching, half-watching while doing something else, occasional direct observation that occurs when the observation feels important and is therefore attended to fully. The knowledge arrives. It is not delivered.

Because of this, the knowledge is not fully articulable even by those who have it. Ask the most experienced channel-cutter how she knows where to place the chisel and she will consider the question for a long time and then demonstrate, which is the truest answer but not always the one the questioner wanted. The articulate knowledge — the part that can be said — is thin. The embodied knowledge is vast. This means that the knowledge cannot easily leave the settlement, cannot be carried in a book, cannot be reproduced in a place that does not have the terraces, the water, the stone, the continuous slow wet reality that the knowledge is a response to. The knowledge is site-specific. It is almost geographical.

---

They make things. The making is constant, low-level, distributed — someone is always in the middle of something — but not the frantic making of people trying to produce enough. The quality of the making has a different driver: things must last in wet conditions, must hold up under the patient dissolution of constant moisture, must be made well enough that the maintenance interval is long, so that there is time between the necessary maintenances to do the other necessary maintenances. Everything is maintained. Nothing is built to be replaced. Replacement implies a pace of deterioration that would be, here, a civic failure.

The ceramics are what they are best known for, in the limited sense that they are known for anything by anyone outside the settlement who has occasion to consider them. The pipes that move water through the settlement horizontally, the storage vessels, the small bowls and the medium vessels and the larger things that store seed and dried food — all hand-thrown, all slightly irregular in cross-section, all fired in a particular way that closes the clay's pores against water infiltration while maintaining a slight permeability, a breathability, that keeps the contents from developing the sealed-vessel dampness that would ruin them. This balance is extremely difficult to achieve and they achieve it consistently, have achieved it across many generations, and the technique is one of those things that is fully embodied and barely articulable. The potter who cannot explain what she is doing produces the correct result. The student who asks for an explanation receives a demonstration.

The textiles are woven from things that grow here: the sedge, processed at a particular stage of growth when it has the right flexibility; fibers from plants growing on the lower slope that are cultivated deliberately for this purpose, though the cultivation is so gentle, so long-practiced, that it reads as wild growth to an eye that does not know where to look. The textiles are dense and heavy, slightly rough against the skin but not unpleasantly so — rough in the way of something honest rather than something abrasive. They shed water at their outer surface and wick warmth from their inner surface, which is what you want in a garment here, and they achieve this through structure rather than through any treatment, the weave itself doing the work of differentiation between outer and inner. They last decades.

They do not make much that is only decorative, but this is not because they are not interested in beauty. It is because they do not recognize the category of only decorative. The channel-cuts have a particular quality of line, a consistency of depth and of slight curvature where the channel turns, that is beautiful and that is also structurally optimal — the curve that loses no water is also the curve that looks right. The moss walls are tended in ways that are not purely structural — certain mosses are selected for certain positions on grounds that include something other than their structural contribution — but the selection cannot be easily separated from the functional, and no one tries to separate it. The thing that works is also the thing that is right to look at. The long practice of working with the given — with water, stone, plant — has produced not an aesthetic of decoration but an aesthetic of fit, of things that have found their correct relationship with their context and have stopped there.

---

The relationship between the living and the dead is a practical one, because the dead are substantially present in the settlement's fabric. The channels cut by hands no longer living are walked every day. The buildings built by hands no longer living are maintained by the current hands and will be maintained by the hands that follow. A repair to a wall is a continuation of the original builder's work; the distinction between construction and maintenance has flattened, here, into a single continuous practice of keeping-in-relationship with a structure that has its own long history of decisions, each of which constrains and informs the current one. A builder considering a repair will consider the original decision — evident in the stone, legible if you know how to read it — and will either continue it or consciously depart from it, knowing that the departure is also a decision that will be read by whoever follows.

The dead are not worshipped, but they are consulted. Not mystically — in the sense that the work they left is consulted, their decisions examined, the evidence of their knowledge present everywhere in the built-and-grown fabric of the settlement. This produces a kind of humility that is not self-deprecating — the people here are not short of confidence in their knowledge and their capacity — but that places the individual clearly within a sequence, as a practitioner among practitioners extending backward and forward, doing the work that has been done and that will be done, responsible for doing it well, not terribly concerned with doing it originally. Originality is a solution to a new problem. Most problems here are old.

The oldest members of the settlement, toward the end of their lives, spend a great deal of time sitting in the covered spaces between buildings, which the others understand, because the time of an elder is worth more spent watching than spent doing, and watching produces an attention of the kind that occasionally speaks, and when it speaks it is worth hearing. They are not revered in a way that stops conversation. They are included in conversation the way you include someone who knows the thing you are trying to figure out — with a certain orientation, a certain readiness to receive. The very old among them sometimes develop a stillness that is extraordinary. Not the stillness of someone waiting — the stillness of someone for whom the distinction between inside and outside has grown thin. The rain falls on them and they do not particularly notice the rain, or they notice it the way a fish notices water, which is to say they are in full relationship with it without the relationship requiring thought.

---

They have a practice — not ritual exactly, though it has the formality of ritual — of walking the full water system of the settlement after significant events. After a death. After a birth. After a change to the system itself, a new channel, a rebuilt cistern. Someone walks the flow from the highest terrace to the lowest, tracing the full path the water takes through every channel and over every edge and through every pipe, and does this slowly, bending to look at each section, checking not just for blockage but for the overall character of the flow. Whether it is right. What is right is known but not defined; it is one of those things held in the body of the person doing the walking, calibrated against all the other walks taken by all the other walkers before, a long comparison of this moment's flow to every previous moment's flow that has been absorbed into the walker's knowledge.

At the end of this walk, the walker sits at the lowest point of the seventh terrace and watches the water leaving the settlement, passing over the final edge, disappearing into the slope below. They sit there for some time. And then they return to the upper terraces.

No one asks what was seen on the walk unless the walker says something first. Usually nothing is said. The walk is its own completion. But occasionally — after a death, sometimes, or after a very significant change — the walker will sit for a while in the covered space between buildings and say something about the flow. Something specific: the third channel on the fifth terrace is carrying more than it should, the moss in the southern cistern has thickened and should be checked, the outflow from the settlement has more sediment than usual. Something practical. Always something practical. But the others receive it the way you receive something said after a long thought, with the weight of the long thought still present in the saying, and the practical content — the third channel, the moss, the sediment — is heard as both itself and as whatever it stands for.

What it stands for changes depending on who has died, or who has been born, or what has changed. The others work this out themselves, separately, in the way things ripen here, over time, until they are understood. Until someone laughs, alone, two weeks later, at what the sediment meant.

---

There is in the settlement, almost always, one or two people who do not quite fit the place — who have arrived from elsewhere and are in the long process of learning to live here, or who were born here but carry, for reasons of character, an orientation more forward-pointing, more urgency-prone, more interested in the single event than in the long accumulation. These people are not unwelcome. They are given things to do that use their energy well. They keep the paths from narrowing. They climb to the highest reaches of the ridge above the settlement to check on things that the settled inhabitants have not felt the need to check on. They sometimes leave, and sometimes come back, and both of these are received without a great deal of ceremony.

But there is something that happens, often, to the people who arrive from outside and stay long enough. Not to all of them. To some of them. The thing that happens is difficult to name. It is not that they become like the others, exactly. It is that they begin to register the grade. They begin, without deciding to, to know which way the water would go. They begin to step around the mosses without looking down, because the step has become automatic, has been integrated, and the moss is simply part of the path now.

When this happens to someone, the others notice. Nothing is said. But a slight adjustment is made in how they are included in things, a slight increase in presence, in assumption. The adjustment is so small that the person experiencing it would not be able to point to a specific change. But they would feel it, probably, as a mild and characterless warmth. As the sense, arriving quietly, that they have come somehow into the right relationship with something larger and more patient than themselves — that the relationship has found, without being forced, the grade that allows it to flow.

---

In the evening — which arrives as a further dimming of an already dim day, the mist thickening to its fullest density, the frog chorus building — the people gather in the covered spaces between buildings and eat. The food is simple and warm, which is what food needs to be here. It is eaten with the usual human mixture of talk and quiet, the talk fitting itself into the pauses of the rain, the quiet comfortable, not loaded. The small dark birds have settled into the moss for the night, invisible, occasional repositioners. The corvid has left the lower terrace and gone wherever it goes. The weasel-things move through the sedge at the walls, dark movements, indifferent.

After eating, some stay and continue talking. Some return to whatever they were doing — the channel-cutter to the sixth terrace, the chisel resuming its steady pace in the near-dark, guided by touch now as much as by sight. Some go inside, through the hanging of woven rush, into the warmth and dark.

The settlement does not sleep all at once. It does not do much of anything all at once. It distributes its activities across the available time the way it distributes its water across the available grade, following the tendencies that have developed over so long they read as nature.

The rain continues. The mist lies in the spaces between the terraces, between the buildings, in the small gap between the palm and the moss wall at the moment of acknowledgment. 

It is not weather. It has not been weather for a long time.

The distinction has stopped mattering.
