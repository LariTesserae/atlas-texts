# What Lives on the Plateau of Approximate Forms

---

## First Principle

The question of what lives here cannot be answered by a list. A list would give you ptarmigan, hare, fox, char, pipit, sedge, sphagnum, and whatever slow thing respires below — and the list would be accurate, and would miss the point entirely. The point is that nothing on the plateau lives *separately*. The distinction between organism and environment, which works adequately at lower elevations, in younger places, has been here long enough to become an open question.

Everything that lives here lives as part of one thing's living.

That one thing does not have a name. It is not a superorganism in the technical sense, because it contains no central coordination, no communication in the chemical or electrical registers we have learned to look for. It is more like a *patience* that has become biological. It is the plateau's long refusal to be finished, expressed in every wet and breathing layer of itself.

What follows, then, is less a census than an attempt to describe the character of that refusal — how it expresses itself in the things that move, the things that root, the things that breathe from below.

---

## The Moss, Which Is Not Background

Begin where you must begin, which is the sphagnum, because the sphagnum is not backdrop. It is not where life happens. It is the oldest ongoing life, and everything else has arranged itself around, upon, and within its terms.

Sphagnum does not merely occupy the plateau. It has been *making* the plateau, cell by slow cell, for longer than most of the animals that walk across it have existed as species. Each plant holds water in quantities many times its dry weight — a living reservoir, a distributed hydrology, an organism that has effectively replaced the watershed. The plateau's relationship to water is not a geological feature. It is sphagnum's decision, implemented over millennia, still in progress.

The moss does not think. But it has structured a world according to principles that have not wavered in geological time, and those principles are these: slow down all water, retain all warmth against the cold, create conditions in which the old does not quite decompose, in which the past accumulates rather than ending. The bog below the living surface is not death. It is storage. The moss holds its ancestors in suspension — tannin-preserved, oxygen-excluded, lasting. There are layers down there representing centuries of continuous moss living. The living mat above them is thin. The memory of living is vast.

What this means for everything else that lives here: everything lives in a place where matter does not behave normally. The dead are kept. The slow is honored. The patient outlasts the urgent. The moss has been maintaining these conditions since before there was anything else here to live in them, and the things that came after — the birds, the mammals, the fish — are all organisms that made some accommodation to sphagnum's terms.

The colors of sphagnum, which cannot reduce to single words, are worth noting as evidence of diversity: there are sixty or more species here, each specialized to its position in the hummock-and-hollow topography, each with its slightly different metabolism, its slightly different contribution to the collective chemistry. The copper-rust growth is one species absorbing particular wavelengths of thin northern light. The yellow of old bruising is another, stressed, producing secondary compounds in response to something in the water. The dark-green-that-functions-as-black is a third, growing at the waterline, adapted to partial submersion. The grey-silver is a fourth, slower, more acid, colonizing the hummock peaks where conditions are most extreme.

The moss does not all agree. The plateau is a negotiation between sixty negotiations, and the result is the living geography you walk across, the hummocks and hollows and waterways that have no functional drainage and no interest in acquiring any.

In the moss, and below it, and woven through it: the microbes. Bacteria in quantities that make every other population on the plateau look sparse. The warm blooms of respiration that keep the surface from freezing are bacterial. The faint sweet-ferrous smell is bacterial metabolic byproduct, methane and other gases seeping from anaerobic depths. The microbial community in a cubic meter of active sphagnum mat is more diverse than any other ecosystem represented on the plateau. It is responsible for most of the chemistry, most of the heat, most of the cycling of what would otherwise be locked. Without the microbes the moss would slow and the bog would freeze and the pools would go still and the character of the whole thing would collapse.

The microbes are not visible. They are responsible for almost everything you can see.

## The Things That Root

Sedge grows at the edges of pools and in the wet hollows between hummocks, growing in the saturated ground the sphagnum has made, dependent on the chemistry the sphagnum has established. The sedge does not look like it struggles. It grows with an unhurried assurance that is not quite vigor — vigor implies pace — but is something more like inevitability. Each year's growth is not dramatically more than the previous year's. The sedge has been growing at this rate for a long time and expects to continue.

The sedge does something the sphagnum cannot: it lifts above the surface. Its leaves move in the wind, creating the continuous susurrus, the sound that keeps pulling attention. The sedge is the part of the plateau that speaks to air. It catches whatever the wind carries and releases seeds in late summer when the wind direction shifts, and the seeds go somewhere the wind decides, and some of them land on wet sphagnum on some other part of the plateau and some of them blow off into the cloud and are not heard from again. The sedge is unconcerned about this. It produces seeds in quantities that accommodate enormous losses.

Below the visible sedge, belowground, the rhizome network extends laterally through the moss in all directions, and it is down here that the sedge participates in the plateau's main activity, which is the slow structural rearrangement of the surface. The rhizomes push through peat, displace older material, create the hollow geometry that determines where water stands and where it moves. The sedge, from below, is constantly editing the plateau's topography, at a rate that is undetectable in the span of a human visit.

There is also cotton grass, which is not grass, its white heads visible across the plateau in the season when they appear, floating above the moss like something left behind by a civilization that prized floating things. The cotton grass exists in conditions that would destroy most plants: waterlogged, acidic, cold, nutrient-poor. It has specialized for this, and the specialization is so thorough that it cannot easily live anywhere else. It is, in this sense, a plateau native in the strictest meaning — something that has been shaped so specifically by this place that the place and the organism have converged.

The dwarf birches are the most complex rooted life on the plateau, which is to say they are complex relative to moss and sedge, which is to say they are still less than a meter tall and have been working on those few centimeters of height for decades. The wind that moves across the plateau at altitude is not a birch-friendly wind, and the birches know this the way trees know things, which is through deformation: every birch here grows away from the prevailing direction, shaped by long pressure into something that looks like prostration, like a low bow, like an ongoing negotiation with force. The silver-white bark is not ornamental; it reflects light and resists cold in ways that help the inner tissue survive temperatures that would damage other bark. The deep furrowing is age, and the age is hard to estimate because dwarf birches in these conditions grow slowly enough that the usual metrics do not apply.

The birches at their small scale contain their own communities: lichens on the bark, tiny invertebrates overwintering in the bark's crevices, the particular fungi in relationship with their roots without which the roots could not extract what little nutrition the bog soil offers. A dwarf birch is a small vertical world, a break in the plateau's dominant horizontal plane, a different structural offer in the landscape.

The birches also do the thing with position that the plateau does generally — stand not quite where you remember them — but this is more disturbing in the birches than in the pools because the birches are visible from a distance and trackable in the memory. This may be why you notice it with the birches more than with the moss. The moss is continuous. The birches are discrete. Discrete things should stay put. On the plateau, this expectation reveals itself as a lower-altitude assumption, brought up here in your pack, increasingly unhelpful.

## The Things That Move

The ptarmigan are the plateau's primary expression of movement, which is to say that when the eye sweeps across the landscape, the ptarmigan are what moves most legibly, at a scale you can track. They are white against the brown and copper of the moss — winter camouflage, which functions here not because there is snow but because the light at this altitude bleaches things, diffuses them, and white against grey-lit sphagnum is less visible than it sounds. The ptarmigan know this because their genetics encode it, refined across generations of this altitude and this light.

They eat the sedge and the dwarf birch buds and the lichen and the berries of low-growing heather. They eat what the plateau provides, which is not rich but is consistent and reliable for the organisms that have evolved to use it. They are compact, heavily feathered to the toe, carrying their insulation as part of their body architecture rather than seeking it in shelter. The plateau does not offer shelter from the cold, and the ptarmigan have interiorized the solution.

What is difficult to describe is the quality of the ptarmigan's attention. They are prey animals; prey animals are generally alert, scanning, reactive. The ptarmigan here are alert but not reactive in the ordinary prey-animal sense. They register you and assess you as probably non-threatening and return to foraging. They register the sound of the pipes and do not register it as threat, because the pipes have been here across many generations of ptarmigan and the pipes have never harmed one. What they register, during the pauses — the collective stillness, the shared orientation, the forty seconds of attending — is something below the audible, something that travels through the wet ground and is felt in their feet, perhaps, or in some other sensory modality not catalogued by human observers because we do not share it.

They attend to the deep note. All of them, across the plateau, in staggered waves.

What they make of it is unknown. Whether they make anything of it, in the cognitive sense, or whether the attending is purely physiological — a reflex triggered by the vibration, the body stilling, the sensory focus narrowing — is a question that cannot be answered from outside a ptarmigan. What is observable is that the pauses are not random, are not individual, and are distributed across the plateau in a spatial pattern that corresponds, roughly, to the distribution of the pipes. The ptarmigan nearest each pipe pause longest. The ptarmigan farthest from any pipe pause briefly and then resume.

They are not afraid. They are listening to something. Whether it is listening in any meaningful sense, or whether it is the body's involuntary processing of an unfamiliar input, amounts perhaps to the same thing.

The hares are different. The hares are urgency made animal — fast in short bursts, alert to everything, their coloration a compromise between seasons that satisfies neither entirely, brown-going-white and not quite matching the copper moss or the absent snow. They eat what the ptarmigan eat and more, and they move across the plateau in paths that seem purposeless until you understand the paths as maintenance of routes: the hares know this landscape in fine detail and they are continuously refreshing that knowledge, keeping the cognitive map current by traversal. The running is not always escape. Often it is just movement for the sake of the information movement generates.

The hares do not pause with the pipes. The hares seem, if anything, faintly agitated during the deep-note intervals, their paths briefly more erratic, their directional changes less deliberate. They process the vibration differently than the ptarmigan. What this means — whether the hares experience something like unease, whether the vibration conflicts with some other signal they are tracking — is another question that cannot be answered from outside a hare.

The fox is different from all of them. There may be only one fox. There may be several foxes. The plateau is large enough and the conditions are such that individual identification is difficult, but the copper is consistent and the gait is consistent and the quality of purposefulness is consistent, and the simplest explanation is one fox with a very large territory and an extensive knowledge of it. The fox does not hunt visibly on the plateau. It moves through. Its prey is here — the ptarmigan, the hares, possibly the small fish at the margins of the pools — but it is not hunting during the hours you observe it. It is making its rounds, maintaining presence, reading the landscape through its nose in the information-dense way that makes the fox's world a different world from yours, richer in olfactory history and trajectory. The fox passes where the hare passed, where the ptarmigan congregated, where the water moved in the night, and reads all of this as plainly as you would read a sign.

The fox, alone among the plateau's animals, seems aware of the inconsistencies. It stops at the place where you are not sure whether there was a pool before, and it investigates with its nose, and it spends longer there than a fox would spend confirming the absence of prey. It seems to be checking something. Then it continues north, toward the cloud that will take it, at its errand-pace, as though satisfied.

The small birds — the pipits nesting in the pipe — are the most domesticated-seeming life on the plateau, which is paradoxical given their location. The pipe is hot and always breathing, and the pipits have nested in it as they would nest in a cliff crevice: for the warmth, the protection, the vertical orientation in a landscape that offers nothing vertical. The pipe does not seem to bother them. They navigate the slow warm breath of the mechanism below as they would navigate wind, compensating without apparent concern, their lives organized around an artifact they did not make and cannot comprehend, making use of it with the thorough practicality that is the characteristic posture of birds toward the world's structures.

They sing from the pipe's rim. The song carries across the plateau in the acoustically clear air, and the other pipits answer from wherever they are — in the sedge, somewhere in the dwarf birch, crossing between hummocks — and the exchanges are mate-maintenance, territory-assertion, the ongoing management of the social arrangements that constitute a pipit's life at altitude. They are not singing to the plateau. The plateau receives their song as it receives everything: without apparent response and without apparent indifference. The plateau has no opinion about the pipits. The pipits are alive in it, and they will be replaced by their offspring, and the offspring by theirs, and the pipe will continue to breathe.

## The Things That Stay Still

Below the surface of the pools: the fish, which are possibly Arctic char and possibly something unnamed, something that has been in these pools long enough to have become something the pools made. They are colorless, or nearly so — the pigmentation of fish that live in low-light conditions in peat-dark water is one of the first things to reduce. They are slow. They eat what the pools contain, which is mostly invertebrates and plant matter that falls from the sedge, and they have calibrated their metabolisms to the bog's energy budget, which is lean. They do not starve. They are the right size for what is available, which means they are small.

The occasional dimpling of a pool surface from below, at intervals too long and too slow for fish behavior, suggests that not all pools contain only fish. The nature of what produces those slow risings is not clear from above. The water is too dark and too deep and too still to see the bottom in most pools. The dimpling could be gas — methane produced by the anaerobic bacteria in the deep peat, rising slowly, breaking the surface in the regular way that gas rises through water. The intervals could be coincidental. The impression of very slow, very large respiration is probably a pattern imposed by the observer on a process that is not biological.

Probably.

The invertebrates are everywhere and mostly invisible: water boatmen, diving beetles, caddisfly larvae in cases made of sphagnum fragments, midges in vast quantities in warmer periods, their larvae in every pool and every wet space between pools. They are the plateau's middle tier of consumption — below the birds and mammals, above the bacteria, eating the detritus and being eaten by the char. They are present in numbers that dwarf every other animal population here. In terms of biomass and metabolic activity, the invertebrates and the bacteria between them are the plateau's real animal life. Everything larger is consequential but not dominant.

## The Thing Below

What breathes through the pipes is not, in the biological sense, alive. The mechanism below is not an organism. It does not metabolize, does not reproduce, does not respond to stimuli in the ways that would allow classification as living.

And yet it breathes.

And yet it has been here long enough that the sphagnum has grown up the sides of its exhaust pipes, and the pipits have used those pipes across generations, and the ptarmigan have incorporated its pulses into whatever they are doing during the pauses, and the pools respond to its deeper notes. It has been integrated into the plateau's life so thoroughly and for so long that its influence cannot be separated from the plateau's character.

If a machine runs for long enough in one place — in the cold, in the wet, in the living presence of organisms that respond to its outputs — does it accumulate something like a relationship? The moss does not know the machine is a machine. The ptarmigan do not know what a machine is. The hares and fox do not know. The fish certainly do not know. For everything alive on the plateau, the thing below is simply part of the conditions of the plateau, a feature of the place, as given as the cold and the cloud. It was here before any current individual. It will be here after.

What it is doing down there — the process that produces the warmth, the slow pressure, the sub-bass note — is consistent with several explanations and can be confirmed as none of them from the surface. The most persistent speculation is that it is not doing anything to the plateau. That the plateau is the exhaust of what it is doing, which is something else entirely, something directed inward or downward, working on the geological substrate beneath the peat, engaged with the old stone that the eroding mountains no longer protect. The pipes would be, on this reading, not outputs of a process but regulators — pressure-release valves, preventing the plateau from receiving too much of what the mechanism produces.

What the plateau would look like if it received too much is not something the surface evidence allows you to determine.

## What Lives Here, Assembled

The attempt at a summary, knowing that summaries are a lower-altitude form:

What lives here is the sphagnum, which is also the earth and the water and the chemistry. The sphagnum contains and has created the conditions for everything else, and is itself an expression of the plateau's age and patience. What lives here is sixty species of moss in negotiation, and the bacteria in their billions in every pore and space, and the invertebrates at every wet margin, and the fish in the dark pools, and the ptarmigan attending to something below hearing, and the hares in their partial camouflage running routes of maintenance, and the fox on its errand, and the pipits in their improbable nest above a machine's exhalation. All of this lives here, and all of it is the living of one thing, the plateau's long refusal to become inert.

And beneath it, integrated into the living of everything above it, the mechanism persists. The mechanism breathes. The plateau accommodates the mechanism. The mechanism accommodates the plateau.

They have had a long time to arrive at this.

Neither of them is finished.

The cloud holds everything under its grey-green light, and the sedge moves, and the ptarmigan stand still for forty seconds, and the pool at the center of the plateau dimples, once, from below.
