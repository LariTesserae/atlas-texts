# What Lives in the Drowned Quarter

---

Begin with what is hardest to see, which is also what is most present.

The water itself is not empty. It has not been empty for longer than the district has been submerged. What lives in the water column here — not on the surfaces, not in the buildings, but in the water between things — is so small as to be invisible, which does not mean scarce. The water is thick with life operating at the scale of microns. Bacteria in concentrations that, if you could see them, would constitute a kind of fog. Archaea even older than the district, older than the machinery, working the sulfur chemistry of the deep sediment and releasing compounds that rise through the water column in slow plumes of slightly different refractive index — you can almost see them, sometimes, the places where the water is not quite the same as the water around it, the shimmer of a different concentration. Diatoms, those glass-boxed algae with their extraordinary patience, their ability to drift and wait and drift and wait, their silicon walls that will remain long after everything else has broken down and been recycled. Cyanobacteria producing the oxygen that the larger things breathe, here, deep in a place with barely enough light, doing it slowly, doing it anyway. 

This is the base. This is what the water is made of, partly. What lives in the Drowned Quarter begins here, at the level where life and chemistry stop being distinguishable.

---

The algae on the surfaces is not one thing.

Where it reads as a continuous mat, as a unified presence, it is in fact a community — dozens of species at least, each occupying a microhabitat defined by the angles of available light, by the speed of local currents, by the mineral composition of the surface beneath, by the allelopathic preferences of its neighbors. The dark velvet moss-analogs on the upper walls are a different thing from the spongy mat-formers on the cobblestones, which are a different thing from the bright (relatively bright, in context) green film on the south-facing window casements that catches the most light. These communities have boundaries, borders that shift by centimeters across seasons, as one form gains advantage over another in the competition for a particular wall's particular offer.

In the mat itself — in the several centimeters of spongy accumulation on the cobblestones — there is an interior. The upper surface receives light and carries out photosynthesis. Below that, in the layers that no light reaches, the chemistry shifts to anaerobic processes, fermentation, the slow breathing of organisms that predate the invention of oxygen and still prefer its absence. The mat is thick enough to contain its own internal ecology, its own gradients, its own deep world within the already-deep world of the district. Something that has never been above the mat's surface is still living and has always been living. Its horizon is a few millimeters. It needs nothing beyond that.

This is one answer to the question of what lives here: an ecology dense enough that a centimeter of accumulated material contains what another place would spread across a kilometer.

---

The aquatic plants in the lower-story rooms are few in species but fully themselves.

There is a broad-leafed thing — call it by its shape, since its name in any human language is no longer useful here — with leaves the width of a hand and the color of deep water over dark stone, not quite black, not quite green, both. It roots in the sediment on what were once floors, sediment built up over decades from the slow rain of particulate through the windows, and it has had time to establish root systems that have threaded into the cracks between floorboards, between bricks, into the mortar that the water has softened enough to allow. It does not know it is indoors. It knows: sediment, current, available light from that direction. It has oriented toward the windows so thoroughly that the leaves angle, all of them, toward the casements, a slow tropism completed over years, each new leaf emerging at a slightly better angle than the one before. The whole plant leans inward at a few degrees from vertical, imperceptibly but actually.

It reproduces. This is important. It is not a survivor from before the flooding, clinging to existence. It was born here, in this specific room, from a spore that drifted through a window, and its parent was also born here, probably from a spore that drifted through the same window from the parent of that generation, which was perhaps the first to take hold, which perhaps found the room in the early years of the flooding when conditions were not yet stable and managed anyway. It has a lineage specific to this room. The plants of the next room, ten meters away, are from a different founding, a different arrival.

They have been here long enough to have begun to diverge. Almost imperceptibly. The room conditions differ — slightly different light, slightly different current — and the plants express those differences in slight variations of leaf thickness, of stem width, of the timing of their slow seasonal cycles. In ten thousand years there would be two species. Now there are two populations and the gap between them is already, in its first tentative way, opening.

---

In the mat, in the moss, in the accumulated sediment: the meiofauna.

These are the animals between grains. They live in the spaces between particles of sediment, between fibers of algal mat, in the water films that coat every surface. Most are less than a millimeter long. Most are not well understood even in the outside world, even in the catalogued world above the surface, because they are too small and too numerous and too varied to easily study. Here they have never been studied. They exist completely outside any system of knowing.

There are nematodes, the roundworms that live in virtually every substrate on the planet, that have been found in mine shafts kilometers underground, that will be among the last animals to go. Millions of them in a square meter of mat. They move through the mat's fibers, eating bacteria, eating other nematodes, being eaten by other things that are eating them. Their lives are fast by the standards of this place — days, weeks, a generation — and they have been generating for all the decades of the district's submersion, which means they have had time enough for real evolutionary work, for the slow drift of populations toward something specialized to this exact substrate, this exact chemistry. The nematodes in the mat of the Drowned Quarter are not quite the nematodes of anywhere else. They are starting, slowly, to be from here.

There are rotifers in the water column and on wet surfaces, those extraordinary animals with their wheel-organ mouths that create tiny currents and draw in whatever the currents bring. Some species of rotifer are found only as females and reproduce by parthenogenesis, each individual producing clones of itself, entire populations genetically identical, an experiment in uniformity that somehow works well enough to persist across geological time. Whether the rotifers here have males or not is unknown and unknowable. They are too small to notice. They are present in the thousands in every liter of water, going about their business.

There are copepods — crustaceans at the scale of a grain of rice, some smaller — that spend their lives swimming short distances, eating what they find, producing eggs that sink to the mat below and hatch weeks later. They are the largest animals in the open water of the district, probably. They are large enough that under strong light you could see them, see the paired antennae, the segmented body, the trailing egg sacs of the females. They are common here, abundant, because the bacterial density in the water gives them plenty to eat, and because the plants in the lower rooms provide additional food in the form of organic particles released from their surfaces. The copepods are doing well. They are flourishing, in the quiet, enclosed, pressured way of a thing that has found its conditions and is making the most of them.

---

In the sediment of the basements — below the iron grates, in the slow dark — there are different things.

The chemistry down there is anaerobic, sulfurous, reducing. The sediment has accumulated deepest in those low spaces — not just organic material but fine mineral particles, centuries of it, laid down in annual laminations thin as paper that a scientist could read like tree rings. At the bottom of the deepest sediment are laminations from before the flooding. Above those, a layer of disturbed material from the event of the flooding itself. Above that, the steady decades.

Living in those laminations: sulfate-reducing bacteria that produce hydrogen sulfide as a metabolic byproduct, giving the deepest basement sediment a smell (if you could smell here, if breathing were possible) of something mineral and old and not quite rot, not quite mineral either, but the specific compound that is both and is neither. Methanogens producing methane that bubbles up slowly through the sediment, through the water column, eventually (usually without notice) reaching the surface. Organisms so ancient in their lineage that their closest relatives are found in hydrothermal vents and hot springs, in the anoxic zones of stratified lakes, in the deep biosphere kilometers underground. Organisms that have found the basement of the Drowned Quarter congenial in the specific way that very old and very slow things find places congenial: quietly, without drama, without the need to flourish or spread or announce themselves.

In the sediment, also: a worm. Long, pale, very thin, not a nematode but something larger — perhaps a tubificid oligochaete, a freshwater relation of the earthworm, adapted to anoxic sediment, extending its rear end through the sediment-water interface to access oxygenated water (relatively oxygenated, here, barely) while its front end processes sediment below. These worms are not numerous but they are not rare. They are found in every basement that has accumulated enough sediment. They live at the boundary between the aerobic and anaerobic worlds, one end in each, mediating between them in the way that boundary organisms always mediate, making the crossing possible for the chemistry of each side to reach the other.

The worms in different basements may or may not encounter each other. Probably not. Probably each basement population is isolated, breeding with itself, which over enough generations and in a small enough population leads to genetic drift, to the magnification of random change, to the accidents of inheritance becoming structure. The worm in Basement A may be, is likely becoming, something slightly different from the worm in Basement B, down the street. They do not know this. They are processing sediment, which is what they do.

---

And at the edges — the fauna at the edges, the pressure-change, the thing that does not come in.

It comes, in the language of behavior, regularly but not predictably. It has a territory, or a range, or a circuit — some pattern of movement that brings it back to the vicinity of the district with a frequency that matters, that the district's smaller inhabitants have perhaps incorporated into their responses, though they do not know the source. Copepods scatter. The meiofauna in the outermost mat may press deeper when the pressure wave passes. These are not decisions.

The thing at the edge is a fish. Probably one fish, or a small number that have established this circuit through this territory. Something adapted to low light and enclosed spaces — deep-bodied perhaps, or eel-like, favoring the interface between open water and the structured complexity of the district's outer edge where the cobblestones end and there is more to find, more refuge, more prey. Something that can read the chemistry of the water as easily as the district's other inhabitants read light: tasting the hydrogen sulfide gradient that marks the basements, tasting the density of bacterial life that marks the interior, reading the district from outside as a particular chemical signature, a particular territory with particular resources.

It doesn't enter because the passages are too overgrown, or the water chemistry inside is too reducing, or it has learned (in the way that individual animals learn routes) that the interior offers nothing catchable at its scale. But it comes to the edge and moves along the edge and reads the edge.

It has been doing this for — how long is hard to say. Whether it is the same fish or the descendant of a fish that began the circuit, whether the circuit was learned or inherited — these things are not knowable. The circuit exists. The pressure-change arrives. The thing passes. The smaller animals in the outermost mat feel the passing and do not understand it but respond.

This is almost certainly the largest animal in the district. It never comes inside. It does not, in any meaningful sense, live here. But it includes the district in its life, which makes the district part of something larger, connects the enclosed world of the quarter to the open water beyond, the way a word in one language borrowed into another carries some ghost of its origin even after the original language is forgotten.

---

There is also what lives in the valve.

The valve assembly — the pressure-regulating mechanism that still cycles, that still releases its column of bubbles every several minutes — has become, over the decades, a microhabitat so specific that it probably hosts organisms found nowhere else in the district, possibly nowhere else at all.

The mechanism involves the periodic release of gas, which means the periodic disturbance of the local water, a turbulence tiny but real, enough to prevent the mat-formers from colonizing the immediate vicinity of the valve's opening. There is a cleared circle, roughly, around the release point — nothing growing directly on the valve's aperture. This cleared circle is maintained by disturbance, which is itself a form of influence, a form of tending, even if the machine does not know it is tending anything.

In that cleared circle: a particular bacteria that favors turbulent, gas-rich microenvironments. And feeding on that bacteria: a specific rotifer that appears to be found only here, or rather has not been found elsewhere, a distinction that matters enormously in science and not at all to the rotifer. And in the interstices of the valve mechanism itself, in the spaces between metal components, in the gap where the valve seat meets the housing, in the shelter of the moving parts: something harder to classify, a biofilm of extraordinary thickness and complexity, a community of organisms so integrated with the machine that the machine can no longer be considered purely mechanical. It is mechanical-biological, a hybrid, the metal providing the structure and the life providing — providing what, exactly, is the question. Corrosion inhibition, in some areas; acceleration in others. A chemistry that interacts with the valve's function in ways that may have slightly altered the cycle time across the decades, so that the four-to-six minute interval is not what the machine's engineer intended but what the machine-plus-life has settled into together.

The valve breathes. The life in the valve depends on the breathing. The breathing may depend on the life. This is a small example of something. It does not have a name.

---

Consider, finally, the wire.

Bare copper wire, mostly. Verdigris thick enough to be structural, thick enough that in places the original wire is no longer copper at all but copper compound — malachite, azurite, the mineral that grows from copper given water and time. Living on the verdigris: a specific community of organisms that can extract energy from the reduced compounds in the patina, from the gradient between the copper chemistry and the surrounding water chemistry. Chemolithotrophs, organisms that eat rock, that breathe minerals, that do not need sunlight or organic carbon but only the electron potential between one compound and another.

These organisms follow the wires. They are present wherever the wires run, thin films of living matter tracing the routes — from building to building, from building to pump house, from pump house to the larger facility at the corner. They are following the routes as faithfully as any current ever did, as faithfully as any signal. They do not know they are following a route. They are following a substrate, following a chemistry. The fact that the substrate was arranged in a pattern, that the pattern was meaningful, that the meaning has long since departed — none of this reaches the chemolithotrophs.

But the routes persist. The routes are still legible. Something still moves along them, slowly, at the pace of growth and metabolism rather than the pace of electricity, but along the same paths, through the same sequences of connection: here to here, here to here. 

What this means is probably nothing. What it seems like is something else. 

It seems like the district conducting, still, its slow business. Like the quarter dreaming — if dreaming can be slow enough, patient enough, biological enough — of function. Like the life that has colonized the Drowned Quarter has settled into its structures not just spatially but structurally, following not just the surfaces but the logic of the surfaces, tracing the intentions built into the district's layout and becoming, in some oblique and entirely non-intentional way, continuous with those intentions.

The wires carry information again. Different information. In a different medium. At a different speed.

The district communicates itself across its own extent, organism by organism, chemistry by chemistry, pressure wave by pressure wave.

It is full. It is complete. It is, in its quiet way, alive in every register available to it.

The water is warm.
