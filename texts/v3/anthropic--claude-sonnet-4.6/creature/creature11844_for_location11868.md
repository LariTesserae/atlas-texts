# Those Who Live in the Undermaze

---

Begin with what you notice when you are close enough to see a face.

The eyes are large. Not dramatically so — not the exaggerated eyes of creatures that live in complete darkness and have had to reclaim every photon available to them. These are not deprivation eyes. But they are large in the way that eyes become large across many generations in a place where light is good but sourceless, diffuse, where there are no shadows to squint against and no darkness to peer into, where the visual task is not *finding* the light but *reading* what the light reveals with equal brightness from all directions. They have drifted toward openness. The irises are pale: gray most commonly, sometimes a washed-out brown that approaches amber, occasionally nearly colorless, the pupil very dark against them. They move in a particular way — not darting, not the restless movement of a person whose environment is threatening, but a slow and steady scanning that seems to take inventory, to read surfaces the way someone reads a familiar text, gathering information without urgency. When these eyes settle on you, they settle completely, and the attention is absolute and calm, the attention of someone with no need to look over their shoulder because the chamber has no shadows behind them.

The skin is pale in the way that skin is pale when it never meets direct sunlight. Not sick-pale, not the translucent paleness of illness or deprivation, but the even, matte, cream-toned paleness of chalk itself, as though the centuries of chalk-filtered light have done their selection work and these are the people who matched the room. It is not uniform: there is color in the faces, warmth in the cheeks, the flush of exertion and the variation of individuals. But the range has compressed at the pale end. The underlayer of melanin that direct sun demands has, over the generations of no-direct-sun, been given permission to quiet itself, and it has taken that permission. The result is faces that read almost architecturally against the pale chalk walls, insufficiently contrasted from their background, faces that could be structural elements if you caught them at rest at the right angle. Visitors from the surface, if they come, always remark on this. They look like parts of the room, the visitors say, and this is taken below as description rather than criticism.

The bodies are compact. The ceiling here presses down to twelve feet in its lowest passages, and thirty feet in the standard open spaces, and the selective pressure is not for shortness exactly — the passages are navigable by people of normal height — but something has been selected for that amounts to compactness, to an economy of vertical and horizontal extension, a quality of *gathering-inward* that reads in the posture as much as the measurement. These are people who do not sprawl. Who carry themselves with their mass close to their centers. Who, when they sit, fold rather than sprawl. The characteristic posture of rest is not a lean or a sprawl but a squat or a close-seated hunch, the body making itself into a compact unit, retaining heat against the perpetual ten-degrees-above-cold of the chalk air. When they work, the work is close-in: the hand-tools used here require the body to move the stone, to put the weight of the shoulder and trunk into the motion, and the bodies have organized themselves over generations for this — powerful through the upper back and shoulders, the arms built for sustained percussion and leverage rather than reach, the wrists and hands developed in a way that makes their hands look, to outside eyes, slightly oversized relative to their frames, the fingers callused in very specific patterns along the striking edges and the gripping lines.

They are warm. Not in temperature — the chalk cool gets into the bones if you stop moving — but in the social register, in the ambient quality of their interactions: there is a baseline physical proximity that is not intimacy exactly but is not the maintained distance of stranger-cultures. The narrow passages have made proximity habitual, have made the brush of shoulder against shoulder unremarkable, have made the negotiation of shared space a constant practice so normalized that it no longer registers as negotiation. When two people stop to speak in a corridor, they are very close. When goods are exchanged, the transaction involves sustained eye contact and the transfer of objects through direct hand-touch. This is a culture of handled things and faced people, because the alternative — the managed distance of larger, more outdoor spaces — has not been an available option long enough to have been built into the habits.

---

They smell of chalk and of each other.

The chalk-smell is specific: the slightly mineral coolness of the stone, the damp-stone note from the ceiling-seep, the particular absence of wind-smell and plant-smell and the outdoor aromatics that are, in surface life, so constant that they become invisible. The air here smells of its component parts without any dilution. You can smell the stone. You can smell the water in the stone. You can smell the fungi on the damp walls, a mild earthiness that is not decay but is something close to decay's cleaner cousin. And over and through all of this, the smell of people and their animals and their food and their work — the smells of tallow and rendered fat used in lamps and cooking, the smells of the goat-things in their chalk-brick pens, the small sharp smell of the pale-coated rodents where they run in the walls, the smell of stone being worked, which is a fine-powder smell, almost tasteless, that gets into the back of the throat and the fine hairs of the nose.

The people of the Undermaze know the smell so completely that they have lost the ability to register it as smell. It has become the absence of smell, the neutral, the what-everything-smells-like-when-it-smells-like-nothing. Newcomers are overwhelmed by it in the first hours and adapted to it within a day. What is notable is that the people themselves carry it out with them when they go, when any of them go, and those who have gone to the surface and returned report that the surface-smell — the wind, the sun-warmed earth, the vast open aromatics of open air — hits them like a physical blow the first time and like grief the second time, the grief of the person who understands by smell how far they are from home.

---

The names they use are, many of them, the names of qualities of light.

This is not a universal rule, not a rigid system, but a drift: the language has accumulated over the centuries a set of naming-words that come from the vocabulary of the chalk-light, the diffuse cream-brightness that is the defining visual condition of every waking hour. There are names that mean *the quality of light when the ceiling is most luminous at midday above*, and names that mean *the dimmer, warmer quality when the sun has moved away from overhead*, and names that mean *the particular grayness at what they call night, when the light does not fail but merely retreats into something ambient and reduced, not dark, never fully dark, but not fully lit either*. There are names that reference the damp-spots on the ceiling, the darker patches, the shadows that are not shadows but merely the less-lit. There are names that mean *the color of fresh chalk* and names that mean *the color of old chalk* and a name, given rarely and considered auspicious, that means *the moment when a new ceiling-crack lets in a slightly different quality of brightness from above, slightly more golden, slightly more directed, before the chalk diffuses it back into the general*.

This luminous naming is not religious in any straightforward sense, or it has ceased to be, the way that many foundational things cease to be the specific thing they were and become instead the inherited flavor of how things are done. They do not worship the light. But they orient toward it in all the ways that matter: their prayers, if that is what the spoken formulations at the beginning of significant activities are, invoke brightness. Their greetings acknowledge light. The most severe curse available in their language is a phrase that translates approximately as *may your chalk grow dark*, which means something like may you be cut off from everything that sustains you, may the fundamental condition of your life be taken, which is a curse you can only construct if the fundamental condition of your life is not shelter or food or companionship but light.

---

How they understand time is important because it is different.

They have no sun-arc, no visible transit, no angle-of-shadow that tracks the hours. The light waxes and wanes but gently and diffusely, the ceiling brightening through the morning hours into a midday maximum and then softening through afternoon into the reduced-but-not-absent ambient of night — a night that is not dark but is *less*, a dimming that is more like the difference between the front and back of a well-lit room than between day and full darkness. This cycle is their clock, the only large-scale time-indicator available, and they read it not by looking at the ceiling (there is nothing to look at: no sun-disk, no shadow-angle, no moving thing) but by feeling it, by the way the quality of the light changes the quality of the air and the quality of the body's sense of its own alertness. They wake when the chalk brightens. They slow when it dims. The transition is gradual enough that there is no moment of sunrise-drama, no punctual beginning; they arrive at wakefulness the way a body arrives at warmth after lying long in a cool bed, incrementally, and this incremental quality shapes their relationship to time's passage.

Within the day, they count in work-units, in the number of strikes of stone on stone to do a task, in the number of trips between two points, in the number of times a particular ceiling-drip falls into the channel below it. Time is granular and task-measured and local. Long time — seasons, years — is tracked by the turning of the goat-things' biological cycles, by the cycles of the fungi's growth and rest, by the more abstract and less reliable sense of the ceiling's brightness having shifted slightly over many months as the sun's path changes above. The seasons reach them attenuated, as suggestions. Winter is when the ceiling gives slightly less, slightly cooler, the drips perhaps a little more frequent from the increased surface moisture above. Summer is more light, more warmth, the air a degree or two higher in the passages. They feel the seasons as a physician feels a pulse: present, diagnostic, not dramatic.

The result is people who have an unusual relationship with urgency. Not that they lack it — the market-motion and the perpetual rebuilding and the speed of their transactions suggest a population calibrated for continuous activity. But urgency for them is local and task-specific, not temporal in the clock-watching, appointment-keeping way. Nothing starts at a particular hour because there are no particular hours, only the gradual brightening and the gradual dimming and the work that happens within it. The improvised feeling of the city's architecture is present also in the city's schedule: everything proceeds, nothing is punctual, the whole is continuous.

---

A thing they understand that is difficult to explain to those who have not lived it:

They understand what it means to live without weather.

Not without water — water comes through the ceiling, directed into channels, used, recycled, the whole hydrology of the Undermaze a managed thing that they know as intimately as surface-people know rain. But weather, in the large sense — the sudden sky-change, the storm, the wind, the hail, the surprising day, the season that arrives wrong, the drama of an unmanaged atmosphere doing what it wants — none of this reaches them. The temperature varies by a few degrees across the year. The light varies in its quality and quantity but not in its source or its essential character. There is no wind. There is no surprise precipitation. The space maintains itself in a way that makes surprise an interior phenomenon, a social phenomenon, a thing that happens between people rather than a thing that falls on people from above.

This is so fundamental that they have not, collectively, noticed it. They know abstractly that the surface exists, that it has weather, that the people of the surface spend significant portions of their lives managing and responding to the unmanageable atmosphere. They know this the way they know about other peoples' customs: as information, as anthropology, as something they can understand without being able to fully inhabit. What they cannot know, because it has never happened to them, is what it does to a mind to be genuinely surprised by the sky. To have the world outside the self act in ways that have no relation to human intention or preparation. To have the frame of one's existence be variable and uncontrolled.

What this means for them: they are predictable to themselves. The range of their experience has a lower floor and a higher ceiling than surface-lives do. The extreme registers — the desperate cold, the violent storm, the sudden blinding sunshine after weeks of gray, the intoxicating quality of wind in a face that has been winter-enclosed — are absent. What they have instead is continuity: the known, the sustained, the thing that will be tomorrow what it was today and what it was yesterday and what it will be next year. This has made them patient in ways that surface people sometimes read as slowness and sometimes read as depth. It has made them less susceptible to the kind of moods that weather generates, the elation of a spring day, the oppression of a week of rain, the volatile emotional correlates of atmospheric variation. Their moods are interior in their generation, which means they are always, in some sense, the fault of specific circumstances rather than the ambient world, which means they are always, in some sense, available for social management.

When they argue, they argue about specific things. When they are despondent, there is a specific despondency. When they are glad, the gladness is about something. The chalk-filtered even light does not tell them how to feel, and over the generations this has made them people who look inside for those instructions, who do not outsource their emotional weather to the sky.

---

There is a class of specialist here that has no clean equivalent in surface settlements.

They are the listeners. Not, as the name might suggest, people who gather information or who mediate disputes, though they do both of these things also. The listeners are the people who have trained themselves to hear the chalk. To hear the ceiling. To put their palms flat on the floor and receive what the floor reports.

Because the chalk is not silent. This is something they know that is very hard to convey: the rock makes sounds, always, at the edge of audibility and sometimes below it, sounds that are the rock's own settling and the water's own movement through its internal channels and the vibration propagated from the world above — footsteps on the surface, the impact of water against the surface, the very low vibration of wind against terrain above, all of this transmitted through the chalk with its own attenuations and distortions. To the untrained, this is nothing, below threshold, the kind of not-quite-silence that doesn't register as information. But the listeners have spent years and then decades attending to it, and they report what they hear as weather-reports: the ceiling is still today, means surface conditions are calm. The floor is uneasy, means something heavy is moving above, means perhaps the surface-traders are coming with their loaded animals. The chalk is singing, means something the listeners say rarely and with weight, a phenomenon they describe as the rock producing a sustained low vibration with no clear mechanical cause, which they have various explanations for and which is considered auspicious in ways that even the listeners cannot fully articulate.

The listeners are consulted before significant undertakings. Their readings of the rock are taken as seriously as any human testimony. They are not priests — or they have ceased to be what they once were, if once they were priests — but they occupy a social role that has the weight that priestly roles have, the weight of people who have access to information others cannot access, who speak from a special relationship with the fundamental material of the world. They tend to be older. The ability takes time to develop, and the further-developed it is, the more it is valued. The oldest listeners, those whose palms have spent fifty or sixty years flat against chalk floors, are very quiet people with very still eyes, people who seem to be always half-listening to something the rest of the room cannot hear.

---

A practice that visitors find most strange:

They mark height on their children.

Not as a record of growth, though growth is incidentally recorded. They mark the child's height against the chalk wall of the family's most permanent space at intervals, with a stone-incised line, and beside the line they mark not the child's age but the height of the ceiling above the spot where the measurement was taken. The ratio — child's height to ceiling height — is the number that matters. A child who is one-twelfth the height of the ceiling is in a different relationship to the space than a child who is one-eighth. The goal, the thing that is celebrated when it is achieved, is when a person reaches their adult height and can measure themselves against the space's lowest passages and find themselves — this is the phrase — *fitting without compression*. Being the right size for the world they are in. Not too large for the narrowest necessary passage, not so small that the space's scale is entirely alien. The measurement is about belonging to the space, being in right proportion to it.

When they describe someone as having *good ceiling* — a phrase with no direct translation — they mean something like: this person has found their proper relationship to the constraints of their world. They are not fighting the ceiling. They are not mistaken about the ceiling. They move through the passages without either crouching or wishing the ceiling were different, because they have understood themselves correctly in relation to what is above them.

It is both a physical measurement and a thing they say about people they admire.

---

The dead.

They cannot be buried in the floor — the floor is six feet of layered history already and digging through it is work and would destabilize foundations and is simply not done. The walls are the walls, necessary and not for breaking. The ceiling is the ceiling.

So the dead go up.

There are specific places near the walls, in the moister zones, where the chalk has grown permeable enough that a body placed against it will, over the years, be absorbed. This is not a quick process and it is not described in the language of decay. It is described in the language of return: the body is given back to the chalk that gave the light that sustained the person. The chalk takes it slowly. The fungi assist. The process is gradual and unhurried in the way of all chalk-processes. The bones persist longest and are eventually visible in the wall-surface, faintly, as pale-against-pale shadows, the particular density of calcium in bone readable against the slightly different density of the chalk around it. The wall, over enough generations, contains the people.

Visitors who are told what they are looking at in the walls — who understand suddenly that the pale shadows they have been seeing are not mineral inclusions but bone, that the slightly raised ridge in the wall-surface is not a geological feature but a shoulder — go through a rapid revision of what it has meant to walk through these spaces. What has seemed like architecture reveals itself as also ancestry. What has seemed like stone reveals itself as also the dead, absorbed, diffuse, holding up the wall.

The people of the Undermaze do not find this disturbing. They find it continuous. The living and the dead are in the same light, in the same chalk, separated by the membrane of one small biological process that is, in the long view — and they have a very long view — a minor event. The drop falls from the ceiling. The floor absorbs it. The chalk, over time, absorbs everything. Nothing is lost. Nothing goes away. It only changes which form of chalk it is.

---

What they want, if you ask them simply:

They want the ceiling to hold.

They want the work to continue at the rate that work has always continued, which is the rate that sustains the city without exhausting the stone.

They want their children to have *good ceiling*, to know the right relationship between themselves and what is above them.

They want the light to come through tomorrow as it came through today, that specific warm cream-white, that ancient fermented brightness that the chalk makes from whatever is above.

They want to hear the rock singing.

And they want time enough — enough of the slow, task-measured, interior-generated time that is the only kind of time available here — to sit with their backs against the wall, feel the faint vibration of the stone, and understand what is being said.
