# Those Who Live in the Drowned Terraces

---

They arrived before they knew they were arriving.

This is essential. They did not set out for this place. They did not find it and decide. What happened is closer to: they were in motion, as they had always been in motion, and the motion slowed, and they noticed the slowing, and then more time passed — geological time, which they had learned to measure differently — and by the time the slowing had become a stopping, they had already built the first walls, already worn the first paths into the moss. They were already here before they recognized that being here was a thing they were doing.

So they do not have an origin story in the usual sense. They have a *deceleration* story. A narrative of gradual stilling.

---

They are not many.

Forty-three, at the last moment anyone counted with confidence, but counting is not something they do carefully or often, because the people here exist in a relationship with enumeration that is — not hostile, not indifferent, but *provisional*. A count is accurate at the moment of its making and then immediately begins to describe a different people than the people it was made of. Two have gone to the higher islands that are the drowned peaks. One has not returned from a descent to the lower terraces, a descent made for reasons that are still being discussed in the low voices people use here. Several are children who were not yet people when the last count happened and are now people in ways the count couldn't anticipate.

So: approximately forty. Perhaps fewer. The terraces were built for more. Or the terraces were built with optimism, which is not the same as being built for more — optimism about what the next season might bring, who might arrive with the driftwood on a slow current from some other height that also surrendered to the water. People do arrive that way sometimes. They come on wood, or on bound reeds, and the current brings them toward the cliff face, and those watching from the terrace edge — there is always someone watching from the terrace edge — will lower a rope if the arrival seems like someone who would take it.

Not everyone takes it. This is noted but not discussed at length. The gesture is made. Sometimes it is accepted.

---

The bodies of the people here are suited to the uncertainty of the ground.

Not dramatically so — not in ways that would seem impossible elsewhere, or cause comment in a market, a village, a port town. But in the way that any body, given enough time in a specific place, begins to reflect the requirements of that place. Their feet are wide and adhesive at the toe. Not webbed — the stories that get told about people who live near drowned places almost always invent webbing, which is the imagination taking the obvious direction — but with a quality of grip, of the kind of unconscious subtle gripping that develops in people who spend their lives on surfaces that are slightly wet, slightly soft, whose geometry shifts without warning when a seam opens or a ledge drops. They walk with a balance that is not cautious so much as it is *distributed* — weight spread, center of mass lower than you'd expect, each step placed with more foot than most people use, as if in constant negotiation with the stone about whether this particular piece of it intends to stay.

They are mostly quiet when they move. The moss helps. But it is also training, years of learning to hear the difference between the creak of rope settling under load and the creak that means something is about to fail, between the far vibration of geological rearrangement and the nearer vibration that means a terrace edge is losing confidence. Loudness interferes with this. They have learned to move at a volume the place allows.

Their skin has the quality of the light: pale but not dramatically so, more a quality of *evenness*, of having never much experienced direct shadow, of living in diffuse illumination that falls on all surfaces equally. They do not look unwell. They look as if they have adapted to a less contrasted world.

Their eyes are what you notice when you are close enough to notice eyes. Not unusual in color — grey-green, brown, ordinary — but in the way they *rest*. They have learned, in this place where depth cues are flattened by the sourceless light, to read surfaces very carefully. They look at things the way people look at things they are not sure about, with a steadiness and duration that people from more legible landscapes reserve for anomalies, for the thing that doesn't fit. Here, everything requires this quality of attention, and so they bring it to everything: to you, to the moss, to the small white flowers, to the flat surface of the green water below, to the sky. Nothing is looked at casually. Everything receives the full slow examination that a person gives to something they genuinely want to understand.

This can be disconcerting, at first. To be looked at that way. It helps to remember that you are receiving the same attention they give to the stone.

---

The language is an incomplete structure.

It started as something else — as many languages, probably, brought here from the different elevations and directions that the people came from during their decelerations — and what has evolved in the warm damp decades of their slowing and settling is not quite a creole, because a creole implies that the original components are recoverable with effort, and here they are not. What happened was not blending so much as *erosion*. The parts of speech that required shared history dissolved first. Then the parts that assumed a stable landscape — words for *below* and *above* lose their usefulness when these categories are temporary. Then many of the nouns, as the things named changed or went under the water or revealed themselves to have been misunderstood.

What remains is a language suited to process. It handles change well. It has seventeen or more constructions for *becoming* and only three for *is*, and these three are used with a quality of caution, a slight uptick at the end of statements that were, in the ancestor-languages, declarative, as if even the grammar has learned to hold certainty loosely.

They have a construction with no equivalent in most other languages: a grammatical marker that indicates an action whose nature will be clearer in retrospect than it is now. It attaches to verbs. It is used often.

The word for *water* is also used for *time*, or not exactly — it is more that they use one word for both, because they experience them as, not the same thing, but aspects of the same phenomenon. Something vast that rises slowly. Something you cannot stop. Something in which things are preserved in altered form. The word is short, two syllables, the kind of word you can say under your breath without it going far.

---

They are people in the middle of understanding what they know.

This is different from being ignorant, and different from being wise, and it is the condition that the place has made them into. They know many things. They know which limestone faces are newly broken and therefore weak. They know how to read the slow undulation of the surface below for what it means about the stone further down. They know how to weave a roof that will last one season exactly and no more, and they know this is not a failure of craft but an accurate reading of materials. They know that the thing in the water does not surface, and they know what it means when the water is more still than usual, and what it means when it is less still, and neither of these is entirely benign or entirely dangerous, and the word they use for it — the thing in the water — uses the becoming-marker, the grammatical signal that its nature will be clearer later. They have not removed this marker as they have come to know the creature better. If anything, the longer they live near it, the more confident they are that the marker belongs.

What they are in the middle of understanding is what they are. Where they are going. Whether this is arrival or passage. Whether the terraces are where they will end or where they are catching their breath between one form of motion and another. They do not experience this as distressing. They experience it the way the stone experiences the water: as a condition, as something that acts on them over long time, as something that is making them into a shape they cannot see from the inside.

They have stopped asking whether the water is still coming or already going. The question requires a fixed point from which to assess direction, and they have learned not to expect fixed points. They ask instead: *what does it seem to want to do today*. This question is answerable.

---

Their knowledge is practical first and metaphysical second and these two things are not well separated.

To know that a ledge will fail is practical knowledge. But to know it in the way they know it — from the sound underfoot, from the quality of the crack at the edge, from the behavior of the moss which thins before stone fails as if it too has noticed — this knowing involves a relationship with the material world that is difficult to describe without using language they would find too certain, too declarative. They would say: the ledge is *telling* you. Not as metaphor. Not assigning intention to stone. More as a description of a perceptual experience that their language handles well — information arriving through channels that are not exactly sight or sound but that are real and reliable — and their language has words for this that other languages tend not to have, the same way that they have many words for different qualities of the green below and only vague gestures for colors they rarely encounter.

---

The craft they practice is responsive.

They do not plan in the way that presupposes a stable future. They plan the way water plans — finding the available direction, following it until it closes, then finding the next available direction. When a terrace wall cracks, they do not consult a design; they look at the crack, and they look at what materials are present, and they make a response. The response may not last. Responses don't need to last. They need to be adequate to the current conditions, and if the conditions change — when the conditions change — a new response will be made.

This makes their craft seem improvised to outside eyes. It is not improvised. It is deeply skilled and accumulated and the skills are transmitted carefully from person to person, child to elder, in long wordless sessions of working together where the teaching is in the doing and the doing is the lesson and if you ask what they are teaching they will look at you for a long time with that full unhurried attention and then they will say, eventually, *how to notice*.

How to notice.

This is the central education. The formal content varies — how to test stone, how to read the water, how to weave a roof for exactly one season, how to hear the submerged vibration and translate it — but the underlying pedagogy is a continuous refinement of the capacity to receive information from the world. To be quiet enough, still enough, patient enough, that the place can tell you what it needs to tell you in the low unurgent voice it speaks with. It speaks in the drip of condensation from a frond. In the feel of the moss under the sole before weight is committed. In the way the green shifts below, in the quality of the undulation that is more from the left than usual, which means something below to the left is moving.

The children learn this most readily, because children who grow up here have not yet learned to expect the world to be loud and clear about its states. They learn the subtle version first and find it natural. The arrivals — the people who come on driftwood from the drowned elevations — take longer. Some never fully develop it. They can be distinguished from the born-here people by a quality of slight inattention, a tendency to look at things for less long than the thing requires.

---

They have a relationship with the drowned valley that is not worship and not indifference and exists somewhere between ancestor-veneration and geology.

Whatever is below the water — the structures, the worked stone, the accumulated life of the people who lived at the valley floor before the water came up — is present to them as substrate. Not as loss, or not primarily as loss. As foundation. The water has preserved certain things and altered others and destroyed others and the line between these three categories is unclear from the surface, is probably unclear from below. But the knowing that it is there — that the stone under the water is made, is cut with intention, is the evidence of people who thought the valley floor was where you lived and did not see the waters coming or saw them and could not move fast enough — this knowing is something they carry.

They do not name those people. The language doesn't have a stable word for *ancestor*, for the same reason it doesn't have strong declarative constructions: because declaring something an ancestor is declaring that the relationship is historical and complete, and they don't experience it that way. The people below are in process. They are becoming something else. The dissolution of their structures is happening in real time, over the span of generations, a slow collaborative project between the water and the stone and the time. The people of the terraces watch it from above, imperfectly, through forty meters of green dark, and they are the next layer of the same process, and the terraces under them will eventually give the same way if the water continues — *when* the water continues — and there will be, after them, other people on some higher narrowing strip of rock, watching the same slow green below, which will be deeper then, and what sinks to the floor will be the stones of these walls, these buildings, these paths worn into the moss.

They know this. They are not distressed by it. Distress would require a belief that continuation, in its current form, is the appropriate goal. They have been told otherwise by the place. They have received the information through all the subtle channels and the message is clear: *continuation takes different forms*.

---

At night — if night is the right word for the shift that happens when the diffuse light thins to near-absence, a darkening that comes from everywhere equally as the daytime light did, a darkness without direction — they sleep on the moss, as suggested, some of them, or in the structures where the warmth is better held, and the sound of the place is the sound of the place: the drip, the low creak, the subsurface vibration that is the sound of the world working. Some of them sleep with an ear to the moss, not consciously, not deliberately, but they have found it conducive. The earth sound carries up through root and stone into the side of the face. It is something like hearing the pulse of a person you are lying beside: informational, but also simply the assurance that the process continues, that the long work is still being done, that you are here in the middle of something that extends in both directions beyond your ability to see, and this is the condition you have always been in, and it is a condition, and you can sleep in it.

The children sleep deeply. The arrivals often don't, at first. They are waiting for a quiet that the place doesn't provide, a stillness they were trained to expect at night. The stillness here is on the surface only. Below the surface — below the moss, below the stone, below the forty meters of green — there is no stillness. There has never been stillness. It is the wrong place to look for it.

They come to understand this, the arrivals, eventually. Some sooner than others. The moment of understanding shows in the sleep: when they finally sleep through the vibration, when they have incorporated it into their rest rather than waiting for it to stop, something in the long muscles of the back releases. They become slightly heavier, slightly more present to the surface under them. The moss accepts this. The moss is patient and has been in the business of accepting for as long as the terraces have existed.

In the morning — in the lightening — someone is already at the terrace edge.

They are looking at the water.

It is the same water as yesterday, green and still and deep, and whatever moves in it has moved. The watcher is not evaluating whether it looks different. They are not looking for change exactly. They are looking the way you look at something you have agreed to pay attention to, not because you expect it to break your heart or redeem you, but because you made an agreement, and agreements are how you make yourself trustworthy to a place, and you want the place to trust you, because you need it to keep telling you what it knows.

The watcher watches.

The water is watched.

This is how the morning comes here, and how it has come, and how — the grammar allows for this, has a construction for it — it seems likely, from what can be observed, to continue coming.
