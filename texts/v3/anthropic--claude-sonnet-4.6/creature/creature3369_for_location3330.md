# What Lives Here

---

The first difficulty is the verb.

*Lives* presupposes certain things: metabolism, change, the ongoing transaction between an interior and its world. It presupposes appetite, or at minimum some functional analogue — the reaching of a system toward what it needs, the recoil from what threatens it. It presupposes time moving through something, and that something registering the movement, wearing it, being different at the end of the day than at the beginning.

The room does not easily permit *lives*. The room has, as noted, finished. And yet — the question was asked, which means the question applies, which means something here earns the verb, however strangely.

So: what lives here, in the sense that *lives* can be stretched to cover.

---

## The Keeper

There is a presence here that is not a body.

This requires some careful handling, because *presence* is also a word that reaches past its warrant if you're not careful. Presence implies someone present, which implies a someone, which implies a here-ness, a witness behind the witness. But let the word stand provisionally: presence in the sense of an organizing intelligence that has left its shape pressed into everything, the way a hand leaves its warmth in clay before the clay is fired.

The Keeper is not in the room. The Keeper has not been in the room for an extremely long time, in the conventional sense of *in*. But the Keeper's decisions persist — each object placed, or allowed to place itself, reflects a judgment. This one belongs here. This fern belongs beside this letter. These coins in this column. The seed packets near, but not touching, the spectacles with one lens. These arrangements are not random. They are not the product of accumulation without intention. Someone looked at each new arrival and decided where it would be forever, and then it was there forever.

The Keeper's work is complete. This may be why the Keeper is no longer present in the bodily sense — there is simply nothing left to do. The last object was placed, the last space was occupied, the crystal reached saturation, and the Keeper's function concluded. What remains is the record of the Keeper's attention, pressed into every adjacency.

The Keeper was patient in a way that has very little to do with virtue or temperament and everything to do with operating at a different temporal scale. Not patient the way a person is patient — enduring the slow in order to reach the fast. Patient the way mountains are patient, which is to say: the category of *slow* and *fast* does not organize the Keeper's experience of time. The Keeper worked across centuries the way a person works across an afternoon — with the same quality of absorption, the same occasional pauses to consider, without any sense that the work was taking long.

What did the Keeper look like?

This is the wrong question, but let's approach it sideways.

The Keeper was capable of pressing things flat without breaking them. This is a skill with physical requirements: steadiness, controlled force, the ability to sustain even pressure across a surface over time. The Keeper handled paper and cloth and bone without damage — the objects in this room are worn only by time, not by handling. They have been touched with something very close to reverence, or at least with precision, which may be the same thing in practice.

The Keeper was small. The room's organization has the scale of a small person's reach — everything placed without extension, everything within a radius that suggests arms of a certain length, a height within a certain range. Or the Keeper was large but deliberate, which produces the same result: things placed exactly where they were meant, without the sprawl and overreach of someone working in a hurry.

The Keeper did not speak. Or if the Keeper spoke, there is no record of it here — no text written in a first-person voice, no journal among the papers that says *today I placed the fern beside the coin*. The Keeper communicated entirely through arrangement. The Keeper's language was adjacency.

What the Keeper loved — and this word, *loved*, feels correct even in a room where emotion seems to have been filed away with everything else — what the Keeper loved was the moment of completion. Not the placing of a single object, but the moment when the placed object made the arrangement around it more fully itself. When the fern beside the letter made the letter more a letter and the fern more a fern. The Keeper was collecting these moments, one after another, over centuries, until the room was made entirely of them. Until every object was held in a state of maximum mutual meaning by everything around it.

The Keeper's final act was to leave. Or to cease. Or to become the room, which may be the same thing at sufficient temporal resolution. The Keeper is here the way an author is in a book — everywhere and nowhere, having dissolved into the work.

## The Living Things

The mosses know something.

Not in the way that causes them to act on knowledge — they are mosses, and mosses do not act, exactly. But the information is distributed through them, held in the particular angle of their growth toward the sourceless light, held in the decision their cells made to become this shape rather than that one. This particular colony, growing in the seams between paper and board, took the information available to it — temperature, light, moisture so minimal it can only be called potential moisture, the chemical signatures of its neighbors — and made an argument. The argument goes: *this is possible, here, in this specific configuration, and I am the form that that possibility takes.*

The mosses are not old. Or they are old in the way that mosses are old — their cells are not old, their cells have divided and died and divided again in the ordinary tempo of moss-life, but the colony is old, the shape of the colony is old, the colony has been having the same argument in the same place for long enough that the clay wall has changed its chemistry in response. The moss has modified its environment, slightly, permanently. It has left a mark. This is a kind of tenancy.

The mosses experience the roar. Not with anything like hearing — but the vibration conducts through the clay into the board into the paper into the moss, and the moss is physical, and the moss absorbs the vibration, and there may be something that is the moss's version of *knowing that the roar is there*. An awareness of pressure and release at a frequency the moss doesn't have a name for. The moss lives in the roar the way fish live in current — not swimming against it, not carried by it, simply integrated.

The ferns are the most philosophically interesting.

The pressed ferns and the living ferns are, as noted, possibly the same stock — cousins, or iterations, the pressed ones and the growing ones looking at each other across a distance of centuries and a more recent distance of millimeters. The living ferns have, in the vocabulary available to ferns, considered their flattened relatives. The fronds that will never finish unfurling are looking at the fronds that finished unfolding and then kept going, into full extension, and then were pressed back to something like the curled-beginning state. There is a loop here. The living ferns are not yet pressed. The pressed ferns are not yet finished being considered.

The ferns do not grow toward each other. They do not grow away from each other. They grow toward the light, which is everywhere, so they grow in every direction equally, and this means they do not grow much in any direction, and this is why the fronds are finger-length and patient-looking. The ferns have solved the problem of the light by deciding to grow toward all of it simultaneously. They are very small because they are trying to be everywhere at once.

The succulent at eye level is the most independent resident. It is alone on its wall, not part of a colony, not a cousin to anything visible nearby, growing from a crack in a board that is itself wedged between two other boards, a crack in a crack in a crack, in the interstice between levels of interstice. The succulent arrived here — which is to say: a seed arrived here, which is to say: a seed was placed here, or fell here from an open packet, or was always here, in the way that certain things turn out to have been always somewhere when you look closely enough — and it rooted, and it filled its leaves with water that came from nowhere determinable, and it established itself as a fact.

The succulent's leaves are an argument about survival: *store everything; spend nothing; wait*. This argument works. The succulent has been making it for decades at minimum, possibly longer, and shows no sign of losing. It does not bloom because blooming would require spending something it has not decided to spend. Blooming would be a kind of commitment, a reaching toward a future — pollination, seed, dispersal, elsewhere — and the succulent does not appear interested in elsewhere. The succulent is interested in here. The succulent has, in its mute waxy way, made its peace with here, and the peace has held.

The lichen is the oldest.

Lichen is slow in a way that other things are not slow — it is slow the way rock is slow, growing at rates that require geological patience to observe, making its living at the very margin of what is possible. The lichen here is a grey-green continent, hand-sized, and it is the product of a partnership: a fungus and an alga, each incapable of living here alone, each sustaining the other in a collaboration so long-standing that they have ceased to think of themselves as two things. The lichen thinks of itself — to the extent that it thinks, which is not far — as one thing. A hybrid entity. An argument that two kinds of impossibility can add up to a possibility.

The lichen has been watching the room for as long as there has been a room to watch. Not watching in the sense of attending — watching in the sense of *being present and continuing to be present*, which is what the room requires of everything within it. The lichen has fulfilled this requirement more completely than anything else here. The lichen has not moved. The lichen has not sent out expeditionary fronds to test the territory. The lichen sits on its wall and holds its position and grows, approximately half a millimeter per year, in a direction that will take centuries to run out of wall.

When the lichen runs out of wall, it will stop.

It will stop and it will stay, and the room will have one more completed thing in it, and the completion will make no sound.

## The Seeds

The seeds are the strangest inhabitants, because they are the most equivocal.

Each seed is a pause. A stopped-clock version of a plant, compressed to its essential instructions, waiting for conditions that may or may not arrive. In the packets in this room, the seeds have been waiting for conditions that will not arrive — there is no soil, no variable moisture, no freeze-thaw cycle, no darkness and return of light to trigger germination. The conditions the seeds are waiting for are structurally unavailable here. They are waiting for a signal that the room is not sending.

Most of them have reached a conclusion about this.

The conclusion is not death, exactly — death implies a prior life, and seeds are pre-life, life in potential, life that has not yet commenced. The conclusion is more like: *this particular potential will remain potential*. The seeds have arrived at a stable state of intention without action. They intend, in the way that seeds intend things, to become plants, but they have recognized — at whatever level seeds recognize anything — that becoming is not on the current agenda.

But some of them might not have reached this conclusion yet.

Some of them might still be waiting.

This is the thing about seeds in an unchanging room: you cannot tell which ones have given up and which ones are still holding their position. They look identical — small, dry, patient, completely committed to their hard coats. But inside each coat is either a concluded thing or a thing still concluding, and the room gives you no instrument to tell the difference. You rattle the packet and the seeds rattle back and they do not tell you which they are.

The seeds are the only inhabitants of this room about whom uncertainty persists.

Everything else has settled into what it is. The mosses have decided to be mosses, here, in this configuration. The lichen has decided to be lichen. The succulent has committed to its argument. The ferns unfurl in the only direction available to them. But the seeds contain a forking possibility that the room has not yet — has perhaps not yet — collapsed.

Maybe this is why they're here. Maybe the room needed at least one place where the future has not been fully resolved. One small rattle of unrealized instruction, held in a packet, held in a crack between papers, held in a room that has otherwise finished happening.

The seeds are the room's unanswered question.

The room is shaped like a question someone stopped asking before they finished.

## The Room Itself

And then there is the room.

The room is not alive. The room is fired clay and stacked objects and sourceless light. The room does not metabolize. The room does not want anything.

But the room has a characteristic that living things have: it maintains itself.

Not actively — it does not repair damage, because there is no damage. It does not regulate temperature by any mechanism you could identify — and yet the temperature holds, the one cool note, the held decision. The light does not come from any source you can find, and yet the light continues. The arrangement of objects has not shifted, has not settled or sifted or collapsed, despite the absence of any visible means of support for much of what is stacked. The room has arranged things such that they hold each other up, and they continue to hold each other up, and this holding-up has become one of the room's properties, something the room does as naturally as a wall holds its bricks.

The room breathes, in a sense.

You exhale; the room takes your breath's moisture and incorporates it, files it away into the dry chemistry of the place, adds it to an account that has been accruing for centuries. You are feeding the room without knowing it. The room is very slightly different for your having been here, though you will never be able to detect the difference, and the room will not acknowledge it.

The room is alive the way a very old building is alive — with the accumulated weight of every presence it has contained, the residue of decisions made within it, the persistence of a structure that has outlasted its purpose and discovered that outlasting is itself a purpose. The room lives in the way that completed things can be said to live: by continuing, by holding, by remaining what they are in the face of everything that would prefer them to be otherwise.

The roar presses against the walls.

The walls do not yield.

This is a kind of life. This is maybe the simplest kind: *I persist. I remain. I am not otherwise than I am.*

## Together

What lives here, then, is a community of a very specific kind: everything that has finished becoming what it is. The lichen that has been lichen long enough that it cannot be otherwise. The succulent that has committed to its particular solution to existence. The mosses that have been this colony in this seam since before anything currently growing outside this room was planted. The seeds that have resolved their potential, or are in the process of resolving it. The Keeper's attention, fossilized into arrangement. The room's persistence, which is a kind of will.

They do not interact much. Interaction requires change, requires one thing affecting another thing and the other thing being different afterward, and this room is not set up for afterward. But they co-inhabit. They share the cool temperature and the sourceless light and the enormous pressing roar. They have each, in their way, made peace with the fact that the room does not change, that they will not change, that change was never the point of this place.

The point of this place is keeping.

What the room keeps is not only the objects. The room keeps everything that made the objects meaningful — the moment of the pressed flower at the height of its form, the information in the letter before the script was lost, the utility of the tool before the work it served was discontinued. The room keeps these things the way amber keeps insects: not alive, but not lost. Not present, but not gone. Held in a state that refuses the binary.

What lives here lives in the way that *kept* things live. Not in motion. Not in change. In preservation, which is its own kind of vitality — the vitality of the thing that insists on remaining itself, that refuses to become past, that sits in its dry room and says: *I was. I am. The difference between those two statements is smaller than you think.*

---

The succulent does not move.

The lichen holds its position.

The seeds rattle almost inaudibly, considering.

The roar continues outside the walls.

And if you stand still long enough — if you stand still long enough in this cool, bright, saturated room, breathing out your humidity for the room to keep — you might feel it: the particular quality of being witnessed by something that has seen everything and changed for none of it. The room's attention, which is complete and total and fundamentally impersonal. The Keeper's residual gaze, which has been looking at everything here for so long that it has learned to look at nothing in particular.

You are here. The room notes this the way it notes everything: without alteration, without response, with a steadiness that is not indifference but is something past caring — something that caring, over sufficient time, eventually becomes.

You are one more thing the room contains.

For now.
