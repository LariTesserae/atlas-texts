## What Lives in the Worn City

---

Begin with this: whatever lives here does not experience itself as living *in* a ruin. It experiences itself as living in the only world there is. This is important. It does not have a concept of *before*. The low wall-lines are not the remnants of something — they are simply what walls are: low, pale, ambient, the natural shape of threshold. The thing that lives here has never seen a tall wall. It has never seen a door. It has never stood inside a room that had a ceiling. It came up from nothing into a world where rooms are volumes of cold air defined by ankle-high stone, and this is what rooms are, and there is no grief in this because there is no comparison to grieve against.

But also: it does not have a word for room. It does not have a word for anything. Words are a different system entirely, and what lives here arrived at its existence through a different procedure.

---

Let it take shape slowly.

The tracks in the soil-seams suggest small rounded feet. This is true. What makes the tracks is small — small enough that the hollow in the moss-corner fits it precisely, small enough that it can move along the base of a wall without being visible above the wall's height, small enough that its body displaces air in quantities too slight for the wind to distinguish from itself. But *small* here is not the whole story. Small is just the size it happens to be.

Its color is the color of the plateau in overcast light: grey, pale, with an undertone that shifts depending on what it is pressed against. This is not camouflage in the active sense — the creature is not *performing* concealment, does not *decide* to match its background — but the palette of its body was arrived at through a very long negotiation with the palette of this place, and they have become, for most purposes, the same conversation. In full sun it reads as almost white. Against moss it darkens. Against lichen it takes on a faint orange at its edges, an effect so subtle that the eye, catching it, is not certain it caught anything at all.

The fur, if fur is the right word — and it may not be, but it will serve — is not soft in the way that implies warmth. It is dense and fine, packed so tightly that its surface sheds water, sheds the thin cold fog, sheds the frost that forms each night on everything else. This is why you find no frost on the den in the moss-corner in the mornings. The fur works the way the moss works: not by repelling moisture but by holding it in a layer so thin and so thoroughly controlled that it neither enters nor accumulates nor freezes. The creature is, in this way, its own small microclimate. A slightly regulated zone.

Its eyes are large. Necessarily, given the fog, the overcast, the nights that drop fast and cold and dark. But large is not quite right either — they are large in proportion, but proportion is doing unusual work in this body. Everything is distributed toward sense over mass: the ears, which fold flat against the skull in wind but open, in stillness, to a diameter that seems wrong for the head, almost comical, certainly impractical-looking and yet completely practical; the nose, which is damp and cold and almost certainly the primary organ of navigation in a landscape where visibility is often a matter of meters; the feet, which are wide and slightly splayed, designed for the smooth stone, for the grip of a surface that offers almost nothing to grip.

Call it a mammal. It is probably a mammal. But the category sits lightly on it, like clothing that mostly fits.

---

Its life is organized by the daily cycle of the plateau. This is not a choice — the plateau enforces it.

Before dawn the frost is forming and the temperature is at its lowest and the creature is in its hollow, pressed into the moss, which is warmer than the stone and cooler than the creature and which the creature has apparently established some long mutual arrangement with. The hollow is not made — the creature did not dig it — but neither is it accidental. Something shaped it over time through repeated occupation, small adjustments, the accumulated impression of a body repeatedly placed in the same position. The moss grew into it. The moss grew *for* it, in some sense that is not quite intention but is not quite random either. These two lives, the moss and the creature, have organized themselves around each other so thoroughly that it is now difficult to know where one ends.

When the frost begins to melt — this is the trigger, warmth not as comfort but as signal — the creature moves. Moves along the interior sides of walls, which you have already noticed are the sides the moss grows on, and this is not a coincidence. The moss-side of a wall is the inside-of-room side, the sheltered side, the side that still — still, after all of this — organizes itself around the concept of *interior*. The creature walks this line. It is following an organizing principle that it did not create and does not understand and does not need to understand, any more than water needs to understand gravity.

What does it eat? This is harder. The plateau is genuinely sparse. But sparse is not nothing. There is something in the moss — in the tiny ecosystem that the moss constitutes, the miniature geography of its surface with its own tiny inhabitants, invisible to you but present, always present — and the creature has tools for this, the nose above all. It moves slowly along the moss-seams, pausing, and you might, if you were very still, hear a faint sound like breathing or like the sound the water makes under the stone, too quiet to be certain, and then it moves on. It has eaten, or it has not, and you cannot tell the difference.

It drinks from the thread-thin water that runs along the wall-base where the moss gathers condensation. It has learned — learning is perhaps too strong a word; it has, over time and iterations, become a thing that knows — exactly which sections of which walls produce water at which points in the daily cycle. This knowledge is precise. It is the kind of precision that looks like instinct but is really just a very complete integration of a self with a place.

---

The echo, the wrong acoustics of the space — the creature does not experience these as wrong. It experiences them as *how sound works*. And it uses them.

In the fog, when visibility drops and the nose and the enormous folded ears become the primary instruments of navigation, the creature makes a sound. Very quiet. You would miss it under the wind. But the wind in the city-walls is already organized, already tonal, and the creature's sound slots into this organization and is returned to it altered in the way all sound here is altered. And the creature reads this alteration. The returned sound tells it something about where walls are, where gaps are, where the space between former rooms opens into former street and where former street ends in former wall. It navigates by the city's amplification. It uses what the ruins do to sound as a map of the ruins.

This is why it does not leave. Not because it cannot — the plateau extends in all directions and the creature has legs and there is no fence — but because the amplification is *here*. The map works *here*. Outside the city-footprint, sound returns from the plateau in the flat ordinary way sound returns from flat ordinary surfaces, and the information is gone. Beyond the city-walls, it is navigating without its instrument. It may have tried this. It returns.

And this is the deepest thing about the creature: it is not haunting a ruin. It is *calibrated to it*. The ruin is the instrument and the creature is the player and the music they make together is the organized wind-sounds and the water-sounds and the small sound the creature makes in fog that comes back telling it the shape of the old streets.

---

It is alone, most likely. Or almost alone. The signs suggest one — one set of tracks, one hollow, one portion of wall-base worn to a slightly higher shine by the repeated passage of one small body. But *alone* is the wrong category here too. The creature does not experience solitude in the human mode, which requires the memory or imagination of company. It experiences itself in relation to the plateau, the walls, the moss, the water, the wind, the sound, the cold, the daily frost-and-thaw — and these relations are rich enough, dense enough, demanding enough that the absence of others like itself does not register as absence.

If there are others of its kind, they are elsewhere, on other high places, in other old cities-becoming-terrain-features, living the same negotiation with different specific walls, different specific moss, different specific patterns of frost. Their loneliness — if you must call it that — is similarly distributed, similarly filled.

They do not look for each other. Or they have not, yet, today.

---

At night, when the temperature falls fastest and the frost begins to assemble on the stone, the creature is back in the hollow, and the wind does its organized work through the room-outlines, and water moves below in its invisible channel, and the lichen adheres in its philosophical way to the stone, and the moss holds water in its saturated wool-like way, and the small rounded body in the moss-corner holds the interior at a slightly higher temperature than the exterior, and the city — the city keeps doing what the city does, which is to amplify everything that passes through it and return it slightly changed.

The creature breathes. Each breath a small negotiation, as all breathing is at this altitude. The exhaled air is the warmest thing present by a significant margin.

It is, for all practical purposes, what the city kept.

Not its ghost. Not its guardian. Not a metaphor for anything. Just the thing that came after everything else and found that the shaped stone and the amplifying walls and the moss-that-grows-only-inside were sufficient conditions for a life, and made a life of them, and has been making it, in the cycling daily way lives are made, for long enough that it fits.

The city is not abandoned.

Neither is the creature.

These two facts, held together, are approximately what remains.
