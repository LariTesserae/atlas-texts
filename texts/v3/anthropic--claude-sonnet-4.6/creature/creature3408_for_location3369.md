# What Lives on the Plateau of Settled Things

---

## First Consideration

The temptation is to begin with the people. They are the most legible presence — they have structures, they have paths, they have practices that can be named and described. But the people are not the oldest thing here, and on this plateau, age is rank. So we must begin elsewhere and arrive at the people the way you arrive at the plateau itself: gradually, retroactively, with the understanding that you are already among them before you have identified them as such.

---

## The Moss

Start here, because the moss is the closest thing this plateau has to a foundation species — not in any scientific hierarchy but in the hierarchy of *having shaped everything that came after*.

It is impossible to say whether this moss is one thing or many. The fronds-within-fronds structure, the repetition of form across every scale, suggests something that does not experience itself as having edges. A colony of moss and *the* moss on this plateau may be the same sentence. Genetic analysis, if it were done, might reveal that the colonies spread from a small number of original progenitors over such an expanse of time that the original individual has become conceptually unavailable — absorbed into the aggregate, which has itself become absorbed into the landscape, which has itself ceased to distinguish made from natural, so that the moss is not a thing growing on the plateau so much as a process the plateau is engaged in.

It has been here longer than the people. It may have been here, in its ancestral form, longer than complex life reached this altitude — a pioneer arriving ahead of anything that would follow. Whatever first made this plateau habitable for anything other than bare stone, the moss was part of that making. The soil in the cracks is partly *made of it* — old moss, fallen, composted, become soil, become the medium for new moss — a bootstrap so old it has become invisible as a bootstrap and visible only as fact.

This means the moss has information. Not in any nervous sense; it has no neurons, no signal architecture, no learning in the familiar mode. But it has, encoded in its distribution, a complete map of everywhere water has pooled in the last hundred thousand years. It holds, in its chemical negotiations with the stone, a record of every micro-change in atmospheric composition since it arrived. It carries, in its genetic variation — high here, low there, specific patterns of relatedness across the plateau's surface — a kind of autobiography of catastrophe and recovery, each bottleneck visible as a reduction in diversity that then expands again as surviving individuals spread across cleared ground. 

The moss does not know any of this. It does not need to. The knowing is distributed across its structure rather than concentrated anywhere, which is perhaps a more reliable way to know something over geological time. You could read the moss, if you had the tools and the patience, the way you read ice cores or sediment layers: a record of what happened, in sequence, without commentary.

What the moss does — its active life, what it is doing now — is grow and consume and transform. It eats the stone through acid secretion, dissolving mineral bonds that took enormous heat and pressure to form, adding them to itself, releasing what it cannot use. It grows into the space between grains of rock and wedges that space apart, slowly, over years, with a force that exceeds what seems possible for something so delicate. It is colonizing the plateau even now, from within. The stone does not resist this — or the stone resists this the way stone resists everything, which is by being patient and eventually losing.

The dew on the filaments is also alive. There are organisms in that suspended water — bacteria, microbes of categories that don't have common names, things that live in the water that collects on moss in the hour before dawn and are dried away by wind when the morning arrives, spending their entire existences in a window of hours at an elevation that would kill most things, on a surface that has developed them specifically for this niche over a longer time than most niches have had to develop anything. The dew is full. The dew is a community. You have to be very small to know this.

## The Stone's Inhabitants

The stone is not dead. This requires stating plainly, because stone tends to read as the backdrop against which life occurs rather than as participant. The organisms that live within the stone are not visible in any ordinary sense, but the evidence of them is everywhere if evidence is what you're looking at.

The lichen that has not been mentioned yet. It has not been mentioned because lichen on very old stone, in cold and intermittent light, becomes the same color as the stone and requires intention to see. But it is here, on every exposed surface with the right aspect — south-facing where south gets more light, north-facing where north gets the condensation — in crusts that are less the thickness of paint and more the thickness of the stone's own skin. The lichen does not have a border, exactly. It intergrades with the stone at its lower surface and with the air at its upper surface, occupying a zone that is both and neither.

The lichen is, like all lichens, a symbiosis: fungus and algae (or, in some cases here, cyanobacteria, which can fix nitrogen from air and which make this partnership more productive in poor-soil conditions). The fungus provides structure and water retention. The photosynthetic partner provides carbohydrates. Together they are something that neither could be alone — and together they have been here, on this stone, for so long that the word *together* fails to capture the relationship. They have co-evolved into a form that is jointly optimized for this specific stone in these specific conditions; the lichen is not found below the plateau in the same form, because the stone below is different and the conditions are different, and the lichen here has spent its long history tuning itself to the ash-grey stone of this specific place.

The lichen grows a centimeter, in some species, per century. It is possible to age it, roughly, by its size, and the largest ones visible on this plateau are very large indeed. They predate the settlement. The oldest ones may predate any memory of the plateau being visited at all. They are — the word seems too small, but language has only so many tools — *ancient*, in the way that ancient means not merely old but of a different order of time, a time that cannot be related to human experience through analogy to human things because no human experience lasts this long and nothing human forgets, and the lichen has forgotten nothing because it has stored nothing, because it has simply *continued*, which is not the same as memory but accomplishes some of what memory accomplishes.

Below and within the surface: endolithic organisms, which live inside the stone. Not metaphorically inside — physically inside, in the pore spaces between mineral grains, in small cracks, in the boundaries between different types of crystal, finding the water that migrates through the rock and the light that penetrates the outermost few millimeters of translucent mineral and doing what they do in there, which is: metabolize slowly, reproduce rarely, persist. Their generation time may be hundreds of years. Their metabolic rates are so low they are nearly impossible to measure. They may be, in any given moment, doing what dying looks like if dying takes a very long time and is barely distinguishable from not-dying.

The stone is full of them, and always has been, and they predate everything else here by so much that the categories start to blur. They are so early in the sequence of arrival that they arguably *prepared* this place for the moss and the lichen, breaking the first chemical bonds, building the first organic molecules in the first micro-cracks, laying the groundwork for a biological community that would eventually, after enormous time, produce enough soil and enough shelter that larger things could get a foothold. All of that complexity — the moss, the dew-community, the people and their lashed poles and stone-fitted walls — is downstream of what these near-invisible inhabitants started, in the first penetrations of the bare rock by the first wandering cells, impossibly long ago.

## The Specific Small Lives

There are invertebrates in the moss, because moss anywhere that has had long enough is a world for invertebrates. Springtails, in varieties tuned to cold and low food abundance — small, sometimes iridescent, sometimes nearly transparent, jumping when disturbed by a force that seems excessive for their size until you understand that jumping is their only escape mechanism and so they have maximized it. They eat the bacteria and the fungal threads and the dead moss and the detritus of the dew-community; they are the plateau's smallest visible decomposers, returning what was borrowed from the mineral substrate.

Mites: tinier still, moving through the moss like something moving through a forest, which for a mite it is. There are predatory mites and scavenging mites and mites that eat fungal spores and mites that eat the eggs of other mites, a community small enough that its entire drama of predation and escape and reproduction and death occurs within a few centimeters, invisible from standing height, constant.

Worms of a sort — not earthworms in the familiar sense, since earthworms want warmth and richness and the soil here is cold and spare, but something convergent upon worms, adapted to the thin soils in the deepest cracks, moving through the accumulated moss-compost with the slow muscular patience of an organism that does not need to move quickly because nothing here moves quickly and competition is not fierce when the population is sparse and the cold keeps everyone suppressed.

There may be flies, in the warmest part of the warmest days of the warmest months — something that waits out most of the year in egg or pupa form, hatches into a brief window of tepid air, mates urgently, lays eggs in the water of the basins or in the wet moss, and is done. These would be small and dark and not obvious except at certain hours when they appear in small numbers above the water basins, hovering as though confused by their own existence, which is justified confusion for something that only exists into visibility for a few weeks per year.

These are not abundant. Nothing here is abundant. The biology of the plateau is characterized by presence achieved through *persistence* rather than through *population* — a few individuals of each kind, doing what they do over enormous stretches of time, producing enough offspring to maintain the few individuals against mortality without ever ramifying into the explosive growth that characterizes places with more energy. The productivity ceiling here is so low that the ecological community has shaped itself entirely to that ceiling, becoming small, slow, frugal, specialized in ways that abundance-ecologies never need to be.

They are, in their smallness and frugality, perfectly matched to this place. They have been made by it, and they are making it, and the distinction has collapsed.

## The People

Now the people, who are the least typical thing here in the sense that they came most recently — though recently is relative and the settlement is old enough to have participated in what the place became — and the most typical thing here in the sense that they have been shaped by the plateau's character as thoroughly as anything else that lives on it.

They are few, and they carry their fewness lightly, which is to say: they have not organized themselves against it. The social structures of crowded places — hierarchies for managing scarcity that comes from density, rules for navigation of proximity, the elaborate protocols of societies where individuals cannot avoid each other — these are absent here or vestigial, residues of a language borrowed from elsewhere and not fully put down even when it stopped being necessary. The people here know each other completely, which is what happens when few people share a long time, and complete knowledge of the others in your community is different from the partial knowledge that is social currency in larger groups.

Their bodies reflect altitude and cold in the ways bodies do: particular distributions of circulation, particular adaptations to thin air that are partly genetic (maintained over the generations of life here, selection being patient and the time long enough to work) and partly developmental (the body of someone who grew up at this altitude differs from the body of someone who came here later, in ways that are set by childhood and persist). They are not a different human; they are a long local version of a human, the way the moss is a long local version of moss. The difference is visible in the way they breathe, which is slower than you expect when you first meet them — not labored, just economical, the breath of organisms that have sorted out how much air they need and take that much and no more.

They are quiet in the ways the description establishes, but the quietness is not a cultural performance; it is not a tradition they have inherited about the value of silence. It is that they grew up in a quiet place and internalized that quiet as the baseline and modulate around it. They speak when speaking adds something to the silence. They do not speak when speaking would only displace silence without adding anything. This is not wisdom, exactly — it is calibration. Living inside something long enough that its register becomes yours.

What they know is mostly local and has no value elsewhere, which is not a poverty. They know the stone — which parts of which outcrops crack cleanly along which axes, what the color and grain of a stone tells you about its behavior when fitted with other stones, where the best building material is and how to carry it to where it's needed without breaking it. They know the moss in a practical sense: which areas to avoid disturbing, which areas produce the most useful fiber, what the state of the moss tells you about the water beneath it, what it means when the moss in a particular crack has dried out unexpectedly. They know the water: the rhythms of the cloud system overhead, which basins fill fastest, which dry fastest, how to read the condensation in the hour before dawn as an indicator of the coming day's weather.

They know the weirdness. They know it the way you know a neighbor who has been eccentric for so long that their eccentricity no longer surprises you — not understood, not systematized, but familiar enough to live with and even, on some days, to find useful. They know that the person who goes to stand near the ambiguous outcrops needs to have a particular quality of attention to make the practice work — not any quality, not casual presence, but something focused in a specific direction that is not exactly concentration and not exactly calm but is the particular condition that comes from having lived here long enough to know what you're doing when you're doing this, which is: offering yourself as a point of stability in a place where stability is not guaranteed. The plateau accepts this, or the plateau uses it, or the plateau and the person reach a temporary mutual condition through it — the mechanism is genuinely unclear, but the outcome is apparently reproducible, which is what any technology needs to be.

There is a child, specifically, learning the path. She has not been told what the path is for — not because it's secret but because explaining it would require a framework she hasn't finished building yet, and the people of this plateau have learned over generations that the path teaches itself to a body that walks it long enough. So she walks it, beside her parent or her elder or whoever is nearby when the walk happens, and her feet find the slight depressions that a thousand years of walking have worn into the stone, and her body does what bodies do when given a groove that fits them: it relaxes into it, it calibrates, it begins to learn a path below the level of decision. She does not know yet that the surrounding space is less reliable than the path. She knows only that the path feels right underfoot, which is the first knowledge, and the knowledge on which all later knowledge of this place will be built.

When she is older and the spatial fluctuations are explained to her — the distances that don't fix, the shadows that angle wrong — she will experience no surprise, only recognition: the framework she has been building will absorb these facts and they will fit. The plateau has been educating her through the soles of her feet before the education she can name has begun.

## What Moves Through Without Staying

There are birds, occasionally. At this elevation and in these conditions, birds are passage rather than resident — something large enough to carry its warmth with it and navigate in three dimensions finds the plateau useful as a landmark, a reference point, a waystation in a longer journey. They land. They find the water basins and drink. The people of the settlement, who do not have many large animals in their life, watch the birds when they come with an attention that is not quite reverence and not quite scientific interest but sits between them.

The birds carry things. Spores, seeds if there are seeds, parasites from other places, information about the state of the lower elevations encoded in their feathers and their fat reserves and their general condition. The plateau incorporates what they bring, slowly, through the vector of what they eat and what they deposit and what they lose in the wind when they shake out their feathers before departing. 

Some species return cyclically, on schedules tied to movements this plateau cannot see — the state of resources at the far end of a migration route, thousands of kilometers away, determining whether this plateau will have visitors this year or will go another season without. The people have learned these rhythms imperfectly, which is to say: they know the birds come in certain seasons and that certain years they don't come and that the absence tells them something about the world below, though not specifically what. The birds are messengers in this way, but in a language that can only be interpreted as good news or bad news, abundance or scarcity, connected or cut off, without further resolution.

What else moves through: wind carries anything the wind can carry, which is more than you might expect. Pollen from plants far below, from different climates, arrives on the plateau and finds no purchase — the wrong altitude, the wrong soil, the wrong associates — and settles into the moss and decomposes and is gone. Spores of things from other elevation zones, fungal and vegetable, arrive and mostly fail, because the plateau is not hospitable to most arrivals, and the inhabitants are too locally adapted to give ground to competitors. But very occasionally something arrives that finds a micro-habitat — a sheltered spot, a slightly warmer rock face, a particularly rich deposit of old moss-soil — and establishes itself, adding one new thread to the community, which is how the community arrived at what it is over the long time it has had to accumulate.

The wind itself moves through but belongs here, or here belongs to it; its ownership of the plateau's sonic environment has gone on so long that the structures of the settlement are built in response to the wind's logic, the sound of the settlement partly the sound of wind engaging with the things built to manage it.

## What Remains Most Itself

The plateau has imposed its character on everything that lives here. The moss is the moss of the plateau, not moss that could easily be transplanted elsewhere and remain the same moss. The people are the people of this altitude and this stone and this particular spatial unreliability, and they would be different people somewhere else, and somewhere else they would eventually belong to that somewhere else the way they belong to this here. The stone-endolithic organisms could survive nowhere else — they have become so specific to this rock chemistry that they are not even themselves in a different geology. Everything here is local in the deepest sense: constituted by the place rather than merely residing in it.

And the place has been constituted by them in return, though the exchange is less visible in stone than in organisms. The moss has changed the stone surface chemistry. The lichen has etched patterns that now direct water flow in ways that influence where the moss can grow. The people's paths have altered the micro-drainage in ways that have changed which cracks fill first and how long the water stays. Everything here is in a relationship that is so long it has become mutual in the way that very long relationships become mutual — each party bearing the imprint of the other's long contact, each partly *made of* the history of their interaction.

This is what the plateau has produced, in ten million years of settling: not wildness, which is a quality of places still in negotiation, still becoming, still full of the energy of unresolved dynamics — but something that wildness eventually becomes when it has enough time. A community of the deeply arrived. Each part knowing its place not as a constraint but as a fact about itself, the way you stop thinking of your body as a choice and experience it simply as what you are.

## The Register

They live — all of them, moss and mite and lichen and springtail and the child learning the path and the elder standing near the uncertain outcrop — in a register that is different from the register of living somewhere younger.

The register is: *we have been here.* Past perfect continuous. The action completed and ongoing simultaneously. The being-here so established that it is no longer an act of being-here but simply a state, the way water in a basin is not the act of filling but the condition of fullness.

What this produces, in those capable of experiencing a register consciously — the people, primarily, though perhaps there is something in the moss that is more than metaphorically analogous — is an unusual relationship to time. Not indifference to it. Not the erasure of past and future. Something subtler: a priority of *now* that is not about urgency but about depth. The now is very deep here. The current moment has enormous amounts of previous moment beneath it, and the people live in that depth the way divers live in depth — aware of the column of water above them, aware that the surface exists, but primarily engaged with where they are, which is far down, where the pressure is constant and the light is a different thing than it is above.

This makes them — and this word is borrowed and imperfect — *patient*, in the way that patience means the capacity to exist within time without demanding that time hurry. They wait out the spatial fluctuations. They wait out the seasons. They wait out the cycles of the cloud system. They wait out the long silences between when the elder stands near the outcrop and when the geometry settles. Not passive — they are building, lashing, fitting stone, learning paths, tending to the water basins, maintaining the structures, doing the daily work of continued existence — but doing all of it without the low-level insistence on resolution that characterizes people in faster places.

The plateau has produced in them the same quality it has in itself: they have become. Not finished, because nothing living finishes — the child is becoming. But *become*, in the sense that the becoming is so well established that it no longer reads as process. They are what this place makes, living in the place that made them, contributing to the making of the place, generation over generation, the cycle so long it stopped being a cycle and became a condition.

## Coda: The Thing That Watches

There is something that the description gestures at and does not name, and which needs to not quite be named here either, because naming it would be wrong in the way that any sharp line drawn through the plateau's ambiguity would be wrong.

The plateau itself — not the stone, not the organisms, not the people, not any single element, but the whole configuration in its settled condition — seems to be in some state that a word like *awareness* fails to describe and a dismissal of awareness also fails to describe. The water reflecting the slightly-wrong sky. The shadows at the wrong angle. The subterranean sound of enormous surfaces recognizing each other. The spatial fluctuations that respond, possibly, to human presence. These are not the behaviors of a simple geological feature. They are also not, or not necessarily, evidence of an intelligence in any sense that intelligence usually means.

What they may be is the behavior of a system that has been organizing itself for long enough that the organization has developed emergent properties that complexity generates when given sufficient time. What they may be is the way that age, in a place, accumulates until the place is doing something that places don't usually do — something not supernatural but *superdurational*, a consequence not of magic but of time beyond the scale that our models were built to address.

The things that live here live inside this, whatever it is. They are not afraid of it. The moss is not afraid — it is the moss, and the moss does not experience the plateau's ambiguity as ambiguity. The people are not afraid — they have a vocabulary, they have the paths, they have the practice of standing near outcrops, they have calibrated themselves to this as to everything else here. The child learning the path feels the rightness of the path under her feet without knowing what wrongness it is the answer to.

And the thing that watches — if it watches, which may not be the right word, which may be too intentional, too directed, when what it does may be more like: *has always been here, and continues to be, and is aware of that continuity in the way that very old things become aware of their own duration without having a self that does the being-aware* — does nothing in particular about them.

It registers them. They register it. Dew forms on the filaments in the hour before dawn. The cloud system completes another circuit. The moss grows by the width of a thought into the next crack in the stone.

This is what lives here: everything that has stayed long enough to become what this place makes, and the place making it still, and the making a love as cold and patient and complete as ash-grey stone, as the specific lushness of moss in a crack, as a child's feet finding the right way without knowing why it's right or what would happen if she strayed from it.

The plateau holds all of this. The plateau has always held all of this. The plateau will hold all of this for as long as the word *always* can be made to stretch, which on this plateau, in this cold, at this age, is further than it can be stretched anywhere else on the world.
