# What Lives in the Canopy Plateau

---

Start with what is hardest to say: most of what lives here does not experience itself as living.

This is not a limitation. It is a form of completion. The mosses, the lichens, the root-mats continuous across half the plateau's breadth — these do not suffer the problem of selfhood. They have no edges they are trying to maintain against the world. They *are* the world, or a portion of it that has not yet found a reason to distinguish itself. The boundary between organism and substrate dissolved here long before anything arrived that could have cared about the distinction. What remains is process: water moving through, carbon cycling, the slow voltage of chemical difference being equalized across root-junction and fungal thread and the breathing pores of leaves too numerous to number. Call this life if you need a word. The plateau does not use the word.

The fungi are perhaps the truest inhabitants, in the sense that they are the most native to what the plateau actually is. They live in the decomposing — in the space between the dead and the living, which on the plateau is not a threshold but a gradient, long and shallow. A fallen trunk takes decades to finish falling, and the fungi are present at every stage, neither hastening nor mourning, simply converting. They fruit briefly, throw spores into air that barely moves, and return their visible portions to themselves. Underground — or rather, undermat, since the ground here is mat — they extend through everything. The mycorrhizal networks of the plateau's trees are so thoroughly interwoven that ecologists elsewhere use the word *network* with some poetry and here would find that the poetry is just description. Sugars move through it. Signals move through it. Whether these signals constitute something worth calling communication depends on definitions the plateau finds uninteresting. The movement happens. The responses happen. The word for it can wait.

Among the plants, if you hold individual trees briefly as individual things, there are those that have been standing so long they have passed through multiple definitions of themselves. The oldest emergents — those whose crowns break the general canopy surface and receive actual sunlight, not the filtered green that the rest of the world runs on — are ancient enough that their heartwood is no longer structurally relevant, has hollowed out and is occupied by other things, and the tree stands on its sapwood and its bark and the column of air in its center where something nests or has nested or might nest. The tree does not appear to notice. It is still extending new growth at its crown, still photosynthesizing at the margin, still making seeds it drops into a space so occupied there is almost no chance of germination — but the chance is not zero, and the tree does not calculate chances. It makes seeds because making seeds is what it does, which is to say: what it is.

The bromeliads deserve a different kind of attention than the trees get.

They are recent things, evolutionarily — recent meaning they arrived after the plateau's forest was already mature, meaning they colonized an already-complete world and managed to find a niche in the only dimension left available: the vertical surfaces. They grow from bark and from the root-systems of other epiphytes and from each other's accumulated detritus, and they have specialized so thoroughly for this vertical, unsoiled existence that they can no longer survive ground-level contact. The soil of the plateau would rot their roots. They need bark, or the mat of dead plant matter that accumulates in branch-forks, or each other. They live in the air while appearing to live on trees. The distinction matters to them if to nothing else.

Their cups — the wells formed by the spiral arrangement of their leaves — are where the plateau keeps its stranger experiments. Each cup is a closed hydrology: water enters from rain and condensation, does not leave except by evaporation and the drinking of the animals that find it. The mineral composition of each cup is different from every other cup, determined by the litter that falls into it, the species of insects that breed in it, whether a bird uses a nearby branch and contributes certain things. Over years, each cup develops its own character. The organisms living in a cup three meters from another cup may be the same species and yet different enough in their tolerances and habits that interbreeding populations, if they met, might find each other only partially intelligible.

The frog in the bromeliad deserves a name it does not have.

Or it has been named by taxonomists, with numbers and Latin precision, but that is not the same as a name in the sense of a name that belongs to the thing rather than to the system that processed it. What it *is*: thumbnail-small, translucent enough that you can see its spine as a darker line through its back, with eyes that are much too large for its body in the way the eyes of things that live in low light are always too large. It was born in the cup. It has never seen ground. It eats the larvae that hatch from eggs laid by insects that came to the cup for water and instead left the next generation. The cup provides, the cup always provides, and the frog has not developed curiosity about what lies beyond the leaf-walls of its bromeliad because nothing in its ancestry was rewarded for curiosity about leaf-walls. Its world is a liter of water. Its world is complete. It sits in stillness that is not patience but the absence of any agitation, which may be what patience is when it has been carried to its end.

---

The insects require a chapter of their own and will not receive one because their diversity exceeds accounting.

What can be said: they are old. Not individually — their individual lives are brief, sometimes absurdly brief, a matter of days or hours in some cases — but their forms are old, their strategies are old, their relationship to the plateau is so thoroughly established that they function as part of it in the same way that rock functions as part of a different landscape. They are not visiting. They are not adapted to the plateau. They are of it, in the sense that the plateau's history and their history are the same history, have been the same history for long enough that the merger is complete.

The beetle whose elytra are the color of lichen: it did not *become* that color through any single step. It became it through millions of steps, each step so small as to be invisible, and the accumulated result is a thing that the eye cannot separate from its background. But more interesting than the camouflage — the ending — is the relationship it implies. The beetle does not hide *on* lichen. It hides *as* lichen. The lichen is not its background. The lichen is its context, its condition, the thing in relation to which it is itself. Remove the beetle from the lichen and you have not a beetle in a different environment. You have a beetle that is wrong — wrong color, wrong texture, wrong set of behavioral responses calibrated to a world that is no longer present. The lichen and the beetle have made each other necessary, and this necessity is so old and complete that to call the beetle a separate organism and the lichen a separate organism is to insist on a distinction the plateau stopped making a long time ago.

There are insects that live nowhere else in the known world. The taxonomy is imperfect because the plateau has been visited rarely and studied in fragments, and the naming keeps finding things that do not fit existing categories, which is not a problem of the plateau but of the categories. The categories were developed elsewhere, for elsewhere, by minds organized around the assumption that there would eventually be a limit to the variation. The plateau did not agree to any limit.

---

The birds are few and should be more, given the food available. The explanation is not predation and not resource scarcity. The explanation, insofar as there is one, is that the plateau does not favor species whose success depends on territory and projection and the claiming of sound-space. The birds that thrive here are the ones who learned — the lineages who learned, over generations — to want less distance between themselves and others of their kind. The ones who signal softly and wait. The ones who stay close to a partner through conditions that make navigation difficult, which is to say: through all conditions.

You find them in pairs. Always in pairs, or apparently always — the sample size is limited and the plateau resists conclusions — navigating the same ten square meters of understory with a shared knowledge of it that looks, in the absence of a better word, like intimacy. Not the intimacy of display, not the charged intimacy of mating season, but the ordinary worn intimacy of two things that have been adjacent for long enough that their presence to each other is ambient rather than acute. One moves along a branch. The other continues doing what it was doing. They are aware of each other the way you are aware of the weight of your own hands — not consciously, not urgently, but completely.

They make one or two sounds and then stop. In the space between sounds — which is most of the time — they move in their small territory with what seems like leisure but is the unhurried efficiency of animals that are not hungry or threatened and have nowhere to be but here.

What they eat: insects, mostly. The plateau provides. What they do with the rest of their time: this is the question. They are not building. They are not displaying. They are not competing or patrolling or engaging in any of the behaviors that wildlife documentation tends to find worth documenting. They are *present*. They are in their space with the fullness of attention that the plateau seems to select for, the slow and total registration of immediate conditions that is what attention becomes when there is never anything distant to attend to.

---

The larger thing.

This requires care because care is owed to uncertainty, and the uncertainty here is real.

There is a large animal on the plateau. It has not been photographed. It has been inferred from the behavior of the smaller animals, who are not afraid of it — or not exactly afraid, not in the way prey animals express fear — but oriented toward it, slightly, always slightly, with the peripheral awareness of the habituated. It is not a threat to them. It is a presence they have grown up around, whose existence is so established that it has been factored into their lives the way weather is factored in, the way the dim green light is factored in: as a condition rather than an event.

What can be said about it is limited to negatives. It makes almost no sound. It does not make paths in the conventional sense — there are no regularly-cleared corridors, no worn trails, and this suggests either that it moves without weight enough to leave marks, which is unlikely given its apparent size, or that it varies its movement enough that no route is repeated frequently enough to become a track, which suggests either randomness or a pattern too large to perceive. The moss closes behind it, as it closes behind everything. The plateau is not a place that keeps evidence of passage.

The animals that sleep near the ground, the ones who would be most vulnerable to a large predator, do not show the vigilance of the preyed-upon. They sleep in the open, or in the open as it exists here, which is to say in the dense tangle of roots and ferns, tucked in spaces that are covered by leaves but not armored. They sleep without rotating sentinels, without alarm systems. They sleep as things sleep when they have not been educated by predation to sleep otherwise.

This tells you something about the large animal. It is not a predator of small animals, or it is such an inefficient predator that it has not established the learned fear that predators typically produce in populations over generations. Or it is something else entirely, something whose relationship to the animals is not easily categorized by the predator-prey schema, which is a schema developed largely for open environments where visibility and speed matter and the plateau has never been either open or quick.

It moves, presumably, at night. The bioluminescent fungi provide a cold light on the fallen logs. The plateau changes quality in the dark but does not become a different place. The amphibians in the bromeliad cups continue their activities — eating, being eaten, the small predation of the small scale, unchanged. Whatever moves through the undermat moves through it in the dark as it moves through it in the day, slowly, with the patience that is the plateau's primary virtue.

Whether it is one creature or a small population is unknown. Whether it is a primate, or a large mustelid, or something the taxonomists would struggle with, is unknown. Whether it has any relationship to the stone arrangements, to the circular depression that holds water, to the trees that grew around an absent thing — this question is not asked aloud in the places where the question would matter because the plateau makes speculation feel like a kind of impertinence.

It is here. That is all that can be said that is certainly true. It is here, and it has been here, and it will continue to be here, and the plateau seems to have made room for it in the same way it makes room for everything: completely, and without record.

---

The organisms that live here share one quality that does not reduce to a biological characteristic.

They have all, in their separate ways and by their separate routes, arrived at the same relationship to the plateau's primary feature, which is its completeness. There is nowhere to expand into. There is nothing unclaimed. The ecological space was full before any of them arrived in their current forms, and they have inherited a world that was already, in every meaningful sense, done. And they live inside the done thing with a quality of presence that looks — from outside, if there were an outside — like a kind of acceptance, though that word implies a decision and there was no decision involved.

The beetle is lichen, is bark, is the plateau's surface doing one of the things the plateau's surface does.

The frog is a cup of water thinking its water-thoughts.

The birds are adjacent to each other in a reduced world and have found the reduced world sufficient.

The large unseen thing moves through a forest it did not choose and was not born into — it was born of, which is a different preposition — and it moves without urgency because urgency requires a destination and the plateau is not a place that accommodates destinations.

---

And then there are the organisms that arrive and then are gone.

Things blow in on wind from the lowlands, if there are lowlands: seeds of species that cannot germinate in the deep shade, insects from other systems that find the food wrong or the humidity wrong or that simply fail to find a mate and die without progeny. The plateau absorbs these. They decompose and become the mat. Their genetic material goes nowhere but they go into the system, they are used. Nothing is lost. Loss is a concept for ecosystems with elsewhere, with outside, with edges that things cross and do not return from. The plateau has edges somewhere, has a rim from which the lowlands begin, but the rim is so far from the center that it might as well not exist. Here at the center, there is no outside for anything to be lost to.

This is the mechanism by which the plateau maintains its quality of completion: not by stasis, which would be death, but by conversion. Everything that enters is converted. Everything that falls is decomposed. Every arrival becomes a resident or becomes food for residents or becomes the mat that the residents walk on. The system is closed in the way that a very slowly breathing thing is closed — exchanging with its surroundings, necessarily, but on its own terms, at its own pace, without being driven or altered.

---

The life of the plateau, taken together, is one life with an enormous number of locally varying expressions.

This is not mysticism. The mycorrhizal networks move carbon between trees based on differential photosynthesis — the shaded tree receives from the lit tree and does not know this, and the lit tree sends without meaning to and does not know this, and the result is a system in which resources are distributed by need across a network that spans the plateau. The atmospheric chemistry above the plateau is shaped by the collective transpiration of the organisms below it, and this atmospheric chemistry determines the moisture available to those organisms, making the organisms partly responsible for their own weather. The animals that eat insects and deposit waste alter the local mineral composition of the mat, and the mat determines what fungi grow, and the fungi determine what trees can access nutrients, and the trees determine what insects thrive, and back.

You could trace any part of this and arrive, eventually, at any other part. Not quickly. The path is long and the connections are indirect and at many points you would need to cross through processes that are so slow they are invisible at human timescales. But the path exists. Every inhabitant of the plateau is continuous with every other inhabitant through chains of dependency and influence that the plateau spent millions of years braiding.

When the large animal breathes out, the carbon becomes the frog's water.

When the beetle dies on the bark, its body becomes the lichen.

The lichen is the beetle.

The water is the frog.

The trees are each other, are the roots, are the mat, are the rot, are the fungi, are the trees.

---

What lives here is the plateau living.

Stand still and become part of the count. Your breath joins the atmospheric cycle. Your warmth is a local anomaly the moss notes. Your footprint holds water and something comes to investigate, not because you are interesting but because the footprint is.

You are not apart from what lives here by virtue of having come from elsewhere. You are incorporated. You are being incorporated. The plateau does not wait for permission.

This is not a threat. It is the plateau's form of welcome, which is: the extension of itself to include one more thing, one more instance of the pattern, one more warm wet metabolizing participant in the great slow exchange.

You are here. You are counted.
