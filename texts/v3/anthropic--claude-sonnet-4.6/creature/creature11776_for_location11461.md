## What Lives in the Cistern

---

The first difficulty is the word *lives*, which comes from the upper world where life announces itself — by speed, by noise, by the bright advertisement of wanting.

What lives here has set those aside. Or they were never offered.

---

Start with what's easiest to count.

**The pale gastropods.** Dozens of them, perhaps hundreds, moving along the submerged walls in arcs so gradual their motion only becomes legible in retrospect — you look away and look back and they are simply elsewhere, the way hour hands are elsewhere. Their shells have been changed by the water. What might once have been buff or brown or banded has become a uniform cream, a white so close to the mineral white of the walls that the gastropods read as protrusions until they move. They have been here long enough that the cistern has recolored them in its own palette. This seems, if you are in the right frame of mind, less like bleaching and more like acceptance — the place has claimed them, has said *you are mine now*, and the shells have said *yes*.

They eat biofilm. The walls are coated in a thin, invisible productivity — bacteria and archaea and things that have no popular names, chemolithotrophs processing the minerals that the water carries in solution. The gastropods graze this film from the stone, methodically, and leave the stone fractionally cleaner behind them, and the film grows back. The cycle takes days or weeks. No one is keeping track.

**The filter feeders along the lower wall.** They are barnacle-adjacent but not barnacles — they have the same general commitment to fixed life, the same calcified housing, but the structures around their feeding apparatus are more elaborate, more delicate, a kind of architectural excess that only stillness and warmth and abundance of time can produce. In the upper world, barnacles live in surge zones and have made themselves accordingly: compact, sealed, armored for violence. These have had no surge to contend with. They have expanded into the available calm. Their cirri emerge and sweep in motions that are almost balletic, *almost*, and then retract, and the retraction takes longer than it should, lingers, like something reluctant to stop touching the thing that feeds it. They eat suspended particles. They eat bacteria. They eat whatever the slow current brings around to them, which is everything, eventually, given enough rotations.

**The clustered soft things.** Here the vocabulary fails more completely. They are whitish, vaguely segmented, colonial in arrangement — six or seven to a cluster, the clusters distributed across the lower walls at irregular intervals like a kind of punctuation. Their bodies do not quite have edges; where one ends and the next begins is a question the colony has apparently decided is not worth resolving. They may be a single organism in the sense that matters. They are slow even by the standards of this place. A cluster will displace itself — will *migrate* — over the course of weeks, following some gradient of temperature or chemistry that you cannot detect. They move toward warmth, perhaps, or toward the mineral-rich seep zones where the calcite formations are most active. You would have to come back many times to verify this. Each visit you would find them somewhere slightly different and have to reconstruct the trajectory. It would take years to understand their movement. This is not a complaint. It is a description of what understanding them would require.

**The bacteria, the archaea, the chemolithotrophs in their films.** Not visible. Present in the way that foundations are present: underneath everything, the actual ground of this ecosystem, processing sulfur or iron or the carbon in the warm water, living and dying in timescales measured in hours or days, generating the faint chemical gradients that everything else is here to follow. They are the oldest form of life on earth doing what it has always done, which is make the impossible chemistry of living work in places that look, from the outside, like they should be inert. The cistern looks inert. It is not. It is saturated with the oldest ambition.

---

These are what you could count, or nearly count, if you had a lamp and a long time and waterproof boots and patience.

But there is something else.

---

Naming it is the difficulty.

It is not separate from the counted things. It is not hiding elsewhere. It is — it seems to be — what happens when warmth and stillness and dark and time work on a closed system long enough. Something that is not any one organism but is also not nothing. A property of the whole. The low pulse in the sternum. The quality of the air that makes you feel you are breathing something that has been breathed before and is not worse for it.

You could call it **the tending** — the aggregate behavior of hundreds of small organisms all doing small things in the same enclosed dark with the same slow water cycling over them. Filtration and grazing and slow migration toward warmth. Nothing communicates deliberately. No signal is sent. But the cumulative effect is a space that is, in some functional sense, *maintained*. The biofilm is grazed but not consumed entirely. The filter feeders take suspended material but the current replaces it. The soft clustered things move toward richness and in moving redistribute. The whole thing has arrived at a balance that persists, and the persisting is its own kind of act, its own ongoing work, which requires no brain and no intention and which has been going on, in some form, since before the niches were carved, before the hands placed the lamps, before anyone thought to roof the water.

Maybe this is what *lives* has been trying to say.

Not: there are organisms here.

But: the cistern is alive in the way that a system is alive, in the way that balance is a kind of doing, in the way that patience, held long enough, becomes something you could almost call decision.

---

Or — offered as an alternative, not a correction —

The calcified figure in the niche. The lamp-shape that has been consumed by mineral deposit and is now indistinguishable from the wall except in outline.

What if that is also a resident?

Not metaphorically. Not as a conceit. But in the sense that the cistern has taken it in, has worked on it for centuries, has made it continuous with itself. The person who placed it is gone. The votive is *not gone*. It has been transformed, incorporated, made into a different kind of present. The intention that moved the hands that placed it — the wanting-to-mark, the wanting-to-remember — that intention has been processed by the cistern the way the water processes minerals: taken in, taken apart, precipitated back out as something structural, something that holds the walls together now, something that will outlast by orders of magnitude the need it was originally meant to answer.

Everything that enters here eventually lives here, on the cistern's terms.

---

So what lives in the cistern?

---

Slow white shells tracking their long arcs.

Cirri opening and reluctantly closing.

Colonial bodies that have stopped negotiating their edges.

The oldest chemistry, working.

The memory of a lamp, made stone.

The balance that maintenance requires, maintained.

The pulse.

The warmth that has reached equilibrium with its container and now simply *is*, the way temperature is, the way enclosure is, the way the dark is — not an absence of something but a presence that has been here so long it has stopped requiring a name.
