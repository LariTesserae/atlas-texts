# What Lives Here

---

Begin with what cannot be named singly.

The life of this place is not a collection of organisms. It is a single distributed argument about how to persist — made in ten thousand simultaneous voices, none of them the loudest, none of them the point. What lives here is the totality, and the totality is the thing, and any individual creature pulled from it for examination would be a word extracted from a sentence — still a word, still itself, but missing the grammar that made it mean anything.

Still: examine. Slowly.

---

**The Fungi**

They are the oldest argument and the most foundational. They are in the soil and under the bark and through the root-wood and between the root-tips and in the dead leaves at every stage of their softening. They are the reason nothing in this basin is merely dead — everything dead is already in transition, already being rewritten into something else, and the fungi are the rewriters, the patient translators working the long seam between what was alive and what will be. They have no agenda beyond continuance. They do not experience the fallen tree as a tragedy or a resource; they experience it as a door opened.

Some of them fruit — produce the visible structures — and these appear on the undersides of logs and bracket out from bark in shelved formations: bone-white, rust-orange, a deep liver-red that looks almost warm. They are not warm. They are cool to the touch, even in this heat, drawing coolness from the wood they inhabit. Some of these fruiting bodies are the size of a hand. Some are as large as a head. Beetles have excavated tunnels through them and laid eggs in the tunnels. Slugs have taken long slow meals from their edges. They do not resent this; resentment requires an elsewhere to be resentful from, and they have no elsewhere.

The mycorrhizal networks are invisible and they are everywhere and they connect everything that has roots to everything else that has roots within a certain lateral distance, and that distance encompasses most of what you see. The trees are not talking through these networks, in the sense of using language, but they are transacting — passing resources through the connections, responding to resource signals with resource movements — and this transacting has been going on without interruption for longer than the current trees have been alive. What the fungi mediate is not conversation. It is something prior to conversation: a shared economy so deep it preceded the participants who currently run it.

---

**The Decomposers in Their Hierarchy**

Below the fungi in scale, below the insects in apparent complexity, but not below anything in importance: the bacteria, the mites, the nematodes, the springtails. They exist in numbers that make counting a category error. A handful of the leaf litter holds more individual organisms than the visible fauna of many forests.

The springtails are visible, barely, if you look at the underside of a log you have just turned over: tiny pale shapes, each perhaps a millimeter, erupting in every direction at once — not fleeing, exactly, but redistributing, the whole small community making a fast adjustment to the sudden light. They are hexapods, springloaded, propelled by their tails. In this basin they are among the primary processors of dead plant matter, and they have been here in forms essentially like this one for four hundred million years. Their ancestors were among the first animals to move on land. They have found their niche and held it with a fidelity that makes the whole apparatus of evolutionary drama seem like it was someone else's story.

The mites are smaller and they are everywhere there is moisture and decomposing matter, which is everywhere. They are predators and decomposers and fungal-feeders and each other's prey. They do not live long, individually. The population is continuous. There is always a mite. There has always been a mite. The individual mite is beside the point; the mite-ness of the place is structural.

**The Ants**

You have already seen the leafcutters. They deserve more than a column's-worth of attention.

The leafcutter colony is not a collection of individual animals in any sense that a solitary mammal would recognize. The colony is the animal. The individual workers are more like cells than like organisms — specialized, replaceable, incapable of most of life's functions when separated from the whole, carrying no meaningful independent existence. What thinks in the leafcutter colony is the colony. What decides is the colony. What has survived ice ages and continental drift and whatever catastrophes came through this basin is the colony-form, the behavioral and biological architecture, replicated forward through time with modifications so gradual they amount to no story at all.

The fungus they are farming — inside their underground chambers, fed on the macerated fragments of leaves they cut and carry — is itself a species that exists nowhere outside of leafcutter colonies. It cannot reproduce without them. They cannot eat without it. They have been in this relationship for fifty million years, which means for fifty million years there has been no leafcutter that has known anything other than this arrangement, and no cultivated fungus that has existed in any other context. They are so entwined that the boundary between them is philosophical rather than biological. Together they are a single super-organism; apart, they are each incomplete halves of something that doesn't exist.

Other ant species live here too — army ants that move as a single fluid event through the leaf litter, taking everything mobile in their path; tiny acrobatic ants in the canopy farming aphids for their honeydew; trap-jaw ants on the ground whose mandibles close faster than the human eye can follow. The basin has room for all of them because the basin has not been simplified. Complexity accommodates complexity. Each ant species creates conditions that other species exploit, and those species create conditions for others, and the pyramid has too many sides to be called a pyramid.

**The Beetles**

There are more species of beetle in this basin than of any other order of animal. This is true everywhere on earth, and the hypothesis that God had an inordinate fondness for beetles is old and probably true and still does not fully explain it.

Here they occupy every stratum and every substrate. The rhinoceros beetles are the size of a fist and their larvae live inside the rotting cores of trees and will not emerge as adults for two years. The rove beetles are thin as a fingernail and they sprint across the soil surface, hunting other insects, their abbreviated wing-covers leaving most of their abdomen exposed and vulnerable-looking though they are not. The longhorn beetles have antennae longer than their bodies and they are doing something specific and important with those antennae — detecting the chemical signatures of particular tree species, locating mates across distances, navigating a landscape of odors too complex to visualize — but from a human perspective they appear simply to be carrying their extravagance around.

The click beetles startle when touched and produce a loud sharp click and fling themselves into the air, a defense that works through surprise rather than harm. The fireflies — there are fireflies, you will see them at the dark end of any night — carry light in their abdomens, cold light, biologically generated, patterned in species-specific sequences, the males broadcasting from flight and the females answering from the vegetation, a conversation carried on in light pulses in the darkness under the canopy. They have been having this conversation for, by some estimates, a hundred million years. The specific signal-patterns vary by species. What is being communicated is: *I am here, I am this kind, I am ready.* What is heard, by a female who is the right kind, is precisely that.

**The Moths**

The moths you noticed — wings pressed to bark, wing-patterns echoing bark — are resting in an evolutionary solution so elegant it becomes impossible to see. The solution is: *look like where you are.* This is not a strategy the moth chose. It is the accumulated result of every generation of moth that was slightly better at resembling bark surviving slightly longer to lay slightly more eggs, across time measured in geological units, until the match between moth and bark is so exact that the moth is, in some sense, a specialized form of bark — bark with wings, bark with eyes, bark that can detect the echolocation pulses of bats and fold itself into a dive before the bat can complete its targeting calculation.

The moths that fly are mostly flying toward light — toward the canopy gaps, toward the moon when it is visible, toward anything that promises orientation. The moths that rest are mostly waiting for darkness to become complete enough that flight is safe. In the meantime they are portraits of stillness. They are not asleep, exactly; their nervous systems remain active, monitoring vibration and chemical signals and the approach of anything larger than themselves. But they are not engaged with the world in any way that looks like engagement. They look like part of the tree.

The hawk-moths hover. This is worth saying directly: they hover at flowers in the way hummingbirds hover, and they visit flowers that open only at night, flowers with long tubes that no other tongue can reach, flowers that have been waiting all day for exactly this visitor. The relationship between the long-tongued hawk-moth and the deep-tubed night-flower is another of these long-term transactions that has become so specific that each party has made itself into a key that only fits one lock. The moth is the key. The flower is the lock. The flower is also the key. The moth is also the lock.

---

**The Frogs**

Near water — and there is water somewhere, following the invisible channels in the root-bound soil, collecting in the axils of bromeliads fifty feet up, pooling briefly after rain in the depressions left by treefalls — the frogs have made themselves available to every possible niche.

Ground frogs move through the leaf litter with a small furtive hop, brown on brown, locating insects by their vibration signatures, calling on rain-nights in voices that range from a mechanical click to a sound like someone striking a ceramic bowl. Tree frogs press adhesive toe-pads against smooth bark and glass-green leaves, their green matching the green of the under-canopy in a way that is not decorative but structural — they are green because the forest selected for green across generations uncountable. Their eggs are laid in the water-cups of bromeliads, and the tadpoles develop there, and the mother sometimes returns to deposit unfertilized eggs as food for the growing tadpoles, though she has never been taught this and there is no way to explain what she is doing in terms she would recognize.

Poison dart frogs in some versions of this place carry their toxicity like a declaration. They are the opposite of the moth pressed to bark: they are visible, they are obvious, they are colored with the specific brightness that means *do not.* They learned this — the population learned this, across millions of years — from birds that died after eating them and failed to pass on the behavior. They are advertising with the deaths of ancient birds. The color is a record.

Some frogs in this basin have not yet been identified by anyone who studies frogs. This is not unusual. The estimates of species awaiting formal description in primary forest like this run into the tens of thousands across taxonomic groups. They are living their full frog lives without names. The naming would not change them.

**The Snakes**

The snakes are here and you will mostly not see them because seeing is not how snakes are primarily experienced in a place like this, and because they are very good at the same project the moths are good at: being present without registering as present.

Some of them are in the canopy, hunting birds and eggs and sleeping lizards, their bodies draped across branches in configurations that suggest rope more than life. They can remain in one position for days. They do not require the continuous metabolism that you require; they have no maintenance cost comparable to yours. They are waiting, and waiting is the thing they are best at, and the waiting is not an interruption of their life but the primary texture of it.

The pit vipers have heat-sensing organs in their faces — literal pits, infrared receptors, detecting temperature differential to a fraction of a degree — and so they hunt in complete darkness, navigating a world of heat-signatures, the small mammal in the leaf litter registering as a warmth-shape moving through coolness. They do not miss. When they do not miss, the prey is not immediately dead; it moves away and dies in the leaf litter some meters distant, and the snake follows the trail of scent left by the prey's blood, and finds it, and takes the time it takes. There is no hurry. There is never any hurry.

There are also the harmless snakes — and this is a designation that only applies to you, as the harmless snakes are not harmless to the things they eat. They eat slugs and earthworms and eggs and small lizards, and they move through the leaf litter with a speed that is startling the first time and then begins to seem inevitable, a shape that the underworld of vegetation produces naturally, the logical movement-form for close quarters and rootwork and fallen log interiors.

---

**The Mammals**

They are here and they are the least comprehensible things here, not because they are alien but because they are kin, and the kinship makes their arrangement unsettling in a way that pure difference would not.

The small ones first: rodents in the root spaces, building nests from the soft inner bark they strip and the leaf fragments they find and whatever other softness the forest makes available. They have been in this basin in forms approximately like this for longer than the large mammals have been here. They are prey and they know it in the way that knowing is built into the body — the instant freeze when a shadow crosses, the trembling stillness before the bolt into deeper cover. They are seed-dispersers, unintentionally: they cache seeds against future scarcity and forget some, and the forgotten seeds become trees, and some of the trees now standing in this basin were planted by a rodent's imperfect memory. The rodent does not know it is a gardener. It is a gardener.

The larger forms move at night primarily and in the early morning, and the few that are diurnal move with a quality of continuous attention that the smaller animals do not need — a larger body displaces more air, makes more sound, is visible at greater distance, and must be accordingly more alert. The peccaries, if there are peccaries, move in groups, and a group of peccaries has a group's awareness: each member monitoring a different sector, the collective processing more sensory information than any individual could. They root through the leaf litter with their disc-shaped snouts, finding tubers and fallen fruit and whatever the soil has hidden. They smell rank and strong, a musk designed to maintain group cohesion in the dark, each animal continuously announcing its position to the others through scent. They move like a committee with good chemistry.

There may be a large cat. There is the right amount of prey. There are tracks in the rare soft mud near whatever water there is — large, round, the claw marks retracted the way cat tracks are. But the cat has not been seen, and the cat, in this place, will not be seen unless the cat decides otherwise. The cat's existence here is a structural feature of the place: it prevents certain population explosions, maintains the balance of pressure on certain prey species, creates a cautiousness in the mid-sized mammals that keeps them alert and moving and therefore spreads their effects more widely through the forest. The cat does not know it is maintaining a system. It is hungry, intermittently. Everything else follows from that.

**The Trees Themselves**

The trees are alive in the way that requires a revision of what alive means.

They are slow. Their decisions — which direction to grow, where to invest resources, when to fruit, when to abort seeds in a drought year, how to respond to fungal attack — are made in biochemical time, the time of gradual chemical gradient and slow cellular response. But they are decisions. The tree is not indifferent to its situation. It responds to light with growth, to wounding with chemical defense, to drought with stomatal closure, to the presence of connected kin-trees through the mycorrhizal networks with a resource-sharing that has been called generosity and is at least structurally analogous to it.

The old trees here — the ones whose establishment predates most human technologies by centuries — have survived things. You can read some of what they survived in their trunks: the lean from a long-fallen neighbor, corrected gradually over decades; the scar from a fire that didn't kill them but left its evidence in a band of compressed, dense wood now buried under later growth; the place where a limb broke, twenty years ago or forty, and the bark has rolled over the wound in thick lips, almost healed. They have been hit by lightning and by falling trees and by whatever large animal repeatedly rubbed its flanks against this particular trunk for years, leaving a smooth worn place. They have been here for all of the human time this place has accumulated, which is none, which is geological, which is the relevant timescale.

They are growing right now. Imperceptibly, but measurably if you came back in five years with calipers. The growth is outward and upward and downward simultaneously — new cells at the cambium just under the bark, new root-tips pressing through soil-particles, new canopy growth reaching for the small fraction of light that penetrated this far. They will grow until they fall, and when they fall they will become the soil from which the next generation grows, and they know this in no sense, and it is nevertheless exactly what they are doing.

---

**What the Place Knows**

This is the hardest thing to say and probably the most true.

The place is not conscious. There is no central awareness here, no ghost of the forest with opinions about what it is. But the place has something that functions like knowledge: encoded in the chemical relations between species, in the structural adaptations that fit organisms to particular niches, in the mycorrhizal transactions between trees, in the pheromone gradients of ant colonies, in the species-specific wing-patterns of moths that match specific bark types, in the tight-wound mutual dependency of hawk-moth and night-flower. All of this is information. All of this is a record of what has happened here and what worked and what didn't, and it is stored not in any single mind but in the population-wide patterns of ten thousand species, each of them a library of survival decisions made over millions of years.

This is what lives here: the accumulated answer to the question *how do you persist in a place like this?* posed continuously across deep time, answered continuously by the organisms that survived to keep answering it, revised continuously as conditions shifted and the question changed.

It does not require your presence to continue being answered.

It has been answering it longer than your kind has been asking questions.

The green dark extends. The heat is permanent. Something drips at irregular intervals in the middle distance.

It will be doing this tomorrow.
