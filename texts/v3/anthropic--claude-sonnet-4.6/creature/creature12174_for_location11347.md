# What Lives Here

---

Begin with the bats, because the bats are the pulse.

Several hundred of them, pressed into the overhang's ceiling with the density of something poured rather than something that arrived individually. They are the warmth. Without them the ledge would be a colder place, more purely geological, a place where cold-blooded patience would reign without interruption. With them it is something else: a place that breathes. The colony breathes as a unit, its collective respiration adding to the warm layer, the radiating layer, the layer closest to the stone. Each bat is small — the kind of small that becomes significant only in multiples — and their individual lives are enormous: thousands of miles of night-flight accumulated across the years, a three-dimensional map of every valley and ridge face within twenty kilometers held in the architecture of a brain the size of a grape. They are not simple. They are not sleeping. Rest, for a bat, is a state of reduced processing, not stopped processing — the same mind that navigates at fifty kilometers per hour in total darkness is still present, still running its models, still receiving and interpreting the faint ultrasonic returns of a world the bat builds new each night from its own emitted voice.

The colony has old animals in it. This is easy to overlook. One of the bats pressed into the overhang has been alive for eighteen years — an outlier, but not impossibly so — and has roosted in this specific overhang for the last fourteen of them, returning each year having completed a migration route of which this ledge is the hinge point, the still center. It weighs eleven grams. It has outlived many things. It has survived encounters that left marks. One wing membrane bears a healed tear, repaired by scar tissue in a long diagonal that catches the lichen-glow when the wing opens, a seam in the living leather of it. This bat knows the ledge the way you know your own bedroom in the dark: not as a learned fact but as a bodily knowledge, a navigational certainty that does not require attention. It is roosted near the center of the mass and has not moved in three hours and will not move for four more.

The colony has young animals in it too. Born this summer, still learning the integration of echo into image, still navigating at the margins of their range. They roost at the colony's edges, not through deference but through the social physics of warm bodies: the center is hotter, the center is more contested, the center requires a kind of membership the young ones are still acquiring. At the edges they feel both the colony's warmth, secondhand, and the cold air's approach, firsthand. This is their education. The cold air is the problem the colony exists to solve.

---

The spiders are a different kind of mind.

Not social. Not warm. Not capable of migration or accumulated memory in the way the bats are — each spider is an island, aware of the web and what the web transmits and very little else. But do not mistake the poverty of their inner life for simplicity of their situation. The webs here are structures of extreme sophistication, and extreme sophistication requires engineering, and engineering requires something, even if that something is not thought.

A spider at the center of a web is doing this: waiting, which is the active occupation of stillness, a directed readiness that has been refined over four hundred million years into the purest form of readiness available to an organism. Every vibration in the web is language to her. She reads the size of a trapped thing by the frequency of its struggle, the urgency of its struggle by the amplitude, the location of it by comparing the tensions arriving through each of her eight legs simultaneously — a triangulation so fast it does not rise to the level of decision, it simply happens in the body, the way your hand catches a falling cup before your mind has registered that the cup is falling.

The webs here are old infrastructure. Layers of seasons on top of each other. The oldest webbing, deepest in the structure, has long since ceased to be sticky — it is framework now, not trap, the skeleton of something whose original function has been transcended by time into pure structure. The newest silk is on the outside, fresh from this week, tactically placed where the thermal draft delivers moths in the dark hours. The spider did not plan this. She placed silk where her instinct directed her to place silk, and her instinct was right, because the instinct is the accumulated rightness of every ancestor who placed silk in similar places and caught enough moths to survive and reproduce. The instinct is older than the ledge.

She has been here for three years, this particular spider. She is large for her kind. She has survived three winters on this ledge, which requires, in the cold months, a slowing-down that approaches stillness so completely that it is hard to distinguish from death — metabolism reduced to a maintenance murmur, the body's chemistry thickened against freezing, everything in suspension except the faint readiness in the legs. And then the warm comes back. And then the moths come. And she is precisely what she was, continuing.

---

The isopods on the wall are community in its oldest sense.

They eat the lichen-crust. They move across it with their slow authority, their many legs carrying them over the surface in a rolling gait that looks mechanical because it essentially is mechanical, a locomotion optimized for this specific surface, this specific gradient. They have compound eyes that function in very low light — the lichen's own glow is sufficient for them, provides everything they need in terms of navigation. They know this wall the way the wall knows water: through contact over time.

What the isopods have, beyond their wall and their crust, is each other — not in any warm or intentional sense, but in the way that a population has itself. The density of them on this surface is the result of many generations of surviving here, and surviving here means finding the crevices on cold nights, means finding each other by chemical trace in the dark, means the population contracting and expanding with the seasons in a slow breathing that is visible only if you watch across months. Right now they are many. In the deep winter they will be fewer. The ones remaining will be the ones who found the deepest crevices, who conserved the most, who happened to be positioned optimally when the cold came down. This is not survival of the fittest in any dramatic sense. It is survival of the well-positioned. It is mostly luck, expressed slowly.

---

In the cups of water there is something that resists quick description.

The flatworms first: thin, and pale, and moving with the flowing motion of things that have solved locomotion by becoming motion itself, the whole body a traveling wave, no part of it still while the animal is moving. They eat the film of bacteria and algae that grows on the cup floor — a film so thin it is not visible except as a slight difference in the stone's color, a slight biological darkening of the mineral surface. The flatworms find it by gliding. They glide continuously, and the gliding is feeding, and the feeding is also searching, because the film is not uniform, it is richer in some places, and the flatworm's ventral surface is tasting all of it as it moves, and adjusting, and tasting again. A flatworm has no brain in any concentrated sense. What it has instead is body, fully inhabited, every part of it processing at once.

The larvae in the deeper cups — the reddish coiled things breathing through gills — are waiting. Their waiting has a different quality from the spider's waiting: it is not directed toward anything in particular, not primed for a vibration or a signal, it is simply the waiting of an animal that is not yet what it will be. These are the middle stage of something. Before this they were eggs in a surface water that doesn't exist anymore, dried to nothing in the summer warmth, and the eggs waited as eggs for the cups to fill again. Now they are larvae. Later — next spring, or the spring after, if the winter doesn't finish them — they will be adults, with wings, capable of rising from the cup and entering the air column above the ledge and navigating by whatever means their kind navigates and finding other water and completing the cycle. But now they are here, coiled in an inch of still water, and here is their entire world, and the world is enough.

---

Now: the thing the cups suggest.

Not the flatworms, not the larvae — something prior. Something that used the cups not as habitat but as tool. Nothing made sits on this ledge; that is established. But the cups themselves, with their not-quite-natural spacing, their too-consistent diameter, their inexplicable stillness in the wind — these are the trace of a using that left no artifact. Only geometry.

What would use cups of water, equally spaced along a ledge, for a long time, with enough regularity and enough force of habit to impress the habit onto stone?

You would need to be here often. Nightly, perhaps — the condensate refills nightly, and whatever the cups held, the cups hold it most fully at night, at the point of maximum fill. You would need to value what the cups offered: water, the small lives in the water, perhaps the particular quality of condensate-water which is distilled, clean, minerally bare in a way spring water isn't. You would need to be small enough that cups an inch or two deep were meaningful vessels, not trivial puddles.

You would need to be here for a very long time. Long enough that your returning, your landing in the same places, your settling weight in the same stone positions, wore the stone. Wore it evenly. Wore it in a pattern.

Whatever this was, it was not random in its visiting. It came to each cup. It had a route.

The route is still there, worn into the ledge floor as a subtle path of slightly smoother stone between the cups, stone worked to a polish that is different from the surrounding surface — not by water, not by wind, but by contact. The contact of something that moved in a consistent direction, cup to cup, for long enough that consistent direction became geological record. It is not dramatic. It requires knowing what you're looking at. It is unmistakable if you know what you're looking at.

Whatever made this path along the cups is not here now.

That is the primary fact about it: its absence. The path exists as an artifact of presence converted entirely to absence. The presence was long — longer than the bat colony, longer than any individual spider's tenure, possibly longer than the lichen-crust's current configuration, which is itself centuries old. The presence is not recoverable from what it left. You have the cups. You have the path between the cups. You have the ledge, which was in some sense shaped around this presence the way the surrounding darkness is shaped around the absence of light — the presence being the original condition, and everything that lives here now being the visitor.

The bats don't know this. The spiders don't know this. The isopods on the wall have walked over the path a thousand times without registering it. The flatworms glide through the cups where the other thing drank.

What would know is the rock, and the rock does not speak. The rock has simply recorded. It will keep the record long after the bats move on, long after the lichen-colony's blue-green light shifts to some new frequency or goes dark, long after all the small urgent lives here have cycled through themselves into new configurations. The cups will remain. The path between the cups will remain, a smoother ribbon in the stone, slightly warmer to the touch than the surrounding surface, as though something of the heat of the long-gone thing has been absorbed and held.

---

What lives here, then:

The colony overhead, which is warmth and navigation and the long memory of wings, which is hundreds of small selves pressed into one thermal body.

The spiders, which are patience refined to its logical extreme, waiting in structures that have outlasted the spiders that made them.

The isopods, which are many and slow and tasting the wall that feeds them, which are community without intention, which are old in the way that body-plans are old.

The larvae in their cups, which are becoming, which are not yet.

The flatworms, which are motion that is also hunger that is also the whole of their life, expressed at once, without remainder.

All of them living in a place that was arranged for something else, by something else, over a time so long that the arrangement has become geology and the geology has become habitat and the habitat has become full of lives that have no idea they are living in a made place.

Full of lives that have no idea something was here first.

You are the second thing here that doesn't know what it's doing in this place.

The first thing isn't here anymore.

But its path is in the stone, cup to cup, worn smooth by returning.
