# What Lives in the Drowned Peaks

---

Start with what is obvious, and follow it down.

The things that live here are the ones that stayed when leaving was an option. This is not a small fact. Every organism on every moss-backed summit has made the calculation — consciously in no case, evolutionarily in all of them — that this is worth it, that the return justifies the terms, that the cold and the wind and the perpetual salt-damp and the isolation and the noise are the price of something. You cannot understand what lives here without understanding that this is a population of committed things. Fanatical, almost, in their attachment to a set of conditions that most life would classify as punishment.

But punishment implies a better alternative that is being withheld. Nothing here experiences it that way. These are creatures for whom this is not a compromise but the exact right arrangement. The wind is correct. The cold is correct. The salt in everything is correct. They have been selected, generation by generation, for the capacity to find this correct, and any individual that found it wrong is genetically gone, has been gone for centuries or millennia, and what remains is a community of organisms in deep, wordless agreement with the place they inhabit.

This changes how you look at them.

---

The birds first, because they are inescapable.

The seabirds working the channels between summits are loud and legible, their lives conducted at a scale that is easy to read from a human vantage. They wheel, they dive, they dispute the same ledge with the same neighbor they have disputed it with for seasons. Their argument is with each other, not with the place. The place is settled. They nested on these ledges when there was more rock above the water and they will nest here when there is less. Their abundance is not casual — it is the abundance of something that has found its specific slot in the machinery of this ecosystem and filled it completely. The channels produce fish. The fish produce birds. The birds are the place's loudest expression of its own fertility, of the fact that the churning cold water is not hostile but simply energetic, and the energy can be eaten.

Watch a bird riding a thermal off a heated rock face and you are watching a creature as perfectly expressed as the mountains themselves. It is finished. It has arrived at its form. Its call is not alarm or distress but the sound of a thing correctly placed, correctly functioning, sending the signal — to others of its kind, to the general frequency of the world — *I am here, this is here, this is how we are*.

The small brown land birds in the sedge and under the gnarled trees are harder to read, faster, twitchier, their motion almost nervous if you don't look long enough. Look longer. The quickness is not anxiety. It is the appropriate tempo for a small warm body in a cold place, where the cost of stillness is too high, where the metabolism must keep running, where to stop is to fall behind the thermodynamic negotiation that keeps you alive. They are constantly moving because movement is their rest, their equilibrium. They are not afraid. They dart between mossy tussocks with a confidence that is easy to miss because it looks like hurry. It is not hurry. It is precision, calibrated speed, the shortest path through a known environment by something that knows it exhaustively.

Their knowing of this place is different from any human knowing of it. They know it by sound — the exactly specific frequency of wind in this gap versus that gap, which tells them weather and direction and the likely fishing conditions by the channel below. They know it by smell in ways we barely have instruments to measure. They know it by the particular resistance of this species of moss underfoot, which tells them something about depth and moisture and what is likely underneath. Their knowledge is not abstract. It has no interest in the geological age of the mountains or the philosophical implications of the sea's rise. It is knowledge that is also action, perception that is also navigation, understanding that is also eating and shelter and the raising of small fast-metabolizing young in nests hidden in the root systems of the gnarled trees.

---

The seals are something else entirely.

Where the birds are quick, the seals are slow. Where the birds are decided, the seals are — not uncertain, exactly, but expansive. They take up space without apology. They arrange themselves on the haul-out rocks with a heaviness that is almost theatrical, the huge smooth body repositioning with a sound like water moving in a skin, the effort enormous and apparently worth it. They do not look like things that have compromised with the cold. They look like the cold is inside them, native, their own temperature, and the rocks they haul onto are the warmer element.

Look at a seal's face.

There is something in the look of marine mammals that is easy to anthropomorphize and almost as easy to misread in the other direction — to overcorrect into pure instinct-machine, into seeing only the reflex and the function. The truth is probably neither. The eyes of a seal are large, dark, and — the only word is *attending*. They are looking at things. The one looking at you is looking at you, not just receiving the visual signal of your presence but doing something with it, evaluating it, placing it in a context of prior experiences with large unfamiliar bipedal silhouettes. Whether this is intelligence in any sense we would recognize is a genuine question. What is not a question is that the gaze has weight. It lands.

Seals in the water are different creatures from seals on the rock. The transformation is absurd in its completeness. The ponderous arrangement of weight on rock becomes something else entirely in the channel — a shape as fluid as the water, the huge body moving through the chop the way the chop moves through itself, no boundary, no resistance, just the action of a body in its element. They hunt the same fish the birds hunt and they bring completely different equipment to the task. They can descend to depths where the light goes and stay there for periods that seem impossible and come back up with something in their mouths, or not, and try again. They know this water in three dimensions. They know the temperature gradients at various depths and what those gradients tell them about where the fish school. They know by pressure-sense things that have no visual component.

What the seal knows and what the bird knows, combined, describes the place more fully than any map.

---

Beneath the surface, where the mountains continue.

The summits that break the waterline are the visible fraction. The peaks descend below the current sea level and the sea has filled all the space between and around them, and this submerged majority is where most of what lives here lives, in terms of mass and number and variety. The bottom slopes of the old peaks are colonized to depths that vary with light penetration: kelp and other large algae in the upper levels, their long forms streaming and folding with the current, anchored by holdfasts to rocks that have not seen direct light in whatever interval it has been since the sea finished rising. The kelp forests — if they are dense enough here to deserve that name, and in the sheltered lee of some of the underwater ridges they may be — function as cities, as everything that metaphor implies: layered habitat, vertical structure, resource differentiation across the strata, populations of small things living in the interstices of populations of larger things.

Fish move through in aggregations that are probably not random, though they appear chaotic at a glance. The shoaling behavior is itself a kind of intelligence distributed across a thousand individual nervous systems, a collective computation: *is this direction safer than that direction, is the current better or worse, what does the pressure change indicate.* They are reading the water the way the birds read the air. The mountains that the mountains are standing on are also being read, all the time, by the things that live against them in the dark.

There are things in the deeper channels for which the Drowned Peaks is not a dramatic environment but an ordinary one. Creatures that live where the old ridge descends into real depth, where the water temperature stabilizes into the cold that does not vary with season, where light is a memory. These are patient things, slow metabolisms, long lives, shapes that evolution arrived at early and found sufficient. They have been here since before the mountains' summits were summits, since before the sea rose to its current level. They have watched the flooding, in their way, which is to say they experienced the gradual deepening of the column above them and made no particular note of it. The flooding that is history from the surface perspective is invisible from their perspective. From their perspective nothing changed.

This is what it means to live deep.

---

The moss, again, but differently.

It would be a mistake to spend long with this place and not return to the moss, because the moss is doing something that is easy to overlook because it looks like passivity. Two thousand years, in some places, of accumulated growth. The deepest layers are not alive — they are the compressed, browning record of prior generations, the organic substrate that living mosses grow on, itself built of the bodies of prior living mosses, a community of the present growing from and on the community of the past without any clear break between them.

A moss colony in this sense is not quite a simple organism. It is not a collective in the way that social insects are a collective. But it is — extended. Its relationship to time is not like the relationship of the birds, whose individual lives span a decade or two. The moss's form of persistence is more continuous, less individuated, a smearing across time rather than a series of discrete flickers. The topmost green surface is now. The brown layers beneath are then. They are still there. They still hold water. The nutrients from their decomposition still feed the living surface. The now of the moss is built on the then of the moss, in a direct physical sense, and the then has not been discarded.

You walk on top of a very long story when you walk across the summit moss, and this is not metaphor. Your boot sinks two inches before meeting resistance and those two inches contain centuries, and the centuries are still here, are not gone, are under your foot right now.

---

The trees.

There is a temptation to read the gnarled, wind-formed, permanently leaning trees as diminished, as the lesser versions of what trees could be in kinder conditions. This reading is understandable and wrong.

A tree in a continental forest is exploring. It is reaching upward in competition with other reaching trees, racing toward a canopy that keeps rising, investing resources in height because height is the prize in a vertical contest. Its form is a record of that competition, of what worked and what didn't, of the sun-angle and the competitors and the available water. It is a dynamic creature in a dynamic situation, constantly negotiating.

A tree on a summit plateau of the Drowned Peaks has concluded its negotiations. Not surrendered them — concluded. It tried various strategies and arrived at the current form through a ruthless optimization process and it has been running the winning strategy for a very long time. The lean into the prevailing direction is not defeat. It is the tree's understanding of the specific aerodynamics of this specific summit, encoded structurally, maintained because it works. The low canopy, flagged to the northeast, is not smallness. It is the exact shape of survival here, the profile that offers the least surface to the winds that would destroy a taller, more ambitious shape.

These are trees that know something the forest trees don't know. They know limitation in the way that very old things know their own contours — not as restriction but as definition. The limitation is what makes them specifically themselves, what makes them this shape rather than another. A tree like this, on this summit, in this wind, is expressing the local conditions as fully as the rock beneath it expresses the last billion years of exposure. They are both, rock and tree, the residue of everything that was removed.

What lives inside the trees is proportional but not miniaturized. Beetles in the bark, running the mycorrhizal networks in the old root systems, the fungal threads that link root to root and tree to tree through the deeper soil, passing signals in chemical language — this tree is stressed, that tree has found water, the load-bearing negotiation of the whole root network under the wind. The trees are individuals with personalities, in the loose sense that they have different microclimates in their canopies and different root systems and different histories of branch loss. The birds know which tree is which, know the specific overhang on one that creates a dry-space useful in certain rain directions, know the exposed root on another that holds a particular kind of grub in its second autumn. The trees are known. The trees are, in whatever sense that term can stretch to cover them, also knowing — not in a way that uses sentences, but in a way that responds to the full specific history of being this tree in this place, which is a kind of knowledge that doesn't require a brain to accumulate.

---

The midge clouds deserve more than a parenthetical.

In the brief still moments after rain, in the hollows between moss-mounds, in the lee of the gnarled tree canopies: the midges. Clouds of them, visible as a kind of vibration against the gray sky, their individual bodies too small to see until you hold your hand in the swarm and they settle on you, and then you see them — almost transparent, lighter than air seems like it could support, each one a specific creature with specific destinations and requirements and the whole cloud a single event that happens to be composed of thousands of individuals.

Midges are ancient. Their basic form is very old. They have been hovering in swarms in moments of relative calm over wet ground and still water since before the mountains were this short. They are not remarkable. They are so unremarkable that they are remarkable: the persistence of the form, the efficiency, the way this tiny body and this hovering behavior and this brief adult life have been sufficient across immense spans of time and geography, have worked in places far from here and places like here and places nothing like here. They are general in a way the other inhabitants of the Drowned Peaks are not. The seabirds are specific. The moss is specific. The gnarled trees are specific. The midges could be elsewhere and would be. They are here because here is wet and the wet is full of the organic material their larvae need.

But there is something about encountering them in this place specifically — in the momentary stillness after a squall, with the wet moss steaming very slightly in the return of light, with the birds still settling their weather-interrupted agendas, with the sound of the channels continuing underneath the pause — that makes the midge cloud feel like something is being said, some annotation being made to the larger text. *Even here, even in this, even in the loudest and most churned and most exposed — even here there is a moment fine enough for this.* The midges are the measure of the finest detail this place can produce, the smallest legible writing in the landscape, and they appear in the margins of the weather to prove that the place is capable of fineness, of the scale of the transparent and hovering, alongside the scale of the ancient red rock and the subsonic mountain-voice.

---

Now the absent ones.

There are things that lived here once, when the summits were higher, when there was more land and less sea. Their traces are in the ruined structure, in the bolts driven into the rock, in the iron cable humming between its stakes. People were here. They built with the understanding that permanence required thickness and stone; they knew the weather. They put instruments on the plateau to record — something. Temperature, probably, or atmospheric pressure, or the height of the sea itself, the sea being the most important variable in a place where the peaks are already drowned.

The people who built the intact structure visible across a kilometer of churning channel, the square silhouette with something still catching the light near the roof — those people or their descendants or the organization that succeeded them may still come, occasionally, in the right boat with the right knowledge of the cross-currents and the gap-accelerated wind. Or the structure has been left, finally, its function complete or obsolete or the organization dissolved. It is not possible to know from here.

What they left, the people who instrumented this place, was a set of intentions that have outlasted their object. The bolts intended to hold the equipment. The concrete pad intended to give it a surface. The cable intended to run between stakes for a reason that was clear at the time. The intentions are still in the rock. The equipment is gone. This is a specific kind of haunting — not by the people but by the purposes, by the reasons for doing things that no longer have their objects.

The cistern full of rainwater and midge-bodies does not know it was cut to hold water for people. It holds water anyway. The rain fell and the cistern caught it, faithfully, the way it was shaped to do, without reference to whether the shaping intention persists. It is an artifact being used by the place for the place's own purposes, which are: the water must go somewhere. If there is a square cut in the rock, the water will find it. The midges will find the water. This is fine.

---

There is something else.

It is harder to name because it has no body in the usual sense and cannot be approached directly.

The Drowned Peaks as a totality — the sound, the old rock, the moving water, the moss, the birds, the seals in the channel, the submerged slopes with their algae and fish, the cold, the flat gray light, the small trees in their final form, the midges in their transient clouds — this entirety is doing something that might be called *being* in a sense that extends past any individual organism or object. Not mystical in the soft sense. Physical. The mountains have been running the same chemical and biological cycles for long enough that they have become self-referring, self-sustaining in a way that feels, from inside it, like presence.

There is a frequency here that is not quite one thing. It is the addition of all the individual sounds and processes into something that cannot be decomposed back into its parts from the inside — you can list the components but the list is not the thing, the list is a description of something that is also happening all at once and has been happening all at once for so long that it has become the place's own signal, the frequency it transmits. When you press your ear to the rock and feel the subsonic trembling from the pressure of moving water against the flanks of the mountain, you are touching the lowest note in this signal. When the seabirds call and their calls return from the wet rock faces, you are hearing the place play itself back to itself, which it does constantly. The place is always in conversation with itself.

What lives in the Drowned Peaks is this conversation.

The birds, the moss, the seals, the fish, the gnarled trees, the beetles in the bark, the midges in their brief clouds, the kelp streaming in the current at the depth the light reaches — they are all participants in it, generators of their portion of the frequency, each one a node in a system that is running the same deep argument it has always run: how to persist, how to change without failing, how to remain essentially this while becoming, slowly, something that could not have been predicted from the starting conditions.

The mountains did not sink. The sea rose. And everything that did not leave, everything that stayed and found the terms acceptable, everything that found in this churning cold loud beautiful difficult place the exact conditions it required — all of that is engaged in the same conversation with the same partner, the place itself, which is also alive in the only sense that matters, which is the sense in which it has been here before life and will be here after and takes no particular notice of the distinction.

---

Stand on the summit plateau in the flat gray light.

Feel in your teeth the low combined sound of everything.

The world repeating in every direction — peak, channel, peak, channel, the dark archipelago and the pale reflected sky between, the birds and the chop and the wind carrying water across the exposed rock in horizontal ropes.

Somewhere below, in the channels, a seal turns in the current and reads the water.

Somewhere in the mossy hollows, something small and fast adjusts its route.

The old mountains hold.

They were here before this arrangement and they will be here after it, in some form, in whatever form remains when the essential has been clarified completely. For now they are covered in life the way they are covered in water — because both sought a lower level and found it here, because this is where things rest, in both senses of the word.

You raise your voice and it is lost immediately.

That is fine. Nothing here needed it.
