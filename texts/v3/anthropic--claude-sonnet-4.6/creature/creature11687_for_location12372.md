# What Lives Here

---

Start with what doesn't live here, because that shapes everything.

No birds. No insects. No creatures that came from somewhere else and decided to stay. The canal district arrived at its current state as a complete thing, and whatever lives here has always been of this place — not residents but constituents, not inhabitants but components. The distinction matters. To inhabit a place you must first be separate from it. Nothing here is separate from anything else.

---

## The Moss

The moss lives here the way a mind lives in a body — distributed, continuous, not located in any single place but somehow everywhere that matters.

It is on the steps. It is on the towpath. It is on the walls between the brickwork courses, in the seams of the wooden doors, in the gaps where mortar has softened into something more inviting than stone. Where the wall weeps, the moss has organized itself into a receiving structure for the weeping, a dark green pile that collects the exudate and holds it and slowly releases it further down, managing the water's descent the way a careful institution manages the circulation of resources: never wasting, always moving, the surplus of one level becoming the provision of the next.

What the moss does most significantly is remember.

It holds the impression of footsteps. Only briefly — the description says so, the moss gently recovers — but *briefly* is doing a lot of work here. Brief to what? The moss has been growing in these seams for decades. The pump has been beating since the district was built. In this context, *briefly* might mean several minutes. The moss registers the weight of a person's passing and holds it for several minutes and then slowly erases it, which means that at any given moment the towpath bears the ghostly record of whoever walked it last, compressed into the pile, waiting to be released.

This is not metaphor, or not only metaphor. The moss actually does this. It is a living pressure-record, a material archive of recent passage. If you knew how to read it, you could tell how heavy someone was, how their weight distributed across the foot — heel-heavy, ball-heavy, the slight lateral roll of a particular gait. The moss holds this and holds it and holds it and then lets it go.

It holds water the same way. It holds warmth. It is the district's memory tissue, its felt surface, the medium through which everything registers and everything eventually passes.

## The Vine

The vine is harder to think about because it is no longer quite a plant.

A plant is a living organism occupying a substrate. The vine has passed through that condition into something else: it has become structural. The wall and the vine are now a single composite material. This happened over a very long time, the vine embedding itself into the mortar, the mortar accepting the embedding, and the current state of the wall is a state that requires the vine to be coherent. Pull the vine free and you would pull mortar with it. Pull enough mortar free and something architectural could become uncertain. The vine is load-bearing, or nearly. It has woven itself into the calculus of the building's structural integrity.

This is a life that has, through patience and slowness and the long application of very small forces, become necessary.

The vine does not know this. Necessity is not something the vine experiences as a condition. It simply grows where it grows, anchors where it anchors, puts out its waxy small leaves in whatever light filters through the canal's canopy. It is not clever. It has not been clever at any moment in its centuries of growing. But the cumulative effect of its unclever growing is that the city, or this corner of it, cannot now easily exist without it. A thing that begins as an interloper ends as infrastructure. The vine has lived here long enough to become what is holding things up.

Something about this seems like a moral but probably isn't. It is just what happens when a living thing and a built thing occupy the same space for long enough. They become one thing.

## The Ferns

The ferns colonize. The word is the right word. They are the principal occupants of the ground-level and mid-level space, the ones that have most thoroughly claimed the horizontal surfaces — flagstones invisible beneath them, visible only as pale outlines glimpsed through frond architecture, the way you can sometimes see the structure of a sentence through the words. This comparison is the description's, and it is exactly right.

The ferns are the most *here* living thing here, in the sense that they most aggressively take up space, most visibly crowd the footprint of the canal district with their physical presence. If the moss is distributed and the vine is structural, the ferns are occupational — they hold territory, they fill volume, they make the towpath what it is: a narrow maintained exception to fern dominion, a corridor kept passable by the regular pressure of human feet in a way that is just barely winning against the ferns' continuous ambient expansion.

Ferns are ancient. Not these individual plants — but ferns as a form predate most of the plant lineages we consider familiar. They were here before the flowering plants. They were here before the dinosaurs. They have been doing this — colonizing moist dim spaces, filling in the middle registers of vertical habitat, using their ancient shallow-rooting and spore-spreading strategy to make themselves at home wherever warmth and wet coincide — for longer than most living strategies have existed.

There is something about the fern's extreme age as a form that resonates with the canal district's quality of continuous present. Both seem to exist outside accumulation. The district is old but doesn't feel like it's getting older. The ferns are an ancient form but they don't carry their age as weight. They are simply doing the thing they have always done, in exactly the conditions they have always done it in, and the conditions happen to be here, now, in this warm enclosed green-air space below the city.

## The Water Iris

The iris lives at the margin. This is its nature, not its compromise: the iris is a marginal creature, a thing of the edge between water and land, and it has turned the canal's edges into a single dense unbroken margin running the full length of the waterway. It has, in some sense, maximized the edge.

This is not nothing. The iris has made the canal simultaneously more present — you know it is there, you can hear it — and less accessible. You cannot reach the far edge. You cannot stand at the waterline without standing among iris. The canal is known through the iris rather than directly known. The iris is the medium through which the water is apprehended.

When the flowers come — purple, yellow, according to season — they are startling in the general green. Not because they are especially large or especially bright but because the iris is so densely itself, so entirely committed to its own iris-ness — the vertical sword-leaf, the architectural flower held at the end of its stalk like a resolved argument — that its flowering feels less like decoration than announcement. Here I am, the iris says. Here I am again. The flower comes and goes and the leaf remains, and the leaf is the iris's real life, the continuous occupation, and the flower is just the thing it does occasionally that other organisms notice.

The iris does not care that there are no animals here. The flowers are not for the absent insects, or not primarily — the iris is an old form too, older than its current pollinators, and something in its flowering feels self-complete. It flowers because that is what it does. Whether anything comes is a separate question.

## The Algae and Liverworts

On the stones at the waterline. On the brickwork in the perpetual drip-zone. Small enough to overlook, close enough to the substrate to seem like the substrate's own surface rather than organisms living on it.

They are the district's most ancient residents. Not in the sense of having been here longest, though perhaps they have — but in the sense of form. The algae are doing what algae have done for billions of years: photosynthesizing in thin wet films on hard surfaces, existing at the precise edge of the possible, turning light and carbon and mineral into living matter at scales that require magnification to appreciate. The liverworts are a form even older in evolutionary terms than the ferns — flat, scalular, overlapping, pressed against stone. The description calls them a close-worked tapestry, and they are: patient, exact, covering what they cover completely, leaving no gaps, interlocking at their edges in a structure that is the sum of many individual organisms doing the same simple thing in proximity until the sum becomes a material.

They are the canal district stripped of everything except the core transaction: energy from light, matter from water and stone, life from the interface between. No flowers. No leaves to speak of. No structure beyond the thallus, the flat ribbon of living tissue. At this level of organization, the distinction between organism and mat begins to soften. A liverwort community is the kind of thing where the boundaries between individuals are less important than the coverage they collectively achieve.

Living wallpaper, you might think, dismissively. But the wall and the seep and the liverwort and the moss are all part of a single slow system, and the liverwort is the part of that system doing the oldest, most fundamental thing: being alive in a thin wet film on a hard surface, because that is what life first was, and this place — warm, dark, wet, enclosed, industrially heated, perpetually seeping — is among the better places currently available on earth for that ancient project to continue.

## The Alder Roots

This is strange to include, because the alders are above — their canopies somewhere in the street-level air, their trunks rising from cracks in the walls at the top, their full above-ground bodies belonging to a different space. But their root systems are here. The roots trail down the wall face like dropped rope. They are present in the canal district without their trees.

What lives here, then: a root system that has been disarticulated from its visible body by the accident of where it chose to grow. The alder committed to a crack in the upper wall and grew upward into the street-level air, but the roots followed gravity downward into the dripping dark, and here they are, vegetable rope hanging from forty feet up, ending somewhere in the damp brick, drawing water upward toward a tree that you cannot see from here.

The roots are the canal district's only upward-pointing inhabitants. Everything else is horizontal or inward-leaning. The roots are oriented toward elsewhere. They are the only evidence here of life that is partly somewhere else — life that has its growing and its fruiting and its seasons in a different register, while its metabolic foundation is here, in the warm wet dark, pulling moisture from ancient clay and feeding it to a tree up in the wind.

There is something almost comforting about this. The district is not entirely closed. Something has roots here and a canopy somewhere worth visiting.

## The Water Itself

The water is alive but this is not metaphor.

It moves. It moves because the pump moves it — the pump is the district's cardiovascular center, its reciprocating heart, and the canal is the vessel through which its function is expressed. But the water also carries things: microorganisms in quantities too small to see, spores from the ferns landing on the surface, organic material from the plant decomposition along the margins, the chemical exudate of root systems. The black water under the iris and the floating pads is not inert. It is dense with tiny life, the kind of life that preceded all the other life currently in this place, the kind of life that will remain after most of it goes.

The water smells of transformation rather than decay. This is an important distinction and the description makes it. Transformation means the process is continuing, not ending. What falls into the canal does not become nothing. It becomes something else that will become something else again. The water is the medium of this continuous becoming — not passive, but active, doing what water does: accepting material, harboring it, moving it somewhere downstream, delivering it into the larger drainage network and ultimately somewhere further still.

The canal does not feel like a vein for nothing. The vein carries; it doesn't produce. But what it carries is alive, and what it moves through is alive, and the things that line it are alive, and the warmth that fills it comes from the machinery that keeps all of it from returning to marsh. Everything here is connected to everything else in a web of dependency so old and so thoroughly established that the web is no longer a useful metaphor. There is no web. There is just one thing, complicated, continuous, currently in the middle of its own existence.

## The Pump

The pump is not alive. And yet.

The pump beats like a heart. Once every three seconds, perhaps. A massive soft mechanical pulse. It has been doing this since the district was built, maintained with the care that goes into maintaining a machine that cannot be allowed to fail. This care is human and outside the district's own operation — someone comes to maintain the pump, engineers or mechanics whose presence on the towpath is recorded briefly in the moss and then released. But the care is continuous, and its result is continuous function, and the function is so foundational to everything else — all these plants, all this warmth, the maintenance of this space against its natural tendency to revert to marsh — that the pump's beating is the beat of the whole district.

If the pump stopped, the drainage would stop. The water level would rise. The pumping station's boilers would cool, the industrial heat that warms the canal air would reduce, and over time — not immediately, but over time — the conditions that make this particular confluence of life possible would shift toward conditions that would suit different life. The marsh would return. Something would live in it, but not this.

The pump is not alive. But everything alive here lives because of it. In a strict ecological sense the pump is the keystone of this ecosystem, the thing whose presence or absence determines whether the rest is possible. A keystone is not alive either, but you still wouldn't take it out.

---

## What Lives Here

Not inhabitants. Constituents.

The moss that remembers and forgets. The vine that became the wall. The ferns that hold the ground. The iris that edges and flowers without asking anything back. The liverworts and algae doing the first and oldest thing, thin films of life on wet stone. The alder roots stretching down from trees whose canopies are somewhere the canal cannot see. The water, transforming everything it touches, moving it slowly toward wherever downstream turns out to be.

And the pump. The heart that is not alive. Beating once every three seconds. Keeping this from returning to what it was. Keeping what this is possible.

They are not in the Greenhouse District. They are the Greenhouse District. The place and its life arrived together as a single thing, and they have been continuous since, and they are currently in the middle of their own existence, not at a beginning or end.

The last footprint in the moss is almost gone now.

Another will come.
