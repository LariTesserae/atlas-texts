# What Lives in the Plateau Mills

---

## The Moss, First

It would be a mistake to begin anywhere else. The moss is not backdrop. The moss is not context. The moss is the primary organism of the plateau, its foundational fact, and everything that lives here lives in relation to it — inside it, on it, from it, because of it.

What the moss is doing here is what living things do when they have tenure: it is making the place more hospitable to itself. It has been doing this for thousands of years before the mills arrived and it is doing it still, in and around the foundations, along the cable trenches where disturbance has exposed raw stone and the moss has moved in on the exposed edges with its characteristic patience — not aggression, not ambition, only the willingness to be wherever conditions allow. The moss has no preference for undisturbed ground. It will take what it's offered.

To live as moss is to live very slowly and very thoroughly. A single shoot of *Sphagnum* — and it is largely *Sphagnum* here, in its many varieties, the genus that builds bogs and blanket peat and the deep breathing sponge-world of wet uplands — grows perhaps a centimeter a year at this altitude. The growth is upward. The dying is downward. Below the living green surface is a column of moss-corpses, compressed by the weight of what came after, gradually losing their structure, their cell walls thinning, their cavities collapsing, their water-storage capacity diminishing as they are incorporated into the matrix of proto-peat beneath. The moss is always dying and always living and the living and the dying are the same process viewed at different depths.

*Sphagnum* has a peculiarity worth dwelling on: its cells are of two kinds. Living cells, narrow, photosynthesizing, containing the chlorophyll that gives the plateau its impossible green. And dead cells — called hyaline cells — large, empty, perforated with pores, built to hold water. A single *Sphagnum* plant is largely composed of dead cells engineered to be useful after their own death. The living cells are the minority. The plant's structure is predicated on the utility of the dead. This is not metaphor — or it is, but it is first biology — and it explains why the moss holds so much water, why stepping into it sends water up around your boot, why the plateau is wet even in drought: the moss body is a reservoir of dead space, and the dead space holds the water that the living space needs. Life here runs on a skeleton of structured death. This is the moss's solution to living in a place with no rivers, no permanent lakes, only sky-water and the water the moss has already captured and is slowly releasing. It has built itself into the infrastructure. It *is* the infrastructure.

The colors of the moss change with what the moss is and what it's doing. The actively photosynthesizing surfaces are the vivid greens — emerald where wet and the light is good, a slightly yellower green where it's drier, a reddish-bronze at the tips of some *Sphagnum* species where the plant produces pigment as a kind of sunscreen against the high-altitude UV that comes through the thin air without mercy. In the deeper cushions and hummocks, where older growth is compressing, the green darkens to near-black. At the waterlogged low points between hummocks, the surface has a sheen, almost glossy, where the mat is so saturated it reflects light rather than absorbing it. The plateau's palette is narrower than it first seems and deeper: greens and blacks and the amber of stained water and the grey-blue of rock and the white of sky in the water-lenses. These are the colors of a place that has reduced itself to essentials.

The moss makes acid. This is important. *Sphagnum* releases hydrogen ions as it takes up minerals, making the water around it progressively more acidic, which in turn inhibits the bacteria that would otherwise decompose it — inhibits them enough that the moss body accumulates rather than rotting, which is why there is peat rather than bare rock, why the depth of organic matter keeps increasing, why the plateau is in the slow business of building itself. The moss acidifies its environment to make itself indigestible. It lives in the conditions it creates, which are the conditions it prefers, which it has made itself. This closed loop — organism making environment, environment enabling organism — is what ecology looks like when it's working, and it is working here, at altitude, in the wet and the cold, in the roar of the mills that the moss takes no notice of.

Sound does not kill moss. Vibration does not kill moss. Industrial proximity does not kill moss. The mills may have changed the local hydrology slightly, the buried pipes drawing from the aquifer the plateau recharges — but the moss has more water than it can use. The moss continues.

## The Invertebrate Interior

The moss is an architecture. Inside it — down in the spaces between stems, in the water-filled cavities of the hyaline cells, in the film of moisture that clings to every surface — a community lives that has no name as a whole and is only visible in parts when you look with intention, with a hand lens, with your face a few centimeters from the surface and your knees wet.

**Springtails.** *Collembola.* There are perhaps a hundred thousand of them in a square meter of healthy *Sphagnum* mat. You cannot see them at the surface — they live in the interior, in the labyrinthine spaces between packed stems, where the humidity is always near saturation and the temperature is moderated by the insulating mass of the plant above and below. They are small — one to four millimeters — and they are various: some cylindrical and pale, some globular and dark, some with scales, some with elaborate antennae they sweep ahead of them as they navigate spaces the diameter of a matchstick. They eat fungal hyphae. They eat bacteria. They eat decaying plant material at the transition zone where living moss becomes dying moss, where cell walls begin to give way. They are the primary force by which organic matter in the moss is broken into finer particles, making it available to the microbial communities below. Without the springtails, the chemistry of the peat-making process would be different. They are the workforce of the plateau's slow manufacture of itself.

Springtails have a forked appendage tucked beneath the abdomen — the furcula, sprung under tension — that can release in milliseconds and fling them away from a threat, many times their own body length, randomly, erratically, impossible to track. It is likely that in a square meter of moss, thousands of springtails are launching themselves in all directions at any given moment, disturbed by the vibration from the mills that comes up through the ground and through the moss stems, so that the interior of the moss community is always in mild chaos, always adjusting, always flinging. They land and resume. The vibration is a constant of their existence. Their neurology has no framework for a time before the mills; their lifespan is a few months at most. The sound and the trembling ground are the world as it has always been for any individual among them.

**Mites.** *Acari.* More than springtails and more diverse, if that is possible to imagine. Oribatid mites in the upper layers, small and heavily armored in dark chitin, their outer surfaces sometimes sculpted with ridges and pits that serve unclear purposes and give them the appearance of having been manufactured for a purpose that changed mid-production. They also eat fungi and bacteria and plant detritus. Some species on high-altitude blanket bogs have been found to eat *Sphagnum* directly, inserting mouthparts into living cells and withdrawing contents — a parasitism so small it has no visible effect on the plant except that a cell here and there has been drained. Predatory mites in the lower layers, hunting springtails and other mites with a directness that seems almost eager, their palps working, their movements quick relative to the general metabolic pace of the plateau. The predator-prey dynamics of the moss interior are complete: there are producers, consumers, consumers of consumers, decomposers, and the whole web is self-contained in a space between moss stems.

Water bears. *Tardigrada.* The plateau has them in quantity. They live in the water film on plant surfaces and in the water inside *Sphagnum* hyaline cells, microscopic animals with four pairs of stubby legs and a mouth that works like a piercing tool, drawing up cell contents or the contents of algae, or eating bacteria. They are famous for something the plateau gives them no occasion to demonstrate: in desiccation or freezing, they can pull their legs in, lose nearly all their water, shut down metabolism to near nothing, and wait — wait years, decades, in this dehydrated dormant state — for conditions to improve. Here, conditions do not require waiting. The plateau is wet. The water bears are active, trundling through their water-film world, reproducing, eating, being the animals they are in the conditions that suit them. Their famous resilience is not required. They are simply living, without drama, in a wet place that meets their needs precisely.

Rotifers in the pools between hummocks, in the surface water and in the thin water-films: wheeled animals, named for the corona of cilia around the mouth that beats in patterns suggesting rotation, drawing food particles in. Their bodies are often transparent, organs visible through the body wall, a kidney-like organ visible processing waste, the jaw apparatus — *mastax* — visible working in the throat. They too can desiccate and wait. They too are not waiting here.

Nematodes by the thousands of thousands in every gram of the deeper moss layers, the most abundant animal on the plateau in terms of numbers — perhaps in terms of biomass — and nearly invisible, smooth wormlike bodies a millimeter long or less, moving with the lateral undulation that is their only mode. They are in the soil, in the peat, in the water films, eating bacteria, eating fungi, eating each other, being eaten by mites, cycling nutrients in ways that are essential and unmeasurable without instruments. Nematodes are in every environment on land with any soil at all. They are not special to the plateau. They are what they always are: the invisible metabolic engine, the thing that keeps the chemistry of decomposition and nutrient release running. On the plateau they do this in cold, in acid, in wet, in the constant vibration of industrial machinery. They do it without noticing any of these conditions because nothing in a nematode notices anything in this sense.

**The single beetle seen in four days.**

A rove beetle, *Staphylinidae*, perhaps two centimeters long, crossing a section of relatively bare rock near one of the mill foundations at midday. It moved quickly for the plateau — which is to say, at a speed that called attention to itself against the general stillness of things — and then was gone into a seam of moss. Its elytra were short, as rove beetles' elytra always are, exposing the flexible abdomen that allows them to pursue prey into confined spaces. It is a predator. What it was pursuing on the exposed rock face was unclear; perhaps it had emerged from the moss interior briefly, disoriented by vibration, and was seeking its way back. It was the only insect large enough to see from standing height during that period. Its presence was brief and total — while it was there, it was the most specific thing on the plateau.

## The Cushion Plants, Internally

The cushion plants — the warm dense domed plants growing where moss thins and rock asserts — are not single organisms in the sense that a tree or a person is single. They are constructions. The tight-packed shoots that form the cushion's surface are individual plants compacted by their own growth pattern and by wind pressure into the dome shape that is their adaptation, the geometry that minimizes surface area relative to volume, that retains heat, that sheds wind, that allows the interior to remain several degrees warmer than the surrounding air even when the surrounding air is cold.

When you press your fingers into a cushion plant and feel the warmth, you are feeling metabolic heat, yes — the accumulated gentle heat of photosynthesis and cellular respiration happening simultaneously in hundreds of packed shoots — but also something more passive: the thermal mass of the cushion itself, which is largely composed of the dead previous years' growth, the compacted stems and leaves of older shoots, still intact because the plant's own acidity and the general acidity of the plateau have inhibited the bacteria that would decompose them. The interior of a cushion plant is slightly acidic, slightly antimicrobial, and therefore slightly preservative. The dead shoots at the core of a large old cushion plant may be fifty years old. A hundred. In the right conditions, more. They have not decomposed. They have become insulation.

The cushion plant's interior is, in consequence, a habitat. A specific and unusual one: warm, humid, sheltered from wind, slightly acid, full of the structural complexity of compacted plant material — channels and crevices and dead-end chambers. Mites and springtails colonize it. Spiders, occasionally — the few spider species capable of life at this altitude favor the cushion plant's interior above almost any other location on the plateau, because it offers what the plateau otherwise does not: stable microclimate, shelter from the wind that at elevation strips moisture and warmth from any body too small to maintain its own temperature against it. A spider in a cushion plant is a spider that has found the best room in the building. It may spend most of its life there. It hunts in the moss adjacent and retreats to the cushion's warmth to digest, to molt, to wait out bad weather.

What kind of spider? Small, dark-colored, probably a sheet-web weaver, *Linyphiidae* — the family that dominates at altitude, that has miniaturized and darkened to absorb heat more efficiently, that has reduced its web-building ambitions to low horizontal sheets stretched between plant stems rather than the elaborate vertical spirals of lower-altitude relatives. The web collects dew as much as it collects prey, which is an incidental benefit in a wet place but real. The spider at the center of its sheet web, upside-down in the characteristic linyphiid posture, waiting. The web vibrates with the mill sound. The spider reads the vibration and waits. Vibration that is prey has a specific character — irregular, repeated at short intervals, the trapped animal's panic encoded in the pattern of web-trembling. Vibration that is mill-sound has a different character: constant, even, below the frequency of a fly's struggles. The spider has learned the difference without learning it, through whatever mechanism allows a spider's nervous system to filter signal from noise. It waits for the irregular trembling. It gets it, sometimes. There are still flies on the plateau, low and close to the moss surface, moving between hummocks in the thin shelter available.

## The Dwarf Willows and Their Relations

The dwarf willows (*Salix herbacea*, or near it — the alpine willows that have collapsed their form from vertical to horizontal as the altitude demanded) are old in the way that changed things are old: they have survived by transformation. Their ancestors were taller. Not much — perhaps a meter, perhaps two — but vertical, a real attempt at the tree form. The wind decided otherwise. Decades of wind pressure, of desiccation at any exposed height, of ice-crystal abrasion through the winter months, killed everything above the ground's protective layer repeatedly until only what grew flat survived to grow old. What grows old now grows flat. The memory of the vertical attempt is gone. These are not stunted trees. They are plants that have arrived at their form through long attrition and the ones that remained are the ones whose growth was already horizontal, whose architecture suited the exposure, and those are the ones whose genetics have propagated across the plateau's rocky sections.

The catkins are out. Yellow-green, soft, wind-pollinated — there is no shortage of wind here. The willows are doing what willows do in the brief window the altitude allows for it: flowering, pollinating, setting seed, the seed cotton-light and white and ready to blow. The seeds of dwarf willows are viable for only a few days. They must land, germinate, establish — in days. They land everywhere: on moss, on wet rock, on the wooden trestles of the mills' pipe infrastructure, on the corrugated metal roofing where they cannot establish, on the rare bare patch of proto-soil between moss hummocks where they can. Most do not survive. The ones that do are the ones that encountered the specific conditions they needed within the specific window they had to encounter them. The plateau's edge populations of dwarf willow are slowly advancing across the sections the moss is also advancing across, and the willow and the moss interact without competing exactly — the willow roots are in the rock seams, the moss grows around and under and eventually over the prostrate stems, incorporating the willow into its mat, and the willow does not object. It is making roots below while moss makes surface above, and they inhabit different vertical strata of the same place.

**Something that comes to the catkins:**

Bees would come, if bees were here. At this altitude, in this exposed place with its industrial ambient noise, standard honeybees are not. Bumblebees possibly — the large alpine bumblebees that can fly in colder temperatures, that heat their flight muscles by shivering before takeoff, that forage at altitudes and air densities where other bees cannot. If one has been seen here it was brief and opportunistic, come up from a colony on the lower slopes to work the willows and the sedges and departed before the light failed. Bumblebees do not navigate by sound. The roar means nothing to them. The flowers mean something. The flowers are brief. The bumblebee — if it comes — comes and goes efficiently, in the transactional way of things that have to return somewhere before cold sets in.

More certainly: midges. Small flies, *Chironomidae*, in the moss pools and emerging from them in their millions in the warmer hours, rising in loose columns above the water lenses before descending again, their bodies so light that the wind's interference with them is constant and their flight is partly drift. They do not bite here — the chironomid midges of high moorland are largely non-biting, emerging from the pool muck as larvae, becoming adults for the sole purpose of mating and dying, their adult lifespans measured in days or hours. They are protein. They are what the absent birds would eat if birds were present. They rise and fall over the pools and the plateau is full of them and they are eaten by almost nothing in the current configuration of the plateau's food web, and so they accumulate in their season, vast numbers rising over the water-lenses, their small bodies catching the bright flat light, visible from a distance as a diffuse shimmer above the pools, a living haze, gone by evening.

## The Raptor

It appears twice in a day. Sometimes not at all. It rides the thermal off the plateau's edge — a column of rising air where the plateau's bright surface heats the boundary layer and the heated air lifts, rising off the rim in the way that elevation and flat sun together produce — and it hangs there, barely moving, wings set in a slight dihedral, the tip feathers spread and slightly bent upward, working the lift.

It is likely a rough-legged buzzard (*Buteo lagopus*) or something near to it — a large bird, pale below, hovering capable, adapted to open treeless country. Or it may be a lesser kestrel visiting from lower elevations, finding the plateau's edge a useful observation point. The distance and the brightness make identification from the plateau surface difficult. From below, it appears as a dark point moving slowly against bright sky, then stopping, then moving, then dropping suddenly into a stoop that may or may not connect with something far below on the slope where the plateau's mammals — the ones that have left the plateau itself for the quieter terrain below the rim — might be moving in the heather.

What the raptor makes of the mills and their sound is not knowable. Birds have hearing concentrated at frequencies human hearing also covers and somewhat beyond. The mill sound is in the range that birds detect. The raptor, above the plateau edge, is inside the sound-field as much as anything else is. Whether it avoids the plateau center, the region of maximum mill sound, is not clear — it may prefer the rim naturally, for the thermal. The rim is where the thermal is. The rim is where it appears.

When it stoops, if it stoops toward the plateau surface, it has learned something that seems obvious in retrospect: that the vibration through the ground disturbs the invertebrate community on the plateau surface enough to produce the occasional exposed target — a springtail flung upward, a beetle crossing open rock — but these are insufficient meals for a bird this size. The raptor's real hunting is below the rim. The plateau is a perch, an observation point, a place to use the air. It is not a pantry.

The raptor does not breed on the plateau. There are no signs: no mutes, no pellets on the rock faces, no scraped depression in the moss that would indicate a nest site. It visits. It leaves. The same individual may have been doing this for years. The plateau is part of its range in the way that a gas station is part of a road-trip: useful for one specific function, moved through, not lived in.

## The Workers

Ten of them on a normal day, arriving before the light is full. They come up the unpaved road in two vehicles, older trucks suited to the track, the sound of their engines lost long before they reach the plateau rim because the mill sound swallows it. They emerge at the top and there is the moment — witnessed if you are there to witness it — of the sound arriving for them again, the renewed confrontation with the roar, which seems to require a moment's adjustment even for people who have made this ascent every working day for years.

They are not of one type. They range in age from perhaps twenty to perhaps sixty. They dress for the wet: rubber boots universally, waterproof trousers, layers visible at the cuffs and collar where the outer layer hasn't closed completely. The boots sink into the moss on the path between the vehicles and the nearest mill entrance and the path has been worn down to rock by this daily passage, the moss long since defeated on this one line, the stone showing through, and even here the moss has begun reconsidering — at the edges of the worn track, where boots pass less often, the green is creeping back.

What they know about the plateau is functional: they know the ground between the mills and the maintenance points. They know which trestles need attention before winter. They know the intake pipe inspection schedule and the drying shed ventilation sequence and the vibration profile of each mill so that an anomalous vibration registers immediately as a distinct event within the mill sound's general texture. They read the mills through the soles of their feet and through their hands on the machinery housings, the way you learn to read anything that communicates primarily through touch when its primary communication channel — sound — has been overwhelmed.

They communicate in the gesture-language of the plateau. There is a vocabulary of pointing and waving and specific handshapes that has accumulated over however long the mills have operated, not a formal sign language but a working one, a practical vocabulary evolved from necessity and probably different in its specifics from the gesture-language that developed at other mills in other loud places, because vocabulary of this kind develops locally, idiomatically, shaped by the specific demands of the specific place. They understand each other completely. Visitors who have not worked the plateau need a few days to learn the basics and longer to become fluent.

Their break — midday, approximately — is taken inside one of the mill buildings at a point where the internal walls and the arrangement of machinery reduce the worst of the noise to something a raised voice can cut through. They eat and talk in raised voices. Outside the building the moss and the water-lenses and the old cushion plants and the mites and the springtails and the dwarf willows continue without pause, in the sound, in the bright light, in the wet. The workers are the only organisms on the plateau that notice the sound as something imposed on the background rather than as the background itself. Everything else does not notice in this sense because noticing requires a memory of the alternative, a before, and the moss and the mites have no such memory.

They leave before dark. This has the quality of agreement, of something decided once and adhered to since without needing to be re-decided. They do not discuss it. They finish what the day requires and they leave before the light fails, and the plateau is returned to what it is without them.

## What Remains at Night

The sound remains. It does not stop for night. The mills run continuously — shutting down and restarting the grinding processes is expensive and mechanically wearing on parts that are not cheap to replace at altitude, so the mills run, and the sound runs, and the darkness that comes over the plateau is a darkness inside the sound, which is its own kind of dark.

The cushion plants continue their slow warmth. The water continues its slow seep toward the eastern rim. The springtails in the moss continue their lives in the interior of the mat, where day and night are not distinguished by light levels because the mat interior has no light levels — it is always dark inside the moss, always the same temperature, always the same humidity. The mill sound does not distinguish between these things either. It is the same sound in the same darkness.

There is a moment — documented by the person who spent a night on the plateau in the research period preceding the mills' construction, whose notes are in the regional archive — when the sound and the dark become indistinguishable from one another, when the absence of visual information and the presence of only auditory information resolves into something that is not quite either. The sound becomes a texture of the space rather than a feature of it. The space becomes the sound. There is no separation.

Whether this is distressing depends on the person. The person who left the notes found it distressing for perhaps two hours and then found it interesting and then found it restful and then slept badly not because of the sound but because the ground was wet. The cushion plant they had arranged themselves beside had, they noted on waking, maintained a perceptible warmth through the night.

The moss absorbed what it could of the sound that hit its surface and passed the rest upward into the dark air.

## What Accumulates Over Time

The plateau is an accumulating place. It accumulates water, moss-body, organic matter, peat. In a different sense it accumulates what has happened to it: the industrial use is leaving marks that will outlast the industry. The mill foundations will not vanish when the mills are eventually gone — the concrete is in the rock, poured into drilled holes and set hard, and the rock does not release concrete. The trestle holes will fill with moss but the pressure changes in the aquifer caused by the buried intake pipes will have shifted the plateau's internal hydrology in ways that may take decades to equilibrate after the pipes are removed, if they are removed. The worn track between the mills will take time to recover — moss is patient but not instantaneous, even on the moss's own timescale. The plateau is recording its use.

This is not necessarily damage. The word damage implies a deviation from something that was complete, and the plateau was not complete in any static sense — it was always in the act of becoming, always mid-process, always en route to a stability it has not reached. The mills are an event in that process. They will pass. The process will continue. The moss will continue.

The springtails, whose lives are months long, have had hundreds of generations since the mills were built. The mills are their deep ancestry. The springtails of the plateau today are the descendants of springtails that have known nothing but this. The moss has grown, in the time since the mills were built, perhaps thirty centimeters. The cushion plants are larger. The slow things continue slow.

## Summary: What Lives Here

**The moss.** Primarily. Everywhere. At all depths. Building the plateau from itself, accumulating, acidifying, water-holding, slow beyond patience, the actual substance of the place.

**The water.** Not alive in the usual sense but the medium of all the life, the connective tissue, the thing everything depends on and that the moss body holds.

**Springtails by the hundred thousand per square meter.** Working the interior. Eating fungi and bacteria. Launching themselves in the vibration. Living and dying in the space between moss stems, which is the world they know.

**Mites, oribatid and predatory.** Armored. Specific. Part of the same interior community, cycling matter, hunting springtails, cycling the hunters.

**Water bears, rotifers, nematodes.** The smallest life. In the water-films, in the moss cells, in the peat. Running the chemistry of the place at the scale where chemistry and biology are the same thing.

**A few spiders.** In the cushion plants. Patient. Reading vibration for what it means. Hunting on small scales at the moss surface.

**Midges in their clouds.** Emerging briefly, mating, dying. Protein without a taker. The season's floating biomass.

**The dwarf willows.** Prostrate, ancient in form if not in body, flowering briefly, their seeds trying the air, most failing, some few succeeding. Old.

**The cushion plants.** Warm and domed. Two hundred years old possibly. Internally complex. Housing what has the sense to find them.

**One beetle.** Crossing rock. Gone.

**One raptor.** Above the rim. Occasional. Indifferent.

**Ten workers.** Briefly. Daily. Gone before dark. The only organisms that experience the place as loud.

**And the mills themselves**, which are not alive, which are the condition for most of what else is here and the condition against which the rest persists, which roar into the bright air and into the dark air without pause, which the moss takes no notice of, which the springtails have no memory of a before without.

The plateau is not a hospitable place in the sense the word usually implies. It is a place that has made itself habitable for specific things through specific processes across specific timescales, and those specific things live here now with the completeness of things that have no other option and need none.
