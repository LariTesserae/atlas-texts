## What Lives in Vel Shamash

---

Begin with the beetles, because they were here first, before the ramps, before the root-panels, before anyone decided this would be a city.

They are diverse in the way that old populations become diverse — not through abundance of resources but through long, patient specialization into every available niche. There are the tunnelers, flat-bodied, reddish-brown, who work the spaces inside the root-mass walls where the fibers haven't fully fused, where there is still organic material to process and darkness enough to disappear into. They are perhaps a centimeter long and they move with an efficiency that looks urgent but isn't — it is simply the movement of a thing that has never wasted motion, that has been selected for economy over so many generations that economy has become character. There are the grazers who work the sealed face of the wall, finding the mosses, finding the thin organic films the mineral sealant doesn't quite prevent. These are flatter still, wider, their bodies designed to press against surfaces. When they sense vibration — footsteps on root-fiber, the pulse of machinery — they go still, and their stillness is perfect. They become part of the wall. This is not dramatic camouflage, not some striking mimicry; it is simply the practice of something that has learned to be unremarkable, and found that unremarkability is a form of survival more reliable than any other.

There are the ceiling-workers, smaller, who navigate the tap roots by grip rather than flight, who have at some point in their ancestry probably had functional wings and no longer do. Their legs are modified for root-fiber — the tarsal segments curved inward, the claws wide-set. They move upside down without apparent effort. You only see them when they drop to a lower root, which they do with a brief controlled fall, a practiced thing, catching at the next surface with the certainty of long habit.

And there are the large ones, the ones that come out from the deepest wall-spaces at what passes for night in Vel Shamash — the six-hour quiet between shifts when the foot-traffic stops and the machine-sound drops to its baseline hum. These are dark, almost black, two or three centimeters long, with the proportions of something that has never needed to be beautiful. Their antennae are long relative to their bodies. They move slowly, with authority, processing the accumulated organic matter in the floor-cracks, turning the city's slow biological exhale back into something the ground-cover plants can use. They are the deep recyclers of this place, the organisms at the bottom of the economy, without which the whole system would, gradually, run dry. Nobody has studied them in any serious way. They appear in the early surveys as a line item, noted and passed over. The workers see them occasionally and do not find them troubling. They have the presence of things that belong somewhere so thoroughly that their belonging has become invisible.

The aggregate of all these beetles — the rustling in the walls, below the machine-sound — is the city's baseline voice, the sound it makes when it is not doing anything in particular. Which is to say: the beetles are the sound of the city existing. Always. Underneath everything else.

---

The birds came later, and they came deliberately, or as deliberately as birds come to anything — which is to say they followed warmth and food and the slow logic of availability, generation by generation, down the warm-air currents from the surface, through the ventilation corridors where nobody thought to stop them because nobody anticipated them, into the root-ceiling of the undercroft where they found, to whatever extent birds find, that this would do.

They are small. The largest among them — a species without a proper name, called *ceiling sparrows* by the workers despite being no relation to sparrows — is the size of a clenched fist. Their coloration is muted: grey-brown above, off-white below, with traces of a color that in surface light would probably be slate blue but in the diffuse filtered light of the undercroft reads only as a slightly different grey. They are not albino, not blind, not diminished in any obvious way. They have simply been, for enough generations, under light that doesn't reward bright color, and their brightness has gone elsewhere, into other refinements.

Their eyes are large relative to their heads. Larger than you'd expect. The iris is very dark, so dark the pupil disappears into it, giving the face a soft, attentive blankness that workers sometimes describe as *watchful* and sometimes as *calm* and sometimes, late in a long shift when the machine-rhythm has become the whole world, as *knowing*, though they would not say this in a report. The eyes have adapted to the cool, diffuse, sourceless light of the undercroft — they work well in it, better than human eyes do without assistance, and the birds navigate the tap-root ceiling with a confidence that their surface cousins don't have in comparable density.

They are sociable in the way small birds generally are — loosely grouped, aware of each other, communicating in sounds that serve navigation and alarm and something that might be companionship. Their vocalizations carry in the underground air. Not loud: this is not birdsong in the surface sense, not the full-throated territorial broadcast of open space. These are intimate sounds, small bells, careful intervals. They punctuate rather than fill. You hear one and then, a moment later, another from a different section of the ceiling, and there is a call-and-response quality to it that suggests the ceiling sparrows are doing what birds in complex spaces do: triangulating, building a constant low-resolution map of the undercroft's inhabited air, knowing where each other is.

They nest in the tap-root tangles, using root-fiber as material, supplemented by the dried-down husks of the pale ground-cover leaves that fall and collect in the floor-crevices. Their nests are difficult to distinguish from the root-work around them. This is probably intentional at some level, some ancestral avoidance of visibility encoded into their building. Several nest-sites have been incorporated into the root-ceiling in a way that makes them structurally part of it — the birds choosing angles and attachment points that, by accident or instinct or some third thing, have reinforced the tap-root clusters around them. The same reports that note the vines reinforcing the duct-joints note the bird-nests, and then pass on without acting. There are a lot of things in Vel Shamash that appear in reports and do not get acted on, because they are doing their work already, without anyone's assistance, and action would only be interference.

Their relationship with the beetles is the central ecological fact of the ceiling-sparrows' existence in Vel Shamash. They take the ceiling-workers, the large dark beetles, from the tap roots. They take whatever they can find in the floor-cracks during the quiet hours. They have organized themselves around the beetle population's rhythms in a way that has been going on long enough that it no longer looks like predation — it looks like a system, a collaboration, something with the quality of an arrangement that both parties have settled into. The beetles do not seem to avoid the birds. The birds do not seem to hunt with urgency. Between them, they process the organic matter of the undercroft's surfaces, and what they process returns, eventually, to the root-system, which is the infrastructure, which is the city.

---

The crawlers are harder.

Not harder to observe — they are visible, slow, unhidden, moving through the gap-spaces in the deliberate way of things that have no predators worth worrying about. Harder to characterize, to hold in the mind as a single image, because they are not quite any one thing.

They are pale, as the description suggests — a pale that goes further than mere absence of color. Their bodies have an almost translucent quality in sections, particularly along the lateral flanks where the exoskeleton thins, where you can see, or almost see, the suggestion of internal structure. The legs — and there are many, the count varies between observed individuals in a way that suggests either variation within the species or multiple species being called by a single name — are long relative to the body, articulated to fold compactly when at rest against the floor. At rest, a crawler looks like a pale stone. In motion, it looks like a pale stone moving, which should be startling but somehow isn't, because the motion is so unhurried, so inevitable, that the mind accommodates it without alarm.

Their heads, if the anterior end can be called that, are low and wide, the mouthparts adapted for ground-level feeding — the pale leaf-flesh of the ground-cover plants, the accumulated organic material in the floor-cracks, possibly root-fibers when other options are limited. They have no obvious eyes. This is the thing visitors notice and can't stop thinking about: the crawlers navigate Vel Shamash without eyes, without what humans would call sight, and they do it with a confidence that humans with full visual apparatus and electric lights rarely manage in new spaces. They know where the walls are. They know where the scaffold is. They know where the vine has reached the lowest rung without checking, without any apparent sensing apparatus that could be pointed to and called a sensor. They simply know, and move accordingly, and they have been moving accordingly long enough that their knowing has the character of fact.

Chemoreception, probably. Some vibration-sensitivity in the legs and the body-surface, reading the floor of the city through contact, building a spatial model through touch and chemistry that is as detailed and accurate as sight but arrived at differently. Workers who have tried to catch crawlers — out of curiosity, not malice — have not found it easy. Not because the crawlers are fast. Because they are rarely where you reach. Something in their sensing apparatus provides enough warning that they have moved, by the time you look, to a location where being was apparently always the intention.

They are ancient. That's the feeling the workers have about them, and it's not inaccurate — the crawler-species of the undercroft predate the city itself, are soil-cave fauna that the city expanded into, that found in the city a better-provisioned version of what they had already evolved for. They don't need the city. They use it. They are not adapted to the city; the city has not changed them. They were ready for this when it arrived, because what they were ready for was darkness and density and slow organic processes, and the city provides all three. In this sense, the crawlers are the most purely native inhabitants of Vel Shamash, more native than the beetles who have adapted, more native than the birds who came deliberately, more native than the humans who built it. The crawlers simply continued being what they were. The city grew up around them, and they incorporated that into their map, and moved on.

At certain hours — not exactly the quiet hours, but the hours at the edges of quiet, when one shift has just departed and another has not quite arrived — the crawlers are most visible in the gap-spaces. Three or four of them crossing a stretch of floor together, which is not coordination exactly, but convergence on the same patch of ground-cover, the same organic-rich floor-crevice. They move through each other's paths without collision. No competition is visible between them. If one arrives at a feeding site while another is there, it waits — not in the sense of holding still and watching, but in the sense of attending to other things, taking in information from the floor, moving along the periphery. When the other departs, it proceeds. There is a patience in this that is not patience exactly, because patience implies a desire being deferred, and it is not clear that the crawlers experience desire in any form that would be recognized. They are systems. They function. The functioning is what they are.

---

Below the crawlers — below everything, or perhaps more accurately between everything, in the spaces that have no name because they have no visible inhabitants — live the organisms that make the whole system possible and never appear in any form that can be directly observed.

The fungal networks, first. The symbiotic film stretched thin as membrane through which the light filters, yes, but also the connections running through the root-mass itself, through the compressed fiber of the walls and the floor, through the soil of the undercroft in its deeper layers. A mycelial network so extensive that trying to describe where it begins and ends is like trying to describe where a body's nervous system begins and ends — it begins and ends everywhere, it is inside the system it serves, it is not separable from what it connects. The root-system of the city runs on it. The root-system of the upper city, extending down through the roof of the undercroft, exchanges chemistry with it. The pale ground-cover plants take from it and return to it. The beetles process material into forms it can use. The crawlers' waste enriches the soil the network mines. The birds' nests decay eventually into the floor and become part of the cycle.

The fungal network is not alive in any way that looks like life from the inside of a human sensory system. It doesn't move at a visible scale. It doesn't respond to presence in ways that presence usually triggers. And yet it is the city's metabolism, the thing that keeps the vegetation from dying in conditions that should kill it, that keeps the air at a consistent chemistry, that distributes mineral nutrition through the root-mass to places where the root-mass thins and would otherwise fail. The botanists who come to study Vel Shamash from the surface don't fully understand it. Their documentation is extensive and their explanations remain partial. The network does what it does for reasons that are, at some level, chemical, and at another level — the level of accumulated evolutionary relationship between the fungal species and the plant species and the soil organisms and the animal inhabitants — not reducible to chemistry, or not yet.

It is, in the end, what you stand on when you walk the root-fiber floor of Vel Shamash. The city is built on a relationship. The relationship extends in every direction, is the ground, is the walls, is the filtered light coming through the root-ceiling, is the dry air that should be too dry and isn't. Everything that lives here lives inside it, including the inhabitants who built the ramps and the iron ductwork and the processing facilities, who have shift-change every six hours and speak quietly and point at things in the root-weave and make notes and then leave. Including them.

---

The humans of Vel Shamash are, in some ways, the strangest things living there.

They are the only inhabitants who know they are in a city. They are the only inhabitants who built anything deliberately, who made decisions about where walls would go and where ramps would lead and what the machines would process. They are the only inhabitants for whom the place requires explanation, for whom arriving means understanding something rather than simply continuing to be somewhere familiar. They carry clocks, because the underground does not provide time in the surface sense — no light-cycle, no temperature-cycle, only the machine-rhythms they themselves established. They carry lights, supplementing the diffuse filtered glow with focused beams, because their eyes are not the ceiling-sparrows' eyes. They wear layers, because the stable temperature of the undercroft is stable at a degree that human bodies feel as cool-bordering-on-cold when inactive.

They are not adapted to Vel Shamash. They adapt to it — which is a different thing, a conscious and continuous effort, an ongoing negotiation between what their bodies expect and what the undercroft provides. Workers who have been in Vel Shamash for years describe a shift that happens around the three-month mark: the machine-sound drops below notice, and the beetle-sound in the walls emerges from below it, and they begin to hear the undercroft the way it actually sounds, rather than the way new arrivals hear it. Around the same time, the ceiling-sparrows' calls begin to function informationally rather than ornamentally — not consciously, but practically: a clustering of calls from one section of ceiling means something is moving through the ductwork up there, maintenance or a fault, and experienced workers look up before they know why they're looking. The birds have been read. The workers have learned to read them without knowing they've learned.

This is the direction of travel for the humans of Vel Shamash. Not adaptation in the biological sense — they are not growing larger eyes, not losing unnecessary pigment, not developing chemoreception in their feet. But something is happening over the years of working there, a gradual calibration of perception to environment, an incorporation of the undercroft's rhythms and signals into the body's background processing. They are being shaped by the city in the way that everything in the city is being shaped by the city — slowly, through accumulation, through the patient pressure of living inside a particular system long enough that the system becomes the default.

The newest workers bring the surface in with them, visible in how they walk (cautiously, looking at the ceiling, looking at the crawlers, startling at the birds), visible in how they speak (louder than necessary, the underground air confusing their distance-calibration), visible in the way they run their hands along the root-fiber walls with an uncertainty that experienced workers have lost. The experienced workers move through the undercroft the way the crawlers move through it — not identically, not without light, not without maps, but with the same quality of unhurried accuracy, the same sense of knowing where things are before they arrive. The city has taught them. The city teaches everything that stays.

There are workers who have been in Vel Shamash for fifteen, twenty years, who surface occasionally for necessities and find the surface strange now — too bright, too loud, too variable. The temperature fluctuates and their bodies, calibrated to the constant cool, register this as information they don't know what to do with. The light changes hour by hour, season by season, and their eyes have learned to rely on the honest diffuse glow of the filtered undercroft, and the surface light seems to be telling them something, urgently, always, that they no longer need to know. They come back down. They descend the root-fiber ramp and the air closes around them and is what it always is, and the machine-sound is what it always is, and somewhere in the ceiling-root above their heads a ceiling-sparrow makes its small sound, its triangulating sound, its sound that says *I am here, where are you*, and the answer comes from the far section, accurate as always, and this is the city doing what it does.

---

What lives in Vel Shamash is: a system.

Not a metaphor — a literal system, a network of exchange so intricate and interdependent that the categories *plant*, *animal*, *human*, *infrastructure* are useful locally and misleading at the level of the whole. The beetles process material the fungal network can use. The fungal network sustains the root-mass. The root-mass is the walls. The walls contain the city. The city contains the beetles. The vines reinforce the ducts nobody asked them to reinforce. The birds read the space and the workers read the birds. The crawlers know the floor and the floor knows the crawlers and the floor is the ceiling of something deeper that nobody has fully mapped.

The system is not finished. This is perhaps its most important characteristic. It is in process. The root-panels in the newest sections are still becoming walls. The vines are still becoming infrastructure. The workers are still becoming undercroft workers, calibrating down toward the thing the ceiling-sparrows already are, which is: perfectly at home in a place that is still deciding what it will be.

The city is being built and the city is building itself, and what lives in it is the building — the ongoing, unhurried, beetle-patient, vine-methodical, crawler-deliberate, ceiling-sparrow-attentive process of a place becoming more itself. Whatever currently inhabits Vel Shamash is the larval form of whatever will inhabit it. This includes the beetles. This includes the network. This includes the humans with their six-hour shifts and their clocks and their notes.

The place is on its way to somewhere. Everything in it is going with it.
