## What Lives Here

---

The first thing to understand is that the residents do not think of themselves as residents.

Residency implies a dwelling that exists prior to the dweller, a vacancy chosen and filled. Nothing here works that way. The organisms that live in the Hanging Gardens are not inhabitants of a place; they are, in a literal and non-metaphorical sense, constituents of it. The frog whose underside has rested on the same moss-rimmed basin ledge for eleven years is not sitting *in* the Gardens the way you might sit in a room. The frog is part of what the Gardens currently *is*. Remove it and you remove something from the definition of the place, the way removing a load-bearing stone changes not just what the arch supports but what the arch *is*. The distinction between organism and environment has, in this place, become a distinction without much practical content.

This is worth establishing before anything else, because it changes how you understand everything that follows. These are not animals that have moved into a structure. They are processes that have been running in a location for so long that the location and the processes have become inextricable from each other. You are not looking at residents. You are looking at the place looking back at you through many eyes, most of them uninterested.

---

### The Frogs

They are everywhere, and the description of their sound as geological is not an exaggeration but an understatement, because geological processes at least pause — they speed and slow with temperature and pressure and the movement of tectonic plates. The frog-sound does not pause. It has not paused in any meaningful sense for longer than anyone alive can verify, and the indications are that it has been continuous in something like its current form for a very long time before that.

There are many species. This is important. The chorus is not a single voice multiplied but a genuine composition in which distinct vocal characters layer and interlock — the low, almost subsonic pulse of the large basin-frogs that barely seem to breathe between calls, the rapid-fire staccato of small frogs that live in the moss channels, the intermittent mournful tone of the mid-level frogs that occupy the wall-crevices and call in long slow phrases with silences between them long enough that you might think they have stopped, and then they haven't. Each species occupies its own acoustic register the way coral species occupy their own depth bands. The layering is not accidental. The calls are partially shaped for distinctness *in this environment*, in this particular acoustic space, with its reverberant stone walls and its moisture-thickened air. The frogs have, over long time, tuned themselves to the room.

Individually, a Garden frog is a small unremarkable animal, plump and wet-skinned and mostly still. They spend enormous proportions of their lives motionless on surfaces, calling or not calling, waiting. Their eyes are gold-rimmed and their expressions, to the degree that frogs have expressions, communicate nothing legible. They are good at being still. Stillness is their primary mode of existence. When they move — and they do move, for water, for food, for the specific crevice where a female has been calling, for reasons indiscernible — they do so with a sudden efficiency that seems slightly at odds with all the stillness that preceded it: a quick, decisive repositioning, and then stillness again, the new position held as though it had always been the position.

Their skin is the color of the moss they rest on, or the stone they rest on, or the bark they rest on. This is not coincidence. It is the accumulated result of every frog in a thousand generations whose color was slightly wrong for its preferred surface, and who was therefore slightly more likely to be taken by a bird before it could reproduce. The survivors are those whose coloration was right. Over time the populations have matched their environments so well that finding individual frogs requires learning to look for the specific wrongness of their shape — the slight bulge of a frog-profile that doesn't quite resolve into bark or moss — rather than looking for color contrast. You walk past thousands of them and see dozens.

They lay their eggs in the moss channels and the basin margins and the small still water that collects wherever water can collect. The eggs are transparent, each containing a dark comma of developing tadpole, clustered in clear masses attached to submerged surfaces. The tadpoles, when they emerge, are briefly among the most numerous animals in the place — tiny black swimmers that fill every water surface, grazing on algae, becoming in turn food for larger tadpoles and for the many things that eat tadpoles. Most of them do not survive. The ones that do are, within a few months, sitting motionless on ledges calling into the dark.

The frogs do not have names for themselves or for their calling or for the sound the place makes. They have no concept of the sound the place makes. They are the sound the place makes.

### The Insects

To call them atmospheric is, again, accurate, but it captures only the spatial dimension of their presence and nothing of its quality. The insects of the Gardens are not a background condition. They are, in terms of raw biological mass and metabolic throughput and sheer generative fecundity, arguably the dominant life form of the place, if dominance means anything in a system this integrated. They are simply harder to attend to, individually, because they are so numerous and so various and so constantly in motion that individual attention keeps losing its grip.

The beetles are the ones that catch the light. In any moment of silver illumination, in any gap of brighter grey, you will see glints — moving points of metallic color, iridescent blue-green and deep red-black and the strange not-quite-gold of chitin under overcast sky. Some of the beetles are large, or large enough to register: the heavy-bodied decomposers that move through the lower substrate layer, the polished dark climbers that ascend the walls with mechanical persistence, the ones with elaborate horn-structures whose purposes are precisely what you expect. Many more are tiny, below easy visibility, felt more as a tickling on skin and a sense of more-than-you-can-see. There are species that have been in this system long enough that they exist nowhere else — their entire evolutionary history and present reality is this place, these passages, these specific substrates. They are, in a meaningful sense, made of the place the way the place is made of them.

The flies constitute their own sub-ecology, with the larval stages doing the crucial work of breaking down the organic material that would otherwise accumulate beyond the place's capacity to incorporate it. The Gardens do not smell of rot in any overwhelming sense because the flies are so efficient at their work, so thoroughly adapted to finding and processing every available dead thing, that decomposition is kept current. The smell is present — it would be, in a place with this density of life and death — but it is held at a low sweet note rather than allowed to build. The flies are, in this sense, sanitation workers, and they are relentless. They are also, at some stages, important food sources for nearly everything else that eats small moving things.

The crickets you hear without easily seeing, their calls a constant rasp that sits in the high-middle register of the total sound — individual cricket calls are unremarkable, but there are so many of them in slightly different positions throughout the structure that what you hear is a kind of aggregate cricket, a statistical creature assembled from thousands of individuals each of whom is producing its small ordinary sound in its small ordinary way. There are species that have gone eyeless across generations in the deeper chambers, investing in sensory resources better suited to navigation in near-darkness: more sensitive antennae, finer vibration-sensing along the leg. Their calls in the deeper passages have a different quality, the same mechanical source but changed by the different acoustics, by the way sound moves differently through denser, wetter, more enclosed air.

The colony-builders — not the termites whose work is foundational history here, but the continuing colony organisms that maintain their own enclaves within the structure — do their work invisibly, which is how they prefer it. You know them by their architecture: the papery swellings in high corners, the earthen tubes traversing walls, the careful sealed chambers where the work of reproduction proceeds in the dark, the foraging lines that move across every surface carrying material in both directions with a coordination that has no coordinator. Up close, a foraging line is individuals. At any distance, it is a single thing doing a purposeful thing, and the intelligence of that thing — where it decides to go, what it decides to carry, when it decides to shift routes — is not located in any of the individuals but in the pattern that emerges from all of them together. This is familiar behavior; it is not magic. But in the Gardens, where the colony-builders have been running this distributed cognition for so many generations in this specific space, their operations have a quality of deep local knowledge that is hard to dismiss. They know, in the aggregate sense in which they can know anything, where the food is. They know where the water flows. They know, apparently, which wall sections are beginning to weaken, because the repair tubes show up there before the weakness is obvious. Whether this is prescience or simply very good perception is a question for which the individual insects offer no commentary.

### The Gastropods

You learn to step carefully, not because the gastropods are fragile — they are not, exactly — but because the act of stepping on a snail or a slug feels like a violation of a more specific kind here, where nothing is incidental. They are everywhere underfoot, on every near-horizontal surface with any moisture, leaving their silver trails across stone and over each other and through moss in patterns that reflect their individual decisions: here a source of moisture, here fungal growth, here the overlap of several trails that suggests a good feeding site drawn many gastropods over many hours.

The snails of the Gardens are primarily small to medium — nothing dramatic in size, nothing that would make a naturalist's eyes widen — but their variety is considerable. Each microhabitat has its own population with its own character: the slightly flatter shells of the moss-bed species, more streamlined for moving through dense fibrous growth; the taller spires of the species that spends most of its time on vertical stone faces, where the geometry of balance is different; the thick-lipped, tightly-sealed species of the outer walls where temperature variation is greater and moisture less guaranteed, which can close themselves down for long periods and simply wait. Their shells carry color in a muted register — browns and creams and soft greys, sometimes faint banding, rarely anything striking — but this is color that rewards looking, once you have adjusted your expectations to the light levels of the place. In the few moments of better illumination, the variation is considerable.

The slugs lack even this visual interest and are therefore, paradoxically, more interesting to watch, because all you have is behavior. They are prodigious eaters of fungus, of decaying plant material, of the algal films that grow on every wet surface. A large slug working its way across a patch of substrate is entirely committed to the task, its body moving with a continuous slow muscular wave, its tentacles extended and working, its radula rasping away at surfaces too small and soft to be described as scraping. They eat enormous quantities relative to their size. They are important. The place would be different without the throughput they represent — the conversion of fungal and bacterial and algal growth into animal tissue that then becomes available to everything that eats slugs, and there is much that eats slugs.

They mate in the air.

This is one of the specific wonders of the Gardens: the hanging mating of certain slug species, which ascend to some appropriate structure — a wall-bracket of old colony material, the underside of a broad ledge — and lower themselves together on a long doubled string of their own mucus, turning slowly in the damp air, their bodies intertwined in a spiral, both of them simultaneously contributing and receiving genetic material in the reciprocal hermaphroditism of their kind. They hang there for a long time. The air moves them gently. Their mucus rope catches what light there is and gleams. There is something about this that the Gardens seems to have specifically enabled — the high walls, the enclosed spaces, the particular humidity that makes the mucus reliable across a long descent — and whether this is intentional is a question that means nothing here, where nothing is intended and everything is purposeful.

### The Lizards

They are the quick adjusters, the repositioners, the ones you see only in motion because in stillness they become wall. There is a visual grammar to spotting them: look not for the animal but for the movement, the small sudden shift at the edge of attention that resolves, when you turn toward it, into a lizard that is now completely still and entirely confident in its stillness, its flanks barely moving with its small fast breath, its eye a dark point of attention directed at you.

The lizards of the Gardens are almost entirely insectivorous — which is to say, they exist to metabolize the insect abundance into a different form of animal tissue, which then becomes available to the birds and the small mammals and the larger lizards. They are a link. They are a crucial link, and they are numerous, but their role is connective more than generative. They connect the insect layer to the vertebrate layer and they do this with great efficiency and no apparent interest in being observed doing it.

Their color is the stone-color and the bark-color and the shadow-color of the specific surface they most frequently occupy, and their camouflage is slightly different for each population, each micro-niche having produced, over generations of selection, a slightly different local match. There is one population in the deepest inner chambers whose skin has a faint luminous quality — not bioluminescence, but something more like the way a pale stone can seem to hold light after the light source has moved. It is hard to describe and harder to explain. It appears to confer no particular advantage in any way currently understood. It may be an accident of genetic drift in a small, somewhat isolated population. It may be something else. The lizard in question is small, fast, and has no opinion to offer.

The larger lizard — there is one species significantly bigger than the rest, a stocky animal the length of your forearm with a heavy jaw and a slow deliberate movement that makes it look ancient even when it is young — is an ambush predator of the other lizards, of frogs, of any small mammal unwise enough to cross an open floor when this animal is present. It spends most of its time not hunting. It sits in particular locations — specific ledge corners, specific channel margins — with the settled authority of something that has been sitting in those locations for a very long time, which it has. Individual animals of this species may have held the same territory for decades. The location knows them. They know the location. They are its specific, embodied familiarity with itself.

### The Birds

They are mostly sound. This is not quite fair — they are also bodies, vivid and quick-moving, seen in the better-lit upper regions of the taller chambers and in the occasional open area where the canopy is thin. But they reach you first as sound, and their sound is qualitatively different from the frog-sound and the insect-sound: it is communicative, variable, apparently contentious, addressed. The frogs call into the general air. The birds call at each other, specifically.

The nesting species are wall-adapted — built and evolved for vertical surfaces, for crevices, for the rough irregular texture of built biological material. Their nests are structures woven into structures, using the existing irregular surface as both anchor and partial wall, adding their own woven grasses and fibrous plant material and occasionally the shed exoskeleton parts of large insects, which are remarkably stiff and light. The nests are not particularly hidden, because in this place there are few predators that operate from above, and the birds nest at heights that most ground-level predators cannot easily reach. The eggs are speckled in the grey-green-brown of the chamber surfaces, which is the standard economy of bird eggs and is as effective here as anywhere.

The birds that do not nest here but use the Gardens as foraging ground — following the insect abundance through from outside, coming through the gaps in the canopy to harvest the density within — represent a daily passage, a tide of visitors that peaks in the mid-morning and tapers through afternoon. These birds do not belong to the place the way the resident species do. They have a more transactional relationship with it. They come for the insects and they leave with insects and they return to wherever they come from and they are not, in the deep sense, part of what the Gardens is. The resident birds know this. There is a distinct social texture to the interaction between resident and transient bird species, a kind of practiced mutual tolerance that contains within it the clear understanding of who lives here and who is visiting.

The bird most specific to the Gardens — the one that exists nowhere else in anything like this form — is a small, grey-brown, almost impossibly quiet species that has been here so long it has partially lost the willingness to use its wings. It walks. It walks everywhere, on the ground and up walls and, improbably, upside-down along the undersides of broad ledges, using its feet and the specific grip of its toes on the textured surface and a constant micro-adjustment of its weight distribution. It eats anything small enough to swallow — insects, small slugs, frog eggs, the fungal bodies of the larger mushroom species that grow in the lower chambers. It nests in the deepest crevices, where even the dim light does not reach. Its eggs are white, because in complete darkness there is no selection pressure for camouflage and the pigment is expensive. It makes no sound that is easily heard. It knows where everything is.

### The Small Mammals

They are the night-shift, or more precisely the dark-shift, because in the Gardens there is not much practical difference between night and the deeper chambers, which maintain their near-darkness regardless of what the sky outside is doing. They have been adapting to this for long enough that several species have arrived at the convergent solution of large eyes backed by reflective layers and long vibrissae and a neurological investment in auditory processing that allows them to build, moment to moment, a working model of the space around them from sound alone.

The pale ones are the most obviously adapted: fur that has gone white or cream or the faintest grey over generations without strong sunlight or the selective pressure of visual predators, eyes that are disproportionately large in their small faces, giving them an appearance of perpetual intensity that does not correspond to any particular alertness — it is simply what their faces look like, the necessary size for the light-gathering they require. They move through the underpassages with a quick, confident fluency that comes from knowing the routes exactly, from having mapped the space through thousands of passages and having that map updated continuously by the whiskers and the ears and the moment-to-moment acoustic reflection of their own small sounds.

They are not solitary. They live in extended family groups that share territories through the lower passages, marking and recognizing and interacting with a social complexity that takes place almost entirely in the dark and in registers of communication — ultrasonic, olfactory, tactile — that human senses are not built to receive. The social life of the small mammals is happening, continuously, in parallel with the visible life of the place, at a layer of reality that is present but opaque. They know each other well. They have extensive opinions about each other. The passage of a dominant animal through a lower tunnel produces ripples of social adjustment in every other animal present that no observer with human senses can easily detect.

They eat everything that doesn't eat them. They are eaten by everything large enough to eat them. They are, again, links: processors of insect and gastropod and frog-egg, converters of the lower trophic levels into forms available to the larger lizard, to the occasional large predator that descends from outside to work the place, to each other, in the moments when the social complexity breaks down into violence.

They reproduce constantly. The Gardens supports them in this. There is always enough.

### The Things You Don't See

There are organisms here that you will not see, or will not recognize as organisms when you see them.

The fungi are everywhere and are important. They are the decomposers that work in parallel with the insects and the slugs, breaking down dead material in the wet dark places, releasing nutrients that feed the moss and the plants and the bacteria, threading the substrate with mycelial networks that are in a real sense another kind of structural material — not as strong as the calcium carbonate of the shell-builders, but present everywhere the shell-builders are not, connecting substrate particles, wicking moisture, metabolizing. The fruiting bodies that emerge into visibility — small and pale and easily missed — are the visible expression of something that is mostly invisible, mostly underground, mostly doing its work without any part of it that you could point to and say: that is the organism.

The bacterial films on every wet surface are a different order of invisibility: too small to see, but the smell of them is present, the green-black stain of them is the color the stone has where it is wet, the work of them is the chemical processing that occurs between larger organisms and their food. They are, in terms of metabolic activity and evolutionary history, the most significant organisms in the place. They were doing chemistry here before anything else arrived. They will be doing chemistry after everything else is gone.

And there are the things that live in the frogs — the parasites and commensals that have their own ecologies within the bodies of the frog populations, their own evolutionary pressures, their own internal diversity. There are the mites on the beetle-bodies and the micro-organisms in the gut-fauna of the small mammals and the fungi that grow in the gill structures of the snails. There is, if you are willing to follow the scale of observation downward without limit, no floor. There is always more. The place folds in on itself at every scale, and at each scale there is life, and the life at each scale is integrated with the life at every other scale in ways that keep turning out to be more important than you thought.

---

The residents of the Hanging Gardens are, in the end, the Hanging Gardens. This is not mysticism. It is just what happens when organisms live somewhere long enough, and change it enough, and are changed by it enough. The place is a fact of biology the way a body is a fact of biology — complex, self-maintaining, irreducibly plural, and, in some functional sense that does not require a nervous system or anything like consciousness, *knowing*. It knows how to keep running. It knows how to recover from small disturbances. It knows how to absorb novelty without losing continuity.

Whether it knows you are there is a question that the place does not particularly address. The frogs call. The insects hum. The birds conduct their disputes in the upper chambers. The small mammals move through the dark with their elaborate invisible sociality. The slugs hang in the air on threads of silver mucus, turning slowly together.

You are, for the duration of your stay, part of what the place currently is.

You will not leave a detectable mark.

The sound will continue.
