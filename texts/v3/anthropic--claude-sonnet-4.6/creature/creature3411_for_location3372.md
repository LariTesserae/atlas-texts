## The People of the Trellis Town

---

They are not remarkable to look at. This is the first thing, and it takes a while to understand why it surprises you — because you have come through a place of such particular strangeness, such vegetable density and patient moisture, that you expect its people to match. You expect them to be strange in some corresponding way, greenish or slow-moving or somehow rooted. They are not. They look like people. They wear ordinary clothing in muted colors, the usual dyes of people who make some of their own cloth and have learned which colors hold and which fade. Their faces are the faces of a temperate-climate people — not pale exactly, but not deeply weathered either, as if the town's compressed temperature range has extended also to their complexions. They do not glow. They do not have lichen on them.

What they have is a quality of *settledness* that takes time to read correctly. You might first mistake it for incuriosity, for the closed quality of a community that does not welcome outsiders. It is not that. When they look at you — and they do look at you, when you arrive, though they don't stop what they're doing to do it — the look is steady and complete and then, having taken you in, simply continues on past you. They have noted you. They have no urgent conclusions to draw. You are welcome to be here and they will be getting on with what they were doing.

This is the quality the place has given them, or perhaps it is the quality that allows people to stay here: the capacity to be fully in process without needing the process to resolve.

---

### How They Hold Themselves

There is a particular posture you notice after a while. Not a slouch, not a stoop — but a kind of lowered center of gravity, a slight forward inclination, as if they are always preparing to look at something close at hand. Which they mostly are. The work of this place is close work: examining root growth, checking mortar, listening to the drainage, feeling the dampness of a wall with the back of a hand. They spend a great deal of time attending to surfaces. Their posture has learned this.

Their hands are generally occupied or recently occupied. Even in conversation — and the conversations you observe are short, direct, often held while both parties are doing something else — the hands continue. Checking something. Holding something. Adjusting something with the small, automatic adjustments of people who have done a thing so many times that the body performs it without consultation.

Their feet are quiet. You notice this because the streets are quiet, and you notice the streets are quiet partly because you cannot hear their footsteps. They move in a way that makes very little noise, and this does not seem deliberate — not tiptoeing, not the exaggerated care of someone trying not to wake a sleeper — but simply habitual. The ground here is soft underfoot, and they have come to use that softness, to step with just the weight they mean to put down.

---

### What They Know

They know the plants the way other people know their neighbors. Not in a mystical sense — they are not in communion, not believers in any green religion you can detect — but in the way of long practical acquaintance. They know which mosses indicate which moisture levels, and they check them the way you might check a gauge. They know which ferns on which walls have been pushing into the window glass and which ones have reached an accommodation with it. They notice, without appearing to look, when something has changed — a new species appearing in a corner that didn't have it before, a section of wall-lichen gone pale in a way that suggests something has altered in the stone beneath.

This knowledge is not written down anywhere you can find. There are records in this town, but they are records of the practical kind: maintenance logs, water levels in the cistern, outputs from the textile work, accounts of trade. The plant knowledge is not in those records. It is in the people. It is the kind of knowledge that transfers by proximity, by watching someone notice something and asking what they saw, by developing over years the habit of looking in the same ways. It is a knowledge with no formal edges, no curriculum. You have it or you are still getting it.

They know the drainage the way you know your own breathing — not consciously, until something changes. They hear a shift in the quality of the trickle below the streets, and they stop, briefly, and then go to check something. The water infrastructure of this place is continuous, and they maintain it continuously, and the maintaining is not an interruption of daily life but is daily life.

They know time differently. Not slowly — they are not slow people, exactly — but with a longer sense of interval. They make decisions that will bear fruit in a decade. They repair things in ways that are meant to hold for thirty years. When they assess a situation, they assess it at multiple timescales simultaneously, and this has given them a habit of not rushing to conclusions, because conclusions that hold at the three-year scale may not hold at the thirty-year scale, and they have learned to wait to see which scale is operative.

---

### The Generations

The older ones — and there are old people here, very old, in the specific way of people who have not been ground down by a hard climate — carry the fullest version of this accumulated reading of the place. They move through the town in a state of continuous reference. Every surface they pass is a document they can read. The dark band in the north-wall lichen: they were children when that happened, when the foundry ran harder for whatever reason it ran harder, and they remember it through the smell first and the mark on the wall second, and now the mark and the memory are the same thing.

They do not necessarily talk about this. They are not a culture of storytellers, particularly. You might spend an evening with one of the older residents and come away with very little narrative. What you would have is a strong impression that they were in a specific relation to a specific place, and that this relation had been continuous and attentive for so long that the person and the place had developed a genuine mutual comprehension — each knowing the other's patterns, each having adjusted to and been adjusted by the other.

The younger ones are in the process of learning to read the same texts. They pay attention, but their attention has not yet become automatic. You see them working at it: pausing to look at something a little too deliberately, asking questions with the slightly formal phrasing of people still learning what questions to ask. They are earnest in a way the older ones have moved past, not because the older ones care less but because they have metabolized the caring — it runs in them without requiring conscious allocation.

The children, when you see them, are different in the way children everywhere are different: noisier, less precise in their movements, more interested in the spaces than the surfaces. They play in the open air of the streets, in the genuine open air that the plant growth has left in the middle of things. They climb on the thick ivy stems at the corners of buildings, which hold them easily. They put their faces very close to the moss on the walls and look at things inside it that you cannot quite see. They are being made by this place even now, in the ordinary way of children: absorbing its rhythms, learning which sounds are normal and which require attention, beginning without knowing it the long project of coming to know this particular ground.

---

### How They Work

The work divides itself quietly and without fuss that you can see. It is not that there are no distinctions — there are people who work the looms and people who work the foundry and people who manage the water and people who maintain the building surfaces, and these are distinct things — but the distinctions don't seem to carry much weight. People move between kinds of work without the ceremony of a status change. The person tending the roof-moss on a Monday may be at the foundry on a Thursday, if something needs doing there. The knowledge required for each is different, but the underlying quality of attention is the same, and they all have that.

The textile work is the work most people outside the town know this town for, if they know it at all. The cloth that comes out of here is distinctive — a weight and a texture that comes from the particular mildew that has lived in the looms long enough to become part of their process, and the long-staple fiber they grow in small plots at the valley's edge, and the water they use in the processing, which carries the mineral signature of the sandstone channels. You can't replicate it elsewhere because it is made of elsewhere. It is made of this specific place in every practical sense.

The foundry work is the surprise: this much green, this much moisture and moderation, and then a hot core of metal work at the center of it. They make things of lasting utility — fittings, tools, the brass components of water systems and looms — and they make them to a standard that assumes they will be used for a generation and then passed on. The foundry workers have the same quiet demeanor as everyone else but carry something more of heat in them, a slight scorchedness, an ease with extreme conditions that reads oddly against the general cool temperament of the place.

The water work is never finished and never starts; it is simply ongoing. The people who tend the drainage and the cistern and the distribution pipes move through the town with the particular authority of people who understand the system that underlies everything. They know where the water goes. They know how the town breathes. They make adjustments in small increments, and the adjustments accumulate over years into a state of function so well-maintained that most residents do not think about it at all, which is exactly how the water workers know they are doing it right.

---

### What They Do in the Evenings

The lights in the workshop buildings and along the main streets are algae-greened and warm and they make the town in the evening look like something seen through a developing photograph. People are inside mostly. The conversations you can hear through walls and window glass are quiet and incomplete — you hear the rhythm more than the content, the gentle back-and-forth of people who have said many things to each other already and are now in the comfortable territory beyond necessary explanation.

They eat simply. The valley is not abundant in food, and the town does not try to make it more abundant than it is. What they grow they eat, and what they cannot grow they trade for, and they have calibrated their appetites over generations to match their supply in a way that is not deprivation and is not excess. There is something almost physiological about this calibration — they don't seem to fight it, don't seem to long for more, don't seem to be exercising restraint. They simply require about what is available. The town has trained them to this, or they have trained themselves, or both over such a long time that the causation is irretrievable.

Some of them read, in the evenings. There is a collecting point for books and papers — not quite a library, more of a deposit and withdrawal system without formal organization — and people come and go from it with the same casual purposefulness they bring to everything else. There is no performance of learning here, no visible intellectual culture. What reading happens happens privately, in rooms where the walls hold moisture and the ferns in the window frame have been growing since before whoever is reading was born.

---

### What They Are Like to Talk To

If you make the effort — and it does require a small effort, a willingness to begin without preamble, to say the direct thing and leave space for whatever comes back — they are interesting to talk to in a way that is hard to characterize. They are not witty. They are not, in any conventional sense, warm. They do not perform interest in you, do not ask questions shaped to make you feel attended to.

What they do is actually attend. Which turns out to be quite different from the performance of it. When you say something to one of them they hear it, and they think about it, and they say what they think, and the thing they say has usually been filtered through a perspective so long-established and particular that it arrives slightly at an angle to what you expected — not contrary, not difficult, but oriented differently, toward a different set of concerns, a different timescale. It is disorienting in a productive way, like having someone point out the thing that was always in your peripheral vision.

They do not ask many questions. They ask the questions they actually want answered. This means conversation with them is sparse and somehow high-density — few words but each one placed deliberately, like stones in a path that must cross water.

They are not unhappy people. This may sound like a low bar, but it is worth stating because the place, taken at a glance, might suggest a kind of austerity that tips into grimness. It doesn't. There is contentment here that is not performed, not the contentment of people who have decided to be content, but something more habitual: a long-standing relationship between people and place that has been, on the whole, satisfactory to both parties, and which no one has reason to want to end.

---

### What Has Stayed and What Has Left

Not everyone who has come here has remained. You sense the shape of the ones who left — not from anything the residents say directly, but from the way certain questions are met with a slight pause, a neutral adjustment of expression, before the answer comes. People come to the Trellis Town with various ideas of what it will be, and some of those ideas are not compatible with what it is. People who need a faster rhythm, more conversation, more variation in the quality of their days, more evidence of progress in their work — they tend to find, eventually, that the town does not accommodate this need and will not try to. It does not push them out. It simply continues at its own pace, and the mismatch becomes clear, and they go.

What stays is a particular kind of temperament, or perhaps a temperament that has been shaped into a particular form over years: the capacity to find satisfaction in process rather than event, in maintenance rather than achievement, in the long slow alterations of a place that knows itself. The people who are here are here because they fit here, or because they have grown to fit here, or because they had nowhere else and found, over time, that this nowhere was not nothing.

They are people in right relation to their ground. That is what it comes down to. They are people who have made the calculation — consciously or, more often, simply by living — that knowing one place completely is not a lesser thing than moving through many. They have the rooted assurance of people who have committed, genuinely committed, to a particular set of walls and drains and moss-covered rooftops, to the particular light on an overcast afternoon, to the particular sound of a loom three streets away. They are unremarkable and very specific. They carry in their bodies the record of the place, the same way the walls carry it in theirs.

When you leave the Trellis Town, you carry something of its quality in you for a day or two — a slowness, a tendency to notice surfaces, a slight adjustment in the timescale at which things seem to matter. It fades. But while it lasts, you feel, obscurely, that you have been for a while in the presence of something that has worked out how to persist, and that some small amount of that knowledge has come with you, and that this is more than you expected when you came over the ridge.
