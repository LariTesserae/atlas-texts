# What Lives in the Vent Field

---

Begin with what you cannot see.

This is where most of them are.

---

## The Chemolithotrophs

The base of everything. Not animals. Not quite plants. Not the bacteria you know from textbooks and spoiled food and the abstract reassurance that life is everywhere — though they are bacteria, technically, or close enough that the distinction is a human problem. They live inside the mineral crust, inside the walls of the tubes, inside the bodies of other organisms, inside a chemical gradient that runs from slightly less hot to slightly more hot and back again in cycles too slow for any individual cell to perceive.

They do not eat. They do not eat in the sense that requires objects, prey, the movement of one thing into another. What they do is closer to *conducting*: they pass electrons from molecule to molecule along chains of protein that span their cell membranes, and in that passing they capture something — a kind of voltage, a stored potential — that they use to build themselves out of the sulfur and iron and carbon dioxide that the vents provide. They eat the chemistry. They eat the difference between two oxidation states. They are alive in the sense that they maintain themselves against entropy, but the self they maintain is thin: a membrane, a handful of proteins, a loop of genetic material that has been copied ten billion times and will be copied ten billion more.

They coat everything. Every surface in the caldera below two meters from the rim is coated in them, in mats a few millimeters thick that look like nothing, like discoloration, like the place where the rock has a different mineral texture. They are iridescent sometimes, in the right light — the very faint right light — because they grow in such dense columns that the columns themselves create optical structure, prismatic at scales close to the wavelength of the red vent-glow. A square centimeter of vent-field floor has more of them than there are humans alive. They have no awareness of this. They have no awareness of anything. They are the reason everything else is possible and they do not know it and neither, in any meaningful sense, do they care.

They are the oldest residents. They were here before the tube worms, before the crawlers, before the flat things on the high tube walls. They will be here after. The caldera's tenure is their tenure. When the floor reaches the rim, they will coat the rim. When the rim weathers into the surrounding landscape, they will coat the landscape. They are not going anywhere. They have never been going anywhere. They are the opposite of going anywhere and they are what going anywhere runs on.

## The Tube Worms

*Vestimentifera*, or something like it. The body plan is known, but the body plan is a suggestion.

The tubes were built first. This is important: the tube worms build their housing before they fully build themselves, secreting the chitinous-mineral shaft that will protect them while they are still in larval form, small enough that the current carries them, young enough that they have a mouth. The mouth closes. This is not loss — or it is loss in the same way that leaving a place is loss: the mouth closes because it is no longer needed, because the worm has found its place and begun the process of becoming that place.

Without a mouth, the worm does not eat. This is where the chemolithotrophs become something other than background. The tube worms are full of them — packed into a specialized organ behind where the mouth used to be, a red-tinged mass called the trophosome that takes up most of the worm's interior volume. The chemolithotrophs live inside the worm. The worm lives around the chemolithotrophs. The worm's blood carries sulfide from the vent water to the chemolithotrophs; the chemolithotrophs fix carbon and share it with the worm's tissue; the worm grows; the worm's blood carries more sulfide. The relationship is so complete that neither party is separable from the other in any practical sense. The worm is a farming operation and a farm animal and a farmhouse. The chemolithotrophs are farmers who have forgotten the concept of outdoors.

This is what the pale feathery fans are for — the structures at the tube worms' anterior ends that catch the red glow and hold it. They are respiratory organs, or something continuous with respiratory organs: thin-walled plumes rich in hemoglobin (hence the red, deeper than the vent-glow, internal), extended into the water-that-is-not-water to gather the dissolved gases that will be carried back to the trophosome, to the chemolithotrophs, to the process. When danger comes — when a crawler moves too close, when a pressure wave moves through the fluid — the fans retract in under a second, pulled by longitudinal muscles into the tube where the worm is safe behind its own mineral walls. Then, slowly, over minutes, they re-extend. They test the water first. They have no eyes but they test the water. The distinction between these two facts is less stable than you'd think.

The worms live for decades. Some of them may live for a century, though this is difficult to confirm because you cannot age them without killing them and the vent field does not give up its dead easily. They grow slowly — centimeters per year at best. The largest ones in this caldera are old enough that the events of their youth, if they had the apparatus to remember events and youth, would include eruptions that rearranged the floor they now live on. They were small then. They survived by being in the right crevice. Their descendants occupy the crevices that were formed by the eruption that almost killed their ancestors. The vent field does this kind of thing constantly and calls it nothing, because it calls nothing anything.

The tube worms are not lonely. Loneliness requires a concept of company and its absence. But they are surrounded: by each other, by the crawlers that move between their tubes, by the chemolithotrophs in their tissues. If the vent field has a social structure, the tube worms are its tenement buildings: fixed, load-bearing, occupied by more life than their exterior suggests, slowly accreting toward something that will eventually be indistinguishable from the floor itself.

## The Crawlers

There are six things moving across the nearest surface right now, if you could see it. There are forty more in the immediate vicinity. There are thousands in the caldera. They are not coordinating. They are doing something that looks like coordinating, because they have all solved the same problem the same way and the solution involves moving in the same patterns toward the same resources, but this is convergence, not conspiracy.

The body plan is crustacean-ish: segmented, many-legged, with the general architecture of an isopod or amphipod but pressed flat and pale and stripped of anything not strictly necessary. Their eyes have been reduced to photoreceptors that can detect the difference between vent-glow and not-vent-glow, which is the only distinction available to them, and this is enough. Enough to move toward or away from heat sources. Enough to avoid the tubes with the most active vents. Enough to find the places where the microbial mat is thickest and graze.

They graze the chemolithotrophs. They also eat dead worm tissue, shed fan-structures, the exoskeletons of their own dead, the mineral-coated bodies of previous crawlers, the films that form on mineral surfaces, the films that form on each other. They eat with maxillipeds that look like brushes under magnification, sweeping surfaces with an action that is continuously adapted to surface texture — harder strokes on bare mineral, lighter strokes on living mat, different patterns entirely for the worm-tissue that requires tearing rather than brushing.

They are cannibals as a matter of course and without apparent social consequence. When a crawler dies, it becomes a resource. This is not callousness. Callousness requires a counterfactual: it requires that care was possible and withheld. For the crawlers, care is not in the vocabulary. The vocabulary is: surface, gradient, hot, cooler, eat, flee, molt, mate, die, dissolve back into the system.

The molting is dangerous. Between the old exoskeleton and the hardening of the new one there is an interval — hours, sometimes days — when the crawler is soft-bodied and vulnerable and, not incidentally, highly nutritious. Other crawlers eat them during this interval. There is no irony in the vent field, or there is nothing but irony, depending on how you hold it. The molting crawlers do not hide. There is nowhere to hide. They keep moving. They eat as much as possible. The new exoskeleton is white at first and hardens to the same pale translucency as the old one, and once hard they are themselves again — which is to say, they are the same as before in the way that the same wave is never the same water.

Mating is quick. Eggs are deposited in crevices between tube structures and abandoned there. The larvae are smaller than you could see without assistance and for a period they drift and eat dissolved organics and begin to explore surfaces. Most of them die. The ones that don't have already demonstrated the thing the vent field most rewards: being in a place that did not kill them, and continuing.

## The Flat Things

The strangest. The ones pressed against the upper tube-walls with their edges haloed in structural color.

They are radially symmetrical and very flat and when you look directly at one — when your eyes find it in the dimness by inference and then confirm it by the halo at its margins — you feel briefly that you are looking at something that is not looking back only because it does not have eyes, not because it is unaware. This feeling will pass. It is not trustworthy. It is the mind reaching for faces in configurations that have none. But it is persistent.

They are *something*. Perhaps related to placozoa, the simplest animals known, animals that are barely more than organized sheets of cells — but that comparison is like comparing a cathedral to a brick because both involve masonry. The flat things have structure. The structural color at their margins comes from ridges of transparent material that builds up as they grow, microscopic in dimension, arranged in precise geometry. This geometry is not random. It is not, so far as can be determined, functional. It does not help them eat or mate or adhere to surfaces. It may be a byproduct of the way they grow, a physical artifact of cellular self-organization — or it may be something for which there is not yet a category.

They eat by pressing their undersurface against whatever they are pressed against and absorbing. Microbial mat. Mineral films. The metabolic waste of the tube worms. They move too slowly to observe without time-lapse. In a week, one of them might cross two centimeters of tube wall. They seem to prefer the upper portions of the tubes, where temperatures are lower and the mineral precipitate is thinner — a choice, if it is a choice, that suggests they are tracking a gradient too subtle for you to perceive.

They reproduce by splitting. Without a seam, without apparent effort, over a period of days, one becomes two. The two become four. The population on any given tube structure is probably descended from a single ancestor that arrived — how? as a spore? a detached fragment? a larval stage that looks nothing like the adult? — and spread by this slow multiplication across the available surface, pressing itself flatter as it goes, thinning at the edges where the structural color first appears.

What they know, if knowing is the word: the difference between mineral and organic surface. The difference between the temperature zones on the tube walls. The direction of gravity, probably — they do not fall. Whether they know anything about each other is not clear. They sometimes overlap, one flat thing sliding over the edge of another in the course of their slow traversal. What happens then is unclear because it happens too slowly. The evidence suggests they pass through each other without harm and continue. The evidence does not suggest what this means to them, or whether means is a word that applies.

They are the hardest part of the vent field to think about. The tube worms are comprehensible in their way: they have chosen a life of radical stillness and radical interior complexity, and you can respect that, map it, explain the sulfide exchange and the trophosome and the fan retraction. The crawlers are comprehensible: they are eating and fleeing and mating, the basic grammar of animal life, compressed into a strange environment but legible. The flat things are something else. They are at the edge of the question of what life is for. They are doing something, slowly, in the dark, in the heat, with their iridescent margins and their absorbed-mat meals and their weeks-long crossings of a few centimeters of tube wall. They have been doing it longer than any individual of their kind has been alive. They will keep doing it after the last individual who is alive right now is gone.

The vent field does not find this remarkable. The flat things do not find it remarkable. Remarkable is a concept that requires departure from expectation, and the flat things have no expectations. They have the wall, and the mat, and the slow crossing, and the split that is not death, and then more of the same. This is enough. This is the whole thing. This is all of it, and it is enough.

## The System Itself

The question of what lives here gets harder to answer the longer you look, because the longer you look the more the individual organisms become difficult to separate from the substrate they have made and the substrate has made them and the whole thing is running together into something that the word *ecosystem* almost covers but doesn't quite.

What lives here is: an argument that has been running for thousands of years between heat and chemistry and biological organization, and has not been resolved, and will not be resolved, and the lack of resolution is the point. The chemolithotrophs make organic matter from inorganic. The worms house the chemolithotrophs in exchange for nutrition. The crawlers eat everything the system produces, including each other, and in dying become part of the floor that the worms grow out of. The flat things do something in the upper registers that feeds back into the mat that feeds into everything else. The vent gases that the system runs on are produced by the geology that the organisms have been slowly transforming by their existence in it.

Nothing here is only itself. The worm is also the tube is also the mineral is also the chemolithotroph is also the gradient is also the vent is also the basalt that the vent passes through. The crawlers are also the mat they eat and also the floor they will become. The flat things are also the structural color that splits what little light there is, sending it sideways across the tube surfaces in a thin halo that falls, finally, on the crawlers and the worm-fans and the mat between them.

The light goes somewhere. Everything goes somewhere. The caldera holds it all in.

---

The floor rises.

Slowly, year by year, the mineral precipitate accumulates and the biological matter accumulates in it and the whole packed tessellating mass grows upward toward the rim. It will reach the rim. It will spill over. Whatever is in it at that point — whoever has made their home in the topmost layer of the accumulated caldera floor — will find themselves suddenly somewhere new. Thinner air. Different light. A world that did not select for them.

They will be the vent field's representatives. They will carry in their bodies — in their tubes, their exoskeletons, their flat pressed margins — the logic of the place. The logic that says: darkness is not absence. Density is stability. The distinction between alive and not-alive is a matter of degree. Chemistry is food and food is chemistry and neither of those things requires a mouth.

What will they make of the outside? The outside, probably, will make of them what it makes of everything: an opportunity, briefly; then matter; then something else's floor.

But they are not there yet. They are here. The caldera holds them. The gases hiss. The vent mouths glow their deep not-quite-red. The worm fans extend, millimeter by millimeter, into the mineral-thick water. The crawlers move. The flat things press themselves against the walls and do their slow crossing in the dark.

They are here, and they are exactly what this place requires, and they have always been here, and they will be here when you are not, which is almost certainly already true, which is fine, which is — from where they are, which is everywhere the caldera's inside is — simply the condition, and the condition is enough, and they have made of enough a kind of home.
