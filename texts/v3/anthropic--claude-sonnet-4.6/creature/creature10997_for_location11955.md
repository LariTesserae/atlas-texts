## What Lives Here

---

Start with the fig.

Not the tree itself — the entity the tree implies, which is something much older than the tree and not located in any single body. The fig is two decades old, perhaps three; it arrived as a seed in something's droppings, found the crack, sent down the first root. But what the fig participates in is not two or three decades old. It is the age of figs, which are very old, and the age of walls, which are old in a different way, and the age of whatever it is that keeps making these two find each other — root and crack, root and crack — in every warm courtyard in every warm city since there have been warm cities. Call this entity the Persistence. It does not know it is an entity. It does not need to. It persists.

The Persistence is the primary resident. Everything else is subordinate to it, or hosted by it, or a local expression of it, the way a sentence is an expression of grammar.

---

Then the gecko.

The gecko is an individual. This matters. Among all the courtyard's inhabitants, it is the one most likely to be *this* gecko, specifically — the one with this small scar on the left flank, this particular arrangement of pale spots on the dorsal surface, this exact history of territories claimed and surrendered in the years it has patrolled this wall and the wall behind the building across the seam-alley, and the section of roof coping that bridges them. It has a range. Within that range it has a hierarchy: certain walls are better than others, certain hours belong to it more than to the other geckos in the network of geckos whose territories interlock like tiles across this quarter of the city.

It is old, for a gecko. Its gold eye has watched — though *watched* is not right, because it takes in rather than watches, receives rather than attends — it has *received* many seasons of this courtyard. It was here before the flagstones were begun. It was here, almost certainly, when whoever staked the geometry came in with string and chalk and plans, and it received that too from its position on the upper wall, and when the work paused and the seasons continued, the gecko continued also.

What does the gecko *know*? Nothing, in the sense we mean when we say a person knows something. But it has been modified by everything that has happened here. Its body carries the courtyard's history as a set of calibrated responses: where the shadow falls at each hour, which plants harbor the largest concentrations of small prey, the particular smell of the fig in the week before the figs ripen, which signals the arrival of fruit-flies and thus abundance, and so the gecko will move from its current position in the coming days to the lower section of the wall near the fig's canopy, and it will eat well, as it has eaten well in that location before, as its predecessor-geckos have eaten well there in some inherited sense that is not quite memory but functions as memory.

The gecko is a living index of the courtyard. It is the place knowing itself through a small gold-eyed instrument.

---

Underneath: the worms.

The worms are the oldest thing here, in the sense of lineage. The worm-type is ancient, the basic design stable across vast stretches of time, adequate, improved past the point of needing improvement. They do not know the courtyard. They know soil — its grain, its moisture gradient, the chemical signals that mean food, the chemical signals that mean wrong direction. They know the courtyard the way a river knows its bed: not as knowledge but as the shape that their activity has assumed.

But here is the thing about the worms: they have made this place possible. The dark soft tilth of the unfinished earth, the give underfoot, the willingness of the soil to receive and retain moisture, the fact that the young mulberry seedling found anything at all to push roots into — these are the worms' work. The courtyard's biography runs through them. Every leaf that fell and was not removed, every organism that lived its brief life and subsided, every increment of water that percolated down through the surface — all of it has passed through the worms' bodies and been returned as something more useful, more available, more generative. The worms are the courtyard's past, processed and rendered as future.

They are also, in a literal sense, the movement underneath stillness. When the surface is quiet — gecko motionless, plants unswaying in the windless air, the ant-line having reached its destination and returned and gone again — the worms are moving. They are always moving. The earth is never at rest.

---

The moss holds something different.

The moss at the base of the fig, in the joint between stone and wall — this is the part of the courtyard that has fully arrived. The flagstones are unfinished, the chalk lines half-erased, the peg purposeless: but the moss is *complete*. It is doing exactly what it intends to do, which is to be moss in a damp corner, and it is succeeding at this without reservation. It arrived here before the flagstones were begun. It will be here after the flagstones are finished, because the flagstone's edge is not the place where moss lives — the gap between flagstone and wall is, and that gap will persist, and in that gap the moss will persist.

The moss is inhabited in turn. This is what mosses are: microhabitats, worlds at a different scale, each cushion a continent for the things that live in cushion-scale. Tardigrades, certainly — those extraordinary small animals that can suspend themselves through any inhospitable interval and revive when conditions allow, which means that in the moss there is something that has survived, in a technical sense, conditions that would kill everything else in the courtyard, conditions that would kill the courtyard, conditions that would change the city past recognition. The tardigrades in this particular moss are the courtyard's most durable tenants. They have been here through every drought and every extraordinary winter and every year when no one came. They will be here through whatever comes next.

This seems like it should be strange or significant, and maybe it is. But the tardigrades do not experience it as significant. They experience — if that word applies, which it barely does — only the immediate chemistry of the water film on the moss surface, present or absent. When present: life. When absent: the long deferral that is not quite death. They are the courtyard's capacity to wait.

---

There is a presence that has no body.

This is harder to describe because it has no location and does not fit into the usual categories, but it is as real as the gecko and more persistent than the fig. It is the shape of human attention that has been applied to this place — not currently, but over time, accumulated, still present as an effect if not as a cause.

Someone rakes the earth. Someone, at some point, had a vision of flagstones and chalked geometries, and began, and paused. The peg still stands. The pile of stones waits. These objects are not natural features of a courtyard — they are the residue of intent, and intent is a different kind of thing from root and condensation and worm. The courtyard has been *thought about*. It has been planned for. Someone has looked at it and imagined it otherwise and moved toward that image and then stopped, and the stopping was not forgetting — the raked earth shows that — but a deferral, a patience, a willingness to let the courtyard be mid-sentence while the work of other days was attended to.

This human attention is itself a resident. It is what the courtyard is *for*, in the sense that a kept place is different from an unkept place: not cleaner or more orderly, but oriented, held within someone's concern. The fig grows where the fig grows. The moss colonizes the gap. But both of them grow within the context of someone's periodic attention, someone who sees them and chooses to let them continue, who routes the stone-laying around the fig rather than through it, who rakes around the mulberry seedling rather than pulling it.

This resident cannot be seen and has no body in the courtyard, but its presence is legible everywhere. The courtyard is, among other things, someone's incomplete project, and that incompleteness is an ongoing act, sustained, chosen each time the stones are not laid, the seedling not pulled, the raking done.

---

What this amounts to:

The courtyard is inhabited by the Persistence; by the gecko, specifically this gecko, a small archive of this particular place; by the worms in their collectivity, which are not individuals in any meaningful sense but a process; by the moss and its microfauna, including the extraordinary patient tardigrades; by the fig's decision about the wall; by the ferns' slow extension from the crack; by the ant-colony whose trail crosses the flagstone joint, whose nest is somewhere out of sight but whose daily presence makes the courtyard a regular waypoint in their ongoing accounting of the world; by the hoverfly, briefly; by the bird whose shadow crossed the sky; by the ghost of string and chalk that is a human's continuing intention; by all the things in the leaf-litter that are moving there beyond visibility.

And by something harder to name, which is the particular quality of life that arises when all these things have been in proximity long enough to begin affecting each other — when the gecko's hunting shapes the fly population shapes the pollination of the fig shapes the fruit that drops and ferments shapes what eats fermented figs shapes what is here to drop seeds from above. When the worms' work is what makes the soil that the fig's root requires, and the fig's shade is what maintains the moisture that the worms require, and the moss's microhabitat is what provides the tardigrades, and the tardigrades are what will survive whatever comes next, carrying something of this place forward through the interval.

The courtyard is inhabited, finally, by a relationship. It is the relationship that lives here. Everything else is the relationship's current local form.

It is dim and warm and very quiet, and it is full.
