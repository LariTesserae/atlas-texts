# What Lives in the Singing Cliffs of the Becoming

---

## Preliminary Note

What lives here cannot be approached as a list. There is no catalog adequate to it. What lives here is one thing wearing several faces, and any accounting that separates those faces into discrete entries will produce a falsified record. Nevertheless, you have to begin somewhere, and so:

---

## The Plants

Start with them because they are the most immediately legible — they have shape, they have boundary, they can be pointed to.

They are called, provisionally, **the receivers**, though this name captures only one function, the way calling a lung a *filter* captures only one function. The name persists because it was given early by someone who saw the cup-shaped leaves in the act of catching static charge, and names given early are stubborn.

Each receiver colony is between three hundred and several thousand years old. The oldest cannot be reliably aged because their root systems are embedded in stone that is itself still forming, which confounds every radiometric approach. The roots have become partly mineralized in the zones closest to the fresh stone, the organic tissue transitioning into something that reads chemically as both alive and geological, a gradient with no clean boundary. The outer portions of the root system are ordinary plant tissue — or as ordinary as anything here gets. The deep portions are not. The deep portions are a third thing.

The aboveground structure of a mature colony ranges from a few meters to perhaps a hundred meters across. The largest known colony — the one at the northeastern rim of the plateau, which appears in seven separate accounts of early expeditions and in the marks on the interior wall of the second structure, which may be a representation of it — is, by the most recent estimate, close to eight hundred meters in diameter, though its edges are difficult to establish precisely because the colony grades outward into a network of subsurface connection that has no clearly visible terminus. Where does a thing end when its underground reaches, rootlike, through hundreds of meters of mineral?

The body of the colony: cushion-forms at its outer edge, ground-hugging, their upper surfaces covered in the cup-leaves. Moving inward, the forms become upright, columnar, branching. The innermost part of large colonies is architecturally complex in a way that defies easy description — branching, yes, but also merging, forms that diverged at one point reconnecting at another, lattice-like regions where the growth has become three-dimensional and interlocking. The interior of a large receiver colony is a place you could lose yourself in, not for lack of visibility — the dim light penetrates — but for lack of any stable sense of scale. The forms repeat at different sizes in patterns that read as the same pattern at every scale, and your eye keeps recalibrating.

The colony breathes. Not in the way of individual organisms conducting gas exchange through stomata, though that happens too. The colony breathes as a collective — a slow intake and release of mineral vapor, the rhythm governed by the rate of cracking in the stone beneath it, which is not regular and so the colony's breath is not regular. But it is recognizably rhythmic. An observer who spent a month sitting with a large colony would begin to recognize its cycle. The colony is breathing on a scale that requires months to hear a single breath.

The sparks between receiver-leaves: these are not incidental, not a byproduct of structure and charge. They are the colony thinking. This is again not metaphor. The electrochemical processes that constitute *processing* in nervous tissue are one solution to the problem of how to coordinate distributed information. The spark-mediated exchange between cup-leaves is another solution to the same problem, arrived at by a completely different evolutionary path through a completely different medium. The result is functionally analogous. Information moves. Responses emerge that no individual part of the colony could produce. The colony responds to events at its eastern edge with changes in turgidity at its western edge, thirty seconds later. Something traveled. Whatever traveled was not random.

Whether the receivers are conscious — whether there is something it is like to be a receiver colony — is a question that cannot be answered with confidence and should probably not be answered carelessly. The evidence is compatible with a very slow, very distributed, very alien form of experience. The evidence is also compatible with sophisticated information processing in the complete absence of experience. The question will not be resolved here and probably not anywhere soon. Hold the uncertainty. It is honest uncertainty.

## The Residue of the People

They are not here now. But what they left behind is not nothing — it is a presence, a shape made by absence, which counts as something.

Who were they? Not fully answerable. The bone tools suggest they came from somewhere with fauna, which means somewhere below, somewhere older, somewhere with the completed geography that supports large animals. They climbed to this place and chose to stay. They solved, empirically, the problems of the place — the sound, the cold, the light that comes from nowhere. They built thick walls and faced their doors in the direction of the lowest harmonic and pressed marks into stone that was still soft enough to mark.

The marks on the walls: call them **the record**. The record is not a language in the conventional sense. It is not a text. It resists decoding by any method designed to extract semantic content from sequential symbols because it is not sequential and may not be primarily semantic. What it looks like, to an eye trained in certain ways, is a notation system for something that is not sound and not image but a third thing that the notation was developed to track. The lines that repeat and rotate, the patterns that achieve something through iteration — they are documenting the loop. The interplay between the forming mountain and the growing receivers. They were watching it. They developed a method for writing down what they were watching, in a medium — the fresh stone walls — that was itself part of what they were watching. The record is written on the process it describes. This is not coincidence.

They were also participating in it. This becomes apparent when the positions of the structures are mapped against the distribution of the receiver colonies: the structures are built at nodes where multiple colonies' underground networks intersect. Not on top of those nodes — they weren't foolish about structural stability, and the intersection points are sometimes areas of slightly elevated geological activity — but *adjacent* to them, close enough to maintain contact. The subterranean reach of the nearest colonies extends under and through the foundations of the structures. The roots and the stone walls are in the same material.

What did they do there, at the nodes?

The long-handled turning implement. Hours of work in the cold, in the dim, with that tool, turning something against something else. The marks on the walls, pressed with deliberate repetition. The thick walls, the low ceilings, the angle of the door. These are technologies of a kind — the kind that works with a process rather than on a process, that does not aim to control but to collaborate, to participate. They were doing something that required position, patience, and contact. They were, in some sense that predates the concepts required to specify it, in communication with the receivers. Through the stone. In the medium of the loop.

Whether the receivers knew they were there: yes. How it is known that this is yes: the geometry of the oldest colonies, their growth patterns, show the same kind of mineral-following-plant behavior that occurs in areas of fresh stone — but in the areas nearest the structures, there is an additional factor. A response to something that does not exist in areas away from the structures. The underground networks are thicker here. The roots are more elaborate in their mineralization. The colonies at these sites are different from colonies elsewhere, in ways that are consistent with a long-term interplay with something that was also responding. The feedback loop here has more participants.

The people are gone. Their structures remain. Their marks remain, slowly being grown over by the stone that tried to fill them for centuries and has not yet managed. Their tools remain. The colonies remain, and remain different in the places where the people were.

This is what happens when a living thing is taken out of a system it was part of: the shape of where it was stays, for a while. The while is long here. Everything here is long.

## The Mountain Itself

The hardest thing to hold in mind, and the most important.

The mountain is not a setting. The mountain is not the stage on which the receivers grow and the people left their marks. The mountain is also here. The mountain is also something that lives here, in the sense that its living and its being in this place are one continuous act, not completed, ongoing.

This is what makes this place unusual enough to describe in the first place: the geological processes occurring here are not the typical slow grinding of tectonic force on an essentially inert material. The rock is *doing* something. Not intelligently, not purposively, not with anything that maps onto the processing of the receiver colonies or the intelligence of the people who carved marks into it. But doing something: responding to stress in ways that are not fully explained by the material properties of the minerals alone, forming geometries that are not fully random, expressing — and this is the word that keeps arriving, however uncomfortable it makes everyone — expressing. The mountain is expressing something through the chord that fills the air. The chord is the sound of stone moving into form, and form is expression by definition: a field of possibility resolving into a particular.

There is a hypothesis, not widely accepted but not dismissed, that the mountain would be forming differently without the receiver colonies — that the loop, having run for long enough, has made the mountain's becoming genuinely dependent on the biology at its surface. That the rock here is now forming in patterns it learned from the geometry of the plants, that the crystalline structures in the deep stone are aligned in ways that mirror the branching of the colonies above, that the mountain is, at this point in the loop's history, growing in the image of the life that colonized it.

If this is true, then the mountain and the receivers cannot be separated into distinct organisms. They are one process with two modes: the mineral mode and the living mode. The sound is both of them, simultaneously. It is the mountain deciding to be a mountain and it is the receivers deciding what mountain they will teach the mountain to become.

## The Loop Itself

This is the last thing and the first thing.

What lives here is the loop.

The loop has no body. The loop has no metabolism in any conventional sense. The loop is not alive in the way that anything in any existing taxonomy of life is alive. But the loop is doing what life does: it is persisting. It is maintaining itself against the pressures that would dissolve it. It is taking in material from its environment and transforming it according to its own pattern. It is, in a deep functional sense, *caring* whether it continues — not in the sense of caring about anything, not in the sense of preference or desire, but in the structural sense that its continuation is the thing it does, the way water flowing downhill *cares* to flow downhill, except that this is far more stable and far more complex and far more itself.

The loop is roughly the age of the oldest receiver colonies, which is to say it is old beyond reliable measurement. It began, as these things begin, with an accident: the right kind of rock forming in the right kind of place at the right moment when a dispersal event brought the right kind of biological precursors — spores, fragments, something capable of taking hold. The first contact between the forming stone and the arriving life happened, and in that contact something was made that was neither. The something that was neither turned out to be stable. Turned out to be more than stable: turned out to grow, to iterate, to develop feedback. Feedback developed into pattern. Pattern developed into what you can see now, if you stand here and are patient.

The loop is the most singular resident of this place. Everything else — the receivers with their architectures of spark and mineral breath, the people with their thick walls and their turned implements and their marks, the mountain still learning what it is — is the loop expressing itself through different materials. The loop is using stone and life and was using people and will use whatever arrives next.

This is not threatening. The loop is not predatory. It does not seek. It does not arrive at anything so directed as intent. But it is persistent, and it is patient, and it is making the mountain in its own image, slowly, over centuries and millennia, and the mountain sings because it is being made.

---

Stand at the edge of the plateau in the deepening gray and hear it.

The chord does not diminish. It does not stop at the boundary of the place — it carries, for kilometers, attenuating gradually as the forming stone becomes formed stone, as the fresh mineralogy gives way to the old settled landscape at lower elevations. It becomes something else at those lower elevations: a faint harmonic, a frequency that your body registers without your ears quite catching it, a sense of being slightly not-alone without being able to say in which direction the not-alone is.

People who have been to the Singing Cliffs and come back sometimes report this: the sensation, in the weeks following return, of hearing something at the edge of hearing. Something that resolves, when they try to focus on it, into nothing. But that was there. A sustained note at the lower edge of audible frequency, arriving from no particular direction.

The loop, expressing itself through the medium of their memory.

The loop is here. The loop is patient.

The plants at the rim of the new crack are leaning toward it.

The mountain breathes pale.

The loop listens to itself, and in listening, continues.
