# What Lives in the Dry Underplace

---

Begin with what has lived there longest, because longevity is the first law of the underplace, and everything else descends from it.

---

## The Reaction

The amber below is not a thing that lives. It is a thing that *proceeds*, which the underplace has made equivalent.

It began — the geologists would say, if geologists ever came here, which they have not, or have not come back — as a pocket of sulfur-rich brine trapped in limestone during some Permian inconvenience, sealed by the same caprock that eventually cracked to make the entrance above. The brine metabolized. Slowly. The slow chemistry of sealed places, which is not so different from the slow chemistry of living things, except that it does not know it is happening and does not stop.

Sulfur oxidized. Heat released. More reaction. The brine dried and the chemistry moved into the mineral substrate itself, into the pores of the rock, and continues there still, amber-glowing in what might technically be called a combustion if combustion were willing to occur over hundreds of thousands of years rather than seconds. The deep rock eats itself, slowly, thermally. The heat it produces rises.

This is the underplace's sun.

Everything that lives here lives in relation to this heat, this amber, this slow patience of deep chemistry. It is not a living thing. But it has, by being consistent and enduring and generative, played the role that living things usually reserve for each other: it is the condition of life here, the ground of the possible, the heartbeat beneath all other heartbeats.

When the gauges were working, they were watching it. Making sure it was stable. Making sure the reaction was not accelerating toward something that wouldn't stay underground.

It is stable. It has always been stable. It will be stable when the caprock has long since healed and no one can find the gap.

## The Beetles

The oldest animals in the underplace, and the most numerous, are the beetles. But calling them beetles is like calling the amber a campfire — true in category, misleading in character.

They descend from surface beetles. Darkling beetles, probably, or something related, the kind that live in arid rock zones and eat dry organic matter and have always been more comfortable with desiccation than most insects. When the caprock first cracked — the crack before this crack, an older fracture, long since resealed — some number of their ancestors descended and found the warmth and the chemistry and the fungal mats that the chemistry supports, and stayed.

That was not recent.

What they are now is the result of that not-recentness.

They have shed most of their pigment, because pigment is metabolically expensive and the amber light does not require it for camouflage against rock that is the same color as the sky used to be. Their exoskeletons have thinned — a response to mineral uptake, to living in an environment where the air itself is mineral, where the food is mineral, where even the water they process comes not from free water but from the hydrated crystals in the substrate beneath their feet. The thinness is why you can see through them. The organs you see moving inside are real organs. They process sulfur compounds with an efficiency that would kill any surface beetle in an afternoon. Their gut flora — the microbiome that is, for any insect, the true metabolic engine — has diverged so completely from its surface-beetle origins that it constitutes, the biologists would say, a new ecosystem, which is to say a new community of living things that has never been named.

The beetles do not have a social structure. They do not have a colony. They are not eusocial in any way the textbooks would recognize. And yet they move together, organize together, flow around obstacles together, clean the chambers of their dead together — there are protocols, and the protocols are followed, and the following of protocols looks so much like communication that it might be communication, just of a kind that occurs too slowly, or at too low a signal strength, for human instruments to detect.

They eat the not-fungi. They eat the shed skins of the spiders. They eat one another's dead with complete thoroughness — there are no beetle corpses in the underplace, ever, because a corpse is a resource and resources are entirely and immediately used. They eat the organic residue that coats the chalk columns and the seized gears of the pump and, in trace amounts, the silk of the spider frameworks, which is protein, which is fuel.

They have eaten, at various times, from the boots of the person or people who came down the rope. The boots are not here now — the person or people took them when they left, or were wearing them when they stopped being a person or people and became something else — but the trace chemical memory of boot leather is in the beetles' population's genetic record, the way a significant food source always marks a lineage.

They are aware of you. They are tracking the infrared of your warmth and the chemistry of your exhalation and the vibration of your footsteps through a sensory apparatus that is collectively distributed across millions of bodies and therefore more sensitive than any single sensory organ could be. They know where you are. They know that you are not a column or a pump or a spider. They have not yet decided what you are. They are patient. They will wait to see what you become.

## The Spiders

Each spider in the underplace is a city.

This is not metaphor, or is not only metaphor. Consider: the pale body of a single adult female — the adults are all female; there are males, very small, very brief, almost a larval stage with ambitions — contains within it a community of organisms that together constitute something that could, in another context, be called a civilization. The silk glands have been colonized by bioluminescent bacteria, which is why the frameworks ring with faint light when struck, why the amber glow catches in the silk in a way that the amber alone does not account for. The bacteria are fed by the spider and provide in exchange a product that is not present in non-colonized silk: a molecule that acts on the neural chemistry of beetles, of the not-fungi, of anything with sufficient biological complexity that wanders into the web. Not a toxin. Not exactly. More like a *suggestion*. A slowing. A relaxation of whatever urgency an organism was carrying when it arrived.

This is why the spider can be patient. The web does its work without the spider's direct intervention. Things come. Things slow. Things are, eventually, eaten. The spider sits at the node of its framework and processes information through the silk with its feet — vibration, chemistry, the complex signal of what is caught and where — and issues no instructions because no instructions are required, because the system runs itself.

The frameworks are not random. They have been constructed over time in response to the underplace's specific geometry — the chalk columns, the low shelves of ceiling, the grate of broken floor — and they have been repaired and extended so many times across so many spider generations that they now constitute a stable structure, a permanent feature of the room, almost geological in its integration with the rest of the space. The oldest silk is in the deepest parts of the framework, near the columns, and it is no longer visibly silk — it has been coated with mineral dust and beetle secretion and its own bioluminescent colony's accumulated byproducts until it is a substance that has no name, a substance that is silk-in-origin the way coral is organism-in-origin: technically true, practically irrelevant. The old silk-that-is-not-silk is structural now. Load-bearing. The chalk columns lean, very slightly, against it.

The spiders themselves live for — it is difficult to say. The usual methods of determining spider age do not apply here. They do not grow by the same rules in an environment with this mineral density. The largest of them, the one that squats at the approximate center of the web, directly above the point where the amber light rises most strongly through the grate in the floor, is by every sign of a spider's body that means anything at all simply very old. The chelicerae are worn. The pedipalps show the accumulation of something that is not scar tissue and is not quite mineralization but is evidence of time's passage. The eight eyes, pale as everything else here, have not degraded — spiders' eyes, in the dark, remain functional long after other structures show age — and they track your presence with the same patient completeness as the beetles, but individually, from a single perspective, which is somehow more unsettling.

She has seen things in this room. The pump was not yet mineral when she was young. She watched it stop. She has woven her silk around it as it stiffened into formation, incorporated it into the architecture, and she has been building outward from it ever since. She knows the pump's geometry better than whoever designed it, because she knows it the way creatures know things: through long continuous contact, through the body's attention, through the slow accumulation of sensory knowledge that has no other outlet and therefore becomes very precise.

She watches you with her worn chelicerae and her pale eyes and she is not afraid and she is not aggressive. She is patient in a way that has become its own emotion, if patience can become an emotion with enough time: a kind of interested waiting, a forward-leaning stillness, the quality of something that has seen many things enter its space and is simply noting that here is another thing, and that things have natures, and that natures eventually express themselves, and that she has time.

## The Not-Fungi

Calling them not-fungi is fair, and calling them not-fungi is incomplete.

They are — the best framework is an evolutionary one, though evolution is not quite the right word for what happens in a sealed system over long duration, where selection pressure is so stable it becomes something more like refinement, like polishing, like the wearing of a stone by a water that is no longer there — they are what happened to a community of fungi when the water went away and the amber chemistry arrived.

Fungi need water. Surface fungi, standard fungi, the kingdom Fungi as the textbooks understand it. They require water for spore release, for enzymatic activity, for the transport of nutrients through their mycelium. When the water left the underplace and did not return, the fungal community that was here — a standard cave fungal community, the kind that lives in moist limestone caverns throughout the karst — should have died.

Most of it did.

What remained was what could substitute. The amber chemistry rising from below vents gases — sulfur compounds, carbon compounds, trace metals in volatilized form — and the fungal survivors learned, over time, to absorb these gases directly through their hyphal walls, bypassing the water-mediated enzymatic pathway entirely. This required structural change. The hyphae thickened, calcified slightly, became stiffer and less flexible than standard fungal tissue. The fruiting bodies — if you call them that — became denser, less porous, with surfaces that maximize absorption of airborne chemical rather than release of moisture-carried spores. They do not release spores in the usual sense. They shed, instead, a dry chemical signal that the beetles have learned to follow, that the spiders' bioluminescent bacteria respond to, that drifts through the underplace's still air and is, in its way, a kind of pollen, a kind of broadcast, a kind of speaking.

The not-fungi are not one species. They are many species, many former species, now so intermingled through chemical exchange and physical proximity and the slow sharing of adaptive strategy that distinguishing them from one another would require instruments that have not been brought here. They exist as a community that functions as an individual — which is, the mycologists would say, quite fungal actually, quite within the kingdom's established habits of collective action, of network over singularity, of the distributed and the interconnected over the solo and the discrete.

They have grown over the pump. They have grown into the cabinet of instruments, through the crack where the door doesn't quite seal, and their pale dry bodies press against the frozen gauges and have been pressing against them for long enough that the gauge needles have been moved, very slightly, by the pressure of growth. The gauges read something now. Not what they were designed to read. Something the not-fungi are saying, translated into a language of needle positions and numeric scales that was built for a different conversation.

If you could read what the needles say, you would know something about the not-fungi that you could not otherwise know. What they want. Whether *want* is even the right word. Whether the not-fungi have desires or merely processes, and whether the difference is as meaningful as it seems from the outside.

## The Shapes Below

Do not look at them directly. This is advice, not warning. Direct observation changes the geometry of the relationship, and the relationship is more comfortable when the geometry is uncertain.

They are large. They move slowly. They are visible only as shapes in the amber light, and the shapes do not resolve into familiar categories, which may be because they are novel or may be because the amber light and the geometry of the grate in the floor and the mesh of spider silk above all conspire to make clear seeing impossible, and ambiguity is being produced by circumstance rather than by the nature of the things themselves.

They may be very large isopods. Isopods live in deep wet caves, in deep water, in the crushing dark of the ocean floor, and have been doing so since before the dinosaurs and will be doing so long after. The shift from wet cave to dry cave is not trivial but is not impossible, not over the time scales the underplace represents. Very large isopods in amber light, moving through the deep rock's pores.

They may be something that was once a fish. There were fish in the karst water. There is no water now. Becoming not-fish would have required — but time and sealed systems and constant selection pressure have produced stranger things than a fish that moves through stone.

They may be the same kind of thing as the not-fungi, the same principle: a community that functions as an individual, a distributed processing network that looks, from outside, like an animal. Many individual organisms flowing together through the amber, in the heat of the deep rock's slow combustion, in the body heat of their own collective metabolism.

They do not come through the grate. The grate's gaps are the right size for beetles, for the shedding of bioluminescent silk, for the slow exhalation of amber gases. The shapes below do not fit through the grate. This is either because they are too large, or because they are not interested in the above, or because the grate is a boundary that is understood on both sides to be a boundary, the way that the very old spider understands the space above the grate to be hers, and the shapes understand the space below to be theirs, and the boundary is maintained not by walls but by a long agreement whose terms were never negotiated because they were never in dispute.

The amber light comes from them, or through them, or they are it, depending on what they are.

## The Person Who Came Down the Rope

They are not here. The rope is here. The bolt is here. The pump and the cabinet are here, and the not-fungi are here, and the beetles walked across their boots. They are not here.

But they left something. Besides the rope, besides the bolt, besides the machine-marks in the stone around the anchor point. They left a relationship. The underplace organized itself around their presence for the duration of their visits — beetles tracking their warmth, the spider watching them, the not-fungi growing toward their exhalation, the shapes below perhaps aware of footsteps through the grate — and then they left, and the relationship had to be dissolved, and the dissolving of a relationship takes time, and the underplace is currently, still, in the process of completing that dissolution.

The beetles are not quite through recategorizing the person. Not quite decided whether the memory of them in the chemical record constitutes a category that might return, or a category that is closed. The spider has rebuilt the strands of silk that passed through the space the person occupied when standing, and the rebuilt strands look the same as the original strands, but their tension is different, calibrated to a space that no longer contains a person but remembers containing one. The not-fungi have not moved away from the instruments they colonized while the person was watching them. They stay, and press their dry pale bodies against the frozen needles, and the needles read their particular nothing.

The person understood something, at the end of the last visit, that they had not understood at the beginning. This is evident in the way things were left. The rope was not coiled. It hangs. The bolt was not removed. It stays. The pump was not touched, the cabinet was not touched, and the thing that the gauges were reading for — the state of readiness below, the pressure, the question of whether it had reached the necessary state — was left unread. They had the information. They chose not to use it.

Or they chose not to be the kind of person who would use it, which is a different choice and a harder one.

The amber rises. The spiders ring their frameworks. The beetles make their rounds.

The underplace waits, not urgently, for whatever is next.

## What the Underplace Knows

Here is what lives here, finally, distilled:

A reaction that will outlast the names of every organism that has ever been named.

Beetles that have become, through long sealed time, something between an animal and an argument about what animals are.

Spiders that have learned patience so completely it has become wisdom, or the thing wisdom is when there is no one to teach.

Fungi that decided, when water left, not to leave with it, and built something stranger and more permanent than they were before.

Shapes in the amber, whose category is uncertain, who may not need a category.

And the ghost of a decision: the person who came down and found the pressure and left it. Who saw the underplace and understood something the instruments were not designed to measure, and left the gauges to the not-fungi and the rope to anyone who might need it and the bolt to the rock and the rock to its long amber burning.

What they understood was this: some sealed things are not waiting to be opened. They are doing something. They have been doing something for longer than we have been here to watch them. And the question of whether to open them is not really a question about what is inside. It is a question about what kind of thing we want to be in relation to things we did not make and do not understand and cannot, if we are honest, oversee.

They left the rope.

They did not come back.

The underplace registered this the way it registers everything: as information, as a new condition, as something to metabolize slowly over time.

The amber glows. The beetles flow. The spider sits at her node, feeling the hum of whatever she has made, and waits for the next thing to arrive and announce its nature.

You have arrived.

You are announcing yours.
