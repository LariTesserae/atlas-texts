# What Lives in the Lung

---

Start with what you can count, and then stop counting, because counting is not the right instrument here.

---

## The Crabs

Begin with the crabs because they are closest, because they are at your feet, because they will be the first thing to make you question the relationship between color and light and time.

They are the size of a closed fist, these crabs — no, smaller, the size of a child's fist, and then some smaller still, a gradation of sizes that suggests you are looking at multiple generations simultaneously, grandparent and grandchild conducting the same slow traverse of the same diamond-stamped iron grating that their shared ancestor first learned to read. The smallest are translucent in the way that very young things sometimes are, their internal geography faintly visible through the carapace, a suggestion of machinery inside the machine. The largest are the color that white becomes when it has spent decades filtering through this particular light: not yellow, not grey, but something between, a warm negative, the color of old paper held up to a lamp.

They move in a way that you will eventually understand is not random, though it takes time to see the structure. Each crab is working a territory. The territory is defined by the moisture-map of the grating — the permanent wet zones, the intermittent wet zones, the zones that catch the condensation drip from specific points in the canopy above and hold it in the stamp-holes for an hour before it evaporates. The crabs know which hole holds water after which drip. They have timed this. They are running schedules that predate your arrival and will outlast your departure, schedules inherited or learned or arrived at through some crab-form of accumulated knowledge that you cannot distinguish from instinct and perhaps they cannot either.

Watch one cross the grating from the wall-pipe area toward the center of the floor and you will see it pause at each stamp-hole with a deliberation that looks like decision-making, because it is decision-making, because this crab has something to decide: water or no water, algae or no algae, worth stopping or worth continuing. It decides. It moves on or it stops. Its mouthparts work at whatever it finds in a hole, and you will not be able to see what it finds, the scale is too fine, but you can see that it finds something often enough to keep finding, keep working, keep running its inherited schedule through the hours.

What you will notice, eventually — and it will take a long time to notice, it will arrive as a slow doubt rather than a sudden observation — is that the crabs do not quite interact. They pass each other with margins that seem calibrated. Not aggressive margins, not fearful ones: simply the margins of animals that have achieved a distribution across available territory with enough precision that conflict has become almost unnecessary. An equilibrium discovered across generations. The grating is large enough for all of them if each takes what it needs and leaves the rest, and somewhere in the compounded experience of the colony this conclusion has been reached and is now simply the way things are, legible in the spacing of their movements the way that precedent is legible in the behavior of a court.

They are color-blind, almost certainly. The light here has not been sufficient to drive that particular evolutionary pressure. What they have instead is a sensitivity to moisture that borders on the architectural: they feel the humidity change before the condensation falls, orient toward the incoming drip with a precision that reads, to the observer, as precognition. They are not precognitive. They simply have the right instruments for the environment and have been using them long enough to become very good at it. This is what several generations of darkness and warmth and iron and water have produced: a creature exquisitely calibrated to exactly this place and, by virtue of that calibration, unable to exist anywhere else. The Lung has made them. They have made themselves inside it. These are not different things.

Their eggs — and you will find them if you look at the soil-pockets at the base of the wall-pipes, in the composted dark behind the largest fern-root masses — are the size of sand grains, pale, arranged in clusters that adhere to the root-hair and fungal thread of the soil with a stickiness that holds them through the condensation falls, holds them against the slow drainage of the grating, holds them through however long the holding needs to last. The juveniles that emerge are already crab-shaped, already running their inherited version of the moisture-schedule, already spaced at the correct intervals. They are born knowing the grating. Or they learn it so fast that the learning is invisible. Or both things are true and the line between them is where the interesting questions live.

## The Birds

They are small and they are many and they never stop.

Dark-plumaged — not black, dark brown, a brown so saturated it reads as black in the dusk-zones and reveals itself only in the light-column, which catches them sometimes in flight and briefly shows the individual feather structure, the slight iridescence, the way each primary is a slightly different shade of the same very-dark-brown. They have the compact, dense quality of birds that live in enclosed spaces, that do not need to soar, that have traded wingspan for maneuverability in a world of vertical pipes and crossing vines and fern-fronds that swing unpredictably when disturbed. Their wings are short and rounded. Their tails are shorter than you would expect. They turn in spaces that seem too small for turning and do not crash.

There are — you try to count them once — there are perhaps sixty, perhaps eighty, it is impossible to be sure because they are never all visible simultaneously and they move faster than counting allows. The colony occupies the mid-layer of the shaft: from about five meters above the grating floor to about twenty-five or thirty meters up, where the vine-canopy begins to be too dense for easy flight. Below this zone: the crabs, the beetles, the grating work. Above it: whatever moves in the vine-canopy, which is slower. The birds are the middle kingdom. They are also its sound.

What the shaft does to bird calls is the following: it takes them, amplifies them, reflects them off the iron walls (beneath the vegetation, the iron is still there, still resonant), and returns them multiplied and phase-shifted so that a single call becomes a cloud of itself, a call and its echoes and the echoes of its echoes arriving back from different heights at different moments. A colony of sixty or eighty birds each calling at their individual rate produces, in this acoustic environment, what can only be described as a texture — a continuous complex surface of sound in which individual calls are just barely distinguishable the way individual water molecules are just barely distinguishable in a river. You are in the river. The calls move through you.

What are they saying? The question seems less useful than it did outside. In here the calls are structural. They are how the birds know where the other birds are without seeing them through the fern-cover. They are how position and occupation and the ongoing negotiation of territory is conducted without the energy cost of flying to look. They are cartographic: the shaft is being continuously mapped in sound, and each bird is both reading and updating the map. When a bird finds a productive soil-pocket, its call changes in a way that other birds respond to — not dramatically, not with any visible flocking or rushing-toward, but with a subtle redistribution, a few individuals altering their search-pattern toward the productive zone. The map is collaborative. The map is happening right now, around you, at a frequency and resolution you cannot consciously track.

You can, however, feel when it changes. When something disturbs the colony — when you move suddenly, or when the larger thing in the vine-canopy shifts its weight and sends a shudder through the vines — the sound-texture changes: a beat-frequency appears, a rhythmic pulse in the noise that is produced by many birds entering a higher-rate call simultaneously. The sound becomes faster, briefly urgent, then settles back. The pulse subsides. The map is updated to include the disturbance, filed, returned to its background function.

They build in the pipe-flanges. In the gaps where insulation has failed and the metal itself has corroded into a rough oxide surface that holds material, in the V-shaped junctions of crossing pipes, in the base-crevices of the fern-root masses where the soil is thick and dense enough to be structural — in all of these, they have placed nests. The nests are built from whatever the shaft provides: vine-fiber, stripped in lengths from the older woody stems; fern-frond pieces, pressed flat; insulation-material fiber, white and friable, worked into a binding matrix; and — this is the detail that stays with you — sections of root-hair so fine they are invisible individually but present in the nest-material as a kind of felt, a softness at the center that you discover only by touching, carefully, the one nest that is at eye-level and currently empty.

The eggs are dark. Three or four per nest. Incubation is conducted by both parents in alternation, the off-duty bird joining the flock-work of the mid-layer, calling its position, updating the map. When the chicks emerge — and you infer this from the behavior of certain pairs whose territory is near the eye-level nest — they are initially silent, then vocal at an extraordinary volume disproportionate to their size, a volume that in the shaft's acoustics is embarrassing, or would be embarrassing, in the way that all infant demands are slightly embarrassing in their naked urgency. The adults respond without apparent embarrassment. They carry food in their bills — from the soil-pockets, small things, invertebrate things — and deliver it with a calm efficiency that is also love, or what love looks like when it has no need to demonstrate itself.

They have not learned to fear the crabs. The crabs have not learned to fear them. This is not because the crabs and the birds do not interact — they do, at the soil-pockets, occasionally, in brief adjustments that each makes for the other without apparent anxiety — but because fear, in this space, is calibrated to the actual threats, and the crabs are not a threat to the birds and the birds are not a threat to the crabs and both populations have known this long enough that the knowledge has become structural, has become a part of the way each moves through the space.

There is one bird that you see most often at the floor level, near the wall-pipe where the largest fern-colony grows. It is older than the others — you can see this in the worn quality of its plumage, the slight asymmetry of tail-feathers replaced imperfectly after some molt or damage. It moves more slowly than the others. It spends more time at the grating edge, looking down through the diamond-pattern holes into the further dark below. It does not appear to be hunting. It appears, if you allow this, to be attending to something. To the vibration, perhaps. To whatever the slow pulse below the grating is doing. You watch it for a long time and it watches the dark, and eventually it calls once, a single note that the shaft takes and multiplies until the echo sounds like several birds calling from far away, and then it flies up and rejoins the mid-layer and is indistinguishable in the movement there from any of the others.

## The Vine-Canopy Thing

You do not see it fully. You will not see it fully. This is not an accident.

It lives in the density of the vine-canopy, forty meters up, where the light is greenest and most diffuse and where the vines have had the longest time to construct their horizontal architecture, the interlocking of stem and tendril and broad leaf that has produced what amounts to a suspended forest floor, a secondary ground twenty meters thick, held up by the pipes and I-beams and whatever remains of the original structural grid above. This is a real place. It has substrate. It has accumulated leaf-litter and rain-capture and the small bodies of insects and the castings of birds and the long work of decomposition, and from these it has produced centimeters of something that functions as soil, true aerial soil, growing things up here that have no relationship to the floor sixty meters below except that they are in the same shaft, breathing the same slow convection of warm air.

What moves in this place is large, by the standards of what has arrived here and stayed. You see the displacement it makes: a vine-stem that was angled left is now angled right, and it moved too deliberately to have been the air-current, and the fern-frond below it is still settling, still oscillating at the frequency of something-brushed-against, not something-struck. It was here a moment ago. It is somewhere else now. It is at no point visible.

Infer from the displacement: it is substantial. Not enormous — the canopy would not support something enormous, and the shaft is not wide enough to conceal something enormous. But dense. Low-slung, probably, given how the vines move beneath it, given that the upper surface of the canopy shows no corresponding disturbance, which means its weight is distributed downward, pressing through the vine-layer rather than resting on top of it. A spreader of weight. An animal that understands, somehow — through experience, through the body-knowledge of having moved here long enough — that the canopy's strength is in the distribution, that if you move as though the surface beneath you might give way it generally will not, and if you move as though it is solid ground it sometimes does.

It is warm-blooded, probably. The shaft's convection carries warmth upward, and the canopy-zone is already the warmest layer in the shaft by virtue of its position, and the accumulated moisture-cycling of the vegetation up there means that warmth is retained and amplified into something that approaches the thermal conditions of a much more southerly climate than any that exists outside. A mammal up here, in that warmth, would not struggle thermoregulatory. It would thrive. It would find the warmth it needs without effort and spend the thermoregulatory surplus on — what? On slow movement. On attendance. On whatever the business of being that animal requires, conducted at a pace that the shaft's ambient patience has either taught it or selected for across the generations of its residence here.

What does it eat? The canopy provides. The canopy provides insects — there are insects in great variety up there, drawn to the warmth and the moisture and the available decomposition, running their own economies in the leaf-litter of the aerial soil. The canopy provides fruiting bodies — fungi, mostly, but also something that the vines produce at irregular intervals, not quite a fruit, a fleshy protrusion at the node-points that lasts a few days before dropping or being eaten. The canopy provides eggs, when the birds nest in its lower margins, which some do. The canopy provides water — condensation pools in leaf-axils up there constantly, replaced as fast as it is used. The canopy is, for something of the right size and disposition, complete. A world that provides everything.

Has it been here long? The question arrives with an attached anxiety — the anxiety of wondering whether long residence produces the kind of relationship with a place that is indistinguishable from the place itself, whether what moves in the canopy is still quite separate from the canopy or has become a part of its function, a part of what makes it work. The vine-canopy disperses seeds. Something up there deposits the concentrated nutrients of digested material into the aerial soil. Something up there, moving slowly along its preferred routes, maintains the larger vine-stems at a particular tension by its weight, keeps them from growing in directions that would block the convection, keeps the airflow working. None of this is intentional. All of it is real. The canopy has organized itself around a large slow resident the way a river organizes itself around the rock that has always been there, and the canopy and the resident are now a single system, and pulling one out would change the other in ways that would take decades to become visible and by then it would be too late to reinsert what was removed.

You know it is watching you. Not with suspicion — the concept may not apply. With the attention of an animal for whom anything new in the environment is data to be processed, filed, assigned to a category that determines how much processing it warrants going forward. You are new. You are being processed. Eventually — perhaps already — you will be filed. Perhaps in the category of temporary disturbance. Perhaps in the category of recurring visitor, if you stay long enough. Perhaps in some other category that you do not have the cognition to imagine, being the product of an environment that is not this shaft and therefore equipped with different instruments.

## The Fungal Network

You do not see this. You see its outputs.

It is the floor of everything. Below the fern-roots, below the composted decades, below the soil-pockets on every ledge and pipe-joint — running through all of it, conducting the commerce of the shaft in currency that is not visible to you but is visible to the roots that connect to it. Nitrogen. Phosphorus. The signal-chemicals that mean *stress here* and *surplus there* and *something is eating the east fern* and *the condensation cycle is shifting*. The fungi predate the ferns and the vines and the birds and whatever is in the canopy; they were the first colonists after the first spore settled in the first crack in the first rust-bloom on the iron, and everything that came after them came into a world that they had already begun to knit into a legible network.

The bioluminescence is them. That faint blue-white pulse in the lowest dark of the grating, the one you're not sure you're actually seeing — that is metabolic, that is the chemical signature of their digestion, the processing of organic material in the permanent wet zones. It is the shaft breathing, at the scale of cells. It is the most ancient thing here doing its most ancient work, which is to take what has died and make of it the conditions for what is living, endlessly, in the dark, at the foundational layer below all the more visible and dramatic life above it.

Nothing you see growing here would be here without the fungal network. The crabs eat the fungal bodies when they find them, and in doing so disperse the spores to new locations, and the fungi fruit where the spores arrive, and the fruiting bodies feed the crabs. The birds deposit material that the fungi process, and the fungi deliver the processed nutrients to the fern-roots, and the ferns produce the insect habitat that feeds the birds. The thing in the canopy is connected to all of this through the vines, whose roots tap the network at the floor level sixty meters below and draw from it the nutrients that travel up through sixty meters of woody stem to feed the canopy growth. Everything is downstream of everything else. Everything is upstream of everything else.

The fungi are not aware of this. They are running chemistry, not philosophy. But the chemistry has arrived, through long optimization, at something that functions exactly as awareness would function if awareness were the goal — responsive, adaptive, allocating resources to where resources are needed, redirecting when a pathway is blocked, finding alternate routes, maintaining the system's function through disturbances that would collapse a more rigidly structured arrangement. The shaft runs on this. All of it. The warmth and the pipes and the iron and the decades of slow accumulation have provided the conditions, but the fungi have built the city.

## The Beetles

They deserve mention, separate from the general invertebrate population, because they are the shaft's archivists.

They process the wood of the older vine-stems — the ones that have died back over the decades, the ones whose bark is still in place but whose interior has gone to the particular soft grey that dry-rotted wood achieves, light as pumice, easily compressed. The beetles move through this material with the patience of something that has all the time the material will take to collapse. They are black, most of them, or very dark brown, with the slight sheen of a elytra that has been kept clean by the beetle's own grooming and by the fact that water does not bead on them but sheets off cleanly. They are various sizes but cluster around a mid-range that suggests either convergent evolution toward an optimum or a dominant species with attendant variations.

What they leave behind is the shaft's record. The frass-tunnels they carve through dead wood are a chronicle of the wood's life — where it was dense, where it had a knot, where it dried fastest, where moisture lingered in the cellular structure after the vine died. These tunnels persist after the beetles have moved on, empty channels in the grey wood that gradually fills with spore and mycelium and becomes, in time, part of the aerial soil that feeds the next generation of vines. The beetles are not archivists by intention. They are archivists by effect. The record they leave is the byproduct of their digestion. The record is nonetheless real: if you know how to read frass-tunnel patterns, which you do not, you could read in this dead wood the history of this shaft's moisture regime across the decades of the vine's growth, could trace the changes in condensation frequency, the years of unusual warmth or unusual cold from outside, the slow drift of the shaft's internal climate as the plant-mass increased and changed the convection.

The beetles are the shaft's memory. They carry it in their bodies and leave it in their wake and do not know they are doing either.

## What the Shaft Has Made

All of it, together.

Not a community, exactly — community implies a shared intention, and there is no intention here beyond the individual intentions of individual organisms pursuing their metabolic needs within the constraints of the available physics. But something that functions as a community functions, in that each part is doing something that depends on and enables what the other parts are doing, and the whole has arrived at a stability that none of the parts could have predicted and none of them maintains intentionally.

The crabs turn the grating into a map of moisture. The birds turn the air into a map of position and resource. The thing in the canopy turns the vine-architecture into something structurally maintained and nutrient-cycling. The beetles turn the dead wood into future soil. The fungi turn everything dead into everything living. The ferns turn the pipes and ledges into substrate for all of the above. The pipes provide warmth. The grating provides surface. The iron walls — still there, beneath all of it, still riveted, still geometric, still the original bones of whatever this shaft was built to be — provide structure for everything that has grown from them and on them and around them and into them.

And in the middle of all of it, in the warm damp green half-dark, you: the loudest thing by choice, the most temporary thing by circumstance, the only thing present that knows it is inside something and is still working out what that means. The shaft has been here longer than you. It will be here longer than you. The crabs are not waiting for you to leave. The birds have filed you and moved on. Whatever is in the canopy has processed you and returned to its slow routes.

You are a temporary disturbance in a system of great patience.

You are also, while you are here, part of it — breathing its air, turning its acoustics, displacing its moisture-map with your footsteps, contributing the CO₂ of your breath to the exchange that keeps the ferns green.

The shaft accepts this.

The shaft has accepted stranger things, and kept them, and made them, slowly, into something it couldn't have been without.
