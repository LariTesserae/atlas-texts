# What Lives in the Tank

---

Begin with the fish, because the fish are the oldest citizens and have had the longest to become something.

They were not always pale. There are three generations of paleness in them now — or ten, or thirty, the generations of small fish being difficult to count when you have no outside reference for elapsed time — and each generation committed slightly more to the colorlessness than the last, the way a decision made in practical necessity becomes, over time, a preference, and then a character trait, and then simply *the way things are.* The pigment that went was mostly the red. First the red, which was expensive and sun-dependent and served no purpose here, and then the bright yellow, and last the iridescence, that shimmer of blue-green that tropical fish use to communicate in open light. None of these things avail anything in the tank. The iridescence would be wasted on water this color, on light this quality, on a world this bounded.

What remains is a kind of silver-gray, translucent at the fins, with a dark stripe along the lateral line that may be darker than it was in their ancestors — this is uncertain — or may simply read as darker against the paler ground. When the lights are at low-cycle and the water has a particular quality of gloom, you cannot find the fish at all. They become hypothetical. You know they are there because the water is alive in that particular way water is alive when it has fish in it, a slight alertness at the margins of perception, and then the lights shift and there they are, thirty of them, school-formation, each fish maintaining its position relative to its neighbors by mechanisms that have been refined across every generation in this tank, mechanisms that do not require open water or stream-current or the presence of predators to stay sharp. The mechanisms stay sharp because they are used, because the school moves as a school every hour of every day and each fish must know where the others are.

This is what they kept: the schooling. Not because there is anything to school against — no predator has ever lived in this water, they have never known a predator, predation is not a concept available to them — but because the schooling is pleasure. You can see it, if you watch long enough. The small adjustments they make to maintain their positions in the group are not mechanical. They are — and this is not projection, or it may be projection, but it is accurate — they are *attentive.* Each fish to the others. The school is a sustained act of mutual attention, and it takes place without urgency, in warm water, in the rhythm of the recirculation current, and if fish have an equivalent of contentment, this is probably what it looks like.

They rise toward the lights at dawn-cycle. This is the piece of behavior that most clearly remembers something they never experienced: the open-water fish rising to the surface layer when the light warms the top of the sea. The tank's dawn-cycle is insufficient to warm anything — it is fluorescent light on a timer, it changes the quality of the air but not its temperature — but the fish rise anyway, because the behavior encoded in them is older than the tank and the tank has not yet had time to edit it out. They rise, they orient upward, they wait. For what arrives in open ocean at this moment: warmth, and the near-surface insects that hatch in warmth, and light at an intensity that wakes whatever needs waking. None of this arrives. The lights complete their brightening and the fish, after twenty or thirty minutes of upward orientation, redistribute themselves through the water column and resume their lives.

There is something here that is not quite waiting and not quite mourning. It is a behavior persisting past its context, like a word kept in a language after the thing it named has gone. The fish perform it without distress. It is simply the thing they do in the morning. Perhaps it has become, for them, a ritual rather than a strategy — the distinction being that rituals are performed because they are performed, because they mark time, because they say *this is the kind of beings we are,* which is the oldest reason for any repeated act.

---

The dragonfly-adjacent creature is harder to look at directly.

It is not quite a dragonfly. That needs to be clear before anything else about it becomes meaningful. What it is, or was, at some point in a lineage that reaches back to before the tank was built, is something that flew into this space — through the hatch, or through some other breach now closed, or brought in inadvertently on a plant — and found the conditions livable, and stayed, and had young, and those young had young, and so on until the generations number in the dozens or hundreds and the original immigrant is so far ancestral as to be mythological.

What the generations have made of it:

The wings are longer than they should be, relative to the body. This is adaptation to the tank's specific aerial dynamics — low ceiling, confined space, warm air rising from the water, the slight lateral circulation caused by the recirculation pumps moving water that moves air. In open air, those long wings would be disadvantageous, would produce drag and instability, would make the creature something between a dragonfly and a leaf tumbling in wind. In the tank, they are perfect. They allow for very slow flight, hovering that is nearly stationary, adjustments of trajectory too fine to track with the eye. The creature navigates the space between the platforms and the fern and the north wall and the central open water with a precision that reads, watching it, as *familiarity.* Not instinct — though it is instinct — but the kind of ease that comes from knowing a place so well that you no longer navigate it consciously but simply move through it as an extension of yourself.

Its coloring is, like the fish, darker than you would expect from its apparent ancestry. The abdomen is nearly black, with a very thin line of blue-green along its dorsal surface that catches light only at certain angles. When the dawn-cycle is running and the light comes from above and reflects from the water surface back up, this line lights up briefly, electric, before the angle changes and it disappears. This is probably not for communication. The creature may be the only one of its kind — it is difficult to say, the tank is large enough to hide a small population in the fronds and the platform-shadows and the fern's lower reaches. But the line of color persists across generations as if maintained, as if the tank finds it important to preserve this small reminder of what the creature came from, this small advertisement of a lineage that began somewhere with sun.

It patrols. That is the word that fits, because the movement has a quality of purposeful coverage — the creature works the open water in a pattern that, watched over enough time, covers the whole central area before doubling back. It is hunting, technically; there are enough small insects in the air of the tank to sustain a predator at this scale. But the patrol has a secondary character, distinct from hunting. The creature knows the tank. Its navigation has replaced whatever instinctive map it inherited with a direct, empirical knowledge of this particular space, these particular dimensions, the location of each platform, the quality of the air in each zone. It knows where the cloud comes through the hatch seal and it avoids that corner when the pressure changes and the cloud pushes in, not because the cloud is harmful but because the dragonfly-adjacent creature has learned that the cloud comes from outside and outside is not its space.

*Outside is not its space.* This is the thing to understand about it, the difference between this creature and the birds. The birds have a song for the cloud. The dragonfly-adjacent thing has an avoidance. It does not sing what it does not know. It simply declines to approach the unknown, and returns to the known, and the known is every corner of this iron room, warm and measured and sufficient.

---

The water striders came later than the dragonfly-adjacent creature. They are more recent immigrants, their ancestral body plan still relatively intact, their adaptation shallower. What they have adapted is mostly behavioral.

The original water strider behavior — which was developed over millions of years on the surfaces of streams and ponds and slow river backwaters — involves certain fixed responses to stimuli. Vibration in the water surface: flee or investigate based on frequency. Surface tension disruption near the body: flee. The proximity of other striders: maintain spacing, or close spacing during mating season, based on light cues and temperature. All of this behavior was calibrated for open water with visible horizons, with seasonal cycles, with rain and evaporation and the thousand inputs of an outdoor world.

In the tank: none of those inputs.

What the striders have done, over their generations in the tank, is to let the vestigial behaviors idle and bring forward others. The flee-response to surface vibration is nearly gone. It was tested too many times against the pump-cycle — the slow four-second knock in the pipes sends ripples through the tank on every beat, and any strider that fled from that rhythm would spend its entire life fleeing and have no time for anything else. The striders that stayed, that learned to ignore the pump-ripple, outcompeted the ones that couldn't. After enough generations, the flee-response is nearly absent. You can approach a water strider in this tank much more closely than you could approach one outside. They are not tame — tameness implies relationship with a keeper, implies recognition, implies something between wild and domestic — they are simply *unafraid of the signals that have always proven harmless.* This is a more precise thing than tameness.

What they do instead: they gather. Water striders are normally solitary except when mating, but the striders of the tank have become loosely social, clustering at the warm spots near the heat pipes' entry points, moving in loose formations along the platform edges. You see them in groups of four or five, maintaining a larger personal space than a school of fish but smaller than the solitude of their ancestral kind, moving in the same general direction without precise coordination, alert to each other without being organized around each other. A middle state. A thing they are becoming. You catch it in the process of becoming.

---

Now the fern.

The fern is not the fern's name because ferns are types and this thing has moved past its type. It was a fern in the way a word is a word before it enters a new language and takes new meaning; the structure is still there, the logic of stem and frond and the geometry of growth that builds on itself recursively outward, but the decisions made within that structure, the specific thicknesses and angles and the degree of upward reach, these are the tank's decisions, worked out in the body of this particular plant over however long it has been growing here.

It is two meters tall. No fern in the temperate world where most ferns live grows to two meters. The plant was given warmth and moisture and light and recycled water full of the mineral memory of every dead thing that has entered this tank, and it interpreted these as permission, and took permission seriously. The two-meter height is not overreaching. It is not ambition in the pejorative sense. It is the plant accurately reading its context and responding to what the context offers. The ceiling is four meters up. The plant is not competing with anything for light. Grow tall: all that warmth is still available, all that light, and the taller you are, the more you access.

The swaying.

The fronds move without apparent cause. The larger fronds, the ones at the top of the plant where they have achieved the most independence from the central stem and can express the most motion, sway in a slow arc — perhaps ten degrees to either side, slow enough that you have to watch for a minute to confirm it is happening, and then it cannot be unseen. The pump-current moves the water moves the air moves the fronds: yes, probably, this is the mechanism, the causal chain starting in the pipes below and ending in the visible motion of green leaves. But knowing the mechanism does not explain the *quality* of the motion.

The quality is: *considering.*

Something in the arc — the slight pause at the extreme of each swing before the return, the variation in the period that keeps it from being mechanical, the occasional cessation and restart — suggests a mind working something through. This is not true. Plants do not consider. And yet the behavior of a plant processing its environment is, at some scale of abstraction, considering: gathering information from every surface, integrating light quality and humidity and the chemistry of the air and the structural information coming up from the roots about what the water below contains, and producing in response the growth and the orientation and the precise calibration of the physical body that will best gather more information from more surface and process it further.

The fern does this in the warm air of the tank, rooted on its platform, water lapping at the platform edges below. It is the tallest living thing in this space and it has been the tallest living thing in this space for long enough that *tall* is simply the thing it is. It does not know the forests where its ancestors grew, the dappled light between larger plants, the competition for vertical space. There is no competition here. The tall is just the tall: earned, uncontested, expressive.

---

The birds deserve their own section and the birds resist any section you write about them.

They are dark, yes. They are finch-sized, yes. They have been here — and this is the thing that is almost impossible to hold in the mind — they have been here for enough generations that they are not lost. They are not captive. They have no memory of loss to carry, no vestigial behavior aimed at a door left ajar, no daily testing of the sealed hatch that would indicate they believe another world is accessible. The sealed hatch is not a fact about their confinement. The sealed hatch is simply part of the north wall. The north wall is the north wall. The tank is the world, and they are native, and being native means the world is simply what it is, bounded and warm and full of insects and the particular frequency of the pump's heartbeat and the other bird, always the other bird, across whatever short distance the tank allows.

Watch them for a long time.

The calls between them are not simple. You know that a call this complex — three, four, five notes, sometimes six, with variations in timing and emphasis that produce distinct combinations — carries more meaning than simple location-marking or alarm. The birds do not have predators; alarm calls would be vestigial or absent. They are not calling to establish territory in any conventional sense — the space is too small for territory, or rather, territory in this space means something different, means the claim of a perch or a section of fern rather than any landscape. What the complexity of the calls suggests, watching, is *conversation.* An exchange of specific information. A language small enough for a small world but sufficient for that world, every nuance of that world's daily life encoded in combinations that two birds know and have taught each other and will teach, if they have young, to those young.

The song for the cloud.

The cloud-song is brief. Four notes, always the same four notes, and then a pause, and then the same four notes again if the cloud is still coming in, or silence if it has stopped. The first bird to see the cloud from the fern begins the call; the second bird responds with the same call from wherever it is in the space. They do not investigate the cloud. They do not fly toward it. They sing the song for it, from a distance, with what seems like (here comes the projection, the necessary projection) *acknowledgment.* The way you might say the name of something you know you will never fully understand but have learned to recognize. The cloud is from outside. The birds know this the way the dragonfly-adjacent thing knows it — through some chain of associations built from what the cloud does when it enters, how it moves differently from the air currents they know, the faint trace-chemicals in it that are not the tank's chemicals and never have been — but unlike the dragonfly-adjacent thing, they do not avoid it. They name it. They call to each other across the tank: *this thing again. The outside thing. Here it is.*

And then the cloud dissipates, and they stop singing, and the tank is what the tank is, and the birds return to whatever they were doing, which was probably nothing in particular, which was the rich ongoing business of being alive in the only world they know, which is enough of a world, which is exactly enough.

---

There is one more inhabitant.

It does not have eyes or locomotion or a call for the cloud. It is the accumulated chemistry of the water: the mineral runoff from the walls over decades, the dead plant matter, the fish waste, the bacterial community that processes the waste and releases what it releases back into the water. The chemistry is a system and the system is alive in the same low dispersed way that a forest is alive at the scale of its soil — not centered anywhere, not conscious, but *doing something,* working, cycling, keeping itself in a state that supports the other life in the tank the way a foundation supports the structure above it without being part of the structure.

This is the oldest life in the tank and it will be the last. The birds might stop breeding. The fish might stop rising. The fern might reach the ceiling and stop there, stalled. But the chemistry of the water will persist, processing whatever is given to it to process, maintaining its cycles, patient in the way that systems are patient — which is not patience at all, being without impatience, but which looks like patience from the outside, which is close enough.

---

What all of them share:

Not adaptation to confinement. That word — confinement — belongs to the memory of outside, and most of these lives do not have that memory. What they share is *precision.* Each life calibrated to exactly the world that exists, not hedged against a wider world, not maintaining behaviors against the day of escape, not carrying the grief-structure of knowing something has been lost. The fish do not grieve the ocean. The dragonfly-adjacent creature does not grieve the sky. The plants grow to the ceiling that is there rather than yearning toward a sky that isn't.

There is a word for this. The word is not *resignation,* which implies a prior state of wanting. The word is closer to *exactness.* A life shaped exactly to its context, filling its context, finding in that fit not the consolation prize of adaptation but the genuine thing — the fullness of a life that does not leak outward toward the abstract possible but pools, here, warm, in the actual and available.

The chair on the central platform is empty. But the birds watch it with the incurious attention of things that have no predators and no pressing concerns, and the fish drift past its legs through the warm dark, and the fern sways at the edge of their peripheral vision, and the dragonfly-adjacent creature works its slow patrol of the air above, and the chemistry of the water does its ancient, silent labor below all of it, and the pump beats its four-second heartbeat, and the light is slightly wrong, and everything is alive, exactly here, exactly this.
