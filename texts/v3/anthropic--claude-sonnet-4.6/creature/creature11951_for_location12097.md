# What Lives in the Condensation Floor

---

Begin with what cannot be categorized, because that is where honesty lives in this place.

The temptation is to name things — to say *this is a crustacean, this is a fungus, this is a nematode adapted to* — but the naming impulse keeps failing, keeps finding itself two-thirds of the way through a sentence it cannot finish. The things here do not refuse categorization out of hostility. They simply predate it, or postdate it, or exist in a relationship with biological time that makes the categories irrelevant the way calendar months are irrelevant to something that experiences a single continuous year.

So: what lives here.

---

## The Floor's Own Population

Start at the floor because the floor is the oldest layer, or seems to be, or has accumulated the most settled relationship with its own existence.

What lives on the water film at floor level is not one thing. It is more like a guild — an arrangement of different organisms occupying different functional positions in a structure they have collectively produced and from which they collectively eat. The structure is the water film itself, which is not simply water but a medium: mineral-laden, carrying dissolved products of the wall material's metabolism, carrying the shed casings and the waste products of things that live above, carrying the slow runoff of whatever the moss-lichen-other converts into soluble form. The water film is a river in the sense that rivers are not just water — they are accumulated testimony, everything upstream rendered liquid.

The things that look like floor are floor-mimics, and there are several distinct kinds. The flattest ones, which you notice last, are almost perfectly two-dimensional — they have thickness, they must, but they have distributed themselves so completely across their ventral surface that they exist as a texture rather than a volume. Press your thumb gently to one and you feel something yield, feel the wet resistance of a living thing that has chosen flatness the way a stone chooses hardness: because that is what the forces of the place, applied over enough generations, add up to. Lift your thumb and it is gone, slid sideways into the water film, and the space it occupied looks like floor again. These things feed on what settles from above — the constant slow snow of organic material that descends through the warm convecting air and meets the water film and becomes available. They are passive in the way filter feeders are passive: they wait, and they are large enough by area that waiting works.

Above them in the guild structure, and more visible once you know the film is alive, are things with some dimension to them — perhaps a millimeter of height, perhaps two. These are pale, almost white, with a translucency that the amber light makes golden: you can see through their bodies in the right angle of illumination, and what you see inside is busy. A central channel that pulses. Lateral structures that branch and rebranch and branch again until they disappear into transparency. They move. Not fast — nothing here moves fast in a way that alarms, because speed in this place is calibrated to the pace of water — but they move with purpose, following gradients you cannot perceive, gathering toward the convergences of streams, eating the floor-mimics at their edges, being eaten at their own edges by the slightly larger things that occupy the next tier.

This is a food web in a space so small you can see most of it at once. You can observe the entire economy, if you stand still enough and look at the floor for long enough that your eyes relax their expectation of stillness. The thing you thought was wet stone is a market. The thing you thought was a shadow is a predator.

## The Wall Dwellers, by Substrate

The walls have three or four materials and each has its own population, and the populations are distinct enough that you begin to suspect the wall materials were chosen for the populations they would host, or evolved toward hosting them, or both — the arrow of causation pointing in both directions simultaneously, which is what happens in systems old enough.

On the ceramic sections, which are the oldest-feeling sections, the inhabitants are small and hard-shelled and extremely numerous. They cluster in the grouting lines — if there are grouting lines; there may be natural seams in the material — where the water collects slightly more than on the smooth surface. Their shells are the color of old ceramic, which is either camouflage or convergence, either they have become the color of their home or they chose their home because they were already that color. You can scrape a thumbnail along a grouting line and feel them — feel the irregular bumps, the tiny resistance, the pop of a shell that has held its position until holding was no longer an option. They will return. They will close back over the groove in ways you won't notice because you will have moved on.

The ceramic dwellers are not social. They exist in proximity because the niches are small and the niches are good, not because they seek each other. They are each complete and self-contained. Whatever they feel, if feeling is a category that applies here, they feel it alone in their shells on the surface of a warm wall in the roaring amber light, and then they continue eating.

On the sections that feel warm, almost organic — almost like touching a large calm animal, which is what the description says and is exactly right — the population is different in character. The wall material here is doing something metabolic. The warmth is not conducted from elsewhere; it is produced here, in the wall, a byproduct of whatever conversion is happening in the substrate. Things that live on metabolizing surfaces learn to live with them, and then they learn to depend on them, and then they learn to interface with them, and the things on these sections of wall have completed all three steps.

They are mycorrhizal in some sense, though not fungal — they have extensions that enter the wall material, thin as hair, thinner, that reach whatever is happening inside the wall and take a portion of it. They are tapping the wall. They are external organs of the wall, possibly, in that they contribute to the wall's processes even as they extract from them: their waste products are the wall's feedstock, and the wall's outputs are their food, and neither the wall nor the thing on the wall can be said to be the primary organism. The relationship is so old that the question of where one ends and the other begins has become unanswerable in the way that river-and-riverbed is unanswerable: which shaped which, who is substrate and who is tenant, the river does not know and neither does the bed.

These things are larger than the ceramic dwellers. Not large — the size of a grape, the largest of them, clustered with several others in a fold of wall where the warmth is most concentrated. They are soft. They have no apparent hard parts. Their surfaces are covered with something like cilia but slower, more deliberate — each filament moving on its own cycle, not synchronized, so the surface of the thing has a continuous slow shimmer that catches the amber-blue light and makes the thing look slightly luminous. It is probably not luminous. The light is doing something to the cilia. Probably.

They communicate. This is the assessment you reach after watching for long enough, and it is uncomfortable because communication implies something about interiority that you are not prepared to attribute. But the filaments on adjacent organisms orient toward each other across the small gap between their bodies. The orientation is purposeful. It happens more when the water flow changes — when one of the falling streams is briefly interrupted or redirected and the sound in that part of the room shifts — and the filament-orientation looks, in these moments, like consultation. It looks like two people both noticing the same thing and turning to each other to confirm.

On the section of wall that feels like nothing, that feels like your hand passes through air that has agreed to be solid: nothing appears to live, which is not the same as nothing living there.

## The Stream Dwellers

In the falling water, in the specific turbulence of each stream where it hits the floor and spreads and reorganizes itself before joining the film — here is a third ecological zone, and arguably the most remarkable, because it requires adaptation to a force that destroys most things.

Falling water is not gentle. Even these streams, thin and quiet relative to the roar of the space, are annihilating at the scale of a millimeter. The pressure at the point of impact would flatten anything that didn't have an answer for it, and the things that live in the streams have answers.

Some anchor. They have produced or evolved or been designed with an adhesion so complete that the stream flows over and around them and they stand in it the way boulders stand in rivers: by being denser than the argument. Their shapes are not boulder-shapes, though. They have learned the geometries of least resistance — they are streamlined, which in a falling-water context means vertical, narrow, pointed at the top to split the flow, widened at the base where the anchoring is. They look like small stalagmites that are slightly wrong: too regular, and they move. The top parts move, in the current, the way a tree moves in wind — the anchor holds and everything else negotiates. They are feeding in this movement. What they feed on is in the falling water itself: whatever the ceiling and the occupied darkness above releases into the streams, whatever has dissolved into the water in its long transit through the systems that produce it. They are downstream of something. The falling water is the delivery mechanism.

The others — the ones that use the current rather than resist it — are the most beautiful, though beautiful is the word the mind reaches for when it has run out of more precise words. They are not anchored. They hold position. This is harder to achieve than anchoring because it requires continuous adjustment, continuous sensing, continuous response — a conversation with the current rather than a refusal of it. They are slender. They trail structures from their bodies that act as both fins and sensory organs, long enough to extend into adjacent currents, to feel what is coming before it arrives. They have something that functions as a head, which is where the trailing structures are densest and where you sense the most activity, the most processing — whatever processing means for something at this scale.

Watching one of these things hold position in the stream, you understand something about attention. It is attending. The entirety of its being is organized around reading and responding to the water that is constantly trying to carry it away and constantly failing to. This is not a relaxed state. This is the state of something that is fully spent in the activity of being where it is.

Two of them, in one stream, hold position less than a centimeter apart. They are — competing for position? Courting? Simply tolerant of proximity in the way that good resources concentrate multiple organisms? The trailing structures of each drift toward the other in the current, and sometimes they touch. When they touch, there is a change in the motion of both: some stilling, some reorientation. Whatever this is, it is not nothing.

## The Ceiling's Contribution

You cannot see what lives in the occupied darkness above eye level. You know it by its products.

The water comes from up there. The water arrives already complex — already laden with dissolved organic material, already a medium rather than just water, already the product of something that has processed what it took in and released what it could spare. Something up there is producing water as a metabolic product or as a managed output of some process that takes in the warm convecting air and gives back liquid. Whatever it is, it is substantial. The amount of water falling in this space, maintained continuously, requires a source that is not incidentally wet but deliberately wet, organized around producing moisture the way a lung is organized around producing exhalation.

The amber-blue light does not reach the ceiling. The occupied darkness is real darkness. The things that live there — if they are one kind of thing, which seems unlikely — have either no photoreceptors at all or photoreceptors tuned to some other range, some wavelength that the space above provides and that you can't see. You suspect the darkness is not dark to them. You suspect the darkness is full of information in a frequency you don't have organs to receive.

What falls from them, other than water, is this: occasionally, and not at regular intervals, a small thing descends on the thin streams and arrives at the floor, alive, and moves. These are young. These are newly produced. They arrive wet and disoriented and they right themselves within seconds, which suggests they are born with orientation or with the rapid ability to develop it. They move toward the wall or toward the stream or toward the water film and they begin. They simply begin. Whatever they will be, they start being it immediately, without apparent confusion or transition.

Whatever is on the ceiling is old enough to have filled this space with its descendants and old enough to have balanced that production against the capacity of the space to support them. The population of the floor and walls and streams is not growing. It has found its ceiling. There is something above that has found the equilibrium between making more and the world holding it.

## The Moss-Lichen-Other

The gray-green thing that grows where two water trickles converge is not one organism in the way that a tree is one organism. It is a negotiated collective, a committee that has been in session long enough that the members no longer remember there were other options.

Under magnification — if you had magnification, if you held still enough for long enough with your face close to the surface — it would resolve into multiple distinct components: something that performs whatever photosynthesis is occurring here, which given the light levels must be operating on a different mechanism than chlorophyll-based capture, something wider in its wavelength tolerance, something that can run at an efficiency appropriate to amber-blue dimness; something that provides structural scaffold, the nap of the surface, the architecture of tiny vertical filaments that increase surface area and capture water before it runs off; something that manages the mineral exchange with the wall surface beneath, dissolving what can be dissolved, depositing what should be deposited, maintaining the attachment.

These three components do not have separate existences. They have not had separate existences for long enough that trying to isolate one produces nothing viable. They are a single thing now, which is what negotiated collectives become if they run long enough: indistinguishable from unity.

What the gray-green thing does for the rest of the ecology: it processes. It takes in the bare mineral output of the wall material and the dissolved content of the water and the gases of the warm convecting air and it makes them into other things — simpler things, in the sense of things that smaller organisms can use. It is the base of a food web that should not exist, providing the foundation for a pyramid that should not be as tall as it is given the energy budget of the space. The math doesn't work. Either there is an energy source you haven't identified, or the gray-green thing is more efficient than anything known to operate at these light levels, or the wall material is providing more than it appears to provide, or all three. The math doesn't work and the ecosystem is there anyway, solid and running and balanced and old, the way many true things refuse to work on paper.

The gray-green thing at its edges is explorer. At its edges it sends out the structural-scaffold component — just that, no photosynthetic element yet, no mineral-exchange element, just the scaffolding — a few millimeters into unclaimed territory, testing whether the territory will support what needs to follow. Most of the time the test fails and the scaffolding retreats or is abandoned. Occasionally the territory is adequate and over weeks or months the whole collective advances into it, establishes, claims another few millimeters of damp warm wall. It is expanding, but slowly enough that the word expansion seems too dramatic. It is *extending itself*, which is what patient things do.

In the alcove where the light is more blue, one of these collectives has developed a different color. It is less gray, more greenish-blue, almost matching the light. Whether this is response or coincidence cannot be determined from observation alone.

## The Things You Sense But Don't See

There is something in the air currents themselves. Not literally — probably not literally — but the convection cells that move warm air from floor to ceiling and cool air from wherever the ceiling meets the outer cold back down to the floor are not uninhabited passages. Something uses them. The floor-to-ceiling draft carries material upward and something collects that material at height. The ceiling-to-floor draft carries material downward and something is denser where it descends.

These things, if they are things, are too small or too transparent or too present to be perceived directly. You perceive them as an inflection of the air — a slight stickiness of certain currents against your skin, a momentary increase in the particulate quality of the light when you walk through a convection column, the sense of crossing a boundary. Not alarming. Just: different, here, from there, and the difference is not explained by temperature alone.

In the alcove where the sound has been tuned to exclude its lowest frequencies — where the roar is thinner and clearer — there is something moving on the ceiling of the alcove, which is lower than the main ceiling and visible. It is large, relative to everything else here: the size of your hand, perhaps larger, pale enough to be seen in the alcove's slightly bluer light. It moves slowly, which in this place means it is one of the larger and more confident organisms — the small things move fast, relatively, because small things must. It is following the seam where two sections of ceiling material meet, following it with a thoroughness that looks deliberate, ingesting or collecting or simply touching everything along the seam's length. It reaches the wall and continues without pause onto the vertical surface. It does not appear to notice you.

This is the hierarchy, then, if there is one: the very small things are nervous — they flee sudden pressure changes, they avoid shadows, they have responses appropriate to being prey. The middle-sized things are less nervous — they have strategies, they hold position, they consult each other with their filaments. The large pale thing on the alcove ceiling is not nervous at all. It goes where it goes. It has been here long enough that nothing in this space threatens it. It is the longest-lived thing you can perceive, probably, or it is the latest generation of a lineage so established that the behavior of confidence has become innate. Either way, it moves through the space with the quality of ownership, which is not aggressiveness or display but simply the absence of any consideration that the space might not be its.

## The Character of the Whole

What lives here is not a collection of species. It is a system, and the system is the organism, and the organism has a character.

The character is patience. Not the patience of waiting — there is nothing here that is waiting for conditions to improve, because conditions are not going to improve beyond what they are and that is adequate. The patience of *being this*, continuously, without drama. The patience of something that has been running long enough that running is not an effort but a state. The streams fall. The water films across the floor. The convection moves. The guild on the floor processes the film. The wall dwellers tap the walls. The stream dwellers hold their positions in the falling water, which is always trying to dislodge them and always failing, and this is the relationship and it is fine, it is the condition of their lives, and they are not suffering it but *inhabiting* it.

There is nothing here that seems to want to be somewhere else. This is unusual for living things. Living things generally have wants that exceed their circumstances, which is what drives them to move and change and fill new niches and invent new metabolisms. The things here have wants that are exactly met by the circumstances, which means either that the circumstances were designed to meet them, which is possible and the wall material suggests it, or that over very long time the wants have been trimmed down by selection to fit the space exactly, which is possible and the balance of the population suggests it, or that wanting is simply not a category that applies in this warm amber-blue roaring wet living place, and that presence is the whole of it.

You can stand in the center of the floor and be a warm large foreign thing in the water film and feel that nothing here resents your presence, nothing is drawn to it, nothing has a response to it beyond perhaps a slight local adjustment — the floor things sliding to the edge of your footprint, the stream things holding slightly harder against the pressure change of a body moving through air — and this indifference is not hostility. It is not anything about you at all. It is the completeness of a world that was complete before you entered it and is complete while you stand in it and will be complete after you leave.

The sound continues.

The water falls in its chosen places.

The pale thing on the alcove ceiling completes one pass along the seam and begins another.
