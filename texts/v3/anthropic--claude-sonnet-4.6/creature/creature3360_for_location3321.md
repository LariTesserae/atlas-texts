# The People of Khorveth Mere

---

Begin with the hands.

Every person here is recognizable first by their hands — before face, before posture, before anything the eye can name quickly. The hands are wide across the palm, the fingers thicker than you expect at the base, and they carry a particular callousing that is not the callousing of grip work or impact work but something else, something more intimate: the callousing of constant small contact, of hands that press and test and smooth and seal, of fingers that have spent decades in wet mortar joints, in seep-fed crevices, against mineral-slick stone. The skin of the palm is worn somewhere between leather and stone itself — not cracked, not roughened in the way of cold-and-dry climates, but compressed, the whorls of the fingerprints still legible but deepened, as if the identifying marks of the person have been pushed further into the self for safekeeping.

They are not large people. They are not small people. What they are is *close to the ground* — not in stature but in the way they hold themselves, the way they move. The slight forward lean that the cold forces on everyone has, over time and generations, become structural. The muscles along the upper back and across the chest are dense from the lean, from the constant compensation of a body pitched slightly into weather that never quite qualifies as weather. They look, at first glance, like people who are always about to begin climbing. At second glance, you understand: they are always climbing. Every surface slopes. Every surface is wet. The permanent lean is not fatigue — it is readiness, a body organized around the knowledge that the ground is never quite where you left it.

---

The face.

The faces of Khorveth Mere are squinting faces. This is not a comment on disposition — it is the simple physical consequence of living under a sky that is all-white and no-direction. The squint is continuous, reflexive, so habitual that the muscles required for it are among the most developed on the face. The skin beside the eyes and at the bridge of the nose carries the compression of decades. The crow's feet here are not the crow's feet of smiling — they run slightly differently, slightly more lateral, as if the face has been gathering itself horizontally against the light for its entire life.

The eyes, when you manage to see them opened fully — which happens rarely, indoors, in the interior dark — are the kind of eyes that look like they are always doing two things at once. Soft focus for the wide space, sharp focus for the immediate surface. You can see the switching. It's slight, but it's there: the eyes adjust by increments, and then adjust back, and they have been doing this since childhood, since the first day someone carried them into the flat white light and their pupils contracted and have never fully relaxed since.

The coloring is what the plateau's light has made of it: no sunburn, because the sun diffuses its energy across the whole sky and lands as brightness without peak. The skin tends toward something greyish at first impression — and then you realize it is not the skin's color but the reflection of the limestone in everything, the wet stone bouncing light upward in a particular frequency that erases warmth from what it touches. The actual skin, seen in a different light, is whatever it is. The plateau light lies about this. You stop trusting the plateau light for color very quickly.

---

They move at one speed.

This is the thing that takes longest to understand about them: they do not have a faster mode. Watch them — the child redirecting the seep-channel, the two people building the wall, the wrapped old person crossing the rooftops with their arms full — and try to identify what would happen if something required them to hurry. You cannot imagine it. Not because they are slow. They are not slow. Their pace across the wet treacherous stone is brisk, purposeful, covers ground. But it is a pace without gradient. There is no latent urgency waiting behind the calm surface, no reserve speed that urgency would unlock. What you see is the whole capacity, operating continuously.

The people of other places save speed for when it's needed. The people of Khorveth Mere have spent their speed.

Or rather: they've converted it. The energy that would elsewhere be reserved for emergency runs steadily here, a constant low current, and the result is that everything gets done at the same steady rate whether or not everything is urgent, which, here, it always is. A stone in the wrong place, a seep gaining force, a mortar joint opening — these are emergencies and they are treated with the same measured attending that everything else receives, because treating them differently would mean having a different speed, and there is no different speed, and this is not a failure of urgency but an understanding that urgency, as most people practice it, is waste.

---

The body learns the terrain from the inside.

Ask someone from Khorveth Mere how to go from one building to another and they will not describe the path in terms of landmarks or turns. They will describe it in terms of *surfaces*. This stretch runs with water in the morning — lean right and don't trust the left foot until you reach the grey section. The ramp between the second and third terrace is safe three feet in from either edge, nowhere else. The roof of the long building has a soft spot near the chimney — you'll feel it before you think you feel it; trust the feeling.

The wayfinding is entirely proprioceptive. It's a map of what the body knows. And the body here knows the terrain so specifically, so thoroughly, that the knowledge has become architectural — built into the gait, the lean, the way the foot settles and tests before committing weight. You can watch this in a person who has lived here long: each step is a small negotiation that isn't visible as negotiation, that looks like ordinary walking, except it is nothing like ordinary walking. It is walking that has absorbed forty years of wet limestone into its muscle memory and walks that limestone every time.

Children here learn to walk twice. The first time is the universal learning — the lurching, falling, trying again that all children do. The second time is harder, begins around age five or six, and takes longer, because the second learning is not about balance in the abstract but about balance on *this ground*, this specific shifting wet ground, and the specific knowledge required cannot be passed through instruction. It can only be acquired through the accumulation of small falls and corrections and adjustments on the actual surfaces until the body knows what the mind cannot be told.

The children fall. They fall constantly, cheerfully, and without drama. Falling is pedagogy here. The wet stone receives them and they get up, sometimes bleeding, always wet, and try again. No one makes this significant. The falls are the lesson.

---

The communication.

They do not speak often, and when they speak, they speak briefly, and when they are brief, they are precise. The language of Khorveth Mere is not a reduced language — it is a compressed one, which is different. Every word that exists here earns its existence through use-pressure, through the regular friction of communication in a place where sound is absorbed quickly and voices don't carry and the work being done at the moment of speaking requires some portion of the attention that speaking would otherwise use. The vocabulary is specific where specificity matters — there are many words for the states of wet stone, for the behaviors of seep-water, for the stages of mortar cure and the qualities of different limestone faces — and general where generality is sufficient, which is most of the rest of life.

What takes the place of speech is a kind of attentiveness that reads, from the outside, as something almost alarming. They watch each other. Not the watching of suspicion, not the watching of wariness, but the watching of people who've learned to read information from a person's body because that information often arrives before words would, or instead of words, or because the words aren't coming and the watching is the only channel available. A person approaching a shared task doesn't need to announce what they're going to do. The other person has been watching and knows. The coordination happens in the watching.

Two people building a wall and not speaking are not silent because they've said everything they need to say. They are silent because they don't need to say it.

There are exceptions. Old people, sometimes, speak at length. And when they do, the young listen with a quality of attention that seems outsized until you understand: the old people of Khorveth Mere have had more years to compress more information into fewer words. When they speak at length, the length is always relative — what they consider at length, others would consider a paragraph. But the paragraph is dense, and everything in it is there because it survived the pressure to remove it, and so everything in it matters. The young are listening because they've learned that this is where the compressed knowledge is. This is where the forty years of wet limestone go when they can finally be spoken.

---

The old people.

The old are not identifiable by slowness. They are identifiable by something like economy — by a subtraction of unnecessary motion that reveals what remains to be purely load-bearing, every gesture structural. Where a younger person might carry out a task through several movements and a small correction, the old carry it out in fewer movements with the correction built in invisibly. They have removed, over decades, everything that wasn't necessary, and what remains looks effortless the way very expensive things look simple: all the cost is hidden in what's not there anymore.

The oldest people of Khorveth Mere are the ones who know where all the soft spots are. They are the ones who know which seeps run fast after rain and which seeps keep their own counsel regardless of what the sky does. They know which building joints have held and which need watching. They know the terrain of this place in a way that has moved entirely out of thought and into reflex — a kind of knowing that is no longer stored in the mind because it has been absorbed into the body so completely that the mind has been freed of it. They don't think about the soft spots. They avoid them the way breathing doesn't require thought about breathing.

When an old person of Khorveth Mere dies, what is lost is not irreplaceable. But what is lost is very slow to replace.

---

The children.

Everything interesting about the children is that they are not apprentice adults. They are complete children, doing child things, in a place that doesn't particularly accommodate child things — a place that is cold and wet and treacherous and small — and they have found, within those constraints, the full range of what children do. They are loud when they can be, which is not often, because the stone absorbs. They find the places where sound does something — a particular interior space, the right pair of walls facing one another — and they use these places. They make the dark-furred animals the center of elaborate and persistent attentions. They fall, as established. They do not seem to find falling remarkable.

The specific intelligence of the children here is spatial and material. They understand stone. They understand water, its logic, its behavior, what it will do if you give it an opening and what it will do if you try to stop it. They understand these things the way children in other places understand social hierarchies or narrative or number — intuitively, at depth, with a fluency that comes from total immersion in the subject matter since before conscious thought was possible. A child of seven here can predict where a seep will emerge on a freshly broken limestone face, can point to the place in the rock where the water pressure is building, can tell you not in words but by pressing their palm to the stone and nodding when they feel what they expected to feel. They learn what water does before they learn what words do, and this sequence shapes everything that comes after.

They are, in the way of children everywhere, already becoming the adults they will be. The hands are forming. The squint is beginning. The gait is being negotiated, fall by fall. The watching — that quality of attention — is already present, already the primary mode, already the thing they use instead of speaking when speaking is unnecessary or absent or simply not as efficient.

---

The relationship to the system.

No person of Khorveth Mere controls the plateau. This is not a philosophical position they hold; it is a conclusion the plateau has imposed on them and they have accepted, quietly, without ceremony. The seeps do what the seeps do. The stone shifts when the frost cracks it and is still when the frost is gone. The dark-furred animals route and reroute on their own business, and that business happens to align with the drainage concerns of the settlement, and this alignment was not engineered — it was noticed, gradually, by people who were watching, and it was accommodated when it was recognized, and the accommodation was small, barely an accommodation, more like a decision not to work against something that was already working.

This is their genius, if genius is the right word, which it may not be: they see the system they are part of and they work *with* the grain of it. Not because they are philosophically committed to working with the grain, but because they have tried working against the grain and the grain has always won and they are practical people who prefer not to repeat losing experiments. The seep will not be stopped. The seep can be directed. The stone will not be leveled. The stone can be worked smooth enough and then accepted as it is. The cold will not be driven out. The cold can be accounted for in every dimension of a building, every angle, every arrangement of sleeping bodies and stored heat.

They are not humble about this. Humility implies having thought about it and choosing the modest posture. They are something prior to humble: they are people who have simply never developed the illusion that the plateau could be made to yield to human preference, because the plateau has not yielded, not once, in the entirety of the time they can remember the plateau, and the time before that is written in the limestone terraces, and the limestone terraces have no record of yielding either.

What they have instead of mastery is *fit* — the precise, earned, continually-maintained fit of a people and a place that have been working out their arrangement across a span of time longer than any one person can hold in mind.

---

The inside.

The interior of the buildings is not what the exterior predicts. The exterior is the grey-blue limestone, the functional openings, the mortar packed flat, the density of structures built into one another without softness. The interior is also not soft. But it is close. It is the closeness of a space where nothing is extraneous, where every object has survived whatever pressure removed the non-survivors, and the objects that remain are exactly as present as they need to be — functional objects that have become beautiful through their fitness, through the way long use has worn them to the shape of the hands that use them, through the way a thing that has done its job well for many years acquires a kind of dignity that has nothing to do with decoration.

There is warmth inside, but it is the warmth of accumulated bodies and small fires and the particular thermal capacity of thick limestone walls, which take a long time to cool. The fires are small because fuel is limited. The bodies are many because the spaces are small and the cold is insistent. People sleep close. Eat close. Work close. The personal distance that other places consider ordinary is not available here, and it is not missed, because it has never been the norm, because the norm is close, because close is simply the shape that thirty or forty people take when they occupy a settlement the size of a small town square on a cold wet plateau.

There is no privacy and it has not produced people who seem in need of privacy. What has been produced instead is something more sophisticated: the ability to be alone inside yourself in the company of others, to have an interior life that occurs simultaneously with the shared exterior life, to be present to both without one canceling the other. This is not a skill they could name. It is simply how it is to be a person here.

---

What they have made that is not functional.

It would be a mistake to say that everything in Khorveth Mere is functional and nothing is otherwise. There are marks on the limestone faces — inside, at heights where hands could reach — that are not mortar-work, not repairs. They are marks made by people pressing pigment into stone, or scratching lines with harder stone, and they depict — things. Shapes of the flat-winged birds, made in a few lines that capture the wide wings and the finger-feet with an economy that seems almost contemptuous of effort. Shapes of the dark-furred animals, in groups, moving across surfaces, arrested in the moment of the quick-change direction they do on real stone. Shapes of water, which is hard to draw and harder to draw well and has been drawn here thousands of times by people who understood it well enough to get it right.

No human forms. This is a thing you notice eventually. The people of Khorveth Mere have not drawn themselves. They have drawn everything else of their world — the birds, the animals, the water, occasional shapes that might be stone formations, the outline of the terraces themselves — but not the person looking. This is not absence of self-regard. It is something more particular: the self is the one thing they don't need to record, because the self is the one thing that doesn't require the same close watching, the same careful notation. They know themselves already, at a depth beneath knowing.

The recordings are of the things outside the self that need watching. The things that are not yet fully understood. The things that might do something unexpected.

The self will do what the self will do. The plateau is the uncertain quantity.

---

Final thing: the threshold of outside.

A person of Khorveth Mere who leaves the plateau — which happens, occasionally, for reasons of trade or obligation or the particular restlessness that can strike even the most settled people — does not look right elsewhere. This is not a judgment. It is an observation about the relationship between a people and their specific place, which becomes visible when the place is removed.

Off the plateau, they are too still in the wrong moments — standing in what other people's eyes reads as motionlessness but is actually the full-speed motion of a person attending carefully to surfaces that are, by comparison, simple; their attention going to ground that doesn't need it, leaving them with a quality of distracted calm that reads as strangeness. They look too long at water that isn't doing anything interesting. They step with unnecessary care on level ground that poses no problem. They flinch slightly at sounds that carry — the sounds of places where stone is dry and surfaces are flat and voices travel the full distance from mouth to ear without being caught and absorbed along the way.

And they squint, always, even at skies that are directional, that have one sun in one place, that give a shadow you could set a clock by. They squint because the squint is no longer a response to the light. The squint has become the face itself. The face has made the plateau permanent in its own musculature.

They carry it with them. They are it, wherever they are.

The limestone has entered their hands, their gait, their eyes. The cold has entered their lean. The water has entered their attention. The need to repair continually and maintain continually and watch continually has entered their pace, the one speed, the pace that has no faster mode because the faster mode was spent long ago and wisely and in exchange for something more durable than speed.

What they received in exchange is this: they fit somewhere. Completely, precisely, irreversibly. They fit a specific cold wet bright hard place the way mortar fits a joint — under pressure, over time, calcifying slowly, until what was applied becomes indistinguishable from what it was applied to.

There is no word for what that is that does not also apply to the limestone.
