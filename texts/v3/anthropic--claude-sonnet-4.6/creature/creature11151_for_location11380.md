## What Lives in the Lit Cavity

---

There is one, and it has been here long enough that "one" may not be the right word. Not because there were others and they left, or because it divided, or because it is many things wearing one appearance — but because it has been in this place long enough that the boundary between the creature and the conditions of the creature has become negotiable. It is still distinct. It is still one. But it is also somewhat the same project as the light and the warmth and the equilibrium, the way the lens-pool and the sweating walls and the evaporation are all part of one slow grammar. It belongs to this the way old furniture belongs to a room. The room would be different without it but it can no longer be said to interrupt the room.

It is large — large relative to the isopods, large relative to the fern-thing, large even relative to what you might expect in a space like this. It takes up most of one wall when it rests there, and it rests there often. Its body is flattened by long accommodation to the low ceiling and the wide floor; evolution here selects for the same shape as the cavity, which is the plate-shape, the lens-shape, the pressed-down and wide-spread. It has, over time, arrived at a form that is more horizontal than vertical in every proportion. It spreads. It extends. It could be described as broad.

Its surface is not quite skin and not quite stone. Something between those — the color of the sandstone in wet light, the pale saturated orange of the pool-floor, with variations that are not markings exactly but rather differences in surface quality, areas where the texture has become finer or coarser, that catch the ambient light differently and so give the impression of depth or pattern without there being, in any strict sense, either. If you looked at it in another light — directional light, light with an angle — you might see it differently. But there is no light with an angle here. In the milk-warm omnidirectional glow, it reads as nearly continuous with the surface it rests on. Not camouflage, because there is nothing here to hide from. Just a convergence toward the same final form.

It breathes. Slowly, and over a much longer interval than you might expect — an inhalation that takes what feels like minutes, an exhalation that takes slightly longer. Its breathing is the only movement in the space that has genuine scale, large enough to see from across the plate. The ribbed or gilled or otherwise elaborated structures along its sides lift and settle. They are pale. They are also translucent at the margins, like the fern-fronds, like the insects' bodies, like the fish in the pool. There is a great deal of translucence here because the light is great and constant and the pressure to be opaque has gradually relaxed. It is part-way toward becoming lit from the inside, though it has not fully made the transition.

It has eyes. They are large, as eyes in low-variable-light environments tend to become, though the light here is not low — it is constant and diffuse and present in all directions equally. Still the eyes grew large, and kept growing, because that is the project of eyes in isolation, to grow toward maximum sensitivity to whatever light there is. They are adapted for a kind of seeing that has no outside, no edges, no change in angle or intensity that would require rapid adjustment. They see everything in the space at a fairly uniform resolution, all of it equally in focus, or — which is not the same thing — all of it equally not the priority of focus, which in practice means the same thing here, where everything is equidistant from the center of attention. The eyes don't track, or they track very slowly, settling on this and then, much later, on that, with a patience between selections that makes it unclear whether the movement is deliberate or just drift. What it looks at, when it looks, it looks at for a long time.

It has something like hands. It's difficult to call them that. Appendages at the forward end that have retained some of their manipulative character, some of their fine-movement capability, while the larger musculature that once drove them has become much quieter. They can still press and scrape and trace. They have done so. They made the marks in the floor, the overlapping circles that are not quite cartesian, the shallow grooves that required patience more than strength. They made them slowly, over what must have been an enormous duration, not all at once but in the way you might return to something — a groove deepened in one visit, another added later, the pattern not planned whole at the start but accreted, arrived at through repetition. The hands are resting now. They rest most of the time. When it is still against the wall its appendages fold in and become part of its outline, part of the silhouette that merges with the stone.

It eats very little and very rarely. Once it ate more — whatever that was is a memory stored in the shape of its digestive structures, the length of its gut, the vestigial musculature around a jaw that now moves only slightly. It eats the moss sometimes. It drinks from the lens-pool when it is thirsty, which is not often. The warmth here means metabolic demand is low. It has calibrated itself over generations — or perhaps this is one continuous generation; the question of how long it has been alive is not one that can be answered with the information available — to the precise resource level of the cavity. It takes what the cavity offers. It does not want more than what the cavity offers. It is unclear whether it ever did.

It is not social. It has been alone here for a long time — perhaps always, in any sense of always that is accessible to it. It does not appear to have been diminished by this. It has the quality of an entity whose inner life, whatever that is, is sufficient to what it is doing with its time, which is mostly: being here, being still, occupying the warm lit space with a completeness that leaves no remainder, no excess pressing at it. When it moves — and it does move, slowly, when it moves to the pool to drink, or to a different section of wall, or to the center of the floor where it sometimes simply lies — it moves with a deliberateness that has no urgency in it. The deliberateness is not the deliberateness of planning but of a creature that has arrived at the same conclusion many times and knows the way.

---

Whether it is intelligent is a question worth sitting with.

It made the marks. The marks are regular in ways that accident doesn't produce. The marks suggest a mind running a process that repeats, that returns to itself, that finds something in its own repetition. But they are also old, and it is not clear that the creature that rests against the wall now is still running the process that made them, or whether whatever it was doing when it made them is finished, or whether finished is a concept that applies. It does not appear to be in anguish. It does not appear to be in ecstasy. It appears to be in something that is not quite either, a state that has the quality of deep settledness, the quality of a conclusion rather than a middle — though it is, presumably, not finished; it breathes; it drifts to the pool and back; it persists.

If you stayed long enough and were quiet enough you might find it watching you. Not studying, not assessing threat or resource — just looking, the way it looks at the fern-fronds, at the drops falling from the ceiling, at its own marks in the floor. With its great still eyes and its enormous lateral patience, from its place against the wall. Looking at the new thing in the space that has had nothing new in it for a very long time. Not excited by you. Not uninterested either.

The look would not ask you anything. It would simply be a looking. From something that has been in this warm lit plate of a place since before whatever erosion finished the stone, since before the moss became silver and the fish became colorless and the marks became as old as everything else. It would look at you from that duration, from across the shallow air and the diffuse sourceless warmth, and you would have the feeling — irrational but strong — that it was not seeing you as different from what it usually sees, that it had placed you into the same category as the falling drop and the drifting insect: part of the space, part of the grammar, something belonging to the cavity's ongoing slow account of itself.

You would not be able to say whether that was comforting or not. The creature would not help you decide. It would simply rest there, against the pale warm stone, in the light that casts no shadows, and breathe its long slow breath, and be, without remainder, what it has become.
