What lives there is, in the first and most essential sense, *time that has learned to hold its own shape.*

But that is the ending of the thought, not the beginning. Begin with what you can see.

## The Visible

Begin with the beetle on your hand.

It is perhaps eight millimeters long. Its elytra are the color of old bronze, not the bright bronze of new metal but the darkened, slightly greenish bronze of a coin that has been handled by many people over many years and carries the chemistry of all of them. Its legs work with a mechanical exactness that seems — in the first second of observation — overcomplicated for the task of crossing a human palm. Six legs, each jointed in multiple places, each joint capable of independent adjustment, the whole mechanism calibrated to traverse a substrate that shifts slightly under each footfall. The beetle does not pause. It does not investigate. It moves with the total commitment of something that does not experience hesitation, because hesitation is a cognitive luxury and the beetle's cognition, such as it is, runs on a substrate too small to afford it.

This is what the visible life of the hollow looks like, mostly. Small. Bronze. Moving with complete commitment in a direction that means something to it and nothing to you.

The other beetles are like this one but not identical. There are species here that have not been formally described. Not because no one has visited, but because the hollow is dense with arthropod life and taxonomy has not kept pace with density. Some of the small rove beetles — the staphylinids, narrow-bodied and fast, flickering through the moss stems like dark sparks — may belong to populations that have been isolated in this microclimate long enough to have become something new, something that has no name yet, something whose namelessness is not a gap in knowledge but simply evidence that the hollow's interior life has been generating variation faster than anyone has been watching. This is what climax communities do when no one is looking. They continue.

The slugs are more visible than the beetles, being larger and slower and making no effort at concealment. They are the grey-brown color of wet bark, with bodies that seem to have solved the problem of locomotion by abandoning all the assumptions that led to legs. They move on a single muscular foot, leaving a trail of mucus that catches light and then doesn't, depending on angle. Their stalked eyes look outward with an expression — if expression is the right word, and it may not be — of absolute presence. The slug is entirely where it is. It is experiencing the surface it moves across with a directness that most organisms with more elaborate nervous systems have lost the ability to access. The slug has the surface of the moss as its entire world, and it knows that world with a thoroughness that is not intelligence but is something that intelligence, at its best, tries to approximate.

When the slug reaches the base of a horsetail stem and begins to ascend, it does so without deciding. It does so because the chemical gradient it follows leads upward. The whole of its behavior is chemistry reading chemistry, surface reading surface, and the result, from outside, looks like intention.

## What Is Mostly Not Visible

Most of the life of the hollow cannot be seen.

This needs to be taken seriously as a description, not as a hedge or an admission of ignorance. The life of the hollow is structurally, systematically invisible, because it lives in the interior of things rather than on their surfaces. The moss body — those forty centimeters of compressed and layered biological material — is one of the densest habitats on earth per unit volume, and it is opaque. You cannot see into it. What lives there lives in a world of interlocking stems and decaying fronds and water films and bacterial mats and fungal networks that connects everything to everything else in ways that have no analogue in anything we build or imagine building.

In the top centimeter alone: springtails, *Collembola*, in numbers that feel like an error when you first read them in the literature. They have been on land for four hundred million years. They were here, or something very like them was here, before the first land plants had finished establishing themselves. They are primitive in the genuine sense — not simplified from something more complex, but original, the early draft that turned out to be good enough to keep. They eat fungal hyphae and decaying material and each other, and they move through the moss in patterns that are not random but are governed by chemical gradients too subtle to detect without instruments. They jump when disturbed, using a forked appendage locked under their abdomens like a spring. The jump takes them an enormous distance relative to their body length. In the microsecond of the jump they are the fastest things in the hollow. Then they land in the moss and are gone.

Below the springtails, deeper in the moss body: mites. The mite fauna of a moss mat is, in terms of species diversity, one of the richest communities in any terrestrial ecosystem. Oribatid mites, slow and heavily armored, moving through the matrix like tanks, eating fungi and bacteria and fragments of dead plant material. Predatory mites, faster and pale, hunting the oribatids and the springtails through a three-dimensional maze they navigate by chemistry and touch. The predatory mites have chelicerae — mouthparts — that are, at their scale, genuinely terrifying. They are terrifying the way a large mammalian predator is terrifying: not in their form, which is alien and difficult to parse, but in their function, which is precise and final and without ambiguity. Something is pursuing something else down there, in the dark between the moss stems. This is continuous. This does not stop.

Deeper still: earthworms, moving through the accumulation of decades, ingesting the old dead material and excreting it transformed. Their bodies are long and cylindrical and seem structurally simple but are not. The earthworm does not have eyes. It has no need for eyes in a world that is entirely tactile and chemical. It perceives its world through its skin, through the tension of the soil around it, through the chemistry of what it moves through. It is, in this sense, maximally intimate with its environment — not separated from it by a layer of interpretation, but in continuous direct contact, reading it with its entire surface simultaneously.

The earthworm has been in the hollow longer than the moss has been at its current depth. Its predecessors ingested material that is now compressed into the lower layers. Their castings are part of the structure of the place. The hollow is built, in part, from the metabolized output of its own inhabitants, across many generations. The worms have been eating the hollow and excreting the hollow and eating that, in a slow cycle that is one of the mechanisms by which the place maintains its depth and its chemistry. They are not visitors to the substrate. They are the substrate, performing one of its self-maintenance functions.

## The Dragonfly

There is a dragonfly at the rim.

It arrived twenty minutes ago and has not moved except for the small rotations of its head as it tracks movement in the air above the hollow. It is large — fifty millimeters, perhaps, from its face to the end of its abdomen — and it is perched on a fern frond at the exact point where the frond clears the rim and enters the open air, giving it sight lines in two directions: back into the hollow, and out across the surrounding low country.

The dragonfly is old in the particular sense that its lineage is old. Three hundred million years ago, before the dinosaurs, before most of the plant lineages represented in this hollow had appeared, there were dragonflies. Not these dragonflies — those were larger, their wingspans sometimes exceeding sixty centimeters, taking advantage of Carboniferous oxygen levels that no longer exist — but dragonflies in the same fundamental configuration, with the same compound eyes, the same wing venation, the same predatory hovering flight. The body plan has been validated by three hundred million years of continuous operation. It is one of the most validated body plans in the history of life on earth.

This particular dragonfly does not know this. It is not capable of knowing this in the sense of holding the information as information. What it knows, in the only sense available to it, is the position of every moving object in its visual field, simultaneously, processed through compound eyes that sample light at a rate faster than human vision can resolve, updated sixty times per second, with a field of view that approaches three hundred and sixty degrees. It is, in terms of visual processing speed and coverage, one of the most sophisticated visual systems in the hollow. It sees things you cannot see because they move too fast for you to see them, and it acts on them before you register that anything happened.

It leaves the fern frond without warning, takes something out of the air — a gnat, or a very small moth, or something that cannot be identified because the capture takes twelve milliseconds and the prey disappears before the eye can register it — and returns to the frond. It eats. The hollow continues around it.

The dragonfly has no interest in the interior of the hollow. It uses the hollow's airspace and its rimming vegetation as a hunting station. It relates to the beetles and the springtails and the earthworms and the mites the way an airport relates to the city it serves: functionally adjacent, dependent on the same infrastructure, otherwise completely separate in every meaningful way. The dragonfly and the earthworm are both animals. Beyond that, their lives do not intersect. They are neighbors who have never met.

## The Fungi

No description of what lives in the hollow is honest without the fungi, and the fungi are the part of this that is hardest to describe, because the fungi are not located in any one place in the way that the dragonfly is located on the fern frond, or the earthworm is located in the lower moss. The fungi are located *through* the hollow. They are the spatial structure of connection itself, rendered biological.

In the moss body, in the soil beneath it, in the root structures of the ferns at the rim, in the decomposing lower layers where the old seasons have compressed into something that is no longer plant material but not yet soil — through all of this, hyphal networks extend in three dimensions, branching and anastomosing, thicker where there are rich sources of carbon, thinner where there is less, but everywhere. Everywhere. There is no part of the substrate that does not contain fungal hyphae.

This is not metaphor and it is not exaggeration. The mycelial network is genuinely continuous across the floor of the hollow and up into the walls. You cannot take a sample of moss from anywhere in the depression without taking a sample of this network. The network has no center. It has no head. It processes information — chemical information, the presence or absence of sugars, the presence or absence of toxins, gradients of moisture — not in a brain but as a whole, the way a city processes information about traffic not in any single location but in the aggregate behavior of everyone moving through it.

The fungi are eating the dead and feeding the living. They receive carbon from the photosynthesizing plants and return minerals from the decomposing material. They are the mechanism by which dead seasons are converted back into present ones. They are, in a functional sense, the circulatory system of the hollow. The hollow would be a different place — a less rich, less stable, less comprehensive place — without them, but "without them" is not a condition that has obtained here in a very long time, and possibly not ever, because the fungi were here before many of the plants, and in some cases the plants arrived here in partnership with the fungi, the relationship between root and hypha already established before the first frond unfurled.

The fungi live in the hollow the way that blood lives in a body: not as a separate resident, but as a property of the place itself, a fluid function without which the structure is not the structure. If you tried to remove the fungi from the hollow you would have, when you were done, something that looked like the hollow but was not, something from which the capacity for self-repair and self-maintenance had been extracted, a form without a process.

## The Moss Itself, Which Is Also an Animal Kingdom

The moss is not an animal, but the moss is alive in the fullest sense of that word, and living in the moss is not like living in a neutral medium. Living in the moss is living with an active partner.

The moss maintains chemistry. It lowers pH in its immediate environment through cation exchange, trading hydrogen ions for the mineral cations it needs, acidifying the water that moves through it. This selects for certain organisms and against others. The life of the hollow is partly the life that can tolerate, or that has come to require, the particular chemistry the moss makes. The moss does not intend this. It is simply doing what it does to stay alive, and the consequence is an environment, and the environment selects, and the selection produces the fauna, and the fauna produces pressures that feed back into the moss through grazing and disturbance and the chemistry of their excretions and their dead bodies.

The moss is also structure. It is three-dimensional architecture at a scale relevant to organisms of one millimeter and below. It is a forest, for those organisms. Its stems are trees, its dead lower layers are soil, its living upper surface is canopy. Animals that live in the moss live in a world shaped at their scale by the moss's choices about how to grow, where to branch, how to hold water. They are adapted to those choices. They are, in the evolutionary sense, responses to those choices.

The moss is ancient. Its lineage is four hundred and fifty million years old. It has been making moss-choices for four hundred and fifty million years and the organisms in it have been responding to those choices for as long as they have shared habitat, and the result is this: a community so interlocked that the word "community" barely captures it, a community that is more like a single organism in its integration than like a collection of separate individuals who happen to live near each other.

The moss is the oldest resident. Everything else is, in some sense, living in the moss's house, by the moss's rules, under the conditions the moss established. The moss does not know this. But it is true.

## What Lives There, Gathered

What lives in the hollow is, in brief: the very old.

Not old as in ancient specimens, not old as in individual organisms with long lifespans — the beetles live a season, the dragonfly a summer, the earthworms some years, the moss stems a few decades. What is old is the *form* of each life. The lineages represented in this hollow are among the oldest on land. Liverworts, mosses, ferns, horsetails, springtails, earthworms, beetles, flies, mites — these are the original inhabitants of terrestrial ecosystems, the ones who worked out how to live on land when land was new, the ones whose solutions were so effective that they required no fundamental revision over hundreds of millions of years.

The hollow is, in this sense, a museum, but a museum in which all the exhibits are alive and operating at full capacity and have no idea they are exhibits.

What lives there is also: the small.

Small is not a trivial category. Small, at the scale of the hollow, means having access to a world of structure and resource that larger organisms cannot perceive. The beetle lives in a forest of moss stems. The mite lives in a forest of dead fern fronds. The springtail lives in a forest of fungal hyphae. Smallness is not a limitation here; it is an access card. The small organisms of the hollow inhabit a world of enormous complexity and richness that is invisible to anything the size of the dragonfly and above. They are not living in the moss. They are living in the *geography* of the moss, a geography that has mountains and valleys and rivers and dead zones and resource-rich zones, all at a scale that ceases to exist for organisms above a certain size.

What lives there is also: the aggregate.

Nothing in the hollow is, alone, significant. The single springtail is nothing. The single fungal hypha is nothing. The single earthworm is nearly nothing. What is significant is the sum, and the sum is enormous — enormous in biomass, enormous in the throughput of energy and material, enormous in the noise it generates, enormous in the stability it produces. The hollow is what aggregation does when it is given enough time and favorable conditions: it produces something that functions more reliably than any single part, something whose complexity is not a problem to be managed but the source of its resilience, something that has become, through the simple accumulation of interacting individuals over long time, something that could not have been designed.

What lives in the hollow, finally, is: completion.

Not completion in the sense of being finished or done or closed to further development. Completion in the sense of having arrived at a configuration that satisfies all the constraints placed on it, that uses all the available resources, that fills all the available positions, that has worked out a dynamic equilibrium between all its competing and cooperating members, that has taken the full measure of the place and responded to it fully. Every niche is occupied. Every surface is inhabited. Every gradient of resource has organisms positioned along it. Every dead thing is being consumed. Every consumed thing is being incorporated.

The hollow is complete the way a mathematical proof is complete, the way a long piece of music is complete when the last chord resolves. Not closed. Not finished. But arrived. Internally consistent. At rest within itself.

---

There is a moment in the hollow, sometimes, in the late afternoon, when the diffuse light flattens further and the whole place seems to pause, the way a breath pauses at the end of inhalation before the exhalation begins. The sound continues — the sound never stops — but the light is very even, and the moss is extraordinarily green, and the ferns hold their positions at the rim, and you are aware, if you are the kind of person who becomes aware of such things, that you are standing in the middle of something that has been arriving at itself for a very long time, and that has arrived, and that does not particularly need you to witness it, but does not, in this moment, object to being witnessed.

The beetle crosses your hand.

The dragonfly knows where you are.

The earthworm, below you, does not know where you are, but it knows the weight on the moss above it, the pressure that was not there before, the chemical trace of something new, and it adjusts its course slightly, by a few millimeters, by less, by enough.

Everything continues.
