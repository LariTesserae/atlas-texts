## What Lives Here

---

Begin with what does not live here, because the absence shapes everything.

No predator. Or rather: nothing that hunts the way predators hunt, with intention organized around another creature's ending. The system has no need for that. What would be eaten here, and to what purpose? The violence is all structural — is all weight and leverage and the slow politics of light. Whatever hunts here hunts in chemistry, in signal, in the rerouting of water. There is no blood. There is nothing here that has learned to want blood. 

This fact should feel like peace, and it does, until you realize it doesn't — until you realize that the absence of predation just means the violence takes a different form, longer and quieter and more total. Nothing here is spared. Nothing here has a predator because everything here is already inside something larger than a predator, something that is using it, is using its death when it comes, will reclaim every calorie it ever gathered and route those calories somewhere the network has decided they're needed. You are not hunted here. You are simply already accounted for.

---

There are creatures, of course. Creatures in the sense of organisms moving through the world with something approximating choice. But they are — and this is the word that keeps returning — *interstitial*. They live in the pauses.

The beetles that work the dead wood are the most permanent of the animal residents, the ones who have negotiated the closest thing to a lease. They are small, most of them, dark in the way that living in darkness makes things dark — not black, not glossy, but the particular brown-black of bark, of rot, of the underside of the shelf fungi they sometimes share space with. They are engineers of a kind, though they would not use the word. What they do to a dead trunk is what the root network does to the living soil: they open channels. They create structure within structure. A beetle gallery in a rotting log is a decision, an interpretation of the available material, a vote cast in the ongoing argument about how dead wood should be disaggregated and returned.

They are not aware of this. They are aware of the tunnel and the next tunnel and the faint chemistry of the fungal threads and whether the wood is the right moisture. That is all. That is enough. The plateau does not require its workers to know they are working.

There are others in the wood: smaller things, things at the edge of the visible, mites and springtails and organisms that exist essentially as ecological process wearing a body for administrative convenience. They do their work. They are the gut flora of the dead matter. Without them the wood would pile up and the accumulation would choke the whole machine. They are not glamorous and they are not rare and they would not make a field guide's cover page, but they are the most essential thing here, they are the reason the factory runs, they are the workers who have never once considered stopping.

---

Higher up — higher in the sense of closer to the canopy's underbelly, which is as high as most things get here — there are spiders. 

The spiders deserve their own accounting.

They are not large. Large would be wrong here, would be conspicuous, would require prey that doesn't exist in large enough supply. What they are is *present*, distributed through the understory in numbers that, if you could see them all at once, would reframe your understanding of what this place is. You are never more than a few meters from one. Usually less. In the particular geometry of a tangled mid-story branch, there can be three or four different species within arm's reach, each occupying a micro-territory defined by silk angle and anchor point and the specific kind of prey each style of web is optimized for.

What they catch is almost invisible: midges, fungus gnats, small things that move through the moisture-heavy air without knowing there is silk in it. The spiders wait in the way that things wait when waiting is the whole strategy. They are extraordinarily patient, which is to say they have no concept of impatience, which is the only real patience there is. They hold themselves against the silk or against the bark and the temperature drops and they slow down, metabolism finding a lower register, and they wait, and something flies into the web or it doesn't, and time passes in a way that means nothing to them.

Some of them build webs that are rebuilt every night. The silk goes up at dusk, when the small flying things are most active — or rather when the particular small flying things that this particular spider has spent its lineage learning to intercept are most active — and by morning the web is often destroyed, either by the canopy's grinding redistributing the anchor points or by simple wear, and the spider eats the old silk (the protein is recoverable, nothing is wasted here, the factory ethos runs through everything) and rests during the day pressed flat against bark, the exact color of bark, invisible.

There is something about the spiders that is native here in a way other things are not. They have the cold patience of the place. They have accepted the grinding and built around it. They have made themselves part of the structure.

---

Consider the salamanders, which live at the boundary between the living mat and the mineral beneath — that dark zone where the root-mat thins and the limestone begins, that three-meter descent from surface to bedrock that might as well be a different world for all the light that reaches it. They are not blind. They are close to blind. Their eyes work in conditions that most creatures would call absolute dark, which means their eyes work here, in the understory, as very functional instruments — they see the thermal flicker of invertebrates, the slight difference in darkness that means beetle larva, the small motions of the things that work the rot.

They are slow and cold and their cold-bloodedness makes them in some ways more native than anything with a regulated temperature could be. They track the temperature gradient downward on cold nights, following the warmth that the earth still holds from whatever sun reached the surface. They track it back up when the soil surface warms slightly in the afternoon. This is their range: a few vertical feet, traveled at walking pace, twice a day. They eat whatever is small enough and slow enough and present. They have no particular relationship with each other except during the brief breeding season, when something in their chemistry wakes up and they find each other by scent in the dark and do what the species requires and then separate and the aloneness, if it is experienced as anything, is not experienced as loss.

They are very old, as a lineage. Salamanders are always old, their body plan conservative and successful and unchanged across geological time. These ones are old in the additional sense of having been shaped by this specific plateau, this specific chemistry, this particular regime of cold and damp and compressed-matter. They fit here the way something fits after long enough. The fit is not comfort. The fit is having no edges that stick out.

---

Above, in the canopy itself, there are epiphytes in extraordinary variety — mosses and ferns and certain flowering plants that have long since decided the ground is a rumor and built their lives on bark. They are technically plants and they technically live here, and they deserve more than a line, but what is most interesting about them is not what they are but what they create.

The accumulation of an epiphyte community on a single branch is its own substrate, its own small ecosystem, complete with decomposition and nutrient cycling and invertebrate residents. A large enough clump of canopy moss is a world. It has its own internal climate, warmer at the center than the periphery, moister, darker. Things live there that have never touched the ground and will never touch the ground, whose entire world is twenty square centimeters of damp vegetative mass sixty meters in the air. There are mites up there. Tardigrades almost certainly. Nematodes threading through it like the mycorrhizal networks thread through the ground, doing a similar work in a similar language at a scale that is very small and somehow no less vast.

The canopy is not one place. It is many places stacked. It is a tower of worlds that do not know about each other.

---

What about the trees themselves? What about the thing that *is* the place?

They are the most difficult to describe as *living here* because they are not inhabitants of the place — they are the place, they are the factory and the product simultaneously, they are the city and the citizens in one body. Asking what it is like to be a tree here is like asking what it is like to be a city. The question doesn't land properly.

But: they are old. Some of them are four hundred years old and show it in the way that four hundred years shows — the bark so thick and furrowed it looks architectural, the branching so complex it looks deliberate, the trunk bases fused with neighbors or with the elevated root-buttresses that hold them against the substrate's shifting. They bear the marks of every crisis in their growth rings, in their healed wounds, in the places where they grew around a fallen neighbor's impact and incorporated the scar. A four-hundred-year-old tree here is a record of four hundred years of this specific catastrophe, this specific grinding, this specific negotiation. It is a library written in wood.

And they communicate, or something happens between them that deserves a word close to that — the chemical signals through the mycorrhizal network, the root-to-root contact where two trees have grown so long in proximity that their roots have abraded against each other and formed union, sharing transport tissue, becoming in some hydraulic sense one tree with multiple canopies. The oldest trees on the plateau — there are perhaps a dozen, identifiable by their size and by the way younger trees have organized around them, leaning toward them, connecting to them first — are nodes in the network in the way that old neurons are nodes, the ones that everything else routes through. Whether this is wisdom is a question no one can answer. It is at minimum age. It is at minimum the accumulated result of having been here long enough to be part of everything.

They are not peaceful. They are pressing against each other constantly, competing for light in slow motion, winning and losing decades-long struggles for position. Two trees of the same species, the same age, rooted three meters apart four centuries ago, have had a relationship for four centuries — a relationship of competition and contact, of wound and response, of root interaction underground and canopy interaction above. One of them is slightly bigger. One of them will outlast the other. When the smaller one finally loses its root purchase and leans into its neighbors and is held there, dying slowly, the larger one will grow into the light the smaller one releases. It has been waiting. Four hundred years is not a long time to wait if you were never going to move.

---

There is one more category: the things that are here in the way that the cold is here, in the way that the grinding is here — not as individual organisms but as *condition*, as property of the place itself.

The mycorrhizal fungi, which are not the trees and not the soil but the negotiation between them. They cannot be extracted from this place. They exist here and only here, shaped by the specific chemistry of the limestone-rooted canopy trees and the specific damp cold and the specific depth of the root-mat. They are the factory's operating system, the invisible text on which everything else runs. They are older than most of the trees, having colonized the original substrate when the plateau was forming, having been here when the first trees took root in what was then just rock and blown soil and the beginnings of moss. They have been here, in continuous unbroken thread, for longer than anything else alive on the plateau. They predate the forest. They are what the forest grew into.

They are not conscious. They are not anything that could be said to experience this place. But they are the place's memory, in a biological sense — they carry in their chemistry the record of everything that has lived here and died here and been decomposed here. The chemistry of a three-hundred-year-old fungal colony contains the trace of every organism it has processed. It is built from them. In this sense, nothing here truly ends. The trees that fell a century ago are in the network, in the chemistry, in the tissue of the trees that replaced them. The salamanders that died in the root-mat are there. The spiders. The beetles. Everything that was here is here still, rerouted.

The factory does not waste.

---

What lives here, then. What *lives* here.

The quick answer is: very little that has a face. Very little that moves at the pace that makes motion legible. Very little that could be called vibrant or dramatic or easily held in the mind as a discrete creature. The birds are visitors. The larger animals, whatever made the paths, come through and leave.

The real answer is: everything. Everything here is alive and the aliveness is not individual and it is not located. It is the process itself. It is the catastrophe-that-holds, the grinding-that-is-also-stability, the cold that makes the turbulence sustainable. What lives here is a *character* of a place: ancient and cold and grinding and patient in the way that only things without patience can be, and so total in its self-consumption and self-renewal that the line between organism and environment dissolved so long ago that asking where the residents end and the residence begins is like asking where the river ends and the riverbed begins, which is a question the river answers only by flowing, only by continuing, only by being the same river it was and a different river than it was, always, in permanent disequilibrium, for as long as the cold holds.

As long as the cold holds.

It will hold.
