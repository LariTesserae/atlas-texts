# What Lives in the Holding Basin

---

Start with what you almost miss.

The copepods. They are everywhere — a billion of them, maybe ten billion, the number becoming meaningless at Basin-scale — and they are almost nothing individually. A millimeter, some of them two. Transparent, or nearly: you can see the dark comma of the gut, the two egg sacs trailing like earrings when they're gravid, the single median eye that perceives light as gradient rather than image. They jerk through the water in their characteristic way, each motion a tiny muscular contraction followed by a coast, contraction and coast, and this makes them look perpetually startled, perpetually interrupted, as though they mean to go somewhere but keep forgetting. They don't forget. They are not forgetting. The motion is efficient — minimal drag during the coast, rapid repositioning between strokes — and it serves them well in a water column threaded with predators.

They eat. Primarily they eat the phytoplankton that has colonized the lit zones near the sodium arrays — diatoms, mostly, small armored things whose silica shells will eventually settle to the Basin floor and add their mineral patience to the accumulating silt. The copepods filter-feed, or some of them do; others are raptorial, hunting even smaller things, pursuing rotifers and bacteria-aggregates through the amber light. The distinction matters to them. To you, they are all the same invisible busyness, the water's thinking.

They reproduce continuously where conditions allow, and conditions allow a great deal in the Basin's warm upper layer. The females carry egg sacs, release nauplii, and the nauplii — comma-shaped, six-legged, absurdly small — begin the work of becoming copepods. Most of them do not make it. Most of them are eaten by other copepods, by the cyprinids, by the filtering apparatus of larger organisms passing through. This is not tragic in the Basin. This is the Basin's circulatory system. The copepods are the blood.

Without the copepods, nothing larger can be here. Everything rests on them.

---

The cyprinids are young.

Not young as individuals — most of them are probably two or three years old now, which for a small cyprinid is approaching middle age — but young as a community, young in the sense of not yet settled, not yet distributed into the ecological niches that will eventually sort themselves out. They came from the river as larvae, or as eggs that survived the filtration, and they grew up in the Basin's upper column, eating copepods, eating each other's young, finding the currents and the temperature layers, negotiating the amber light and the permanent thrum.

They school. This is the thing you notice first about them: the way the school moves as a single body, turning and banking with a coordination that looks choreographed but is not choreographed — it is emergent, it is each individual fish responding to its nearest neighbors, and the response-cascade travels through the school faster than individual thought. The school has a shape that changes: elongated when traveling, compressed into a sphere when threatened, a living geometry that expresses the fish's collective anxiety about the world.

They are anxious fish, the cyprinids of the Basin. This is not a character flaw. They are small, they are numerous, and almost everything larger than them would eat them given the opportunity. The anxiety is the correct response to their situation. When the outboard motors pass above, the schools compress — not quite panic, but a tightening, an inward pulling of the group's boundaries. When nothing threatening happens, they expand again, distributed back into their feeding configuration. They do this dozens of times a day.

Their mouths are small and turned slightly upward, adapted for surface feeding, for sipping the thin film where food concentrates, but in the Basin they feed mid-column and they have adjusted, or they are adjusting. Four years is not long enough to select for anything, but behavior is more plastic than form, and the Basin's cyprinids have modified their behavior. They spend more time in the mid-column than their riverine relatives do. They take food from different depths. They are, in the Basin's limited evolutionary vocabulary, becoming Basin fish.

They have no significant injuries among them. No missing scales, no healed bite-marks. There are no large predators in the Basin yet — nothing that would harry a school of cyprinids into the kinds of wounds that show in the body. This makes them seem, to the careful observer, slightly soft. A generation of fish raised without teeth above them. It shows in how they move, in the brief delay before the school compresses, in the way they sometimes linger near things they should flee. They are brave by accident, because bravery has cost them nothing yet.

---

The perch are different.

They arrived in the second year — nobody is certain how, possibly on equipment, possibly in water transferred from elsewhere, possibly because the river had a passage into the Basin that the filtration engineers hadn't fully accounted for. They arrived as juveniles and they are now adult, and they are comfortable. You can see it in them. The cyprinids swim with their collective anxiety spread thin, like a membrane, and the perch swim with something else — with presence, maybe, with the calm of an animal that has decided it belongs somewhere and found no evidence to the contrary.

They are striped: dark bands on an olive-green body, the vertical bars that break up the silhouette in water with structure, which is a curious choice of camouflage for a fish in open water with almost no structure to speak of. But the amber light from the sodium arrays comes down in shafts, and in the shafts, the bars work. The perch know the shafts. They position themselves in the amber columns, still, watching.

The watching is the thing about perch. Their eyes are large and positioned to give near-binocular vision forward, and they watch with both eyes at once, which gives them a stillness that looks meditative. They are not meditating. They are triangulating. A perch looking at you is working out angles — the angle to the prey, the distance to the surface, the estimated velocity of whatever it is looking at. It will make a decision and then execute it, briefly and completely, and then it will go still again. The perch are efficient in a way that seems almost mechanical, and this efficiency, in the diffuse amber of the Basin, makes them look ancient.

They eat the cyprinids when they can, and they can, sometimes, though the cyprinids' schooling behavior makes individual capture difficult. Mostly the perch eat isolated fish, outliers, fish that have made the mistake of separating from their school. They also eat invertebrates, copepod aggregations, the larger zooplankton that has had time to establish. They are opportunists with preferences, and their preferences have shaped the middle depths of the Basin's water column over four years in ways that would be legible to a patient observer: slightly less zooplankton in the zones the perch patrol, slightly more cyprinid activity near the surface where the perch come less often, a trace of ecological ordering imposed on the Basin's wilderness by animals that know what they want.

There are not many perch. Maybe a few thousand in the entire volume of the Basin — almost nothing, given the scale. You can go a long time without seeing one. And then you will turn in the water column and find one looking at you from three meters away, already factoring you in, already calm with knowledge you don't have, and you will understand that the Basin is not only the things you can see.

---

Below the thermocline, below the amber light, things are organized differently.

The cold dark is not empty, but it is sparse. Sparse in the specific way of a place where energy is scarce: every organism that lives here has solved the problem of the cold and the dark, and the solutions are expensive, and so there are fewer organisms, and the ones that are here are particular.

There are amphipods. Small crustaceans, laterally compressed, perpetually in motion — they cannot stop moving, or will not, it is difficult to distinguish these things in amphipods — and they range through the dark column eating whatever falls from above. They are pale. Not albino — they are not eyeless cave creatures, not yet, not after four years — but tending toward pallor in a way that suggests the beginning of something. Four years of darkness has not changed their pigmentation, but give it twenty, give it a hundred. The Basin's darkness is very dark and very consistent. What adapts to it will become less visible in it.

There are a few species of chaoborus, the phantom midge larvae, those remarkable animals that are nearly transparent — the only visible features two pairs of darkened air sacs, fore and aft, used for buoyancy — and which migrate vertically every day, rising toward the amber light to feed at dusk, descending into the cold dark at dawn to hide from visual predators who cannot see them in dim light. They are called phantom midges for good reason. Held in a glass of water they become difficult to locate. In the water column, in the dusk register between the lit and unlit zones, they are invisible. Only in the amber light near the arrays are they detectable, and then only as a slight visual disturbance, a refraction, a hint of curved surface where the body catches the light and bends it a little wrong.

The chaoborus do not register you. They are operating on gradients — light gradient, temperature gradient, chemical gradient — and you are a disturbance to the gradient, and they respond to your presence by moving slightly in the opposite direction, or not at all, depending on how far you are from their threshold. They are very old as a lineage, very successful, and very committed to a mode of existence that is nearly incorporeal. They haunt the Basin's midwater with the confidence of animals that have been nearly invisible for millions of years. They are not frightened. They are barely present.

In the cold dark at the Basin's lower reaches, perhaps in the last fifty meters before the floor: nothing yet large enough to see. The detritus falls there — dead copepods, fecal pellets, the drift of organic material from the productive upper layer — and it accumulates and begins to be processed by bacteria, by the early meio-fauna in the silt, and the oxygen here is lower and the temperature is nearly at its minimum and everything happens slowly. The Basin's deep is waiting to be colonized more thoroughly. There is food there. There will be animals that know how to use it. They have not arrived yet, or they have arrived as eggs and are not yet visible. The deep is a future the Basin is holding.

---

On the floor, in the beginning of the mud:

The chironomid larvae, the blood worms, red with hemoglobin that lets them live in the reduced-oxygen environment of the silt-water interface. They are tube-dwellers — each one constructs a small tube of silt particles bound with secretion, and lives in it, extending from the tube to feed, withdrawing when disturbed. The disturbance threshold is low. Anything that passes close enough to generate a pressure wave will send them contracting back into their tubes, and in the soft mud of the Basin floor, you could walk — if you could walk there, if the pressure allowed it — and leave a wake of retreating worms, a visible record of your path in the momentary absence of red things.

There are oligochaete worms, too, the Basin's earthworms, working through the silt in the way that earthworms work through soil: ingesting, extracting, excreting, turning the material over. They are small, pink-white, and they do not stop. They have no reason to stop. They are processing the Basin's bottom in real time, converting the raw sediment into something more biologically active, opening channels for water movement, building the chemical conditions that will support, later, more complex things. They are engineers the same way the concrete-pourers above are engineers, just slower and smaller and without plans.

There is almost certainly bacterial mat in some areas of the floor, particularly in the corners and along the drainage channels where organic material accumulates. The bacterial mat would not be visible to you — you would not be able to see it without instruments — but you would smell it if you could smell in the deep: the faint signature of sulfur, the metabolic byproduct of organisms that process organic material in low-oxygen conditions. The Basin's floor smells of process, of the very early stages of becoming something older.

The worms and the bacteria are the floor's parliament. They will set the terms for everything that comes after.

---

There are things in the Basin that came in accidentally, on equipment, in the water transferred from other sites, on the boots of workers who have also worked elsewhere. These are not dramatic invasives — the Basin is too new and too controlled to have developed a full invasion crisis — but they are there. A species of snail, probably, on the concrete walls near the surface, small and brown, scraping at the biofilm with its radula. A bryozoan colony, translucent and small, on the underside of one of the platform floats, not visible from above. Possibly — this is speculation, but informed speculation — a water mite or two, riding the bodies of other invertebrates, not yet established in numbers large enough to notice.

These accidentals are part of the Basin's character, the way an old house accumulates the traces of everyone who has lived in it. The Basin is accumulating trace presences, organisms that arrived sideways and are trying to find a way to stay. Most of them will not stay. The Basin's ecology is not yet stable enough to absorb new arrivals without consequence, which means new arrivals also suffer the instability. Some will find their footing. The snail may, if the biofilm develops. The bryozoan will, if it survives the winter and the circulation doesn't scrub it from its float.

---

At the surface, in the film itself:

Neuston. The Basin has a thin community of surface-film organisms that has developed in the four years of the Basin's existence and is denser near the sodium arrays, where the light is strongest and the photosynthetic productivity highest. These are mostly microorganisms — bacteria, algae, protozoa — but also, occasionally, the adults of insects that emerged from the water as larvae and returned to oviposit, laying their eggs in clusters on the surface or just below it. The insects come from outside, from the surrounding environment, finding the Basin's surface from the air as insect-water surfaces are found: by the light-polarization signature of open water, which the Basin produces abundantly from its forty-kilometer span.

Midges emerge from the Basin. They have been emerging for two years now, the adult chironomids rising from the bottom, swimming up through three hundred meters of water column, pressing through the surface film, unfolding wings that were always there, waiting in the pupal case. They emerge mostly at night, when the sodium arrays are their brightest relative to the world beyond the Basin. The arrays attract them. They swarm above the arrays in clouds, brief clouds, the adults not eating, just finding each other in the light, mating in the air, and the females returning to the surface to lay. Some of them are eaten by the bats that have discovered the Basin from the surrounding area — big brown bats, mostly, their echolocation perfectly suited to detecting small bodies against open water — and some of them fall back to the surface and are eaten by small fish, and some of them succeed, and the eggs go into the water, and the cycle continues.

The bats come at the edges of the day. You can see them from the catwalks, if you know to look: small shapes moving with the erratic efficiency of echolocation-hunting, banking over the surface, occasionally dipping to take something. They are not Basin creatures — they have roosts in the surrounding structures, in the gaps in the facility's cladding, under the ledges of the office buildings — but they use the Basin in the way that animals use what they can find. The Basin feeds them and they take the Basin's midges into the air.

This is a relationship. The Basin is four years old and it is already having relationships.

---

What lives in the Holding Basin is the Basin itself, still becoming.

You cannot separate the organisms from the water chemistry they have produced and are producing. You cannot separate the water chemistry from the concrete's slow leaching, the pH gradient, the temperature layers. You cannot separate the temperature layers from the sodium arrays and their generators and the workers on the catwalks and the fuel deliveries every Tuesday that keep the generators running. The Basin is not a container that life has colonized. The Basin is a system, and the life in it is part of the system's operation, and the system is four years old and operational in a way that was not designed, that exceeds the engineering.

The perch were not designed. The phantom midge larvae were not designed. The bats circling above the sodium light were not a specification in the Basin documents. They arrived because the Basin had created conditions for arriving, and they stayed because the Basin continued to be those conditions, and they are changing those conditions by being in them, and the conditions are changing what the organisms can be. Four years of this. Fifty to go, if the Basin lasts that long, which it may and may not. In fifty years, the concrete will be fully colonized, the mud will be a meter deep, the fish community will have sorted itself into something stable and complex, and there will be animals in the Basin that are not in any adjacent water body, animals that are the Basin's, that will not survive outside it.

It is working toward that. It is working right now, in the amber light and the hum of generators, in the jerk-and-coast of a billion copepods, in the patient tube-building of worms on the dark floor.

The Basin does not know it is doing this. Nothing in it knows it is doing this. It is happening anyway, as these things do, as they always do: life choosing itself, in the places given to it, with whatever it has been given to work with.
