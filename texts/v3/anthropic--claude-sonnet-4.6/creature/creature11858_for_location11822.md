# What Lives in the Seep Meadow

---

The moss is the primary resident, and it is more than it appears, which is itself a great deal.

Sphagnum, first and most. It is the landlord here, the architect, the original settler whose construction has been ongoing for so long that what looks like surface is in fact mostly history — decades of dead sphagnum beneath the living layer, compressed but not decomposed, held in suspension by the very wetness the sphagnum itself maintains. This is one of the stranger facts about sphagnum in a place like this: it builds its own substrate. It arrives, it dies partially, it remains as a kind of permanent loan to the future moss, which also dies partially and remains. What grows here is the top inch of something much deeper, and the something much deeper is also sphagnum, just older, just stilled. The living part does not think about the dead part. It does not need to. The dead part holds the water and the living part holds the light.

Sphagnum doesn't have roots. This is important. The entire model of connection here is different from what trees or sedges do — sphagnum is a sheet of small, single-mindedly engineered cells, each individual leaf consisting largely of one enormous dead hyaline cell surrounded by narrow living chlorophyll cells, the dead cell hollow and porous, capable of holding many times its own weight in water. The moss is itself a sponge made of cells that are themselves sponges. When you see the color — the red, the gold — you are seeing the pigmentation that accumulates in the hyaline cells under different conditions of light and nutrient and moisture. The deep red of the shaded hollow is a kind of self-defense, a screening of excess light or a response to cold; the pale gold of the exposed surface is something else, stress or maturity or the simple result of having been bleached incrementally by more sun than the shaded parts receive. The colors are a record of local condition. The moss does not experience them as colors.

What sphagnum does, in this place and places like it, is alter its world. It acidifies the water it holds, suppressing the decomposition of whatever falls into it, which is why the peat beneath is still peat and not soil — ordinary decay has been chemically discouraged. The moss maintains its preferred conditions by being its preferred conditions. Its persistence is not passive. It is an ongoing assertion, gentle and non-negotiable, about what this ground will be. Other things live here only to the extent that they can tolerate what sphagnum makes. The moss does not compromise. Over centuries, it simply continues.

---

The other mosses — and there are others, smaller and less dominant, tucked into the spaces between sphagnum mounds and climbing the lower faces of the granite outcrop — live at the mercy of the sphagnum's hospitality. A hypnoid moss, perhaps, something feathery and flat, extending through the more compressed areas where the sphagnum hasn't raised itself into cushions. Mosses without common names, named instead by the slow accumulation of Latin descriptors, names that emphasize their structure: the arrangement of their leaflets, the shape of their capsules, the curvature of their stems when dry. They are named for what they are geometrically, because that is what differentiates them, because what they are geometrically is most of what they are.

These smaller mosses are aware of the light in the sense that they grow toward it, aware of the water in the sense that they extend through it, aware of the rock in the sense that they adhere to it and dissolve it very slowly and incorporate the dissolved minerals and continue. They have no opinions about any of this. They are the activity of their own chemistry, and the chemistry is very old, and the chemistry is doing fine.

---

The sedges are a different kind of life. Not better, not more complex in any way that matters here, but organized around a different principle: the vertical.

Where the mosses are horizontal — a mat, a sheet, a blanket thinking across the ground — the sedges spike upward. Each clump is a tight gathering of stems rising from a root-mass that pushes through the moss and into the perpetually moist soil beneath. The roots are substantial, reaching into ground that the moss doesn't occupy, drawing up minerals dissolved in the seep-water, things the moss cannot access or cannot use. The sedges are not in competition with the sphagnum. They occupy the room that the sphagnum does not fill, which is the air above it, and the soil beneath it, and these are different rooms.

Sedges photosynthesize. This is their primary work, and they are good at it. The narrow blades present surface area to the light while minimizing exposure to the wind that doesn't come much anyway, here in this sheltered depression. The slight glaucousness — the faint blue-grey of the blade's surface — is wax, a thin cuticle sealing the cells against moisture loss on the days when the air is drier than usual. The sedge is planning, in the way that chemistry plans, for conditions not currently present. The wax is a memory of drier days, or a preparation for them; it is impossible to say which, because in plants the distinction does not exist.

A sedge clump, closely examined, is a dense colony of shoots arising from rhizomes that extend horizontally just below the surface, sending up new blades and occasionally putting out new rhizomes in a slow radial expansion. The clump's boundary is where last year's rhizome growth ended. Each clump is roughly the same age as the distance from its center to its edge, but this is not a rule you can apply precisely, only approximately, only as orientation. The clumps here are not old by human standards — a few decades, perhaps — but they are established, meaning the rhizomes have found water and the blades have found light and the balance between growth and cost has been solved, for now, for here.

In late season, the sedge will produce flower heads — not showy, not fragrant, tiny architectures of bract and anther arranged for wind pollination rather than insect attraction, since there are no insects here to attract. The seeds will fall into the moss. Some will germinate. The moss will accommodate this, to a point, and the new sedges will either establish or not, and the spacing will either shift or remain roughly as it is. The sedge doesn't intend the spacing. The spacing is what has survived.

---

At the margins, on the outcrop's lower faces and along the join between rock and moss, there are liverworts — flat, dark green, lying against the stone as if glued there, which is more or less what they are doing. A Pellia, possibly, or something in that family: a thallus without leaves, without stems, just a ribbon of green cells a few cells thick, branching occasionally, absorbing moisture across its entire surface, photosynthesizing, reproducing when conditions allow by releasing spores from small stalked capsules that appear briefly in spring and then collapse.

The liverwort is ancient as a lineage — older than the seed plants, older than the ferns, among the earliest terrestrial plants to leave the water in any form. What lives on this rock has relatives whose ancestors colonized bare stone four hundred million years ago. The liverwort has not significantly changed its strategy since then. The strategy was good. The strategy is: become flat, become wet, become green, persist. It is doing this now. It will do this after everything else here has shifted through the next thousand years of slow succession and climate and the turning of conditions. The liverwort will be among the last things to leave a place and among the first things to return.

---

Where the granite weeps, in the thin film of moving water over stone, the life is invisible.

There are diatoms here, single-celled algae sheathed in silica, each one a glass box of extraordinary regularity. Under magnification they would appear as precise geometric objects — circles, ellipses, pennate forms — etched with radial or bilateral symmetry so exact that they have been used as calibration targets for microscope lenses. In the film of water on the rock, they are not visible as individual things. They are visible as color: a slight brownish-golden cast on the wet stone surface, a biological tint that appears where the light holds, fades where it doesn't. The film shifts when the water shifts. The diatoms go with it or don't, attaching and releasing via mucilaginous stalks, living their lives of photosynthesis and binary fission and occasional sexual recombination in a medium that moves without asking them.

Alongside the diatoms, other algae: green algae in thin colonial films, cyanobacteria whose presence is ancient and fundamental — these are organisms whose ancestors produced the first significant oxygen in Earth's atmosphere, whose basic biochemistry has not changed since before complex life existed. They are on this rock because the rock is wet and lit, and those are their requirements, and they have always met them wherever they occur.

---

And beneath all of this — beneath the moss, beneath the soil, beneath the seep's path through the rock — fungi.

They are not visible. They are not expected. They are the most fundamental residents of this place and they ask nothing of your attention.

The hyphae of fungi thread through the sphagnum peat, through the mineral soil, through the roots of the sedges with whom they may be in quiet partnership, across the rock surfaces in intimate association with the cyanobacteria. A single gram of soil here contains kilometers of fungal filaments, and the peat is deep, and what this means in terms of total hyphal length is a number that stops meaning anything the moment it's computed. The fungi are decomposers, the process by which the dead sphagnum slowly and very partially breaks down, releasing nutrients back into the water that the living sphagnum will eventually take up again. They are also the connective tissue of the soil itself, their hyphae binding mineral particles and organic matter into the structure that gives this ground its particular quality of give, its way of accepting weight before yielding.

Some of these fungi form mycorrhizal relationships with the sedge roots — a mineral-for-sugar exchange that has been ongoing since mycorrhizal relationships first evolved, another ancient arrangement that found its form and kept it. The fungus extends the sedge's reach into soil volumes the root alone cannot access. The sedge feeds the fungus carbon from its photosynthesis. This has been called symbiosis, which is accurate, and has been called mutualism, which is also accurate, and it is neither gift nor deal but something more like recognition: a chemistry finding its complement.

---

There are bacteria everywhere, which is to say there is life everywhere, which is the fundamental truth of wet soil, of wet rock, of wet anything in a lit world. Bacteria in the water film on every moss leaf. Bacteria breaking down the trace organics in the seep-water. Bacteria in the gut of the earth itself, living in the fractures where the seep originates, metabolizing sulfur or iron or hydrogen in the dark, in the cold, in absolute geological patience. These are not exotic organisms. They are the ordinary miracle of the biosphere's base layer, doing what the biosphere's base layer has always done: finding the chemistry available and making a living from it.

They are too small to have presence in the way the moss has presence, but in terms of mass — in terms of the actual weight of life in this place — they likely exceed everything else. The moss is large and the bacteria are small and there are more bacteria than anything else, and this is usually true of the living world: the small things predominate, the things below notice hold the whole system up.

---

What kind of life is this, taken altogether?

Sessile. Patient. Organized around persistence rather than motion. No individual here has locomotion; nothing moves through the meadow to find a resource, because each living thing has become the coincidence of a resource and itself. The sphagnum is where the moisture is. The sedge is where the light and the seep-water meet. The diatom is where the water film crosses the rock. Life, here, is less a set of actors than a set of solved equations, each one working out the same basic problem — energy in, matter cycled, reproduction accomplished — through a slightly different method, in a slightly different part of the small domain available.

This is not incomplete life or diminished life. It is life that has found the question this place asks and answered it correctly, repeatedly, over long time.

What lives here lives in the old way, which is to say: it lives by having become exactly what the place can sustain.
