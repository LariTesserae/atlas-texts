# The Inhabitants

---

They are not old. This is the first surprise.

You expect, arriving from the lower world with its slow erosions, its sagging timbers and worn thresholds, its faces carved by time into something legible — you expect age. You expect the custodians of a changeless place to be themselves changed, to have absorbed the stasis into their bodies the way stone absorbs heat. But they are not old. Or rather: they age, visibly, from birth through the stages the body requires, and then at some point — different for each person, but unmistakable — they stop. Not dramatically. Not overnight. The hair whitens if it will, the hands thicken, the lines around the mouth and eyes settle into their permanent arrangement, and then that is the face. That is the face from here on. The body does not decline from that point. It maintains. The woman with white hair who carries limestone blocks on the eastern terrace carries them with the same ease she carried them at forty. The man who has been old as long as anyone can remember still walks to the river and back every morning, still cuts stone with a steady hand, still sits on the same step in the late afternoon with the same expression of complete, uncomplicated presence.

They are not immortal. People die here — of accidents, of infections that run their courses, of a kind of swift failure that occasionally takes the young for reasons no one can predict or prevent. But the deaths are not attrition. They are not the grinding-down. When someone dies on the plateau they are spoken of as having arrived somewhere, which is interesting, because they were already somewhere — which implies they are now somewhere more, or somewhere further in, and the language accommodates this without strain.

---

The bodies are suited to the place in ways that are probably not coincidence and cannot be entirely explained by selection. They run lean without hunger, their metabolisms calibrated to the cool air and the modest diet of a high limestone plateau. Not thin — the word is *efficient*. Each person carries exactly what they need. The subcutaneous fat distributes itself sensibly, insulating without excess. Hands are broad-palmed and strong-fingered, with short nails worn smooth by stone, and the skin of the hands has developed a texture not quite callus — harder than ordinary skin, but sensitive still, able to feel the grain of limestone through the hardened surface, which is necessary for the work. The feet are similar: wide, stable, the soles dense, adapted to cobbled streets and open rock pavement with equal confidence. No one here walks uncertainly. The body's relationship to stone ground is solved, has been solved for generations, and the solution is legible in the way feet meet the plateau without hesitation.

Their eyes are remarkable. Not in color — the colors are the usual distribution, brown and grey and hazel and occasional pale variations — but in the quality of their attention. The light here, which comes from a high-altitude sun through clean cool air, is a precise and unforgiving light, and the eyes have adapted to live in it: quick to contract, comfortable with glare, able to pick out fine distinctions of texture and shadow at distances where another eye would see only mass. They can read the face of a limestone cliff from fifty meters and tell you where the good splits are, where the fault lines run, which way the block will come free. They can track a bird across the open plateau sky against that deep blue and tell you what it is by the proportion of its wings before it's close enough to name by pattern. And they meet your eyes directly, without the sidelong hesitation of crowded places, because here there is space enough and stillness enough that looking is not aggression. They look at things fully. It is their custom.

---

The children are the most startling thing.

In a changeless place you might expect childhood to be brief, hurried through to arrive at the stable state sooner. The opposite is true. Childhood here is vast. It occupies a span that would be, in the lower world, enough time to have lived a significant life — and it is not extended by coddling or delay but by density. The children learn slowly because there is much to learn and no urgency, and the learning is not separated from the living. A child of twelve has been cutting limestone for four years, badly and then less badly and then with a growing accuracy that surprises no one except herself. A child of eight can navigate to the river and back alone in any weather, can read the sky for what it will do, can distinguish between the call of every bird on the plateau. This knowledge accumulates without being taught as such. It is absorbed the way moisture is absorbed here: intermittently, distributed, patient.

The children play the games. You have been told about the games — the same game, always, the game the grandparents watched from below. From the outside this sounds like impoverishment. From the inside it looks like the opposite. The game has been played long enough that it has become a depth rather than a repetition, the way a song played ten thousand times is not the same song it was the first time but has become a different, richer thing, stratified with all its previous playings. The children playing the game are playing all the games that came before, and they know this, and it makes the game serious in the best sense: worth doing fully because it carries weight. They argue about the fine points of the game with the passion of scholars. Small differences in interpretation are treated as significant. The game has a jurisprudence.

This is their characteristic approach to everything, in miniature: to find in the familiar not a reason for boredom but a reason for precision.

---

They work.

The work is stone — always stone, primarily. The cutting and laying and shaping and fitting of stone. The maintenance of the terrace walls. The repair of the drainage channels. The slow, careful extension of a building where a family has grown, each new course of block indistinguishable from the old when the work is done. But there is also the work of the animals: the tending of goats at the wild end of the gradient, which is less herding than negotiation, an ongoing relationship between the keeper and kept in which the kept are not quite kept and the keeper is more companion than owner. The milking, the shearing of coarse thick fleece which becomes the twisted rope and the rough cloth. The birds that nest in the wall joints are not kept and not managed but they are watched — their habits noticed, their preferences accommodated, the wall joints left unmortared in certain places where they have chosen to build, a small deliberate hospitality.

And the grass-rope work: the braiding of the plateau grasses into rope and basket and the flat woven mats that soften the stone floors. This work is done in the evenings and early mornings, in the low light, by people sitting in doorways or on terrace walls, their hands moving with automatic fluency through patterns they learned young and have not had to think about since. The work is not a duty. The work is the shape that time takes when the hands need something to do while the rest of the person is elsewhere — thinking, listening, watching the bats pour out of the escarpment at dusk.

---

The social structure resists simple description not because it is complicated but because its logic is spatial rather than hierarchical. There is no chief, no elder council, no visible authority and no apparent enforcement. What there is: a deep organization around the terraces. Each terrace is a unit — not of family, not of occupation, but of something that encompasses both without reducing to either. The people of a terrace maintain its walls together. They share the retaining wall on the lower edge the way neighbors share a fence, except the shared thing is massive and the maintenance is constant and the shared labor is understood not as obligation but as participation in a thing that benefits everyone and would be diminished by anyone's withdrawal. This is the governance. Not law. Participation.

Within each terrace the arrangement of families and individuals is various and unforced, people living near people they have chosen to live near, the choosing apparently happening quietly and without drama over the course of a young person's movement through the city during the long years of childhood. By the time someone is grown enough to establish their own household, the place that will be their household is known to them and probably to everyone else. Not assigned. Known. The way a water channel is known — it is simply what happens on this ground.

Disputes exist. They are quiet, the way everything here is quiet, and they are long, in the way that disputes among people who are not going anywhere tend to be long. They are worked out in the open, on the terraces, in the presence of people who have not been asked to adjudicate but who are present anyway, who cannot help but hear, who occasionally contribute a phrase and then fall silent again. The resolution when it comes is not a verdict. It is more like a precipitate — the slow settling of the matter to its natural position after enough agitation. Afterward no one references it. It is done. The wall does not remember it. The cobbles do not remember it. The stone does not hold grievance.

---

The knowledge they carry is the other thing. It is different from the knowledge of other places.

In the lower world knowledge accumulates in objects and texts, in marks made on surfaces by people who know they might not be there to speak it, who fear forgetting and make artifacts against it. Here there is almost none of that. A few marks on stone — not writing, not quite, but something between writing and drawing, symbolic notations whose meaning is clear to those who know them and invisible to those who don't. The marks indicate water. They indicate fault lines in the rock. They indicate where a body is buried, because the plateau buries its dead in the rock, not in soil, the body lowered into a prepared cavity in the limestone and sealed, becoming part of the stone in the only way available to organic material, which is slowly and incompletely.

The knowledge lives in the people themselves. In their hands and their eyes and their bodies' relationships to this specific landscape. It is not, therefore, stored — it is inhabited. The woman who knows where the best limestone for lintels can be found knows it the way she knows how to walk: not as information retrieved but as a capacity exercised. The man who knows the fault patterns of the western escarpment, who can predict where and how the cliff face will calve in the next hard winter, knows this through forty years of looking, through something in him that has aligned itself to the rock's own logic over time.

This is why no one is afraid of forgetting, which is the great fear of places where knowledge is external: written down and therefore potentially lost, burned, translated wrong, misread. Here the knowledge is in the people and the people are in the place and the place does not change and so the knowledge does not erode. It does not need to be preserved because it is not separate from the people who hold it in the way a text is separate from the person who wrote it.

The child who has been cutting stone for four years, badly and then less badly, is not learning a skill. She is becoming someone for whom this specific stone, in this specific light, with these specific tools, yields in predictable and manageable ways. The skill and the person are becoming the same thing. In thirty years she will know the stone the way the stone does not know itself, which is: from outside, with perspective, with comparison, with the particular intelligence of a creature that has time and intention and the ability to be surprised.

---

They are not incurious. This seems important to say.

The assumption is that people who live in a changeless place and do not find it troubling must have made some accommodation with not-knowing, must have suppressed the questions that would otherwise disturb the stasis. The assumption is wrong. They are curious in the way the plateau itself is curious about the sky: steadily, without urgency, committed to looking carefully at what is present rather than restlessly after what is absent. They notice things. A new seam of harder limestone in the eastern face — they notice this and discuss it and go to look at it and touch it and think about what it means for the next generation's quarrying. A bird that hasn't been seen before on the plateau, coming down probably from higher country to the east, resting in a tree cluster near the ridge — it draws a small attentive crowd, people standing at a respectful distance, watching without disturbance, asking each other what they see. The strange can arrive here and be received. It is received with the same full attention they give to everything.

They have philosophy. Not written, not systematized, but continuous — a running conversation carried through the community over centuries about what this place is and what it means to be in it. The central question, as best an outsider can understand it, is something like: *what does it mean to arrive?* They use this word — the word that also describes dying, the word that describes a stone reaching its place in the wall, the word for water finding its channel — for many things. A child learns to cut stone with a fluency that looks natural rather than learned: she has arrived. An old man sits on his step in the afternoon light in an attitude of complete sufficiency: he has arrived. The city in its arrangement on the plateau, the terraces stepped down from the central ridge, the drainage finding its way to the river: it has arrived.

The philosophy is not quietism. It is not the peace of people who have given up. It is something more like the peace of a problem fully solved, a fit so complete that the pieces no longer show their jointure, which is the highest standard of the stonemason's art and which they apply, apparently, to life.

---

In the evening, when the bats have poured out and dispersed and the sky has gone from blue to something more complex — grey and pink and then grey again, the last light leaving slowly the way it always leaves at altitude — the city does something that might be called gathering but is not organized enough to be called that. People move, by some mechanism of habit or attention, toward the highest terrace. Not everyone. Enough. They stand or sit on the broad retaining wall of the uppermost terrace and face north, where the plateau's rim is visible and the lower country spreads below, hazed and dark and far. They do not speak much. They watch the light leave the lower world, which it leaves more quickly down there, the darkness pooling in the valleys first and then rising. Up here on the plateau the last of the day is still usable — the air still faintly lit, the stone still showing its pale color, the grass tussocks still distinct.

What they are doing in this time is not worship, not quite. It is attention. They are paying attention to the day ending in the way they pay attention to everything: fully, without hurry, without the overlay of expectation that would tell them what to feel. They are watching what is there. When it is done they go back to their houses. The stars come out—and the stars here are extraordinary, the altitude and the clean air and the absence of any competing light bringing them in hard and close. Someone, always, stays to watch the stars for a while. Usually an older person. Sometimes a child, sitting alone on the high wall in the dark, small against the enormous sky, looking up with the same full attention they have practiced since they were able to look.

The dove has stopped calling. It will begin again in the morning, the same three notes.

The river murmurs to the west.

The dogs are inside, or nearly inside, pressed against warm doorways.

The city is dark. The city is still. The city has always been like this. The city will always be like this.

The people sleeping in it sleep deeply, without the twitching unease of people who fear tomorrow will be wrong, will be less, will be harder to endure than today. They sleep the sleep of the arrived. Their breath rises and falls with the small sounds of the plateau at night — the occasional wind, the distant call of some hunting bird, the cold murmur of the river. They are so still in sleep, so suited to this place, that the walls around them seem less like shelter and more like continuation: the stone going on, and the people a part of it, warm and brief and here, fully here, as here as anything has ever been.
