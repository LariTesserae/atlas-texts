# What Lives in the Sediment Sea

---

Start with what you expect to find here, and find it: the pressure-tolerant, the slow, the anatomically conservative. The things that evolution sent into the dark millennia ago and then, apparently, left alone. Polychaete worms the color of old candle wax, threadlike, moving through the upper centimeters of mud in a way that is less like locomotion and more like a gradual rearrangement of matter — the worm here, the worm there, the worm having been in both places for so long that the mud between retains the record of passage the way memory retains experience: structurally, invisibly, in the changed arrangement of what remains. They process the sediment. This is their vocation and their body plan and their complete relationship with time. They eat what has settled. They move in the direction of what has settled. When they die they settle. The cycle is closed in a way that feels less like ecology than like grammar — a sentence that is also its own subject.

Foraminifera in the surface millimeters of the sediment, still living, extending pseudopods into the particle-thick water in search of bacteria, in search of organic films on mineral grains, in search of the distributed nourishment that is the only kind of nourishment the deep floor offers. Their shells — which they are building even now, even here, even in the dark — are geometrically precise in the way of things that are built by chemistry rather than choice. Spiraled. Chambered. Each new chamber added in the same radial relationship to the last, the same proportional size, the same wall thickness. The foram does not decide these things. The foram *is* these things, the way a crystal is its lattice. When the foram dies its shell will join the sediment and eventually, under pressure, become rock — become the specific rock that this place will one day be, if the tectonic logic holds long enough, if subduction does not swallow it. The foram is building the future of this place one chamber at a time, without knowing that the place has a future or that it is here.

Nematodes between the sediment grains. They are everywhere in the deep seafloor, more everywhere than almost anything else that has a nervous system. They are very small. They move between the particles in the interstitial water, the water that exists in the spaces between grains, the water that is the real substrate of benthic life — not the sediment, not the water column, but the thin film between, the everywhere and nowhere of the pore space. The nematodes are in that space. They have always been in that space. They will be in that space for as long as there is a deep seafloor, which is to say: indefinitely, by any measure of time that applies to a person. They are among the most successful animals that have ever lived, if success is measured in abundance and duration and phylogenetic conservatism rather than in complexity or legibility. They are not legible. They are not complex. They are *successful* in the deep way that things which have found their niche and stayed in it for five hundred million years are successful — not through triumph but through fit.

---

But.

The expected is not the whole of it. The expected is the floor, and the floor, as has been established, is not quite a floor.

---

Near the seeps, something different has organized itself around the chemical gradient the way life elsewhere organizes around light. The bacterial mats are the primary producers, yes, but they are not the beginning and end of what the gradient supports. Above and through and into the mats there are organisms that do not occur in the textbooks children read, that do not appear in the popular imagination of the ocean, that exist in the margin between what life was expected to do and what life actually did when left alone with a methane seep and geological time.

There are snails. Small, white-shelled, moving slowly over the bacterial mat in a grazing pattern that mirrors, at this depth and in this dark, the pattern of snails on a sunlit rock at low tide — the same broad arc, the same radular scraping, the same patient coverage of available surface. They are not the same snails. They are entirely different snails, built for this pressure, built for this dark, built for a diet of sulfur-oxidizing bacteria rather than algae. But the pattern is the same pattern. Evolution, working from different starting materials in different conditions, arrived at the same solution: a shell, a muscular foot, a radula, a grazing arc. The convergence is either reassuring or troubling depending on how you think about the scope of the possible. The snail does not know it is a convergence. The snail knows, in the way snails know things, that the mat is here and the mat can be eaten and the eating is the correct response to the mat being here.

Amphipods in the water just above the sediment surface, moving with the quick twitchy energy that is characteristic of amphipods everywhere, at every depth — they are among the animals that do not seem to have adjusted their temperament to the deep, that maintain at four degrees and six hundred atmospheres the same nervous busyness they have at sea level and twenty degrees. They scavenge. This is their gift to the deep-sea economy: the ability to detect organic material at great distances in water and arrive at it quickly and consume it comprehensively before the slow animals can get there. They are first responders. They are the short-term memory of the deep floor — what arrives, they process; what they process is no longer there. They keep no record of what they consumed. The record, if it exists, is in the sediment below, in the chemical trace of the consumption event, in the slightly different isotope ratio in the thin layer that was deposited when the amphipods were feeding. Future geologists, drilling cores, will not know to read this as a feast. They will read it as a change in bottom-water chemistry. This is the same thing.

---

The tube worms — the ones mentioned already, the ones thirty meters apart, the pale red plumes — deserve more attention now.

They are not alone in the way a single animal in a landscape is alone. They are alone in a different way: each one is an ecosystem. Packed into the body cavity of each tube worm, in the tissues surrounding the trophosome, there are bacteria — sulfur-oxidizing chemoautotrophs — in such density that by mass, by metabolism, by any thermodynamic measure that matters, the bacteria are the organism and the worm is the infrastructure. The worm has reduced its gut to vestigial status. It has no mouth in functional use. It has given up eating in the conventional sense in favor of being a delivery system — it extends its plume of gill filaments into the sulfide-oxygen gradient, extracts both, delivers both to the bacteria packed into its tissues, and the bacteria do the chemistry and the worm lives on the products of that chemistry. 

This is not symbiosis in the sentimental sense. This is a merger. The worm has incorporated another organism so completely that the boundary between them is no longer a boundary but a zone of negotiation, and the negotiation has been stable for millions of years, and the stability is what the partnership looks like from the outside. What the partnership looks like from the inside is not something that either participant is in a position to report.

Each tube worm lives for potentially two hundred years. Perhaps more. The mechanism of aging in the deep, under these conditions, in the absence of the oxidative stressors that animal life encounters at the surface, is not fully understood, and the result is that the tube worm may be among the longest-lived animals on Earth — not because it was designed for longevity but because the conditions here, which seem hostile by surface measures, happen to be very easy on the biochemical machinery of aging. Time is different here. The worm is different in time. The two hundred years the worm lives in is not the same two hundred years that a parrot lives in, or a bristlecone pine. It is two hundred years of the deep floor — which is to say, two hundred years of almost no change in conditions, two hundred years of the same temperature, the same pressure, the same chemical gradient, the same dark. The worm does not experience novelty. The worm has no mechanism for experiencing novelty, no cognitive structure that would register it as meaningful if novelty occurred. The worm is continuous with its environment in a way that animals with nervous complexity cannot be, because complexity requires a self that stands in relation to the world, and the tube worm has rationalized the self down to something much closer to a process than a thing.

---

In the sediment itself — not at the surface, not in the bacterial mats, but down in the anoxic layers below, in the grey-black mud where sulfate has been consumed and methane is the dominant carbon compound and the chemistry has crossed into a regime that surface life never encounters — in there, deeper than any of the animals, deeper than the worms and snails and nematodes, there is something else.

The word *life* applies. The word *organism* applies, technically, in the sense that these are bounded entities with membranes and metabolism and heritable genetic material. The word *alive* is harder. What is alive, really, in a cell that divides once every thousand years? That runs its metabolism at a rate so slow that the instruments designed to detect metabolism almost miss it — that show up as a faint signal at the edge of measurement, a slight departure from chemical equilibrium that could be life and could be something else and is, after exhaustive analysis, determined to be life?

These are the deep biosphere organisms. The cells that live in the pore water of sediment that has been on the seafloor for millions of years, that has been compressed and transformed and partially lithified, that by every reasonable reckoning should be as lifeless as any rock. They are not lifeless. They are metabolizing. They are, if the rate is extrapolated, replicating — infrequently, at intervals that make no sense against any familiar scale of time, but replicating. They have been in this sediment for longer than our species has existed. They were here, some of them, before the sediment layer that contains them was deposited at the surface — they came down with the marine snow, already ancient, and continued living as the sediment compacted around them, as the overburden increased, as the temperature and pressure shifted, as the chemistry changed from oxic to anoxic to methanogenic.

They have adapted to each transition, or they were already adapted, or — and this is the troubling possibility — adaptation is the wrong framework entirely for something that changes so slowly that the boundary between genetic change and physical chemistry becomes unclear. They may be doing something that is not quite evolution because it is not quite reproduction, not quite survival because survival implies a threat and there is no threat they have ever encountered that was faster than they could respond to over geological time.

What are they, these cells in the ancient sediment?

They are the patience made biological. They are what happens when the logic of life is applied to timescales that life was not designed for and has made work anyway. They are the record and the recorder both — having been here long enough that they are part of what this place is chemically, part of what this place means geologically, part of the reason the methane cycles the way it cycles and the sulfate depletes the way it depletes and the seafloor has the character it has.

They are, in a real sense, what the seafloor thinks — if the seafloor thinks — which is to say, they are the distributed, low-rate, chemically-mediated processing that gives the sediment its character, that makes it something different from mere geology, that constitutes, in the only sense available here, a form of inner life for this place.

---

Occasionally — rarely, at intervals that do not correspond to any season because there are no seasons here, corresponding instead to events in the water column far above, events that have taken weeks or months to become relevant to the floor — occasionally something falls.

A whale. A large fish. Something that was living in the world of light and current and temperature variation and predation and reproduction and all the processes that make surface life what it is, and that has died, and that has sunk, and that has arrived here.

The floor responds.

Not quickly, not by any surface measure. But the response is comprehensive and it is in some sense the clearest demonstration of what this community is, of who lives here and how. The chemical signal of decomposing tissue moves through the water — moves slowly, diffuses into the particle-thick column — and the amphipods find it first, and then the hagfish, if hagfish are near (they may not be; the floor is vast and hagfish are not evenly distributed across the vast), and then the sleeper sharks if sleeper sharks are near, and the community of scavengers assembles around the carcass in a succession that has the logic and timing of an ecological process that has been refined over millions of whale-falls, each one a localized island of productivity that exists for years, sometimes decades, before the last of the organic material is processed and the seep community, if hydrogen sulfide is produced by the final phase of decomposition, makes its own temporary claim.

The whale-fall community is not the permanent community of this place. It is a visitation. It is what happens when the surface reaches the floor, when the economies of two worlds briefly intersect and the floor, for a time, becomes legible in a way it usually is not — becomes clearly alive, clearly responsive, clearly in negotiation with what has arrived.

And then the carcass is gone, and the community disperses, and the mud returns to its condition, and the only evidence is a slightly different chemical profile in the sediment below the site, a slightly different layer, one that future instruments might read as an anomaly and that will eventually be read as nothing at all, as the sediment above and below it compresses and the boundary blurs and the record is absorbed back into the record.

---

What lives here.

Everything listed above, and more that is unlisted — undescribed because undiscovered, because the floor is vast in a way that makes most of its area unknown, because the instruments have visited a small fraction of what exists and found things that were unexpected and then moved on, and the unexpected things remained in the dark and the sediment, continuing their business, uncatalogued.

What lives here is life in its most reduced form and its most patient form and its most ancient form — life that has stripped away what it does not need and found that what remains is enough, that chemistry and time and the gradient between chemical states is sufficient to support existence, that existence in this form is not diminishment but a different kind of fullness, not less than life at the surface but differently organized, differently paced, differently in relation to time.

What lives here lives at the rate the floor suggests. It has learned — which is to say, it has been selected into — a relationship with time that treats geological events as weather. The things that live here have been here long enough that their presence is part of the character of the place, that you cannot fully describe the sediment sea without describing them, that they and the sediment are not occupant and place but something closer to partners in a long negotiation that neither party would recognize as a negotiation because neither party has a framework for recognition.

They are not waiting for anything.

They are not surviving.

They are not heroically adapted to extreme conditions, because the conditions are not extreme to them — the conditions are simply the conditions, the only conditions they have ever known or that their lineages have known, the conditions that they have made their specific and adequate home for longer than most of what we call natural history has been happening.

They are here.

They have always been here.

They are the place's knowledge of itself — its only knowledge, its complete knowledge, the chemical and biological record of everything that has happened in this water and on this mud and in these sediment layers, maintained not as memory but as ongoing process, not as archive but as continuous present tense.

The sediment sea does not know it is the bottom of something.

But what lives in it knows — in the only way anything knows anything here, which is through chemistry and gradient and the slow structural record of having been — that this is where it is. That this is what it is. That the pressure and the dark and the patience are not circumstances to be endured but the very substance of existence as it has been worked out here, in the cold and the ancient and the thoroughly occupied dark.
