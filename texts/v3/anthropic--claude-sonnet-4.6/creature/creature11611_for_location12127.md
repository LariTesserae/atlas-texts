# What Lives on the Canopy Plateau

---

Begin with what is not one thing.

The first difficulty in describing what lives here is that the word *lives* wants to refer to individuals, and the plateau resists individuals the way it resists topography — by wearing the concept smooth, by accumulating so many of them for so long that the category dissolves into something more fundamental. What lives here is not a collection of organisms. It is a condition. It is the state of being alive, expressed at scale, the way a river is not a collection of water molecules but the behavior that water molecules produce when there are enough of them moving together in one direction for long enough.

With that said: here is what lives here.

---

**The frogs live here.**

This is the most important fact about the plateau's fauna, the fact from which the others radiate. The frogs are not a species but a radiation — perhaps forty species on this plateau alone, possibly sixty, possibly more, the count depending on whether you are counting by morphology or by call or by genetics, and these three methods disagree in ways that suggest the frogs themselves have not entirely resolved the question. They have been on this plateau, or on its predecessors, for long enough that isolation has done its work: species found nowhere else occupy single bromeliad fields, exist in populations of a few thousand individuals spread across a few square kilometers, live and die without any knowledge that there is a world below them.

The smallest are thumbnail-sized and translucent, the color of strong tea held up to light, their internal organs visible as dark suggestions through the skin. They live in bromeliads. A single frog, a single bromeliad, an entire life. They are born in the water trapped in the rosette, develop there, mature there, call from the rim of the cup at dusk in voices that are not quite high enough to be audible to most human ears but which are the dominant sound-structure of the frog chorus at a frequency most people feel in their sinuses rather than hear with their ears. They eat whatever falls into the water or onto the rim: insects, other tadpoles, each other occasionally, dead organic matter when necessary. They are utterly adapted to a life that takes place within a radius of perhaps thirty centimeters, and they have been adapted to this life for a very long time, and they are, in their stillness and completeness, among the most undisturbed creatures on the plateau.

The largest frogs sit on open canopy surfaces and are the size of a human fist, their skin rough and dark and mottled in ways that make them almost indistinguishable from the bromeliad-mat they sit on — except that the mat does not breathe, and if you watch for long enough the pulse becomes visible in the throat, and the eye is too bright, too wet, too present to belong to anything dead. These frogs eat whatever they can fit in their mouths, which at their size includes small birds, include smaller frogs, includes the large moths that rest nearby and that are themselves the size of hands. They sit with immense patience. They are ambush predators whose only movement is the strike, and the strike is very fast, and then they are still again. You could walk past one a dozen times before recognizing that the particular knot of texture near that bromeliad base has been in the same position for six hours and that there is a slight movement, a regular movement, in the region of its jaw.

Between smallest and largest: every variation. Frogs the color of poison warnings — electric blue, acid yellow, red-orange the shade of emergency — which are indeed poisonous, which advertise their poisonousness in the most legible way possible, which move slowly through the bromeliad fields in daylight because they have nothing to fear from anything that has learned what their colors mean. Frogs the color of nothing, the color of background, pressed flat against stems in the moments before a bird's shadow crosses them, gone vertical, gone invisible, resumed after the danger passes. Frogs with adhesive toe pads who live in the vertical world of stem and leaf and hanging root, who navigate three dimensions easily where most life here navigates two. Frogs who call together in synchronized choruses that have the emergent complexity of music — not because any individual frog intends music but because forty thousand individual frogs each responding to the calls around them produces, at the scale of the plateau, a standing wave of sound with its own rhythm, its own structure, its own repetition.

The frogs have filled every available role on the plateau that a small, cold-blooded, moisture-dependent animal could fill. They have done this slowly. They are still doing it. Some of the species on this plateau are so recently diverged from a common ancestor that the divergence is still in progress, still incomplete, the two populations still occasionally interbreeding at their contact zones, still not fully resolved into separateness. On this plateau you can watch, if you know where to look and what you're looking at, speciation as a present-tense act rather than a historical fact.

---

**The bromeliads live here**, and they are not decoration and they are not passive. They are the engineers.

Each bromeliad on the canopy surface — and there are perhaps two thousand bromeliads per acre across the ten square kilometers of the plateau, which makes the total a number large enough to be meaningless — is doing several things simultaneously. It is catching water. It is catching organic debris: dead insects, leaf fragments, bird droppings, the shed skins of arthropods, the bodies of things that died in the water it holds. It is digesting these things through specialized cells on the inner surface of its rosette, supplementing what it takes from the substrate because the substrate, the canopy mat, has been occupied for so long that its nutrients have already been spoken for. It is providing habitat — the water, the shelter of its overlapping leaves, the protected interior — to dozens of species per individual plant, and the ecological work happening in those interiors would, if you could observe it at the scale of a human habitat, have the complexity of a city's food supply chain.

But the bromeliads also do something more structural. They are the interface between the aerial environment and the mat. Their roots penetrate the canopy surface and trade carbon for minerals with the fungal networks threading through the accumulated organic matter below. Their dead matter, when they die, adds to the mat. Their living mass is the largest component of what makes the canopy surface walkable, compressible, alive. Remove the bromeliads and the plateau becomes a different place; the water vanishes, the frogs vanish, the insects that depend on frog-inhabited water vanish, and the birds that eat those insects and those frogs vanish, and you are left with open canopy at altitude, which is a much simpler and much less extraordinary place.

The bromeliads are the reason. They have been the reason for a long time.

They flower once and die, the way their kind does, sending up a bright spike visible from a distance — red or orange or purple, depending on species, each color tuned to the visual system of a particular pollinator — and in the weeks of their flowering the bromeliad in question becomes the center of tremendous activity, hummingbirds, insects, other things, all drawn in, all leaving with pollen, all carrying the bromeliad's genetic information to other dying bromeliads across the mat. The dying and the flowering happen simultaneously. This is efficient. This is ancient. The seeds are wind-dispersed, light, equipped with hooks and hairs that catch on surfaces, and many of them fall immediately into the mat and fail. Some of them land on a piece of exposed bark or a section of particularly dry moss and hold, and swell with the morning condensation, and send down the first root, and begin the decades of accumulation that will eventually produce a new rosette and a new pool and a new world.

---

**The insects live here**, and the insects cannot be discussed as a group because the group is a fiction imposed on perhaps fifteen thousand species by the inadequacy of the category. There are beetles here whose elytra are the color of hammered copper, who feed exclusively on a single genus of bromeliad and have done so long enough that their mouthparts have changed to fit. There are ants — several species of ant — who have colonized the canopy mat and who run trails between their nest sites in the hollows of old branch-junctions and their foraging grounds, which is everywhere, which is the whole surface, which the ants patrol with the thoroughness of something that believes it owns the place, which in a functional sense it does. The ants are not the largest animals here. They are among the most powerful, in terms of their effect on every other species' behavior, on where things go and when, on what is available and what is defended.

There are moths of extraordinary size resting through the day with wings that have developed the art of resembling dead leaves to a pitch of precision that seems unnecessary, that seems excessive — every vein of the wing mimicking a leaf vein, a particular dark spot mimicking insect damage, the edge of the wing even showing a slight irregularity that mimics the irregular edge of a leaf partially eaten by something the moth has never been and does not need to convincingly reproduce in order to survive the daytime of this specific plateau. But evolution does not stop at sufficient. It continues past sufficient as long as anything continues to select for it, and here the selection pressure has been constant for a very long time.

There are flies. There are wasps. There are midges so small they appear as clouds rather than individuals, hovering over the bromeliad pools in the morning, drawn by something in the water, doing something in the water. There are beetles that eat the midges. There are spiders that eat the beetles. There are parasitic wasps that lay eggs in the spider egg sacs, and the eggs hatch inside the silk and eat the spider's eggs, and the spider does not know, and builds another egg sac, and the same wasp, or its daughter, finds it.

This is the fundamental texture of the plateau's insect life: complete chains of dependency, running in all directions, supporting each other and undermining each other simultaneously, in a system so intricately self-referential that no single element is truly essential and the whole is therefore extremely robust and has survived every extinction and perturbation thrown at it for longer than the relevant records exist.

---

**The birds live here**, though *live* requires qualification. Some of them live here permanently — plateau birds, found nowhere else, shaped by the same isolation that has shaped the frogs. Small, insectivorous, dull-colored mostly, though *dull* is a failure of attention: the dullness resolves, at close range, into intricacy — a complex pattern of brown and buff and dark, a subtle iridescence at the throat that only shows at certain angles, markings that serve a purpose you would need to know more about the bird's visual system to understand. These residents know the plateau the way anything knows a place it has never left: they know its daily rhythms and its microclimates and which bromeliads have the most productive pools and where the large moths rest and where the ants are most aggressive and which parts of the mat are most likely to collapse unexpectedly into the gap beneath.

But there are also the passage migrants, birds moving through, stopping on the plateau because it is there, because it offers something, because it is a landmark or a fuel stop in a journey whose endpoints are elsewhere. These birds have the quality of visitors who do not quite understand the place they are visiting — they are slightly hesitant, slightly wrong in their behavior, taking a moment too long to decide something, reacting to the resident birds' alarm calls with a beat of delay that marks them as outside the system. They stay for hours or days and then they leave. They carry pollen from plateau bromeliads into the lower world. They carry parasites that the plateau has never encountered. They bring, occasionally, seeds of things from lower elevations that will fail in the mat and add their failure to the slow nutrient base of the soil.

The large birds are few. There is something raptor-like, seen at extreme altitude — above even the plateau, riding thermals that the plateau's sunwarmed surface generates through the afternoon — that circles without intention, or with intentions that are not visible from below. Its shadow crosses the plateau every hour or so. The frogs go still. The small birds vanish into the bromeliads. The shadow passes. The frogs resume.

---

**There are spiders**, and the spiders are everywhere in the way that only spiders are everywhere: not flocking, not schooling, not coordinated, but distributed across every available surface in a density that makes itself known only when you move through a field of bromeliad stems and find that every third space between stems is already occupied by a web, and the webs are not visible until you have walked through them, and the spider at the center of the web you have just destroyed is sitting in the ruins with a patience that suggests this has happened before and will be repaired.

Some of the spiders are large. Large enough that their presence registers as animal presence rather than invertebrate presence — you notice them the way you notice a mouse or a lizard, with a small adjustment of your understanding of your surroundings. They sit at the centers of orb webs stretched between bromeliads, bodies marked in patterns that are either warning coloration or camouflage depending on the angle, the light, the viewer's sophistication. At dusk the large spiders become active, and their activity is the activity of ambush: they become very still at the center of their rebuilt webs and wait for the moths that are also becoming active, and the moths have good eyes and can see the webbing in most light conditions, but dusk is not most light conditions, and sometimes the moth hits the web and the spider moves.

The small spiders are so numerous as to be atmospheric. They are in the moss. They are in the bromeliads, hunting in the frog pools, small enough to walk on the water surface. They are under the lichens. They are in the air, occasionally, riding silk threads on the midday updrafts — a behavior called ballooning that carries them across the plateau and occasionally off its edge, into the lower world, where they will live or die depending on whether their particular adaptations transfer to a completely different environment, which they usually do not, which is fine, because there are always more.

---

**There is the thing that moved in the canopy.**

This requires its own section because it is the largest animal on the plateau and the least visible, because it occupies the deep interior of the canopy in a way that nothing else does, because the evidence for it is almost entirely indirect and the direct evidence — the brief sighting, the smell, the branch depressed under significant weight — is so quickly re-absorbed by the plateau's continuous activity that it becomes uncertain within minutes of occurrence.

It is large. It is a mammal, from the smell. It is something that eats the upper canopy — there are signs of this, branches stripped, bromeliad rosettes eaten, the characteristic destruction of a large herbivore moving through dense growth, though the eating is not desperate or wasteful, is instead measured, selective, the eating of something that knows what it wants. It moves slowly when it moves at all, which may be why it is so rarely seen despite being so large: it matches the pace of the plateau, which has no urgency in it, which is doing everything at the pace of growth rather than the pace of hunger.

It is likely a relict species. Something that exists here and perhaps in one or two other high isolated places, cut off by the lowering of the forest around the plateau over geological time, shaped by that isolation into something that no longer fits cleanly into any lower-world category. It is probably not dangerous. Nothing on this plateau is primarily dangerous to humans, not because the plateau is gentle but because humans are too large and too strange an input to register in the system's behavior — the plateau has no idea what to do with a human and so does nothing, which is experienced as safety but is more precisely experienced as irrelevance.

The large animal in the canopy has not been formally described. There are rumors of it in the oral tradition of the people who used to come here and no longer do, and the word for it, imperfectly transmitted and imperfectly understood, refers to something that lives at the top of the world and comes down only when the world calls it, which is not very often.

It is there now, somewhere below the surface you are standing on, moving among branches that have not been in direct sunlight in forty years. It is eating something. It is breathing the cold dark air of the under-canopy. It has a heartbeat. 

You cannot hear it over the frogs.

---

**The fungi live here**, and they are the secret infrastructure.

Fungi are not plants, which means that the assertion that vegetation has won completely is not quite accurate, because the fungi have won something else — they have won the inside of everything. They thread through the accumulated mat, through the roots of every bromeliad and moss and fern, through the bark of every branch in the buried canopy, through the soil forming slowly at the very bottom of it all, and they do what fungi do, which is to make connectivity. The phosphorus that a bromeliad needs is forty meters away in the decomposing heartwood of a tree that died before anything currently alive on the surface was born; the fungal network finds it, trades carbon from the bromeliad's photosynthesis for it, runs the transaction invisibly and without apparent effort, and the bromeliad grows. The ant colony that stripped a section of canopy surface to make room for its fungal garden — ants here grow fungi too, in small chambers under the mat — has altered the local chemistry in ways the fungal network around it has incorporated and redistributed.

The fungi are what makes the mat possible. They are the reason that accumulated dead matter does not simply sit and compact and kill everything above it. They break it down selectively, extracting what is useful and converting the rest to the chemical language that roots read. Without fungi the plateau would be a place where things accumulated and died on their own accumulation, choking the system with its own history. With fungi the history is processed and returned to the present tense.

They are invisible. They are ubiquitous. They have been here, in some form, longer than anything else that is alive on this plateau, and they will be here, in some form, after the last bromeliad dies and the last frog goes silent.

---

**The old things live here**, which is a category and not a taxon.

There are lichens on the exposed stone at the plateau's rim that are, by the best available estimates, somewhere between three and eight thousand years old. They grow at a rate measured in millimeters per century. They are the color of the stone they grow on — iron-gray, iron-dark, textured like the stone but differently textured, differently reflective in the right light, betraying themselves only when you crouch and look carefully and see that the surface of the stone has a surface of its own, a living surface, one that has been building itself since before the people whose charcoal lies in the mat were born. The lichen does not know it is three thousand years old. It knows, in whatever sense a lichen knows anything, that there is light here, and moisture, and mineral, and that these are sufficient.

There are trees below, whose roots penetrate the mat from below, whose trunks are somewhere in the permanent dark of the under-canopy, that are not as old as the oldest lichens but are old in a way that most living things are not. Their rings — if you could read their rings, and you cannot without killing them, and even then the rings of such trees are inconsistent, compressed by slow growth into something difficult to read — suggest they were established before the plateau's current human absence, before the period of whatever presence there was, before even the earlier period of presence before that. They are the oldest animals on the plateau except that they are not animals. They are the oldest living things except for the lichens. They hold the structure up. They have always held the structure up.

What the old things have in common is not age exactly but durability — a quality of having been selected so thoroughly for this specific place that they are no longer, in any practical sense, vulnerable to it. They have absorbed the plateau's threats so completely that the threats are no longer threats but only conditions, the way an old person has absorbed so many disappointments that disappointment is no longer something that happens to them but something that is simply part of what happens.

---

What lives on the plateau lives, above all, in relationship with what else lives on the plateau, and this relationship is so old and so complete that the individual organisms are like words in a sentence whose meaning no single word contains. The bromeliad is a word. The frog is a word. The fungus is a word. The large shadowed herbivore moving below is a word. The sentence they form is not comprehensible in any language available to the people who have been here, which may be why the people who were here built small things and left slowly and came back rarely and eventually stopped coming. Not because the plateau refused them. Because the sentence was already complete.

You can stand on the mat, in the morning, in the fog, with the frogs loud around you and the smell of something enormous passing through the growth below and the slight give of the living surface underfoot, and you can feel — not know, feel — that what lives here is not any of the things you could name.

What lives here is *this*.

All of it. At once. For a very long time.
