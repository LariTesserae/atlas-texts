## The Ones Who Live in the Hanging Gardens of the Foundry Terraces

---

Begin with the workers, because they are the most obvious, and the most easily mistaken for ordinary.

They wear clothes that were once a color and are now something else — the color of long use, which is not quite brown and not quite gray but occupies the territory between them. Work trousers with knees that have been patched and re-patched, the patches not always matching, a record in fabric of the times the wearer knelt on iron grating, on brick, on the particular ridged pattern of the catwalk floors. Their boots are heavy and old and extremely well-cared-for in the specific way of people who know that boot failure at elevation is not an abstract concern. The uppers have been treated with something dark and waxy, applied so many times the original leather color is a matter of archaeology. The soles are thick. The laces, when they break, are replaced with whatever is available — wire, in a pinch; cord salvaged from packaging.

Their hands are what you notice first, or should notice first. The hands of people who work with hot metal and wet plant matter and old machinery, which means hands that are simultaneously calloused and cut and stained with a darkness that has gotten under the skin and is simply part of the skin now. They have a particular grip — not crushing, not aggressive, but a grip that closes with complete confidence, that has never entertained uncertainty about its own ability to hold. They reach for things without looking, their hands knowing the radius of their environment in the way that hands know things the mind has long since stopped consciously tracking.

Their faces are weathered by the specific weather of the terraces: high-altitude sun on south-facing skin, the moisture that condenses and sits, the dry interior air of the engine rooms alternating with the wet warmth of the outer terraces. Some of them are deeply tanned in an uneven way, the backs of their necks darker than their faces, their forearms darker than their wrists. Some are simply weathered without much tan, wind-written rather than sun-written, the lines of squinting against bright high-altitude light written permanently around their eyes.

They are not young, most of them. That is, the ones you see going about the ordinary business of the place are mostly middle-aged and some are old, and they move through the terraces with a patience that is close to the patience of the place itself — not slow, not diminished, but unhurried in a way that suggests they have taken the measure of everything that could be hurried and found it not worth it. They pause. They look. They make small adjustments with the care of people who know that small adjustments accumulate into either function or failure, that the difference between the gauge needle holding and the gauge needle falling is a series of small adjustments made correctly and on time.

But sometimes you see the young ones. They move differently — faster, slightly less certain in their certainty, still learning which sounds are background and which sounds require attention. A young worker on the terraces will look up when the slow mechanical hammer sounds its blow; an older one won't, has already classified that sound as part of the baseline and processes it below the level of conscious attention. The young ones look at the views more. They are not yet done being astonished by the elevation. This is not a failing. The older workers were, once, young workers who looked at the view, and something in their bearing suggests they remember this, and consider it correctly.

---

There are also the gardeners, though they would not use that word for themselves and might be confused if you did.

They are the workers who have drifted, over years or decades, toward the plant-side of the terraces' life. Their tools include the expected wrenches and gauges but also, hung alongside them, worn-handled pruning implements and vessels for carrying water, and their knowledge includes the growth rates of the woody vines, the bloom times of the improbable yellow flowers above the boiler vents, the places where the condensation pools are reliable enough to support something that needs more moisture than the ambient air provides. They know which ferns are doing well and which are struggling, and they manage the struggling ones with attention, not rescue — they have a realist's relationship to the plant life, understanding that something doing poorly in one location might be moved to another, or might not be moved and might fail, and that the failure is also part of how the gardens function, because failure makes space and space is colonized by something else that finds it suits.

These people carry, in their pockets, small things: seeds sometimes, wrapped in cloth or paper, sourced from the plants already growing here and pressed into cracks where the conditions seem right. They do not plant gardens exactly. They make suggestions to the environment and see which suggestions the environment accepts. Their craft is a kind of patient conversation, conducted over years, with a counterparty that does not use language.

They have favorite places. You notice this by watching them — there is a beam junction on the third terrace from the top where the ferns have grown to exceptional size, the fronds nearly a meter, and one of the gardeners will sometimes pause there for longer than the work strictly requires, touching nothing, simply being in proximity to something she is proud of without quite saying so.

---

There is a type of person this place makes, or selects for. It may be that the terraces attract a particular temperament, or it may be that the terraces create it in the people who stay, or — most likely — both of these are true and reinforcing.

The type: someone who is comfortable with scale but not overwhelmed by it. The terraces are enormous — the machinery vast, the elevation vertiginous, the accumulated time of the place a kind of scale as well, temporal rather than spatial. People who are not comfortable with scale leave, or never come, or arrive and find that they are in the wrong place, feel it in their bodies as a low persistent anxiety and make their way downward. The ones who stay are not necessarily larger personalities than those who leave — they are not grander or more heroic. They are people in whom the encounter with the enormous produces something like settling rather than something like panic. As if they have been waiting their whole lives to be somewhere that justifies the amount of space they internally contain, and they have found it, and can now exhale.

They are also, and this may be related: people who are comfortable with partial understanding. The terraces are not a place where any single person comprehends the whole. The machinery has its experts; the plants have theirs; the structural systems, the water management, the electrical systems — each domain of knowledge is held by someone, but no one holds all of them, and the place as a whole is understood collectively in a way it is not understood individually. The people who live and work here have accepted this. They know what they know and they know they don't know the rest, and they have made their peace with both of these facts and carry them lightly.

---

Now consider the children.

There are children up here, because there are families, because there are living quarters built into the interior spaces where the temperature is most stable, where the machinery's warmth is most reliable. The quarters are not large but they are arranged with the ingenuity of people who have thought hard about small spaces, every surface doing more than one thing, everything stored or folded or stacked. The ceilings in the living quarters are low, and the children navigate them without ducking, not yet tall enough to require it, and this gives them a fluency in these interior spaces that the adults have acquired through habit but the children simply have.

The children play in the terraces. They know every shortcut, every gap in the structure that opens onto an unexpected view, every pipe that can be climbed if you're small enough and brave enough. They know where the lizards sun themselves before the lizards are fully visible, because they've been watching them do it long enough to know the time of day and the direction of the sun and the specific flat surface each lizard prefers. They speak to the birds without embarrassment, in the conversational way of children who haven't been told yet that birds don't understand, and the birds sometimes answer in ways that reinforce this belief, or at least don't strongly disconfirm it.

A child of the foundry terraces has a particular education, not entirely in books but in space and matter and consequence. They know that height is real before they know what the word *vertiginous* means. They know that machinery requires attention before they can articulate why. They have touched old iron in every temperature and weather condition the terraces produce and know in their hands what the metal feels like when it is sound and what it feels like when it has begun to go. They have watched adults stand at a problem for a long time before touching it, and they have absorbed this as the correct protocol: look first, look long, then act.

They are sometimes frightened by the height. This is healthy. They manage the fear with a matter-of-factness that children develop when they live with something daily — the fear does not go away entirely, but it becomes a familiar fear, a fear they know the shape and limits of, a fear that tells them useful things and then steps aside.

---

There is a small library in the interior, three connected rooms, its shelves bolted to the walls against the vibration of the machinery. The library contains what you would expect — technical manuals, engineering references, catalogues of parts, records of maintenance going back longer than anyone currently living has been here — but also what you might not expect: novels, and poetry, and field guides to birds and plants that were not written with the terraces in mind but have been annotated over decades by successive readers who added their own observations in margins, correcting the field guides for the specific conditions up here, noting what applies and what doesn't and what is entirely absent from the text because the text's authors couldn't have imagined this place.

The librarian is old. She has been the librarian for longer than any living resident has been alive, which may be impossible, mathematically, given typical lifespans, but seems to be true, and no one examines it too closely. She knows where everything is. She has opinions about the novels that she shares when asked and does not share when not asked. She reads constantly, always in the middle of three or four things, each bookmarked with a different strip of material — a piece of copper wire, a leaf pressed dry, a torn corner of paper with writing on it that may be a note to herself or may be something she found this way and left. She is not warm, exactly, but she is interested, which is a form of respect that her patrons have come to prefer to warmth.

The library is where the children go when the weather turns. They sit in the corners with field guides and technical manuals and the novels, reading in whatever order their attention takes them, and the librarian watches them with the satisfaction of someone who is growing something, and who knows it.

---

There are also the *watchers*.

Every community this old has them — the people who have arrived, for one reason or another, at a relationship with the place that is primarily observational. They are not idle; they contribute, they work, they know things that are necessary. But somewhere in their history up here, they shifted from being people inside the operations of the terraces to people who also watch the operations, who have achieved sufficient distance that they can see the shape of things as well as the detail of things.

An older man who sits on the highest accessible terrace in the late afternoon and watches the light change on the clouds below. He has been doing this for decades. He has seen things that don't happen often — certain weather formations, certain effects of the sun at particular angles — and he has seen them enough times to know they will happen again and to know approximately when, which is a form of knowledge that is almost no one else's business and so he keeps it mostly to himself. But occasionally he will say to someone, tomorrow, watch the clouds, around midday, and tomorrow at midday the clouds do something worth watching, and the person he warned is grateful but slightly uneasy, because this is the kind of knowing that shouldn't quite be possible.

A woman who walks the entire terrace system every day, all levels, the same route, at the same pace. She has worn a slightly different quality of surface into the floors along her path, not visible so much as feelable underfoot, a subtle difference in the friction. She notices things on her walk: the new growth of the vines, the health of the ferns, a gauge that is reading slightly high, a bolt that has backed out a quarter turn. She does not necessarily fix all of these things. She notes them. She knows who needs to know about each one and she tells them, briefly, and she continues. Her walk takes two hours. She has made it ten thousand times or more. She is not bored by it. She is never bored by it.

---

And the ones who come and go.

Supply chains reach the terraces, because the terraces require material that cannot be grown here, parts that cannot be made here, expertise that the resident community does not contain. So there are visitors — traders, specialists, technicians with particular knowledge, travelers for whom the terraces are a destination or a waypoint. They arrive by whatever routes lead here, and they are always briefly undone by the elevation, and they are briefly undone by the density and vitality of the plant life, and they are briefly undone by the scale of the working machinery, and then they do their business and descend.

These visitors leave traces. Not always physical ones — sometimes a piece of knowledge, a technique someone learned from a visiting specialist and incorporated into practice, now indistinguishable from local knowledge. A word or phrase that arrived with a trader and found it suited the place and stayed when the trader left. An idea. A question that no one had thought to ask in quite that way before, and which opened something.

The residents watch the visitors with a slight calibration in their attention — not unfriendly, but assessing, noting the moment when the visitor adjusts to the place, or fails to adjust. Most adjust. The terraces are strange but they are not hostile, and most people, given a little time, find that they can inhabit this strangeness, that it offers them something they didn't know was available, that the warm high air and the layered density of the living and working systems does something specific to their nervous system, releasing something tightly held, opening something that had been closed.

Some visitors don't want to leave. The residents know this too, can see it in the behavior — the way someone's pace slows, the way they begin to learn names for things, the way they drift to the high terraces in the late afternoon and stand looking at the clouds below with an expression that is recognizable to anyone who has lived here long enough. When this happens, the residents don't interfere. The place makes its own case. Either the visitor comes back, eventually, and stays, or they don't, but the encounter with the terraces has happened, and something has been left behind in them like a mark, a particular quality of knowing that high-altitude warmth is possible, that the made and the growing can become indistinguishable, that plants have no business being as lush as they are this far from the ground and yet.

---

What are they like, finally, the ones who live here? What is the quality they share?

It is this: they have stopped distinguishing between the made and the grown. They live in a place where the distinction has been blurring for so long that it no longer organizes their perception. The iron is alive in the sense that it has been colonized and is being colonized and will continue to be colonized. The plants are structural in the sense that they have become load-bearing, emotionally if not literally, part of what holds the place together in the imagination of the people who depend on it. The machinery breathes. The rust is patient. The ferns have engineering in them.

The people who live here have absorbed this. They don't think about it. They simply perceive the world as a place where the categories are more porous than other people believe, where the wall between the built and the living is a suggestion rather than a fact, where something can be industrial and lush and ancient and functional and made and wild all at once without any of these terms canceling the others out.

They are, as a result, very hard to surprise. Not because they are jaded but because their threshold for the impossible is calibrated against a daily life that would seem impossible from the outside, and against this calibration, most things that might alarm someone else simply register as: interesting. Another thing the world is doing. Another thing worth watching.

They watch well. They have had practice.
