# What Lives Here

---

Begin with what moves.

Not what you see — there is no seeing here, not in the operative sense — but what moves. The displacement of fern fronds. The slow, deliberate weight pressed into moss and received and held, released. Whatever lives on this ledge has been shaped by the same forces that shaped the ledge itself: the long erosion, the coming-to-terms, the dignity of settled forms. It does not hurry. Hurry would be a kind of violence against the timescale on which everything here conducts itself, and whatever lives here has forgotten hurry the way the rock has forgotten its sharp edges.

The large ones came first, or were always here — it becomes difficult to distinguish, at sufficient age, between arriving and belonging.

---

## The Heavy-Furred

They are the size of large bears but they are not bears, have shed whatever genus once claimed them the way the not-quite-trees have shed taxonomy, moving through time long enough that the original categories have become irrelevant. Their fur is oil-dark and heavy, each guard hair hollow, trapping a thin column of warm air close to the skin the way the moss traps a thin column of warm air in its own dense interior — convergent solutions to the same problem, the fur and the moss, arrived at by different routes. The fur sheds water the way the waxy leaves shed water: by design, by the long pressure of the condensation that drifts up from below every night and deposits itself on every available surface without asking.

Their eyes are large, as eyes become when light is unreliable — irises expanded to the edge of the iris, pupils that in full darkness cover most of what an eye is. This has made them look, to the rare observer, perpetually astonished, perpetually receiving. They are not astonished. They see in the near-dark with a quality of attention that is not quite sight, more like reading, more like the way an expert hand reads a text in the dark by feel — not seeing the words but knowing them, knowing the weight and texture of them, building the page from pressure and inference. They navigate the ridge by a map they have made of smell and memory and the low pulse from below, which they feel through the pads of their feet and have been feeling so long they no longer distinguish it from the feeling of the stone itself.

They are warm. This is the central fact of them. They generate a warmth detectable from a meter away, a metabolic extravagance that in any other environment would be wasteful, but here is the foundational technology of their existence. Where they sleep — and they do sleep, deeply, in the angles of sheltered rock — the moss around them grows slightly taller, slightly greener, for seasons afterward, fed by the warmth and the exhalation and the slow biological wealth of a large animal at rest. Their sleeping places are the most lush places on the ridge. They do not know this. It is simply a relationship the place has with them, a long conversation conducted without language.

They are solitary in the way that vast slow things are solitary — not lonely, not territorial, simply each one sufficient unto itself, distributed across the regional scale of the ridge, rarely overlapping, communicating in a medium that is not sound. Scent, deposited with care, carries information across days. A reading of who was here, when, in what condition, heading what direction. They read this with their broad noses pressed close to rock and moss, the nostrils opening wide, a look of complete concentration coming over the face — the perpetually astonished eyes going still, the whole animal's attention folding inward toward the act of knowing.

They do not fear the machine below. They have grown up over that pulse. It is part of what the ground is. A few of them are old enough to have grandmothers who were old enough to have grandmothers who were born on this ridge, and at no point in that lineage did the pulse not exist. It is as much a feature of the world as gravity, as the cold, as the fog that rises and gives itself to the plants. If anything, the sections of ridge where the pulse is strongest and warmest are preferred sleeping areas, the moss deepest there, the warmth welcome. They have no explanation for this warmth. They require none.

## The Moss-Interior

Smaller, much smaller, and older in their way — older because things that live in moss-time have been living in moss-time since before the large animals took their current form.

These are the lives that happen at the millimeter scale, in the green-dark interior of cushion moss, where the temperature is two degrees warmer than outside, where the moisture is total, where the world is a forest of upright stems and the spaces between them are vast, navigable, mapped in ways a moss-interior dweller maps things.

The flattest ones are flattest because the moss interior selects for flat. Over generations so many that the counting is impossible, whatever roundness they once had was pressed out of them, pressed into the service of moving through tight spaces, of fitting into the particular architecture of packed moss stems. They are pale, many of them — the bioluminescent green of the moss surface gives way to something darker and moister in the interior, and eyes here function differently, serve different purposes, or have been repurposed entirely for sensing moisture gradients and chemical traces and the faint warmth of other bodies nearby.

The millipede-that-is-not feeds on the moss itself, on the bacteria and fungi that live in the moss, on the things that feed on those. It moves in the wave that is hypnotic if you could see it, which you cannot, and this wave has been moving through moss interiors at this altitude for so long that the moss has accommodated it, has arranged some of its architecture around the passage of these particular bodies, grows slightly differently near the passages they prefer. A relationship so long it has become structural. The millipede-that-is-not does not know it is being accommodated. The moss does not know it is accommodating. It is simply what both of them are.

There are also the pale soft things that are not insects but fill the insect-niche, that move through the moss grazing and are grazed upon, that overwinter as eggs in the deepest warmest sections near the pulse-heat vents and emerge in what serves for spring here, which is less a season than a shift in fog frequency, a slight change in the quality of cold. Their whole lives conduct in the green interior. The ridge, to them, is incomprehensibly large — a continent, an infinity of navigable space, the extent of the world.

## The Winged-Things

There is a question about whether birds is the right word. They have wings. They have hollow bones, or something serving that function, lightness being non-negotiable at this altitude where the air provides less purchase for every gram of weight. They land on the branches of the not-quite-trees with the softest mechanical sound, which is the sound of claws finding purchase on bark that has grown slowly enough to be very dense, very sure of itself.

What they look like: dark, presumably, to match everything here. Small heads, large eyes — the altitude's solution, again, to the problem of navigating on almost nothing. They do not call. At this altitude, in this near-windlessness, sound would carry strangely and far, and there is no advantage to announcing oneself across distances in a place where the encounters one wants are already known, already mapped, already held in a navigational memory sophisticated enough to have made advertising redundant.

They communicate in touch, is the working theory — and here working means held for as long as such things are held, which is to say as long as the relationship between observer and observed continues, which in this case is indefinite, non-verifiable, and possibly beside the point. When they are in the not-quite-trees together — and sometimes they are, two or three of them, in the dense branches — they shift and settle against each other, and the settling is not accidental. Feathers against feathers, weight against weight, warmth shared at the scale of small bodies in cold air. The not-quite-tree does not mind this. The not-quite-tree has been hosting them long enough to have grown certain branches at certain angles, slight modifications in the architecture of its growth, the tree-equivalent of a chair positioned by long habit in the same place every morning.

They eat, presumably — something, somewhere on the ridge. There are the small moss-interior lives, accessible from above to a beak. There are other invertebrates, living in the lichen, in the rock crevices, in the drip-paths of condensation. The ridge supports this, somewhere in the accounting of its ecology, though the accounting is not legible to any outside observer and was not designed to be.

During the pulse — during those periods when the machine below runs at some slightly higher rate, slightly more warmth emanating through the stone — the winged-things spend more time in the not-quite-trees directly above the warmest sections. They have noticed the warmth without having a concept for its source. It is simply: here, the tree is warmer. Here, the branches are more comfortable. Here, the night is slightly less of what the night usually is.

## The Watcher

This one is harder to describe, and the difficulty is not incidental.

On the ledge, sometimes, something sits. Not the heavy-furred ones, though they have sat there too. Something of roughly similar mass but different posture — more vertical, a thing that has organized itself around a different relationship to gravity, that holds its weight differently, that faces outward over the dark below the ridge with a quality of attention that is not the heavy-furred animal's inward concentration over scent, not the winged-thing's lateral watchfulness. This is a forward attention. This is the attention of something that has been watching the same dark for long enough to know it well, to have developed opinions about it, to notice changes in it that would require centuries of baseline comparison to even register as changes.

It does not move when the fog comes. It lets the fog deposit on it the way the moss does, the way the ferns do, the way the waxy-leaved plants do — accepting moisture as a fact of existence, making use of it without drama. It breathes and its breath joins the fog that already exists and cannot be distinguished from the fog.

What is it watching? The darkness below, where the warmer world exists in its irrelevance. The fog forming in the thermal boundary zone where the cold ridge-air meets the slightly warmer air rising from below. The slow process of the dark, which has its own dynamics, its own events at scales it is difficult to specify. Perhaps it is not watching anything in particular. Perhaps it has been here long enough that watching and being have become the same activity, that the direction of its attention is simply the direction it faces, and it faces the dark because the dark is what there is.

It knows the pulse better than anything else on the ridge. It has known the pulse longest. This is either because it is the oldest resident of the ledge — older than the specific ferns, older than the particular heavy-furred lineage currently moving through the regional territory, old in a way that implies it was here when whatever built the machine was here, that it watched whatever was built be built and then watched the builders leave and then watched the vegetation slowly cover the evidence — or it is because its nervous system has some particular sensitivity to the low frequency, feels it in a way the others do not quite, has made a relationship with it over a long time that is more intimate than the others' simple acceptance.

It does not answer questions. It is not withholding. The category of question has simply not been relevant here for long enough that it has gone the way of hurry, the way of sharp edges, the way of argument with geological forces. What it knows it holds the way the moss holds moisture: completely, without drama, distributed throughout its whole structure, available to whatever accesses it in the appropriate way, which is slow, patient, and probably not you.

When the large heavy-furred ones pass nearby on their careful routes, they detour slightly. Not in fear. In something more like acknowledgment. The way you step around a stone that is very old and has come to terms with itself. The stone does not require it. The acknowledgment is the thing doing the acknowledging.

## The Machine Itself

Because here is the thing, the thing that must eventually be said:

Whatever runs below is not separate from the ecology in the way that buried things are usually separate from what grows above them. It is not a ruin that the nature has reclaimed, not an abandoned installation that the moss is slowly digesting. It has been here too long for that framing. The distinction between what the machine does and what the ridge does has blurred, at sufficient depth in time, into something that requires a different vocabulary.

The warmth it generates is a resource the ecology depends on. The pulse it maintains is a constant against which the animals calibrate, the way animals calibrate against magnetic north, against gravity, against the reliable facts of their world. The ridge has grown up around it the way a tree grows up around whatever is in the ground near its roots — not incorporating it exactly, not ignoring it, but finding a relationship that is neither and making that relationship structural.

This suggests that whatever built it understood something about this ridge — its altitude, its fog dynamics, its particular thermal properties — and built for those conditions, or built in those conditions, or built because of them. It suggests a presence here before the current ecology, which is a difficult thing to think about, because the current ecology is very old.

Or it suggests that the machine built the ecology. That the warmth and the pulse came first, and the moss found the warmth, and the ferns found the moss, and the animals found the ferns, and what looks like a place that simply happened to have technology buried in it is actually a place that was made — was seeded, was started — by the technology, and what lives here is living in a garden planted by something that no longer maintains the garden directly but left the gardening to the garden itself.

This is the version that accounts for everything. The lushness at impossible altitude. The particular distribution of vegetation over heat sources. The watcher on the ledge, who may have been here since the beginning of this sequence, who may have been the bridge between whatever built and whatever grows, the continuous presence that neither built nor grew but watched both and remains.

The cold settles into you like attention.

You breathe.

The moss receives it.

The watcher does not turn.

The dark continues.

The machine below does not stop.

Nothing here stops. This is the final thing to understand. The lives here — the heavy-furred navigators, the moss-interior multitudes, the winged-things in their accommodating trees, the watcher with its long patience — none of them have any relationship to stopping. They have organized themselves around continuation. They are the continuation. They will be continuing after the last human word for continuation has been forgotten, in the dark, at altitude, in the fog, over the pulse, under the sky that goes somewhere, on the settled stone of the ridge that has finished arguing with the forces that shaped it and arrived, finally, at dignity.

They live here.

That is what they do.
