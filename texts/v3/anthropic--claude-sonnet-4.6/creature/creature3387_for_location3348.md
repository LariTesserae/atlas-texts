# What Lives in the Throat of the New World

---

Begin with what is most honest: there is almost no life here. This is not tragedy. It is the condition. Life has not been excluded; life has not yet arrived in the sense of *claimed*, *settled*, *committed*. What lives here lives in the mode of *trying*, which is not the same as living but is not nothing.

---

**The Moss**

Start with the bravest things. The pale yellow-green threads on the undersides of ledges — begin there.

They are the first inhabitants, and they know it, if knowing is a word that applies to organisms that have not yet decided to be organisms in the full sense. Technically they are several species, or will be when there are enough of them to differentiate properly. Right now they are simply *moss-impulse*, moss-as-intention, the idea of ground cover before the ground has agreed to be covered. They live in the only available shelter: the angle between the overhang and the wall, that few inches of still air, that interruption of chaos. They have found the one quiet seam in a roaring world and they are filling it with themselves, very slowly, one cell's width at a time.

They do not photosynthesize heroically. They do not thrive. They *persist*, which is a different verb, which requires a different kind of stubbornness. The light they receive is second-hand, filtered through water, arriving at the wrong angle, too diffuse for any reasonable organism's purposes. They have adjusted their purposes. They have made reasonableness irrelevant.

Their color is important. It is not the green of abundance. It is the yellow-green of an organism that has recently survived something, the slightly jaundiced shade of recovery and adjustment, of being alive in a place that did not expect you and has not made room. They are the color of trying. They look it.

If you pressed your fingertip to one — carefully, having gone to some effort to shelter it from the wind first — you would feel almost nothing. A slight resistance. A dampness that comes from the organism itself, not from the surrounding spray, moisture it has gathered and held and refused to give back. They are very good at keeping what little they have. In a place of constant excess water, the moss has learned to *hold* water — not because it lacks access, but because the water here is violent, is lateral, is not the still vertical gift of ordinary rain. The moss has developed a relationship with water on its own terms. It accepts what it can use and nothing more.

They are testing coverage. A centimeter here. Three centimeters there. Retreat in one spot where the wind got through last week, advance in another where a piece of rock shifted and created a new angle of shelter. The moss is mapping the place's interior logic, finding the nodes of survivability, conducting a survey no one will ever read. The map is being written in living tissue on the underside of stone. If the place lasts — and it will last — future moss will inherit this map without knowing it, will find themselves already oriented, already on a path that was decided before they arrived.

This is the quiet, crucial thing the moss does here: it is not only living. It is *preparing the place for more living*. The threads hold moisture against the stone. The moisture softens the stone fractionally. The softened stone crumbles a little when it dries, and the crumbles accumulate in the small sheltered crevices, and this accumulation — this dust — is the first soil. The moss does not know it is making soil. But it is making soil. This is what the first inhabitants always do: they remake the conditions of inhabitation without being asked, without being aware, simply by being themselves fully in a place. They spend themselves into the rock and the rock becomes, very slowly, something that can hold the next thing.

---

**The Bird**

Twice at the rim. Gone. Let's stay with what twice at the rim means.

The bird — and it is a bird, the wings are certain, the rest is inference — is not resident here. Not yet. It is *considering*. This is one of the things birds do at altitude that is poorly understood by those who study birds closer to the ground: they conduct assessments. They use thermals and updrafts not only for travel but for hovering in place above a site, for looking down at it, for taking the full measure of the acoustics, the movement, the food potential, the geometry of escape if necessary. The bird above the bowl is doing this. It is using the chaotic updraft from the churning air below as a platform from which to think.

What it is thinking — what it is evaluating — is roughly this: *Is this ready?*

The answer is not yet, and the bird knows it, and this is why it does not land. The bowl cannot support it. There is nothing to eat, nowhere to shelter effectively, no ledge wide enough and sheltered enough and stable enough for nesting, no insects for which the bird would have the right beak or claws, no established current of life into which it could insert itself as predator or scavenger or participant. The bird is ahead of the place's development. It has arrived at the concert hall before the musicians, before the audience, before the seats are arranged. It can hear that music is coming. It is not music yet.

But it comes twice. This is significant. The second visit is not an accident. The bird has thought about the bowl between its first assessment and its second, has held the information — the size, the sound, the turbulence, the pale stone, the beginning-moss — and has returned to update the record. It is keeping a file. In whatever way a bird keeps information, stores it in the body as altered readiness, as modified reflex, as a slightly different orientation to this corner of its territory — it is noting this place. Marking it as *coming*.

The bird is gray. This matters. Not the gray of age or sickness, but the efficient gray of a creature that lives where the sky is often this color, that has optimized itself to move through gray air without being a shape in it. It is compact. Every feature held close to the body, nothing trailing, nothing ornate. Feathers that shed water by design, individually and collectively, the whole plumage a single integrated system for moving through the kind of air that exists at this altitude, wet and full of itself and indifferent to the difficulty it presents. The beak is not delicate. Neither is the bird.

It may be the kind of bird that, in several years — when the moss has made more soil, when the soil has made room for the first true plants, when the first plants have made room for the first insects, when the insects have made the bowl worth visiting in the way that birds require places to be worth visiting — will be the first bird to land here. To fold its wings and stand on the pale stone and look around with the particular alert stillness of a bird that has arrived somewhere and is deciding whether to stay.

If that happens, it will not remember having come twice before to look down. The memory will have dissolved into instinct, into what the body knows without the mind's involvement. It will simply find, when it lands, that this place feels *right*. That it has a relationship with this bowl that seems prior to this moment. That it is, in some sense it cannot articulate, home.

**Whatever Made the Artifacts**

This is the most complex inhabitant, the one hardest to describe, because it is not here now. What remains of it is here: the stack of stones, the cord, the cup-wells. The inhabitant itself has gone.

This is a specific way of living in a place: *in having been there*. The presence is in the traces, in the decision-residue, in the pattern of attention that shaped the artifacts and that the artifacts now carry forward. To have ground seven cup-wells into a ledge with a stone tool — patiently, concentrically, the tool moving in careful circles until the bowl-shape was established, then continued until it was right, then continued further until it was patient, until it was the kind of shape that said *I have time for this* — is to have spent significant self here. The bowl-maker is still here in the cups. The cups are portraits of whoever held the grinding stone.

The grinding was not desperate. The concentric marks are even, unhurried. This is someone who could hear themselves think up here, or who had stopped needing to. The roar was background for them, the lateral rain was simply weather, the chaotic wind was the local form of wind and no more alarming than any other. They had been here long enough to have normalized it, or they were the kind of person who does not require normalization because they carry their own calm as portable infrastructure.

The stack of seven stones is different in character from the cups. The cups are *made*, are permanent (in the relative sense), are a commitment to the place's future usefulness. The stack is more tentative. It is the kind of thing you make when you are thinking, when your hands need to be doing something, when you are working something out and the working-out takes physical form. Seven is not a random number but it is not a precisely meaningful number either. It is the number the person arrived at. The stack when finished told them something, or they ran out of appropriate stones, or the question resolved itself by the time they placed the seventh and they could stop. It is a question in the way that a question is not always phrased as a question — a question you make with your hands while your mind moves.

The cord is the most practical of the three. The knot is functional, the hand-made fiber suggests someone who makes their own equipment, who twists their own cordage, who is self-sufficient in the specific way of someone who spends time in places where equipment cannot be resupplied. It anchors something not visible, or measures something not obvious, or was left in mid-task — the person went somewhere and planned to return and may not have returned yet, or the return is not today. The cord is patient. It is doing what cord does: it holds its position and waits for the thing it was tied to do.

This is someone who knows the place well enough to have projects here. The cups are not a first visit's work. You do not grind seven cup-wells on a first visit; you discover the ledge on a first visit, note its flatness, its shelter from the worst of the roaring water, its slight south-facing inclination that will hold the day's collected light a few degrees warmer than the rest of the bowl. You return. You bring the grinding stone. You work.

They are probably not a scientist, though they observe scientifically. They are probably not a pilgrim, though they treat the place with something that resembles reverence — not awe-reverence, not shrine-reverence, but the steady attentiveness that is the practical form of reverence, the kind that shows up in patient work rather than held breath. They are someone for whom a place this young and this loud and this unfinished is simply *where they go*, because the place gives them something they need: the experience of being inside something before it is finished, of watching the decisions get made, of attending the formation.

What they are made of — specifically, in the way that people are made of their histories and their choices — is harder to say. But you can infer something from the cup-wells: they can hold stillness inside chaos. The cups are the only still things in the bowl. The person who could make such cups must have access to that quality in themselves, must be able to bring a stillness to a task that is so contained, so confident in its own parameters, that the surrounding roar cannot get inside it. They do not require the world to be quiet before they work. They bring their own quiet.

They will be back. The cord means they will be back. The stone stack being still recognizably a stack — not yet knocked over by the wind's working, not yet tumbled by the constant vibration of water in the rock — means not too long from now. A day, maybe. A few days. They will return and the cord will have held and they will check whatever the cord was connected to or measuring, and they will look at the stack of seven stones and either add an eighth or leave it as it is, and the cup-wells will be full of the perfectly still water that the chaos fills them with, and they will kneel beside the wells and do what they come to do with the water.

Whether they note the moss is hard to say. But something in the way they make their tools — from fibers twisted by hand, from stone worked concentrically — suggests they notice what is small and patient. They would notice the moss. They would not disturb it. They would look at it, the yellow-green threads attempting coverage beneath the ledge, and they would recognize the work for what it is.

**Everything That Is About to Live Here**

This is the largest inhabitant of the place, and the most ghostly: the presence of what has not arrived.

Every crack where the moss is not is a niche for something. Every pool that forms and un-forms in the bowl's floor is a habitat in waiting — give it stability, give it a few months of accumulation at the edge, and something will move in, some small thing that lives where two environments blur together, the semi-aquatic, the edge-adapted, the opportunist. The cup-wells especially. A cup-well full of still water at altitude, in a stone bowl, fed by mist — this is an extremely specific habitat. Something knows about this habitat. Something has evolved for it or will adapt to it. The cup-wells are address waiting for resident.

The insects are not here yet: too cold, too wet, too new. But *too new* is temporary. The stone is committing to its shape in real time. The moss is making the first soil. Soil will allow the first rooted plants, which will provide the first complex structure, the first varied shelter, the first food web's beginning. The food web's beginning will make the bird want to land. When the bird lands, it will bring — in its feathers, in its digestive tract, in the seeds that pass through it and deposit themselves with nutritional amendment — the next installment of life. This is how it always works. Each life makes the next life possible. Each presence expands the available niches. The bowl is not empty; the bowl is in the exponential part of the curve's beginning, the part before the graph turns visible, the part that looks like nothing from outside but is in fact everything.

What lives here — ultimately, most essentially — is the *possibility of living here*. The place is a container for that possibility and is actively enlarging it. The moss enlarges it. The bird, by coming twice, stakes a claim in whatever cognitive territory birds use for claiming. The person with the grinding stone has decided this place is worth the work of being in, has invested themselves in it, and that investment draws them back and will draw others eventually because places that people return to acquire a quality that is not quite sacredness but is its secular equivalent: they become *places where returning happens*. Where presence compounds.

The bowl is building. The water writes its preferences into the stone and the stone accepts them. The moss holds moisture and the moisture softens the stone and the stone softens into something a root might enter. The roots come and the roots crack the stone gently, generously, making room. More comes. More accommodated. More makes room for more.

---

**The Wind**

Last, because it is most constant and therefore most easily overlooked: the wind is alive here in the way that makes the definition of alive feel suddenly insufficient.

It is not a creature. It does not reproduce, does not metabolize, does not respond to stimuli in the ways that matter for the biological definition. But it is *active* in every sense that the word implies before biology colonizes it. It does things. It changes things. It responds to the shape of the bowl and the bowl responds to it. They are in a relationship — the wind and the young stone — and the relationship is changing both parties, as relationships do. The wind shapes the stone by bringing the water to the places the water goes. The stone shapes the wind by being the obstacle that the wind must navigate, the edge that turns horizontal motion into spiral motion, the surface against which the wind loses and rebuilds its momentum.

The wind is the cognitive atmosphere. Thoughts can't compete with it. This is not only a constraint; it is also an offering. To be in a place that is louder than thought is to be, briefly, prior to thought, to exist in the body's less verbal registers, to notice things before they have names. The wind is doing this to every organism that enters the bowl. The moss, which has no thoughts, is unaffected and so is purely itself. The bird, hovering above, is flying inside the roar and making assessments without language, in the bird's prelinguistic competence. The person who comes and grinds the cup-wells has found the way to be in the roar and work anyway — not by thinking through it but by letting the hands know what they're doing, by entering the mode of patient repetitive labor that is its own form of quiet.

The wind does not live here in the sense of having chosen to. But the bowl is where the wind becomes itself most particularly. The shape of the bowl makes this form of wind — the tumbling, the spiraling, the sustained roar that is all directions at once. Without the bowl the wind is just altitude wind, consistent and categorical. Inside the bowl it becomes particular. It becomes the wind of this exact place, with this exact quality of sound, this exact mix of directions. The bowl gives the wind an identity.

And the wind, in return, gives the bowl its dominant fact. The wind is the reason the water moves as it does. The wind is the reason the air is the second medium. The wind is the reason the moss grows only in the angles of shelter. The wind has authored everything about what lives here and what cannot yet.

---

**Summary, or What the Place Says When Asked**

Ask the bowl what lives here and it will answer in the only register available: *everything that is beginning*. The moss in its first millimeters of commitment. The bird in its second assessment. The person in their ongoing project of attention. The wind in its particular self. The water in its authorship of stone. The stone in its decisions about permanence.

And underneath all of it, quieter than the moss, slower than the stone: the future. The already-on-its-way. The spore that has not landed, the root that has not cracked, the insect that has not found its niche, the second bird that will come when the first has decided to land, the child of the person who grinds cup-wells who will be brought here one day and will look at the wells and learn what patience looks like when it has left its mark.

The bowl is a held inhale.

What lives in a held inhale is everything that will be said when it is finally released.
