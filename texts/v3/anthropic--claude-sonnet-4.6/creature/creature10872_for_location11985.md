# Who Lives in the Canopy Steppe

---

Begin with the children, because the children are easiest to find.

They are found by sound — but not the sound of the forest, which you have already learned to inhabit as a medium. A different sound: small percussive impacts against wood, rhythmic but irregular, the rhythm of practice rather than performance. A child is somewhere above you, climbing a trunk you could not climb, using handholds you cannot see from below. The handholds are there — have been there for generations, chiseled into the wood at intervals that suit a child's reach, which is to say: an adult has thought carefully about a child's reach and acted on that thought in a way that lasted. The child is perhaps twelve, perhaps younger, it is difficult to say, because these children are smaller than expectation and more deliberate — the lightness of high altitude and the demands of vertical life have produced bodies that conserve, that move with a density of intent unusual in children anywhere else. The child reaches the fork. The child does not look down.

There are always children aloft. This is the first thing to understand about living in this forest: the upward orientation begins early, is installed early, is not an achieved skill but a grown-in disposition, the way certain animals know water. A child who could just barely walk has already learned to weight a branch, to feel with the feet for flex and green-wood give, to read a trunk's surface topology as legible text. The fear of heights, if it existed in any of them once, was worked out so early that it left no trace. The children move in the canopy's middle registers the way children elsewhere run through grass — with that same particular quality of motion that is not athletic performance but simple locomotion, the body doing what it was made to do.

---

Now follow the sound of work.

Work, here, has a specific acoustic signature: the long draw of fiber through fiber, the soft impact of knotting. Everywhere there are people weaving. Not as ceremony, not as specialty craft — as the fundamental technology, the way another culture might use metal or fired clay. The material vocabulary of this people is cord and basket and woven mat and lashed joint, and the complexity achieved within this vocabulary is the complexity of a tradition that has had no reason to abandon itself, no external pressure to convert to anything else, centuries of refinement with nowhere to go but deeper. A woman — forty, perhaps, or fifty-five, it is again difficult to say; age presents differently here — sits in the fork of a branch fifty meters up, her back against the parent trunk, her feet braced against two diverging limbs, and works at something in her hands. She is making cord. She has been making cord for hours. She will make cord for some more hours after this. The cord she makes today will eventually become part of a structure you will sleep in, if you sleep in their structures, and the cord is being made with an attention to consistency of tension, regularity of twist, uniformity of fiber selection, that would require instruments to verify but requires no instruments to produce — it is in the hands, in the body, the discrimination happening below conscious attention. She does not look at her hands. She looks at the middle distance, which is to say: she looks at the forest. Whatever she is thinking, she is thinking in a visual register that takes the forest as its primary field. She hums, occasionally, without apparent awareness that she is humming. The hum is part of the work.

A man nearby is doing something to a joint where two platform timbers meet. The joint has lasted thirty years and will last another thirty with this attention. He works with a tool that is essentially a hardwood chisel, re-cutting the joint surfaces, clearing accumulated moisture-soft wood from the interface, reseating the connection with a precision that is felt through the hands and confirmed by a knock — knuckle against wood — a sound he reads diagnostically, the way a healer reads breath. He has been doing this for a decade. His father did it before him, on different platforms, and his son will do it in the decades after. The platform, in a sense, is not a thing that was built and is maintained but a process that began in some distant past and is still in progress, the people as much a part of the platform's continuation as the wood is.

---

They are small. This should be said directly.

Not dramatically small, not in a way that reads as pathological, but small in the way that high-altitude populations tend toward compactness, small in the way that a body given significant vertical work as its primary task optimizes for the ratio of strength to mass rather than mass alone. Their arms are disproportionate by lowland standards — long, and finished with hands that are thick-palmed and long-fingered both, the palms calloused to a specific topography that maps to the surfaces they grip most frequently: bark, cord, the edges of woven panels. Their feet are functionally prehensile — not dramatically, not simian, but when you watch someone descend headfirst down a trunk, wrapping their feet around it with a grip that is autonomous and sure, you understand that the foot has not been confined to a flat surface long enough to lose the flexibility it was perhaps always capable of.

The faces tend toward broad cheekbones, darker pigmentation, a vertical fold at the eye's inner corner that gives the impression of steadiness, of a gaze that makes long commitments to what it regards. This is not illusion: they do look at things for a long time. In conversation, they hold eye contact past the duration a lowland person might find comfortable, not aggressively — genuinely, with the quality of attention of someone for whom information arrives slowly and matters entirely. The forest does not provide constant social stimulus. What stimulus it provides is real.

Their hair, where it is worn long, is managed in ways that keep it from catching on surfaces — tightly plaited, tightly bound, or shorn close to the skull. You do not understand how important this is until you watch a person move through dense branch-work and see how a body streamlines, becomes a single continuous negotiation with a complex three-dimensional space, and understand that everything about the body's presentation is part of this negotiation. Loose cloth would be an embarrassment here. Loose anything. The clothing — where it exists, which varies — is close-fitting, woven rather than sewn, and worn in layers that can be adjusted. The cold that falls at night is a serious cold. The layering has its own system, a logic of addition and subtraction through the day's temperature cycle that becomes second nature.

---

They do not live on the ground.

This must be understood as something more than a behavioral preference. The ground floor, for these people, is a different country — a country they visit with purpose, for specific resources, and leave. The stone-ring hearths you found there are not the hearths of habitation but the hearths of process: for specific tasks that require fire and that require fire to be conducted safely, meaning: not inside a woven structure fifty meters up, not on a wooden platform maintained over decades, not anywhere near the irreplaceable architecture of their vertical life. Fire is managed, here, with a sobriety appropriate to the stakes. They bring it up in forms that reduce the risk — coals in clay-lined containers, low smoldering things — and they tend it carefully, and it does not get away from them, because the penalty is total. The culture that would let fire get away from them did not persist to have a culture.

So: the hearths below are for specific things. Bark preparation. The rendering of certain materials collected from trunk surfaces. The processing of foods gathered from ground-level plants, which are a supplement to but not the foundation of the diet — the canopy provides more, and they know the canopy's provisions intimately, have named all of them, have noticed patterns in availability and timing that constitute a multi-generational calendar, a phenological knowledge so specific and so deeply integrated into daily planning that it does not read as knowledge exactly but as ambient awareness, as part of the texture of consciousness.

They eat high. They sleep high. They are born, mostly, in the fork-structures, in the basket-cradles built there for the purpose. The cradle swings when the branch swings. The infant — from the first days, from the first hours — inhabits a world that moves, that yields, that has flex and pendulum and the slow large sway of the canopy in high wind. The vestibular system of these people is calibrated to a moving world from the beginning. An adult, suspended in a platform or a cradle, barely registers the forest's motion. A visitor, in the same position, grips.

---

What they know.

Here it is necessary to slow down, because this category matters and is large.

They know this forest. This is not a sentimental claim; it is a description of a cognitive load that would, from outside, appear impossible. The forest, at this scale, with this complexity, with this vertical depth — the number of species involved, the number of relationships between species, the patterns of seasonal change, the geography of wet zones and dry zones at ground level and wind channels at height and fruiting cycles and die-offs and resurgences, the forty-year cycles and the ten-year cycles and the cycles that complete over a century and so must be known by transmission rather than direct observation — the information content here is staggering. And they carry it. They carry it in memory, in song, in the designs of their woven things (which are not simply decorative, which encode patterns of the forest as mnemonic, as reference, as knowledge externalized into a medium that can be transmitted to the hands directly), in the specialized names they have for aspects of tree-surface and cloud-formation and branch-angle that other languages have no terms for.

The old woman in the canopy knows which trunk in this sector will develop a particular fruit in thirty-two days, because she knows the flowering cycle of this organism and she has been watching it for sixty years. She knows something her daughter knows and something her daughter will have to learn still and something she herself is not yet sure of, a pattern she is watching develop over a span that may exceed her remaining lifespan — a pattern she is already naming, already beginning to describe, so that when she is dead the naming will survive her and someone else will see it through. This is the structure of knowledge transmission here: not school, not archive, but the continuous verbal construction of observation, the narrating of the forest to one another as a practice, as daily activity, as something that is also companionship and also art and also necessity, because the silence is a silence that nothing survives in.

They know the architecture of these trees — not abstractly, but in the specific instance, each tree differently. The trees are individuals to them in the way that people are individuals. Longer-lived individuals, subject to their own histories of injury and healing and growth-response, with their own structural personalities, their own weaknesses and strong passages and preferred load-bearing zones. A person does not simply climb a tree. A person climbs *this* tree, in the way that *this* tree is best climbed, having been shown by someone who learned it from someone who learned it, the accumulated expertise of generations of reading one specific organism's surfaces and logic.

---

What they think about.

This is harder to access. This requires time and a certain quality of sustained attention.

But: they think about the forest the way people elsewhere think about weather — as the ground condition of all other thought, the thing beneath everything, the field in which all other meaning-making takes place. Their metaphysics, to the extent this word is usable without distortion, is organized around continuity rather than event, around process rather than origin — the question *how did things begin* is less interesting to them than the question *how do things continue*, and the answer to that question is always relational, always reciprocal, always involves a specific list of participants. Things continue because other things continue to make them continue. The forest runs on this, and so does everything else, as far as they understand. They are part of the run of it.

There is not a word in their language, as best can be understood from outside, that means *nature* as distinct from *the world in which people live*. This distinction does not appear. People live inside the forest, which is not a backdrop; the forest is not scenery behind a human story; the human story is one story being told simultaneously with many others in the same place, and knowing the others is required for telling yours correctly.

They have stories in which the trees speak — not as metaphor. Not as a literary device in a tradition sophisticated enough to know it is a device. The trees speak because the forest does, in fact, communicate, and they have been listening carefully for long enough that this seems straightforwardly true. The chemistry of it — the root-network transfer, the chemical signaling between canopy organisms — they don't have these words, but they have something better: the observations that inspired the understanding. They noticed what the conceptual framework hadn't yet caught up to.

They argue. They grieve. They fall in love with the wrong people and negotiate the aftermath. They have humor that tends toward the understated and the dry, a humor appropriate to a life that requires steady attention and cannot be managed through irrationality — the comedy, when it comes, arrives through the slight deflation of what is too serious, the small absurdity embedded in the critical moment. Someone slips on a root and does not fall, exactly, performs instead an elaborate save that is aesthetically equivalent to a fall in terms of embarrassment, and the laughter is warm and brief and recovers quickly, because there is work.

---

What they do not have.

No wheel, which in this landscape would be useless. No large domesticated animals, which the forest has no room for at ground level and which would not thrive at altitude in any case. No metallurgy — fire is managed too carefully, too minimally, for smelting, and the ore does not exist here, and a tradition has not developed that requires it. No writing — but do not mistake absence for impoverishment, because the weaving carries what writing elsewhere carries, and the memory here is a trained instrument, maintained through daily practice, capable of holding and transmitting information with a reliability that outsiders consistently underestimate.

No concept of land as ownable. Land here is the forest and the forest is old and the forest was here before anything they can name and will be here after and does not recognize the concept. What is recognized is use-relationship: this is where my family climbs, where we have always climbed, where we have made the structures and know the trees individually. This is not property. The difference is precise and significant and they would find the conflation confusing if pressed.

---

The question of number.

They are not many. The forest at this scale feeds a density, not a mass. A group numbers in the dozens — sometimes forty, sometimes twenty-eight, sometimes, after a difficult year, fewer. Multiple groups exist within the forest system, separated by distances that take days to travel at canopy level (faster than ground, once you understand the architecture), and these groups are in contact — periodic, structured, meaningful contact, the gathering that happens at intervals in a specific high clearing where two adjacent canopy systems almost meet and the sky is briefly visible, where marriages are arranged and knowledge is exchanged and the young are shown to one another and the old tell what they've been watching. These gatherings are anticipated for months. They are not, when they happen, particularly dramatic. There is too much practical work to do — too much to exchange, too much to discuss — for drama. The feeling is more like relief. The relief of being known by more people than you see every day. The relief of the extended context, the validation that your local experience is part of something larger.

Strangers — genuine strangers, people from outside the forest system entirely — are rare enough that no established protocol for them exists. What exists instead is improvisation based on general principles: wariness without hostility, observation before commitment, a period of being watched that is patient and long. They will not decide quickly. The forest does not teach quick decisions.

---

The overall impression, assembled from its parts:

People who have learned to be a consequence of this place rather than an imposition on it, and who have found in that orientation not diminishment but a particular kind of freedom — the freedom of deep belonging, of not needing to construct a world from scratch because the world was here and has already been thought about, has already been learned, can be entered and inhabited and added to rather than built new in every generation. They are serious in the way that people are serious who know that attentiveness is survival. They are not solemn — seriousness and solemnity are different, and the distinction matters here. They laugh. They argue about the quality of someone's cord work. They have aesthetic preferences and act on them. But they are very present. The forest requires presence, and they have been shaped to supply it.

What the forest produces in them, over time, over centuries, over the long generations of living inside something at continental scale and learning to read it — what it produces is not smallness but a different relationship to scale. They are not diminished by living inside something enormous. They are fluent in it. They know its registers and can move between them. They have names for things that have no names elsewhere because elsewhere those things have not been noticed, not for long enough, not by enough attentive people over enough generations. They are, in this sense, the forest knowing itself — one of the ways the forest has developed to be aware of what it is.

The trees have held this. The trees hold this still.
