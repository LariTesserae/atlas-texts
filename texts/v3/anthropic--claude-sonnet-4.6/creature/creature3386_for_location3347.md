# Those Who Live in the Wet City at the Bottom of Everything

---

Begin with their relationship to time, because without this nothing else about them makes sense.

They are not long-lived, individually. This surprises people who first encounter them, who expect that a civilization of such accumulated depth must have produced beings who persist for centuries, who remember personally the laying of the oldest stones, who carry the history in their own tissues. No. They live approximately as long as the fauna that share their streets — a few decades, some of them longer, none of them more than a lifetime in any register you would find remarkable. What is long-lived is not them. What is long-lived is what they *are to each other*, and this is a distinction that the city has given them enough time to stop explaining and start simply demonstrating.

They have been building community for so long that community has become something they do biologically, the way other organisms do breathing. Not a practice. Not an institution. A function.

---

They are medium-sized.

This seems like a trivial observation until you have been in the city long enough to understand it as an adaptation. Medium-sized: able to move through the dense three-dimensional infrastructure at a useful pace, able to navigate the maintenance corridors and the reconfiguring interior spaces, small enough to share a city this dense without requiring unshared space, large enough to not be easily lost in the complexity, large enough to matter to each other across meaningful distances. They fit the city the way the city fits the basin. The fit is not coincidental. They and the city have been fitting each other for longer than reliable records extend, iterating toward mutual accommodation with the unhurried patience of things that expect to still be here.

Their bodies have a quality of moisture management that they are not consciously aware of but that you would notice immediately: they do not dry out, and they do not waterlog. The city is always wet and they are always exactly as wet as they mean to be. They have been here long enough that their physiology and the city's ambient humidity have arrived at an arrangement, sealed it, stopped renegotiating it. Wet surfaces do not impede them. They do not slip on the gleaming streets the way you would slip. Their relationship with the water underfoot is fluent.

They smell, faintly and without offense, of the city. The ozone and old stone and warm water that characterizes the place is in their skin. Not because they are unwashed but because the chemistry has been exchanged long enough that the boundary between inhabitant and inhabited is, as with so much else here, no longer clean.

---

Their senses are distributed in a way that the city has shaped toward its own complexity.

Their hearing is extraordinary, but not in the sense of detecting faint sounds — in the sense of navigating enormous sound. They have learned, or rather have evolved the capacity and then learned *within* that capacity, to hear the city as signal rather than noise. Where you hear only the overwhelming continuous roar of everything at once, they hear — not silence, nothing so simple — a texture of sound in which meaningful things are embedded and from which meaningful things can be extracted without effort, the way you extract speech from a conversation without consciously filtering out all other frequencies. They hear the infrastructure. They hear when something changes in the low tectonic moan of the settling city, when the note of a particular conduit shifts to indicate stress or blockage or reconfiguration in progress. They hear each other in crowds so dense and loud that you would have given up and resorted to touch.

Their vision in low light is better than in bright, which makes sense: the managed daylight of the upper streets is almost too much for them. They navigate best in the quality of light that reaches the deep streets — the reflected, multiplied, diffuse gleam off wet surfaces. That light suits them. They look at each other in it the way you look at each other in full sun: clearly, without effort, with the satisfaction of adequate illumination.

What they touch, they know in detail. They are not unusual in having hands — they have hands, approximately, the right number for their kind of body — but the hands are extraordinarily informed. They read surfaces the way you read print. Every texture of the city's materials, every temperature differential in the walls, every vibration that travels through the stone and substrate, arrives at the hands as information. When one of them runs a hand along an old wall, this is not an absent-minded gesture. It is a query. The wall answers.

---

They talk constantly.

This is not a flaw and not a cultural trait and not merely a consequence of their social nature, though it is all of those things. It is also a technology. Acoustic communication in a city this loud requires, first, that you have developed the vocal apparatus to produce signals that carry over the ambient noise; they have done this. Their voices occupy frequencies and dynamic ranges that travel well in the city's specific acoustic environment, not by being simply louder but by being *shaped* to carry in these conditions — the way a ship's horn carries over harbor noise not through volume alone but through the specific characteristics of the sound itself. Second, it requires that you have developed density of information in communication: they say more per unit of exchange than you might find comfortable. Conversations that seem to you to be brief — a few syllables, a rising intonation, a particular quality of pause — are, to them, complete. Long conversations are for pleasure, for the working-out of novel problems, for the exchange that is its own purpose. But functional communication has been compressed by millennia of necessity into a very efficient form.

They gesture, too, but the gestures are not illustrative. They are not making their words clearer with their hands. The gestures *are speech*, a parallel channel running simultaneously, carrying different content — the hands saying one thing while the voice says another and the two together say the third thing, the thing that is actually being communicated. You will spend your first significant period here understanding words but missing conversations. This is common. It is not held against you.

---

Their faces are expressive to a degree that might initially seem theatrical.

They are not performing. The expressiveness is the resolution of a system that must communicate nuance across noise and density and the constant churning motion of the city, that must be readable from moderate distances and through the sort of perpetual semi-distraction that a city this loud and active produces in its inhabitants. Their faces have been, over generations and generations, refined toward legibility at the scales this environment demands. Small, subtle expressions are invisible here and so have been abandoned: evolution, pressed by selection, has retained only what the city makes visible. What is left is vivid.

When they are glad, they are unmistakably and completely glad.

When they are troubled, the trouble is all over them, unhidden, present in every line of the face and position of the body, because in a city this dense there is no point in hiddenness — you are never far enough from anyone for concealment to protect you. They learned this, or they were selected toward understanding it, and now they do not waste effort concealing the emotions that are in them. This is alarming at first. It becomes, after a while, restful.

---

They trust the city. This is the deepest fact about them.

Not trust in the way you trust a machine — not the mechanical confidence that pressing the button will produce the predicted output. Trust in the way you trust something you have been in relationship with long enough that its intentions, to whatever extent a city has intentions, are legible to you. They live inside an entity of extraordinary complexity that has been modifying itself without fully centralized direction for longer than any individual has lived, and they are not afraid of it, and their lack of fear is not ignorance. They know what the city is doing. Not in detail — not any individual of them, not in any specific sense. But collectively, across the distributed network of their community, across the accumulated understanding of people who have spent their lives learning one building, one district, one system of conduits and flow and reconfiguration, and have taught what they learned to the next people who would need to know it — collectively they hold a working model of the city's behavior that is detailed enough to amount to a relationship.

They know what it does when it is healthy and what it does when something is wrong. They know which signs in the walls and floors are stable accommodations and which are new stresses. They know the city's patterns the way you know the breathing patterns of someone asleep beside you: not because you have studied it but because you have been there long enough that deviation from the pattern wakes you.

And the city, for its part — the city's self-modifying systems, the iterating infrastructure, the crystalline lattice that carries something like information through something like a nervous system — responds to them. Not personally. Not with directed intention. But the city's long optimization has taken them into account, the way a river in its long optimization of its course takes into account the vegetation on its banks. They are part of what the city is solving for. The water-channels in the walls go where they go because people live beside them. The light-management systems in the upper reaches calibrate to the spectra they can actually use. The drains are enormous because they understood early that the drains would need to be enormous, and built them that way, and the city has kept them.

The city is not *for* them in the sense that a tool is for its user. They are not outside it, deploying it. They are *in* it, and it is *in* them, and the preposition that would most accurately describe their relationship to the city does not exist in any language you know.

---

Their social structure is dense in the same way the city is dense.

No space between people is unoccupied. This is not claustrophobia-inducing in their experience, because they do not have the same expectation of unoccupied space that you might bring to the question. Unoccupied social space, to them, reads the way an empty district reads in the city: as something gone wrong, as a sign that something is missing that should be there, as a problem requiring attention. They move through their relationships with the same fluency that they move through the streets — always in the presence of someone, always in negotiation with the near field of their social world, always with the sound of people around them serving the dual purpose of noise and information.

They have private thoughts. They value private thoughts. The city has taught them that interior space is precious because it is scarce, and they maintain their interior lives with the same care they bring to maintaining a useful pocket of quiet in an acoustic environment this overwhelming. Their privacy is in their minds. Everywhere else, they are present to each other.

They raise children collectively, which is the only thing that makes sense in a city where every surface is a surface for something. Children are not the project of two people here. They are the project of the district, the building, the cluster of buildings, the neighborhood as it understands itself — whatever unit of organization has cohered around a set of people who have been adjacent long enough to accumulate obligation toward each other. The children grow up knowing everyone in their immediate world by name and texture and voice-frequency and the specific way their footsteps sound on wet stone. They are, from the beginning, thoroughly legible to the people around them, and the people around them are thoroughly legible to them, and this is understood as the most important competence a new person can develop: the ability to read and be read.

---

They disagree constantly, loudly, without apparent distress, and then continue.

Disagreement here does not carry the stakes it carries in social systems organized around scarcity of connection — in a city this dense, this continuously occupied, there is nowhere to go after a disagreement that puts you beyond the network of relationships you depend on. You will see each other again. You will need each other. The disagreement is required to be survivable, and so they have developed, over the long time they have been disagreeing with each other in close quarters, a practice of disagreement that keeps the relationship intact while doing full justice to the conflict. They are not conflict-avoidant. They are conflict-fluent.

They will argue with you. They will argue with you in ways that make clear they find your position interesting. These two things are not contradictory. The argument is a form of engagement. The engagement is its own statement.

---

What they make, they make to last.

Not because they are unusually cautious or because they have philosophically committed to permanence, but because they live in a city whose oldest layers are still in use, whose foundations are indistinguishably fused with geology, whose infrastructure is the product of so many successive decisions building on so many previous decisions that nothing can be removed without implicating everything. They have internalized the logic of accumulated consequence. When they build a thing or write a thing or establish a practice, they are thinking about it in the long register — not necessarily consciously, not necessarily with explicit intention, but with the ingrained sense of people who know they are adding to a palimpsest, who know that what they put down will be what the next generation builds on, who have seen what it looks like when you do this badly and when you do it well.

The things they make to last, last.

The things they make for now, they make to be absorbed cleanly — to reconfigure, to decompose into useful substrate, to join the city's continuous reorganization without snagging, without leaving waste that future arrangements will have to work around. Permanence and transience are both applied deliberately, chosen for, designed toward. They do not make things accidentally. The city has taught them that accidents in dense systems propagate and accumulate and become, eventually, geological.

---

Their religion, if you call it that — their sense of what is sacred, their practices organized around what exceeds ordinary accounting — concerns water.

Specifically: the water in the walls.

Not the rain, though the rain is appreciated. Not the drains, though the drains are venerated in a more civic register, honored for their scale and their reliability and their fundamental role in making the city possible. Not the condensation cycling through the upper tiers. The water in the walls: the groundwater, the ancient seep, the moisture that moves through stone that has accepted it, finding its level, arriving at its arrangement, going where it has always been going. That water has been moving through those walls for longer than the inhabitants have existed. It predates their civilization. It may postdate it. It requires nothing. It is doing something in the oldest layers of the city that has no relationship to their presence and would continue without them.

They find this holy.

Not because it is beyond them but because it is *beside* them — because it is there, proceeding, having its own process in the same material they have built their lives in, indifferent and perpetual and not diminished by their proximity to it. The city, with all its noise and adaptation and self-revision and turbulent presence, contains this thing that is simply itself, has always been itself, will continue being itself. They are comforted by this in a way that they struggle to articulate when asked, because the comfort comes from the part of them that understands something in wordless registers.

When something important happens — a beginning or an ending or a transition that requires marking — they find a place where the water can be heard in the walls and they sit with it for a while. They do not pray, exactly. They listen. They let the sound tell them what it knows, which is not advice and not comfort in the ordinary sense but just: *this is what continuity sounds like. This is what has been going on. This is what will keep going on.*

Then they get up and rejoin the noise.

---

They are not difficult to talk to.

This surprises people, who expect that inhabitants of something this ancient and self-sufficient and foreign-scaled would be closed toward newcomers, sealed in their own accumulated ways, possessed of the particular aloofness of things that have been here a very long time and seen many arrivals and departures. This is not what they are. The city, by being always in motion and always reconfiguring and always absorbing new elements into its continuous reorganization, has shaped them toward openness in the same way it has shaped them toward fluency in three-dimensional navigation: because in this environment, closed is a liability. The thing you are not open to is the thing that takes you by surprise. They prefer to know. They prefer to ask. They extend the preference to you.

They will want to know where you came from and what you have seen and what the light is like where you are from and whether you have considered staying. The questions are genuine. They are not being polite. They have been here long enough that genuine interest is what they have instead of politeness, and they find the formality of polite interest slightly sad — all that effort to approximate the thing you could just be having.

You are not unwelcome here. You were told this when you arrived, in the sound before you could think clearly, in the push of the noise on your chest, in the wet air filling your lungs with the chemistry of a place that has been occupied for so long that occupation is its nature.

The city is breathing. You are inside it. They are also inside it, and they are looking at you from down the gleaming street with expressions you can read clearly because legibility has been selected for, and the expression says: *you have arrived somewhere. We are curious about you. Stay long enough that we can be curious properly.*

The water moves in the walls. The drains run deep. The light multiplies in the wet surfaces and fills the streets with something almost like warmth.

---

They are exactly what you would expect, if you had spent long enough expecting them.
