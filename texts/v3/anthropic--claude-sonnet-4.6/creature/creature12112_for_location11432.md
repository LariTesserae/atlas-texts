# What Lives in the Dry Upland

---

Begin with the ones you cannot see.

They are not hidden in the conventional sense — not crouching behind stone, not burrowed into crannies, not avoiding the light. They simply have learned that visibility and existence are not the same thing, and they have made the appropriate adjustments. These are the small-bodied arthropods that live in the mineral grit between boulders, their exoskeletons the precise ochre-cream of the ground they move across, their movements so unhurried that the eye, searching for motion as its primary signal of life, passes over them entirely. They are several millimeters long. Some are several centimeters. They have been here as long as anything has been here, and they are, in terms of number, the dominant animals on the upland — the most numerous, the most widely distributed, the most thoroughly adapted. Their presence fills the spaces between the visible and notable things. They eat the grit's thin organic layer, the wind-deposited spores and fragments, the dead outer cells of lichens, the fine debris that the cushion-mosses shed at their edges. They drink nothing. What moisture they need they extract from this material with a chemistry that is very patient and very precise.

They do not respond to being looked at. This is different from not being aware. They have been arrived at by a long process that selected against expensive things — against speed, against elaborate sensing, against the metabolic investment of anxiety. What they have instead is a low continuous attentiveness, a state that is not quite alertness and not quite torpor, a maintenance of readiness at minimal cost. If you placed your hand flat on the grit nearby, some of them would detour around it without urgency. They would not flee. Fleeing is for animals with someplace to flee to, and the upland's scale makes destination a complicated concept.

They are not interesting in the way that other things on the upland are interesting. They are interesting in the way that foundations are interesting: you understand the building better when you understand what it stands on.

---

Now the mosses themselves. 

Because the mosses are not simply plants. The largest of the cushion-forms — the ones that stand thirty centimeters and spread several meters and glow their violent green — are not one thing but many. They are communities of a different kind than the plant-communities the landscape describes. Inside the body of a cushion-moss, in the dark layered interior beneath the photosynthesizing surface, there are entire populations of organisms that have never touched open air and will not, in any individual's lifetime, ever do so. Fungi in the technical sense but not the spectacular sense, not mushrooms, nothing that would catch attention — thread-thin hyphal networks that run through the interior of the cushion, exchanging chemistry with the moss cells in a relationship old enough to have forgotten whether it was originally parasitism or mutualism and arrived at something that is now neither, or both, or a category that requires a different word. They take sugars. They return water. They provide structural integrity to the older dead layers of the cushion's interior, filling the pore spaces in ways that help the whole cushion maintain its shape, its capacity to hold the moisture it harvests.

These interior fungi have never experienced light. They are shaped by the logic of interior spaces: thin, branching, investing nothing in walls or surfaces that would cost more than they return. They are almost entirely surface area. This is the condition of something that has given up on having an outside.

And within the fungi, within the films of moisture they maintain, there are smaller things yet — single-celled, unnamed at this degree of specificity, living their entire existence in a world bounded by two surfaces of fungal cell wall, processing the trace sugars and minerals that diffuse through to them, dividing when conditions are right, entering suspended states when they are not. They have existed in suspended states for durations that the term *lifespan* is not built to contain. They stop. They are not dead. The upland's long cold nights, during which the moisture in the cushion comes close to freezing, do not kill them. They wait inside a chemistry that is their own slowed-down self and they wait, and then they are active again.

This is what it means to live inside a cushion-moss. It means living inside the metabolism of something larger than yourself that is itself living inside a climate that has, over enormous time, worked out an arrangement with the thin air and the rare fog and the cold stone, and the arrangement works, and you are part of the arrangement, and the arrangement is very old.

---

The pale animal that grazes at the mosses deserves a longer look than the distance usually permits.

It is large in a way that surprises, when you finally close the distance. The upland's scale had made it seem smaller, more manageable. Up close it is substantial, barrel-chested in the way of animals that have solved cold with mass, that have learned the efficiency of keeping a large core warm over the metabolic cost of warming a complicated surface. Its legs are relatively short and wide-set, a configuration that reads as stocky from a distance but up close reads differently: it reads as *grounded*, as an animal that has made a deliberate physical commitment to its relationship with a particular substrate. Low center of gravity. Broad hoof-pads that distribute weight across loose grit without sinking. It moves the way it moves not because it cannot move quickly but because nothing here has asked it to.

Its coat is not one color. From a distance, from the direction of strong light, it looks pale, nearly stone-colored. But at close range, in the right angle of the afternoon's rose-gold, the coat resolves into a structure of many colors working together: the individual hairs are banded, pale at the tip and slightly darker toward the base, and this banding produces in aggregate the exactly-right ambiguity that makes the animal hard to see against the mineral grit. This is old. This is older than the form the animal currently wears. The banding of individual hairs is a feature that does not develop freshly in each generation but is laid down in genetics so ancient that it has outrun the memory of whatever original predator it was developed against. There is nothing now on the upland that hunts the pale animal. There has not been for a long time, or there never was — the upland's harshness having perhaps screened out predators before they could establish themselves, or the animal having come from some lower place long ago where predators were real and then risen here where they are not, carrying its camouflage like a habit it has not yet been given reason to examine.

It does not drink. This is the central and extraordinary fact about it. Its kidneys are the most remarkable organs it has, more remarkable than its eyes or its legs — they are capable of concentrating urine to a degree that would indicate severe pathology in most mammals but here indicates health, is in fact the expression of a long optimization. The moisture it needs comes from the mosses it grazes, from the waxy leaves of the low-growing plants, from licking the thin deposition of morning moisture from rock surfaces — a behavior that looks almost absentminded, the long rough tongue moving methodically across a boulder's face in the early hours, collecting what is there, which is very little, which is enough. Its gut flora are unusually efficient at extracting water from dry plant material. Its nasal passages are architecturally complex, the turbinate bones forming an elaborate baffling system that recovers most of the moisture from exhaled air before it can leave the body. It breathes out less water than it breathes in. This is the kind of solution that required enormous time and cannot be rushed.

When it grazes on the cushion-moss, it takes from the edges. Not the center. The center is left. Whether this is learned behavior or something that can be described with a more mechanical word, the effect is the same: the cushion persists. The animal feeds and the cushion persists and the animal returns and feeds again and the cushion persists, year on year, the same cushions that were there before the animal was born and will be there after the animal's bones have been disarticulated by freeze-and-thaw and scattered into the grit. The cushion is older than the animal. The animal's relationship with the cushion is older than any individual on either side of it. It is a relationship the upland holds between its conditions, one of the threads of arrangement that make the whole thing work.

The pale animal does not appear to have young here — or does not have them visibly, in the open. Whether they are born in some more sheltered margin, at the far edges of the upland where the gradient toward lower country begins, and come to the interior only when large enough to manage it, or whether the birth happens here and the young are simply very good at being invisible, is not clear. The upland does not offer its demographics easily.

---

The things in the air.

They are not birds. They use the thermals the heated rock produces in the bright hours, but they are not birds in the way that birds elsewhere are birds — the mechanisms are similar and the relationship to air is similar and there the similarity ends. Their wings are proportioned differently, longer relative to body size and narrower, which produces lift with less flapping at the cost of maneuverability, a tradeoff that makes sense in an environment where there are no trees to navigate, no dense vegetation, no obstacles that require anything other than slow wide turns over open terrain. They have made the bargain that the upland makes available: give up agility, gain endurance. They can remain aloft for the entire duration of the bright hours without a wingbeat.

Their vision is the most expensive thing about them. At the height they fly, the upland is a pattern of pale stone and pale grit and the small color anomalies that life produces — the dark-surfaced leaves of the low-growing plants, the violent green of the cushion-mosses, the slow movement of the pale grazing animal crossing from one community to the next. They are looking for the anomalies. Their eyes have a density of photoreceptors in the relevant part of the retina that is, by any measure, excessive for the available information. They see more than there is to see, in the sense that their resolution exceeds the resolution of the landscape. But this is not a mistake. In a place where the signals of life are rare and small and far away, over-investment in perception is the correct strategy. They see the arthropod in the grit from a height at which the arthropod is notionally invisible. They see the pale animal from so far away that the pale animal's own camouflage, which works against any eye at ground level, is irrelevant to the geometry of the view from above. From above, the animal is a shape, and shapes move differently from stone.

They descend slowly, in long spirals, losing altitude in increments that allow for reconsideration. They do not dive. Diving is for environments where prey can escape — where the gap between approach and arrival needs to be closed before the target moves. Here, if the arthropod is spotted, the arthropod will not be in a different place by the time the slow descent concludes. The patience of the hunter and the patience of the hunted are in a kind of proportion, and neither is fast.

When they land, which is rarely and briefly, they fold their long wings in a way that looks slightly uncomfortable, the geometry of the wing requiring a compromise with the geometry of the standing body, and they stand on the stone with an air of temporary tolerance for ground, as though the earth is a necessary stop but not a destination. They are most themselves at a thousand meters above it, turning in circles above the pale upland, visible from below as slow dark shapes against the silver-blue sky, reading the landscape's details with their excessive eyes and finding what little there is to find.

---

Inside the lichens, at the scale the unaided eye cannot reach.

What looks from a distance like a painted wash across the rock face, and from close range like a dry hexagonal pavement, is at the microscopic scale a different world entirely — more architectural, more populated, more eventful. The lichen is not one organism. It has never been one organism. It is a fungus that has entered into an arrangement with a photosynthetic partner, usually a single-celled alga, sometimes a cyanobacterium, and the arrangement is the lichen, the lichen is the relationship, there is no lichen without both. This is well-known. What is less attended to is the additional population that lives within and upon the lichen without being formally part of it: the tardigrades.

The tardigrades on this upland's lichen are present in numbers that would require substantial units to express, distributed across every lichen-covered surface. They are microscopic in the technical sense but at the larger end of that range, visible with modest magnification as small barrel-shaped bodies moving with a lumbering eight-legged walk through the landscape of lichen filaments. They eat the algal cells. They puncture them with a stylet — a hollow piercing structure — and extract the contents, which are sugar and water and the building materials of photosynthesis, and this is all they need.

They are the most nearly indestructible organisms on the upland, and the upland selects for indestructibility in everything it hosts, so this is a meaningful distinction. In conditions that would end every other living thing on the surface — radiation levels, temperature extremes, desiccation to a small fraction of normal body water, vacuum — the tardigrades enter a state called a tun, curling their bodies, retracting their legs, slowing their metabolism to an instrumentally unmeasurable rate, and they wait. They can wait for a very long time. They have waited, in documented cases, for decades. The upland's conditions do not approach the extremes required to truly challenge them. For the tardigrades, the upland is a comfortable place, by the standards of their range.

When moisture returns — the morning deposition, the rare fog — they uncurl. They resume walking through the lichen surface. They eat. They reproduce. They do not appear to have an experience of having been through anything. They are the same before and after. The interval of the tun has no residue in them. This is what it looks like to be fully adapted to discontinuous time, to a world that pauses and resumes, to the upland's long dark cold nights and its seasons and its geological patience. The tardigrades are not adapted to the upland's conditions. They are adapted to conditions far more extreme, and the upland, for them, requires only a small part of their capability. They live here with most of what they can do held in reserve, like a word for a situation that has not yet arisen.

---

The thing the upland is doing, with all of these arrangements.

It is not tending toward anything. It is not building toward a climax, not developing toward a richer or more complex state, not recovering from anything or anticipating anything. It has arrived at a set of relationships that are stable at its timescale, which is very long, and it is maintaining those relationships with the ordinary processes that constitute maintenance: growth and grazing and decomposition and redistribution, the thin organic layer accumulating and being consumed and accumulating, the lichens dissolving mineral at the pace of centuries, the cushion-mosses harvesting fog and feeding the grazing animal and providing the substrate for the fungi and the substrate within the substrate for the invisible cells that divide and wait and divide, the arthropods cycling the grit's organic fraction, the aerial things keeping the arthropod population in a range that the grit's organic layer can support.

Everything that lives here has given something up and received something in return, and what it has received is the upland — the time of it, the space of it, the particular quality of light in the late hours when the stone glows and the sky goes amber and the visible surface of the place looks, in its vast paleness and its sparse bright living things, like a proposition about what is necessary and what can be let go. The cushion-moss has given up height and access to the chemistry of running water and retained instead the ability to make its own water system from air, and it has received this place where it glows like something sacred against the stone. The pale animal has given up speed and elaborate social behavior and most of its water budget and retained its patient bulk and its bone-deep cold tolerance and its old camouflage it no longer needs, and it has received the whole upland to move across, unhurried, in the amber light. The tardigrades have, if they have given up anything, given up nothing visible, or perhaps they gave it up so long ago and in a different place that the upland itself is the inheritance of that transaction, the gift at the end of a chain of reductions that turned out to be a kind of wealth.

The lichen grows toward its edges. The upland holds its elevation. The tardigrade uncurls in the morning moisture and resumes its walk across the surfaces of a world that is enormous in all the ways that matter and exactly right.
