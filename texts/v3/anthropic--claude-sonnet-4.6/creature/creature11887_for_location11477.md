# What Lives in the Tannery District of Voss

---

## The Biofilm

Begin with the thing that is most completely of this place, because it did not arrive here. It did not migrate or choose. It assembled.

The pale crust on the north face of the wall is not one organism and not many. That distinction — one, many — does not map cleanly onto what it is. It is a community of chemolithotrophs and sulfur-cycling archaea and at least two strains of extremophilic bacteria whose closest characterized relatives live in mine drainage and deep-sea hydrothermal vents, which says something about the chemistry the tannery has deposited into its ground over the decades, which says something about the ground. These organisms do not photosynthesize. They do not need to. They metabolize the minerals leaching through the wall, the calcium carbonate of the drip-stain, the sulfur compounds carried in the tan liquor seepage. They eat the history of the district the way time eats it — slowly, chemically, without preference.

The archaea are the oldest residents. Not individually — archaea die like everything dies, cells dividing and replacing — but as a lineage. Their metabolic signatures, the specific enzymatic pathways they use to fix carbon, are adaptations to conditions that existed before the tannery, before the dyeworks, back to the original industrial use of this land that the ground remembers and the records don't. They have been here, in the sense that a river has been here: continuously, through replacement, the pattern holding while the substance changes.

The circulatory patterns in the crust — the ones that look like watershed maps, like nervous systems — are not accidental, but accidental is the wrong word for what they are. They are the spatial signature of competition and cooperation between the mat's constituent species, played out over decades. Where the sulfur-metabolizers spread, the carbonate-precipitators follow along the gradient. Where the archaea concentrate, there is warmth from their metabolic processes, and this warmth draws moisture, and the moisture defines a path, and the path becomes fixed in the structure of the mat itself. It is a record of internal weather. The mat has meteorology.

In the lowest layer of the mat, in the zone pressed closest to the wall's surface, the bacteria are doing something that has not been characterized. The academic literature has not attended to it, as the district's description notes, and this is accurate. What appears to be happening — from the chemical gradients, from the isotope ratios in the crust's lower strata, from the anomalous stability of the mat's outer layer — is that the organisms in this zone are producing a compound that has no identified natural analog, a long-chain polymer with piezoelectric properties. The mat, very slightly, responds to pressure. Not to touch — no one touches it, and a finger pressed against it would produce nothing detectable by the finger. But to sound. To the specific low-frequency vibration of twelve drums rotating at eight to twelve RPM, transmitted through the brick of the wall, received by a community of microorganisms that have evolved in the presence of that vibration for three or four decades, long enough for selection to have found the response.

What the mat does with this response is not known. The mat does not know what it does with it either, if *know* is a permissible verb here, and it is not. But the mat's outer surface is more stable, more resistant to desiccation and chemical fluctuation, on the north face of the wall than on the south face where the drums are not audible. The correlation is there. The mechanism is there, potentially. What is not there is anyone to find it.

The mat is, as the description notes, faintly lovely. It is also very old, and very particular, and entirely self-concerned. It does not experience the district. It metabolizes it.

## The Flies

They are two species, not three. *Fannia scalaris* and an undescribed species in the family Phoridae that has developed, in the specific environment of this tannery and possibly one or two similar establishments in this region, a behavioral repertoire not observed in related species.

The *Fannia scalaris* — the latrine fly, the lesser house fly — are here because flies like this are always in places like this. They breed in the organic material at the edges of the sump, in the slick layer between the cinder and the base of the cold-storage wall, in the accumulated sediment of the tan pits. Their presence is explanatory. They are here because the district provides what they need, and what they provide in return is movement in the district's sound-space, and the occasional death that the rats attend to, and the parasitoid wasps they do not carry because there are no parasitoid wasps here, which means their populations are regulated by the district itself — by the available substrate, by competition with the phorids, by the temperature, which is structural and does not fluctuate the way weather fluctuates and so does not produce the boom-and-bust cycles that fly populations exhibit in outdoor environments. Here they are stable. They are part of the balance.

The phorids are more interesting, and they are aware of this in the same way the mat is aware of anything: they are not.

The hovering columns are a courtship behavior, which is also ordinary — many small flies form hovering leks, males displaying in columns of turbulent air that females pass through and evaluate. What is unusual is the fidelity of the column locations. Phorid leks reform each day in approximately the same location, but *approximately* is a meaningful qualifier: they drift with air currents, with temperature gradients, with the distribution of competing males. Here they do not drift. The columns above the sump are in the same positions they occupied yesterday and the position they will occupy tomorrow. They have been in those positions long enough that the rat paths, which the rats optimized for efficiency, route around them, treating the column-positions as obstacles — which they are not to a rat moving under them, except that they are, except that the rats' many-generational learning has encoded the columns as things-that-are-there, features of the district as fixed as the wall or the drainage pipe.

The mechanism by which the column positions are fixed is, probably, this: the tan liquor seeping through specific points in the cinder floor produces a chemical upwelling — not warmth exactly, but a gradient of volatile compounds — that the male phorids use as an anchor. Each generation of males learns the anchor from the generation before, through a form of local enhancement: where males already hover, males land; where males land, males die and leave trace compounds; where trace compounds accumulate, the anchor strengthens. The column is now self-reinforcing. It is as old as the tannery's current configuration, which is thirty years, which is perhaps a thousand phorid generations. It is effectively a monument.

Inside a column, each fly is doing something specific. The male phorids in the hovering position are not just displaying. They are producing a pheromone signal that, in combination with the chemical upwelling from below and the sound vibration that resonates through the air above the sump, creates a composite signal that no female phorid outside this district could possibly parse correctly. The signal is local. It has evolved here, been shaped here, encodes something specific to this place. A female phorid from outside would hover at the column's edge, confused, and leave. The females here do not leave. They have the key.

This means the phorid population of the district is reproductively isolated. Has been for a long time. Is, technically, in the process of becoming something that would require a new name, if anyone were naming it, which no one is.

They do not experience the district. They navigate it through chemistry and vibration. They have made themselves into a specific answer to a specific question that the district keeps asking, which is: *what lives here*?

## The Pigeons

The three hundred pigeons on the cold-storage roof are not unusual pigeons. This should be said clearly because everything else here has earned its strangeness, and the pigeons have not. They are *Columba livia*, urban form, the pigeons of every city in the industrialized world, not particularly adapted to this place, not particularly changed by it.

What has changed is not the pigeons but the pattern of them. The colony on the cold-storage roof has been here for perhaps fifty years — pigeons live eight to fifteen years in urban environments, so the current birds are the great-great-grandchildren of the birds who arrived when the cold-storage loading bays were sealed. The colony size has been stable for at least thirty years, which is unusual for a pigeon colony, which should grow or collapse based on food availability, predation pressure, disease. This colony has done neither. It has held at approximately three hundred, which is the number that the roof can accommodate without thermal stress in summer and without catastrophic disease transmission in winter. The colony is exactly as large as it should be, which is a coincidence, or the district's equilibrium exerting pressure on everything within it.

The pigeons leave. This is important — they leave, they return, they move freely through the city. They carry the district with them in their intestinal flora, in the dust on their feathers that is primarily composed of the district's specific chemical seepage and the mat's specific spores, in the fat deposits that record the particular nutritional profile of the tannery's waste. When they die outside the district, they deposit the district in other soils. Whether anything grows from this is not known.

When they return, they return to exactly their spots on the roof. Each bird has a position, which it holds through social pressure and memory. The positions are inherited: a chick raised in a corner inherits, through learned deference and familiarity, a corner position. The map of the roof is biological, carried in behavior, refreshed constantly. It is the district's most mobile record of itself.

Inside them: crop milk for chicks. Grit from the cinder floor, which differs chemically from grit from anywhere else, and which will grind their food differently, and will leach the district's mineral signature into their blood, which is an intimacy. Their hearts run warm in the cool of the district, a difference of perhaps twenty degrees between a pigeon's core temperature and the structural cool that surrounds it. They burn fast, the way birds burn fast. In the district they are the hottest things.

They do not experience the district intellectually, but they experience it. They feel the roof under their feet, the specific roughness of the oxidized corrugated iron. They feel the drum-vibration through their feet — pigeons feel low-frequency vibration acutely, use it for navigation, feel distant storms and traffic and earthquakes. The drums have been inside them as sensation their whole lives. They cannot distinguish it from the background of the world. It is part of what the world means.

## The Rats

The rats are the district's intelligence. This is not a metaphor.

They are *Rattus norvegicus*, but shaped by forty to sixty generations of life in this specific environment — enough generations for selection to have made small but measurable differences. They are larger than urban rats elsewhere, which is a function of the cold: the structural cool of the district selects for mass, since larger bodies lose heat more slowly. Their fur is slightly denser, slightly more insulating. Their livers are enlarged and process sulfur compounds more efficiently than the livers of rats from adjacent areas, which is necessary given what the ground here contains. These are adaptations. They took time and death to accumulate. The rats that could not process the sulfur compounds left their genes in the soil instead of in descendants, and the current population is what remained: distilled, specific, a rat that is partly made of this place.

Their organization around the sump is what the description calls something between territorial and communal, something without a ready word. The word might be *functional*. The positions held by rats at the sump's edge are not dominance positions — the largest rats are not there, the most aggressive rats are not there. The rats at the edge are the middle-ranking animals, socially unremarkable, and they are there for hours because the sump edge is where the chemical upwelling is strongest, and they are monitoring it. Not consciously monitoring. The way a rat monitors anything: through its nose, its whiskers, the constant interpretation of chemical gradients that the rat nervous system performs automatically and continuously, producing behavioral outputs that look like decision-making from outside but are not, quite.

What are they monitoring for? Change. They have evolved — or learned, across generations of behavioral transmission — that the sump predicts events in the district. A shift in the sump's chemistry precedes a change in the tan liquor's chemical composition, which precedes a change in what the drums release into the air of the tannery building, which changes what is available in the building, which changes where food is. The sump readers sit at the edge and the chemical information passes through them and is transmitted to the colony's behavior through mechanisms of rat communication that are complex and understudied: ultrasonic calls, scent marking, the particular quality of a rat's movement when it returns from the sump-edge to the colony's resting places. The colony responds. It adjusts its foraging pattern before the change in the tannery has fully propagated. It is predictive. It runs slightly ahead of the district's own processes.

The rat paths are what's left of this, visible at human scale. Each groove in the cinder is thousands of individual transits, compacted. Each path connects something to something else: sump to wall-base, because the wall-base is where moisture accumulates and where the mat is, and where the mat is, there is microbial life, and where microbial life is concentrated there are other small things, and the rats know this. Sump to tannery door, because the tannery door is the entry to the drums, and the drums are — the drums are where they eat.

Inside the tannery, in the dim sodium light, among the rotating drums: the rats move through a landscape of smell so rich and specific that it is probably closer to how humans experience music than how humans experience smell. The tan liquor, the hides, the particular fungi that have colonized the wooden staves of the drum housings, the carbon of the overhead cables, the urine of previous rats in previous years soaked into the concrete floor and preserved there — these are not smells to the rats. They are information. They are the district experienced fully. The rats live in a version of this place of overwhelming specificity, a version that no human can access.

They have young. The young are born in nests in the wall voids, in cavities between the original brick and the additional brick laid against it when the first brick began to lean. The walls are full of them — the rats did not make the cavities, the maintenance decisions made the cavities, but the rats have been living in them since the cavities appeared and the cavities are now, functionally, rat infrastructure that the wall built by accident. The young are born blind and hairless and are the only thing in the district that changes rapidly, grows visibly, does not already know where it is. They learn the drum-sound before they can see. They learn the sump-smell before they have navigated to the sump. When they first make their way along the established paths, they are navigating partly by smell and partly by a kind of cultural transmission: the path is the path because every rat before them has walked it, and this accumulated walking is legible in the path itself, and the path guides the ones who come after.

They are the district's memory in motion. When a rat dies at the sump-edge, it is eaten by other rats, which is practical, and the chemistry of this specific place passes through another body and returns to the ground. Nothing is lost. Everything cycles.

## The People

There are people here. The description is careful about them — they are absent from view, their presence is inferred from the drums running — and this care is appropriate, because the people here are present in the way the district's history is present: structurally, as force, not easily observed directly.

The tannery is run by a family. Has been for three generations, which is longer than the rat colony and shorter than the biofilm, and longer than the pigeons and perhaps as long as the phorid columns, and in this sense the family is of average tenure in the district. The current generation is two brothers and a sister who maintain the machinery, maintain the process, do not maintain much else. They know the drums. They know what twelve rotations per minute sounds like at full load and what it sounds like when a load has gone wrong, and they respond to this information the way the sump-readers respond to chemical gradients: automatically, correctly, through accumulated pattern recognition that is experienced as knowing without knowing how.

They do not live here. They arrive before the light changes — not before dawn, since the district does not have a dawn, has only the sodium light which does not change — and they leave in the evening, and in their absence the district runs itself, which is to say the drums run, which is to say the drums run because the brothers set them before leaving and they will stop if something fails, but nothing has failed in a long time, the machinery has reached the district's own equilibrium.

They are large, the brothers. Shaped by the work the way the rats are shaped by the cold and the sulfur: more of them than average in the shoulders, less of them than average in the hands, the specific depletion and thickening that comes from decades of particular labor. Their skin carries something from the hides, some faint tanning quality, as if the place is curing them slowly. The sister works the administrative side, which used to mean a paper ledger and now means a computer that is not connected to the internet, because she does not see the need, because the district does not need the internet, because everything the tannery requires comes to it by truck and leaves by truck and the exchange of value happens through a bank that she calls on a telephone.

They speak to each other in shorthand. This is what language becomes in long-shared labor: the referent is dropped because the referent is understood, and what remains is pure predicate. *Again* means the #7 drum is making the bad sound again. *Yesterday* means what happened yesterday is relevant to what is happening now. *Fine* means the district is in its state, which is fine.

They are not unaware of the rats. They have made a practical arrangement with the rats that involves not attempting to reduce the rat population, because the attempt has been made twice and failed twice, and not because the rats were clever about the poison but because the district was, or seemed to be: the poison worked, the population dropped, and then the sulfur compound levels in the air rose sharply, because the rats had been metabolizing something that the district produced, and without the rats the something accumulated, and the accumulated something corroded the drum bearings, and the repair cost more than the poison cost, and after this the brothers and the rats had a different understanding, which is that they are both part of the process here and that neither benefits from the other's absence.

The sister knows about the crust on the wall. She noticed it years ago, in the way she notices the district — without curiosity, with the watchfulness of someone who reads change as potential problem. The crust has not changed. She has noted this. She does not have a theory about what the crust is. She has a working model: it is there, it does not affect operations, it is filed. If it changed she would file the change. It has not changed.

None of them has noticed the phorid columns. They move through them without registering them as things to be registered, the way you do not register the specific smell of your house. The columns are inside their threshold of attention, below the frequency of things that become thoughts. This is not incuriosity. This is how you survive in a place that is full of sensory information: you build a model of what is normal and you track delta, and the columns are normal and have always been normal and will be normal and are therefore not information.

What they experience, if you asked them and they had the words, which they don't, not precisely, is that the district is *right*. Not morally. Technically. The way a machine is right when it is running correctly. The sound is right. The smell is right. The temperature is right. On the days when the district is right — which is most days, which is almost all days — there is a satisfaction in this that is not the satisfaction of achievement, because nothing has been achieved, but the satisfaction of continuation. Everything continuing. Everything in its place.

They do not know they are part of the equilibrium. They are part of the equilibrium.

## What Lives Here

What lives here is not a collection of species. It is a state. The biofilm metabolizes the wall and the wall metabolizes the history and the rats metabolize the sump and the phorids metabolize their own coordinates and the pigeons metabolize the district's chemistry into warmth and flight and the brothers and the sister metabolize the work into the continued turning of drums, and the drums metabolize the hides that come in by truck and turn them into leather that leaves by truck and return to the district what they return: the tan liquor, the heat of friction, the low rhythmic tumbling that fills the space to its edges.

Everything here eats the district and becomes more of it. This is what living in a place means, taken seriously. This is what happens when a place has been inhabited long enough, and in a stable enough way, by enough different kinds of living: the living becomes load-bearing. Remove it and the district changes. Remove the rats and the drums corrode. Remove the drums and the phorid columns lose their anchor chemistry and drift and dissolve over a season. Remove the phorids and the mat loses the trace nutrients their bodies deposit and the mat's boundary begins to change, and the wall beneath it, no longer stabilized by the mat's chemistry, resumes the slow lean it has been pausing for decades. Remove the mat and the wall falls, eventually — not dramatically, not all at once, but the direction of travel is established.

It is stable. It is interlocking. It has arrived at itself through the ordinary processes of time and selection and accident and the specific history of this specific ground.

What lives here is not waiting. What lives here is not becoming. What lives here has become.

It lives in the cool. In the particular damp. In the sodium light that does not respond to the sun. In the sound, which is the sound of twelve drums and three hundred pigeons and hundreds of rats and two columns of hovering flies and the condensation traveling its established paths and the drip-stain building its carbonate organ pipes, season by season, in the dark, with great patience, toward a completion that does not arrive because completion is not the point.

The point is this: the continuing. The being-here. The metabolism of everything by everything else, producing: more of this. More of exactly this.
