# Who Lives in the Vertical Quarter

---

They are compact people, most of them.

Not universally—there are exceptions, there are always exceptions—but the proportion runs higher here than in open-country places, the tendency toward a kind of gathered physical economy: not small so much as *close-fitting*, as though the body has understood the terms of the space it inhabits and agreed to them. Broad through the shoulder, shorter in the reach, with a density to their movement that is different from the economy of people who work in open places. Those people's economy comes from conserving energy across distances. The economy here is about negotiating volume—about taking up exactly as much space as you need and no more, about moving through narrowed passages and crowded workfloors without bumping, without waste, without the kind of territorial broadcasting of the body that wider spaces encourage.

You notice this most in the upper tiers. Watching two workers cross a cable bridge from opposite directions, meeting at the narrowest point, the brief sideways turn and pass—it is practiced without being performed. They don't look at each other as they negotiate. Their bodies have done this a thousand times and handle it below the level of attention.

---

The skin does something specific when it does not see much sun.

Over generations, over however many generations this place has been continuously inhabited—and it has been many, this much is legible in the structure itself, in the way the lower tiers show so many hands and the upper tiers show so many fewer, meaning the place has been contracting upward over time, the lower levels sealed or repurposed, the living concentrated higher—over all that time, and over whatever came before in whatever harder, closer-to-sky places these bloodlines moved through: the skin has arrived at a quality that is harder to describe than *pale*. Pale implies an absence. This is not absence. It is an adaptation, a refinement, a turning up of gain on whatever light is available. The eyes have done something similar: irises of very light colors—grey, a washed amber, the uncertain color of certain mineral specimens held to low light—with pupils that dilate widely and quickly and are slow to contract. In the lantern-lit tiers, many of the faces look like they are perpetually in a state of gentle surprise, the eyes too open, taking in everything.

The effect is not vulnerability. The effect is attention.

---

They know each other by sound before sight.

The codes of the lantern-signals are learned in childhood, yes, but so is the more fundamental literacy of this place: the learning of voices at distance, of gaits by their vibration through floors, of coughs and work rhythms and the specific way a particular person handles certain tools. A child in the upper tiers knows the sound of the family's particular workshop before it can read, recognizes the signature rhythm of a relative's hammer, knows which approach of footsteps on the walkway outside means stop playing and which means nothing unusual. This is not mysticism. It is the natural development of sensory literacy in conditions that demand it.

The result in adults is a form of peripheral awareness that people who live here cannot fully describe to people who don't. Not exactly hearing—broader than hearing. A continuous ambient processing of the state of the place, reading the foundry rhythm for changes in tempo, feeling the cable-winch's vibration and knowing from its pattern what is being moved and in what urgency, registering the change in the mountain's small sounds that means moisture somewhere is shifting. They don't think of this as a skill. They think of it as knowing what time it is, knowing how the day is going.

When something is wrong—when a crack has moved, when a machine sounds different, when the pressure from the lower vents has changed—they know before they know. The feeling is not fear. It is more like a change in weather pressure: you become slightly more present to the room you're in.

---

They sleep in shifts and they sleep heavily.

The shortage of space means that sleeping arrangements are time-shared in many households: a hammock or sleeping platform serves two or three people whose work schedules offset, so that the arrangement is rarely occupied by more than one simultaneously and is rarely empty. This is not considered deprivation. It is simply how things are arranged. The hammock conforms to the body's weight, and people who have slept in hammocks all their lives carry their backs differently—there is a particular way the spine has learned to curve, a comfort with spinal flexibility, a residual sway to the walk that is almost imperceptible but marks the Quarter-born to other Quarter-born who know what to look for.

The sleep is deep because the work is taxing and the air is cool and the body earns it. Dreams here have the quality of place: enclosed, textured, lit from multiple angles by lights at different heights. People describe their dreams as architecturally complex. Not labyrinthine—they are not lost in these dreams—but layered, with a familiarity to the layout that does not resolve to any single real place but carries the aesthetic of this one. Narrow passages that widen. Cold surfaces. The feeling of the rock being close without being threatening. They wake in what passes for morning—a change in generator rhythm, an agreed-upon signal in the lanterns—and their return to consciousness is rapid. They are not people who emerge slowly from sleep. They arrive in the day fully, and begin.

---

The hands.

Everyone who works here has hands that are, over time, distinguished by their particular form of damage and development. This is not unique to this place, but what is particular here is the variety within a small space: the foundry workers carry burn scar tissue on the backs of the hands and forearms, old burns and recent burns layered, the skin textured and thickened; the cable crew carry rope-callus on the palms in precise locations corresponding to grip; the maintenance corps carry something more subtle, the hands of people who touch things carefully and constantly, a fineness to the fingertip sensitivity that is difficult to describe but visible in the way they handle instruments, the way they will close their eyes while pressing a palm against a vibrating pipe, reading it. The engineers and ledger-keepers have ink somewhere; it never fully washes out of certain skin creases. The children have small hands beginning their education toward whatever their hands will eventually know.

There is a culture around hands—around what they can tell you, around trusting them. *Listen with your hands* is a phrase the maintenance corps uses that has spread into common speech and widened in meaning: to give full attention to something, to let the body receive information rather than just the mind. When a senior maintenance worker places a hand against a joint in the structure and goes still, they are not performing. They are reading. And people around them go quiet, and wait for the verdict, because the hands have been right before and wrong less often.

---

The children are underfoot.

Not neglectfully—the place is too dense with hazard for children to be unsupervised in any serious sense—but they are present in the work, present in the margins of the work, learning by proximity. There is not a formal school in the usual sense; there is a tier set aside for purposes that include education, and teachers who work there, but much of what a child in the Quarter learns is learned where the learning is: at the foundry's edge, watching metal pour; at the cable-point, learning the light-codes; in the maintenance corps' off-hours rounds, following a grandparent or an older sibling with a lamp and a notebook. The education is dense because the knowledge is specific. You need to know this, and this, and this, because the place requires it. There is less that is taught for its own sake and more that is taught because it is needed.

The children are curious in a directed way: not the sprawling undifferentiated curiosity of children with wide horizons, but a curiosity that goes deep quickly, that learns a thing and immediately asks *how does it connect to the other things*. The geometry of the place has taught them that everything connects to something else, that there are no isolated systems, that the pipe turns because of the jut of rock and the jut of rock is because of the fault and the fault is part of why the cavity formed, and the cavity is why there is a home. They ask questions with the assumption that the answer will matter. Adults here answer questions from children with seriousness, because the children are going to work here, and they should know.

---

They have a specific relationship with the rock that is not religious but is not entirely not religious.

There is no formal theology of the mountain. There is no priesthood, no ceremony centered on stone. But there are practices that have the structural features of relation: you do not strike unexpectedly at a wall face without sound first, a particular rhythmic knock that—if it is never answered, if the rock does not send back a different vibration warning of hidden instability—grants a kind of permission. You do not seal a new crack without documenting what it sounds like, because a crack has a voice and if it changes the change means something. When a section of structure must be removed, the removal is preceded by a period of time—variable, determined by the engineers but felt by everyone in that vicinity—in which the section is left in place though no longer used, as though the structure is given notice.

These are practical habits that have accreted meaning. The knock before striking was originally a check for hollow space. The documentation of crack-sounds is engineering. The period before removal is a buffer allowing final inspection. None of this is myth. But ask someone why they knock, and they will say *because it's right*, and mean something by that, and be unable to fully articulate what, and not feel the need to.

The mountain is a presence. Not a god, not a spirit in any formal sense. A presence: a very large, very slow something that they are inside of and that continues to become itself around them, and with which they have an arrangement. The arrangement is practical and it is also—in the way all genuine arrangements between parties of unequal scale eventually become—something like respect.

---

There is a social hierarchy and it runs in a direction that surprises visitors from open-country places.

Down is older. Down is history. The lower tiers—even the sealed ones, even the ones no longer actively used—carry the weight of having been here longest, and the families who can trace their habitation to the lower levels have a form of social authority that is real even though they do not currently live there. Up is newcomer, upper is more recent, upper is also, in certain configurations, more precarious: the cable-hung structures at the top, the thinnest materials, the widest variance in temperature, the most dependence on machines that someone else controls. The highest tiers are not prestigious. They are where people live who have not yet negotiated their way to a more stable position, or who prefer the particular quality of life up there—the view of the winged pale things in the thermal columns, the different acoustics, a kind of relative privacy in the increased difficulty of access—to the greater solidity below.

The maintenance corps cuts across this hierarchy in the way that necessary people always do. They are down in the lower structures when the lower structures need them. They are up in the highest tiers at odd hours. They know the whole of the vertical extent. In a place where most people live and work within a few tiers, within a social geography whose bounds are half a dozen levels in each direction, the maintenance corps is anomalously everywhere. This gives them a knowledge-authority that is separate from the hereditary kind, and is in some tension with it, and this tension is very old and has never been fully resolved and everyone knows it and they work around it with a practiced social finesse.

---

What they value, stated and unstated:

*Stated:* reliability. The person who says they will do a thing and does it. This is a community value, actively reinforced, enforced in small social penalties against those who are careless with commitments. In a system where one person's failure to maintain a joint means someone else's floor fails, this is not an abstract preference. It is survival knowledge that has been socialized into virtue.

*Stated:* knowledge of the place. This is status—not merely knowing the light-codes or the maintenance schedule, but knowing why things are as they are, what earlier iteration they replaced, what the rock is doing in that section, what the foundry contract history means for the current workload. The people who know the most about the structure and its history are not simply respected: they are listened to in a way that has a quality of need to it. The Quarter is old enough and complex enough that no single person knows all of it, and the partial knowledges of many people interlock. Losing an elder is not only grief; it is a gap in the record.

*Unstated, and therefore most governing:* the assumption of continuance. No one says it. No one needs to. The practices of the place are organized around a premise so deep it has never required articulation: that this place will continue, that the rock will be navigated and the water will be collected and the machines will be maintained and the children will learn and the work will go on. This is not optimism. It is not the enthusiasm of expansion. It is the far more durable thing: the understanding that the place was here before, that it will be here after, that the individual life is one layer in a geological accumulation of lives, and that the work of one's own layer is to be the kind of layer the ones above can build on.

Visitors sometimes interpret this as a lack of ambition. It is not. It is an ambition so long in its horizon that it doesn't register as ambition to people whose horizons are shorter.

---

They do not talk much about what is above the cavity.

There are people here who have been to the exterior—who know the cold of open places, the brightness of sky, the particular quality of wind without walls—but they are not the majority, and those who have been there have usually returned. Not because the outside is hostile, or forbidden, or frightening, but because there is something about returning from the open to the enclosed that is, for people who grew up here, a settling. The Quarter is the shape that fits the self. The outside is too large in a way that is not threatening but is—tiring. The eye works differently out there. The ear does not know what to do with so much unstructured space. The body casts only one shadow.

There are children who have not yet been to the surface, who know the exterior only through descriptions and the occasional object brought in: a feather, an unusual mineral, weather reports traded back through the passage-sledge workers, who are the quarter's most reliable cartographers of what things look like when there is sky. These children imagine the outside with great fidelity to certain details—they know it is cold, they know it is bright, they know the horizon exists and is not a rock face—and great strangeness in others. They cannot imagine, quite, what it would feel like to look up and find not a ceiling but more space. The upper thermal columns and the pale circling wings are the closest they have come to sky, and they have made of this whatever they need it to be.

Some of them want to go. Some of them are curious about it in the directed, deep way that Quarter children are curious about things. Some of them will, in time, go up through the passages with the sledge-workers and emerge into the light and stand there for a while, eyes too dilated for the sun, hands knowing too much about surfaces to find comfort in open air—

and some of those will stay.

and most will come back down, into the cold and the dark and the overlapping shadows and the sound of the mountain settling, and they will feel themselves returned to the shape of themselves, and they will go back to work, and the lantern-signals will flicker, and the foundry will beat its slow rhythm, and the pale wings will circle in the upper thermal dark, and none of this will require comment.

It is simply how things are.
