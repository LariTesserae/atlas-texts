# What Lives in the Canopy City

---

Begin with what isn't there.

No eyes that you can see. No motion you can catch. No sound that answers to the category of *animal sound* — no call, no rustle that suggests departure, no alarm. The first and most important thing about what lives here is that it does not perform its living for you. It has no interest in being perceived. It has been here longer than perception was something that happened near it, and it will be here after, and in the time between — this time, your time, the brief warm interruption of you — it simply continues. You are a weather event. A warm front. Temporary.

But let's not let the plants have all of it. They are living, yes, and in such density and complexity that *living* becomes an insufficient word for what they're doing — they are *building*, they are *doing geology*, they are making soil out of themselves and then making more of themselves out of the soil, and all of that is life in a form so patient and structural that it barely resembles what we usually mean by the word. The plants are the city. But the city is also inhabited. What lives here lives *in* it, in the specific sense of using structure that was made by someone else's effort.

---

## The Moss Itself

Start here, because you must.

The moss is not background. The moss is not the stage on which life happens. The moss *is* a form of life as complicated as anything else in this place, and it has interiority — not consciousness, but something analogous in function, a responsiveness, a record. Press your finger into the moss layer and you are pressing into decades. The bottom of what your finger enters was alive forty years ago and is not alive now, but it is still *doing something* — it is holding water, holding structure, holding the chemical memory of every organism that died into it. The moss is a library written in cellulose and mineral accumulation, and what lives in it includes the moss's own history.

The growing tips are where the present tense is. Each shoot — and there are millions of them in half a hectare, tens of millions, a number that ceases to mean anything — is a separate individual extending into available space, testing the light, adjusting its geometry toward what's there. A single cushion moss colony is one organism and also thousands of organisms and also an argument about the relationship between individuality and collective that it will never resolve because it sees no need to. It simply grows. The growing is the answer.

What the moss *wants*, if want is permitted: water, which it has; low light, which it has; surface to cling to, which is being continuously manufactured by its own death. It wants nothing it doesn't have. This is rare, anywhere. It is the defining quality of the moss as a life-form in this specific place: it is here because here is exactly what it needs, and it is making here more here as it goes.

There is something inside the moss that the moss does not know about. The moss doesn't know about anything. But inside the compressed lower strata, in the dark between matted stems, in spaces the size of sand grains and smaller: a civilization of animals so small they have no common names, or have names only specialists use, or have not yet been named because no specialist has pressed their face this close to this particular square centimeter. Tardigrades. Rotifers. Nematodes beyond counting. These are organisms that have survived — not in this place, but in principle — boiling, desiccation, the vacuum of space, centuries of being a dormant spore. They are functionally immortal under the right conditions, which these are. They eat bacteria. They eat each other. They are eaten by things not much larger than themselves. They are the bottom of a food web so compressed that every link is a fraction of a millimeter long.

They do not experience the moss as large. They do not experience anything as large. The moss for them is a world the size of continents, with weather and geography and catastrophe. A droplet migrating down through the strata is a flood. A root hair pushing through is a geological event. They live their whole lives in what you would call a cubic centimeter, and those lives contain, in proportion, what lives contain.

## The Invertebrate Substrate

Now slightly larger.

In the channels — the organized waterways the moss has shaped, always-wet and darker than everything around them — there are things you cannot see without kneeling and looking very carefully, and even then you will miss most of them. Stonefly larvae, perhaps, if the water is cold and oxygen-rich, which it is. They are brown and flat and segmented, moving slowly over the channel floor in their preference for moving slowly, in their evolutionary selection for not being seen. They breathe through gills that are almost invisible even when you know where they are. They eat algae, mostly, the thin films of single-celled life that coat the wet stone beneath the water. In the channel, the stone shows: one of the few places where the covering project has not yet achieved coverage, where the surface is still mineral and exposed, though even here it is colonized in sheets too thin to see.

The stonefly larva will live here for a year or two or three. This is years of its life. Then it will emerge, transform, find its brief winged adult form, mate in the cold air above the ledge, and die. The body will become food. The eggs, laid on the water surface, will sink and become the next generation. The next generation will not know any of this. They will know the channel, the wet stone, the algae film. They will know cold water. This will be enough. This is what it is to be a stonefly larva: cold water is enough, if the cold water is good.

In the moss — not the channels but the body of the moss, the upper living strata — there are springtails. They are there in numbers that would be alarming if they were larger, if they were visible as individuals, if your eye could resolve them from the background they match perfectly. They are a millimeter long, some of them, up to perhaps three in the larger species. They are white or translucent or the pale green of young moss tips, and they move with a sudden spasmodic hop when disturbed, which is the origin of their name: a forked appendage under the abdomen, spring-loaded, that fires and throws them into the air when something touches them.

They are disturbed constantly. Everything in this place is touching everything else. The springtails must be in a state of near-continuous accidental launch, tiny creatures rocketing through the fern layer and landing on a frond and walking back down toward the moss, where they eat. They eat fungal spores, algal cells, dead organic matter. They are the sanitation department. They process the enormous quantity of dying material that the moss produces, that the liverworts produce, that the ferns shed, and they pass it back as something more available to the bacteria, and the bacteria make it available to the plants, and the plants make more material to die. The springtails are in the loop. They are part of the mechanism of the place.

There are mites. There are always mites. They are the most numerous animals in terrestrial ecosystems by density, and this is a dense ecosystem, so the mites here are beyond what numbers can convey. Hunter mites eat the springtails. Detritivore mites eat the same things the springtails eat, in competition with the springtails. Some mites are parasitic on other mites. The mite community in a single square meter of this place would require, if you tried to describe it fully, more time than the place has existed. Some fraction of them are species with no scientific names. Some fraction may be species that exist only here, on this ledge, having speciated quietly in the isolation of high elevation since the ledge stabilized. They will never be described. They will not be missed in any way they would understand.

The spider you can almost see. The one at her sheet web between dwarf birch branches, the one the color of wet bark. She is large enough — four millimeters, perhaps five — to have something like presence in this place. You can see her abdomen, which has a pattern: pale and dark banded, the pattern that is common across many families because it works, because it breaks up the silhouette against the dappled background of fronds and bark. She is motionless. This is not patience in the sense that implies waiting is a cost. For her, motionless is default. Motion is the exception, expensive, saved for what requires it.

Her web is doing the work. It hangs between the branches at the height where midges fly, or slightly below, where the smaller flies travel, and it is a passive trap, a sheet of silk with threads above it to deflect flying things downward into the sheet, and the sheet doesn't stick — it's not sticky silk, not a spiral orb — but it's irregular enough that an insect landing on it can't get traction, tumbles, slides toward the center, and that's when she comes.

She comes very fast when she comes.

The web, in the hours before a midge arrives, scintillates with droplets. She is patient with the droplets in the sense that they are not her problem. They make the web more visible, perhaps, but there are many webs in the fern layer, many web-builders, and the things they catch are here in numbers sufficient for all of them. This place has never had a shortage of small dying things. This is one of the things it offers.

## The Things Inside the Things

The fungi are here but not visible as fungi. Fungi do not present themselves here the way they do in a forest — no brackets, no caps, no visible fruiting bodies in this season, in this cold. What the fungi are is thread. What the fungi are is *relationship*: miles of hyphal thread in every cubic meter of this place, penetrating every surface, wrapped around root hairs, passing through the moss layer, connecting root system to root system in the underground network that is the most important thing happening here that cannot be seen.

The dwarf birches are connected to each other through fungal intermediary. This is established ecology, not speculation: the mycorrhizal networks of birch and other pioneer species at elevation form genuine communication and resource-sharing webs, and the birches on this ledge — packed together, root systems braided — have been sharing through this network long enough that the network is an organ, is a nervous system of slow chemical signal, is the reason why the birches as a collective respond to insect pressure or mineral shortage before any individual birch would have time to respond alone.

There are fungi that eat only dead moss. There are fungi that eat only the dead fungi that eat only dead moss. There are fungi living on the liverworts that may be benefiting the liverworts in ways not yet characterized, may be parasitizing them, may be doing both to different individuals of the same liverwort species at the same time and the difference may depend on a factor as small as a mineral concentration differential in adjacent cells. The fungi negotiate constantly, have been negotiating since before anything on this ledge had leaves. They predate everything else here. They will outlast most of it.

The bacteria are too small for even this register of attention. They are the base state of life, the condition of the soil, the workers in the cycling of everything. They are in the water that moves through the channels and in the water that moves through the tissue of every living plant and in the water that is condensing on your wrist. They are breathing. They are the reason the place doesn't simply accumulate dead material until nothing can grow. They are very old. All bacteria alive today are approximately four billion years old and also approximately twenty minutes old, both, because they do not age in the way organisms that have sex age. They divide. The bacteria on this ledge have been on this ledge since something carried them here, and they have been alive since the beginning of life, and they have never been anything but themselves.

## The Wren

The bird that passed through — call it what it is. A wren, almost certainly. Possibly not the common wren but something close, at this altitude something that has learned to be slightly smaller, slightly denser, slightly more reluctant to fly when it could walk. It came in along the base of the fern layer, below the lowest fronds, in the space that exists between the moss surface and the frond cover — a space the size of a hand's width, but a hand's width that extends for meters in every direction, a whole horizontal landscape at ankle height that the wren navigates as if it were a forest.

The wren finds things in the crevices of the moss. Things too small to specify — fly larvae, spider egg cases, the occasional springtail or mite that has come near the surface. It finds them by sound, by the absence of sound, by the quality of a particular silence in a particular patch of moss that differs slightly from the silence in adjacent patches because something small is trying to be very still underneath. The wren's auditory resolution is fine enough for this. The wren's bill is fine enough for this, narrow and slightly curved, designed — *arrived at*, let's say, through the design-without-designer of selection — for extracting small things from narrow spaces.

It does not live here. This matters. The wren has a territory somewhere below, in heavier cover, where the temperatures permit the overwintering of the insects the wren depends on. It comes here in the season when things are active in the moss, in the productive cold months, and it works the place like a harvester working a field — systematically, efficiently, with full understanding of where the density is and where it has already been. It leaves when it has taken enough.

The wren does not sing here. There is no male territory to defend on this ledge, no mate to court, no purpose for song. It moves in silence except for the occasional sharp alarm note — not alarm now, nothing alarmed it this visit, but the alarm note lives ready in it, always ready, because the wren is a small warm thing in a cold world and small warm things in cold worlds have learned to keep their alarms close.

After it leaves, the moss begins to reprocess what the wren did to it. Compressed fronds of moss slowly re-erect. The disrupted surface of a channel, where the wren stepped, stills back to its usual flow. In a few hours there will be no sign. In a few days, what the wren left behind — what passes through a wren, what a wren deposits — will be incorporated into the moss layer, will become substrate, will become the feeding medium for the organisms the wren came to eat.

The wren makes a contribution. The wren does not know this.

## The Things That Have Always Been Here

The midges above the channels — they are not passing through. This population, or a population indistinguishable from it in every way that matters, has been above these channels since the channels formed. They don't travel far. They don't have reason to. Their larval stage is in the wet moss of the channel margins, in the film of water over the stones, and their adult stage is the brief flying phase during which they mate, and the mating swarms are here, over the channels, because that is where the adults emerge and they do not fly far from emergence and the swarms have memory only in the sense that emerging adults orient to swarms and swarms are always here.

They are eaten by the wren when the wren visits. They are eaten by the sheet-web spider when they drift low enough. They are eaten by each other's larvae in the channel margins, the larger eating the smaller, the whole midge population cycling through itself. For every midge taken by a vertebrate predator, hundreds die in the moss. This is the math of small things: most of the dying is invisible, is internal to a community that processes its own dead before anything larger can notice.

The midges don't land on you specifically. They don't have the biting habit of their relatives, or if they do it's not you they want — your chemistry isn't right, or you're too warm, or the vibration of your breathing deflects them. They move around you. You are an obstacle in the air, a thermal anomaly, something the swarm adjusts to the way water adjusts to a stone. You are not food to them. You are not anything to them. You are briefly *present*, and then you are less present, and then the swarm has redistributed and you are gone from their pattern.

This is the predominant relationship between what lives here and what visits here: adjustment, redistribution, the small non-event of a large thing passing through a world organized at a different scale.

## The Slow Ones

Return to the bottom of the moss layer. Return to the zone of compression, the dark, the near-peat, where the oldest parts of this place are still slowly integrating into earth. Here, time runs differently. Not metaphorically: the organisms that live here are operating on generational timescales measured in months or years, in an environment where organic matter breaks down slowly, where chemical signals persist, where the chemical signals from organisms dead for decades are still present in detectable concentrations.

The nematodes in the deep moss are responding to signals from a community that no longer exists as individuals, only as residue. They are orienting to the traces of the dead. Their behavior is shaped by ghosts — not metaphorically, but chemically: pheromone gradients, the attractants and repellents left by organisms whose bodies have been incorporated into the substrate. The nematode community navigates by a map that keeps being updated with information from before.

This is not unusual, here or elsewhere. The deep ecology of soil systems is always partly a conversation with the dead. What is unusual here — what the specificity of this place creates — is the *depth* of it. The ledge is young, geologically, but the moss layer has been accumulating for long enough that the conversation goes back further than it can go in most places at this altitude. The nematodes at the base of the moss are in contact, through that continuous gradient of chemical signal, with organisms that were alive when the first pioneer mosses were laying down the first centimeters of this city. They carry, in their navigational responses, an inheritance that goes back to the founding of the place.

They do not know this. They navigate.

## The Ones You Don't Know Are There

Somewhere in the mid-strata of the fern layer, in a space defined by three overlapping fronds of hart's tongue, there is something whose name I don't know.

Not a species without a name — or possibly a species without a name, but that's not what I mean. I mean something I haven't come to yet, something the description hasn't found a way to reach. A small thing using a small space in a way particular to itself. Something whose life is organized around a feature of this place so specific — the angle of moisture accumulation on a particular class of frond, or the temperature differential between the inner and outer surface of the moss at a particular depth, or the chemical profile of a particular liverwort species, or something invisible to me entirely — that describing it requires first understanding what it knows, and what it knows is local, is intimate, is the knowledge that comes from living so completely inside a place that the place and the life become difficult to distinguish.

What is it? Small. Darker than you'd think, darker than it needs to be for concealment in the greens, suggesting a history of living in light conditions darker than these, a history that predates this ledge, that was carried here from elsewhere, from a lower place or a wetter place or a place now gone. Slow-moving, in the manner of things that have learned not to be worth chasing. Feeding on something that is abundant — it must be abundant, the place is too remote and too small to support a specialist on something rare.

It has been here longer than the ledge has been colonized. That is impossible, but it is nearly true: a population of something arrived early, found the conditions good, and has been here since the beginning of here. It adapted to the place as the place was forming, was involved in the forming of the place the way all organisms are involved in their habitats — changing the local humidity, changing the decomposition rates, changing the pressure on the moss in small and specific ways that the moss responded to. The moss is different, in the areas this thing frequents, from the moss elsewhere. The difference is too small to measure, but it is there.

This is the deepest fact about what lives in the Canopy City: the distinction between *inhabitant* and *place* is a visitor's distinction. No one inside the city makes it. Everything here is habitat for everything else. Everything here is simultaneously the organism and the environment, the shaper and the shaped, the city and the citizen. The moss is real estate and it is alive and it is an animal's shelter and it is a plant's competitor and it is a fungus's medium and it is a dead organism and it is a living organism and it is soil-in-progress and it is the product of thousands of generations of organisms that died into it.

What lives here is not a list.

What lives here is a single continuous process that has been running on this ledge for long enough to have produced a city, and the city is its body, and the body is the city, and the cold is inside both.

---

You leave. Your warmth goes with you.

The water you contributed to — the mist that condensed on your sleeves and was briefly part of your thermal cycle before it evaporated again — rejoins the general movement of water through the place. In a few hours it will be in the moss. In a day or two it will be in a plant. Eventually, perhaps, it will be in a midge, will be carried aloft in the brief winged phase, will be part of something's flight, will evaporate at a height where it joins the cloud layer that sits above this place and produces, the next morning, more mist, which will condense on a frond tip and become a droplet and fall.

The city does not notice you left. But the water does, in its way, remember the warmth. For a short time the channels run slightly less cold. The moss in the area of your passage releases a fraction of its stored water, triggered by the thermal gradient you created, and the water moves.

Then that too passes.

The place continues.
