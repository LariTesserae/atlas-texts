## Who Lives Here

---

Begin with the ones you almost don't count, because they are so thoroughly of the stone that the distinction feels arbitrary.

The sponges have been here longer than anything else currently visible, and they know it in the way that things without nervous systems know things — which is to say, entirely, bodily, without the possibility of doubt. They have grown into the limestone rather than onto it, infiltrating the matrix of the rock over decades, until what you see protruding is only the visible expression of something that extends much further inward. They are barrel-shaped in the open surfaces, encrusting in the sheltered ones, and they pump water through themselves with a patience that makes patience seem like too lively a word. A sponge here processes the equivalent of its own volume many times each hour, extracting bacteria and dissolved organics from the warm current, contributing filtered water back to the city's general circulation. They are the city's slow breathing. They have opinions about nothing. They are, nonetheless, enormously present.

Under every horizontal surface of sufficient size — the underside of any lintel that has not yet fallen, the roof of any arch, the ceiling of any space where a ceiling survives — something pale and feathery is attached. Crinoids, sea lilies, their name for once accurate: they have the quality of plants arranged by someone who cared about the composition, arms extending in careful radial symmetry, each arm feathered with smaller arms, each of those feathered further, all of it filtering the slow current with aristocratic patience. They are ancient animals wearing an ancient shape, and they give the undersides of the city's remaining architecture a quality of lining, of interior decoration, as though the city has been upholstered from within by something that found the structural bones and decided to make them beautiful in its own terms. When a current shift moves through — the eddy caused by a large fish turning in an open space two blocks away — the crinoids respond together, a slow opening and closing, like a collective breath. They do not think. The response is older than thought.

---

Now the ones with something more like interiority.

The moray eels occupy the city the way a guild occupies a trade. Each crevice of suitable size has been assessed and assigned, and the assessment was completed long before you arrived and will not be revisited on your account. They are distributed through the walls at roughly two-meter intervals where the walls are intact — closer where the collapse has created more opportunity, more sparse along the open-faced structures where cover is minimal. During the day, which is not now but which the city experiences as a different configuration of the same darkness, they rest in their crevices with only the head visible, and the head performs a slow, continuous respiration — mouth opening, closing, opening — that looks like threat but is just breathing, just the necessary work of passing water over gills.

At night, which this is, the morays are more present in the space of the streets. A large one — perhaps a meter and a half, maybe more, the dimensions difficult to assess in the dimness — makes its way along the base of a wall, unhurried, following the wall not because the wall directs it but because the wall is where the crevices are, where the small fish shelter, where the crabs huddle thinking themselves invisible. It moves with the specific grace of something that has no limbs and doesn't miss them, the whole long body expressing a single continuous intention. It investigates gaps by inserting its head and a portion of its neck, holding the position for a moment — smelling, really, the chemical sense of a creature that navigates by tongue-flicker — and then withdrawing and continuing. It is not always successful. It is not always hungry. Success and hunger are two different states and it distinguishes them correctly.

They are not social animals and they know it. The city's population of morays maintains a system of spacing that amounts to a property arrangement, not negotiated but enforced through the simple mechanism of prior residence. A small eel will not attempt to occupy a crevice in which a large eel's scent lingers. The large eel does not need to be there; its chemical presence is sufficient. The city is thus divided into territories that overlap with the city's architecture but are not identical to it, a second map written in a language of pheromone and familiarity that has nothing to do with the original urban planning.

---

The fish that live here full-time — not the passers-through, not the open-water species that drift in from beyond the city's edge, but the residents — have a quality of knowingness about the specific geometry of this place that makes watching them move through it feel like watching someone navigate their own apartment in the dark. The groupers know where the corners are. They know which passages have slightly accelerated current and position themselves in the downstream side of those passages during active feeding, letting the flow bring them something. They know which of the courtyard areas collects sediment-stirred prey when a storm passes overhead — they have learned this through repetition, through the reliable pattern of small prey emerging from hiding in the hours after turbulence. They are not intelligent in the way the word usually means, but they have invested the architecture with meaning through experience, and the investment shows in how they move.

The groupers rest on the sediment floor with the confidence of animals that are not prey in this place, that know themselves to be at or near the top of the relevant hierarchy and have internalized that knowledge into posture. They sit — groupers sit, this is not the wrong word, they perch with their pectoral fins doing the work of arms, holding position with minimal effort — on rubble or bare stone, and they watch. Their eyes move independently and they use this. One eye monitoring the near ground, one eye monitoring middle distance. Both eyes available to swing toward the same target when something interesting consolidates. They watch you with the speculative attention of an animal deciding whether you are large enough to matter, and you are, and so you are filed under *not prey* and *not threat* and *not yet understood* and held there, in that provisional category, for as long as you remain.

The damselfish are smaller and considerably more committed to their positions on the matter. They have territories — patches of algae they farm, genuinely farm, clearing unwanted species and encouraging the kinds they eat, weeding in a sense, with the single-minded attention of gardeners who became gardeners because it was the only thing they could imagine doing. These territories are on the walls, mostly, in the algal-turfed patches at mid-height, and the damselfish defend them against everything: against other damselfish, obviously, but also against urchins that might graze the wrong things, against larger fish that wander through without aggression but are nonetheless large and liable to disturb things simply by being present. The damselfish will rush a grouper thirty times its size to make a point about whose wall this is. The grouper acknowledges this by angling its body fractionally away, not out of fear but out of the minimal social concession that permits the shouting to stop. It's the architecture of a system that works.

---

The octopuses deserve their own consideration because they are, in the city, something different from the other residents. They are *engaged* with it in a way the others are not. The distinction is between inhabiting a space and having a relationship with it.

An octopus settled into its daytime den — the collapsed doorway, the gap between two stones that it has perhaps improved by moving one of the stones, using two arms as levers while the others anchored — is not simply occupying the space. It is evaluating the space, continuously, against the template of what a good den is and how this den measures up and what might be done about the shortfall. It gathers things. Not in the magpie sense, not for beauty, but for function and perhaps for something adjacent to comfort — shells near the entrance that might shift and alert if something approaches, rubble cleared from the interior, occasionally an object of unclear purpose that was investigated and not discarded. The dens become, over time, slightly sculpted. Slightly personalized. There is a word for the accumulation of material around an octopus den: a midden. The word implies archaeology. In the case of the drowned city, the archaeology goes in both directions.

At night the octopus that had been a threshold becomes an animal in motion, and the motion is something to observe carefully if you can track it, which is difficult, because camouflage does not stop at rest. A moving octopus continues to process its visual environment and render its skin accordingly, which means that at the edge of your visual range the octopus is not a shape moving against a background but a moving background, a traveling sample of wherever it has just been. You lose it against the wall and find it against the sediment floor and lose it again where it flows over rubble and then it is simply gone, transitioned into invisibility somewhere in the middle distance, and what you have left is the impression of something that was paying extremely close attention to you the entire time.

They eat what the city provides, and what the city provides is substantial: crabs that shelter in the same crevices the octopus learned to check in its first weeks of life here, fish that can be ambushed from above when the octopus flows off a ledge like dropped cloth, mollusks worked open with a beak that can manage almost anything given sufficient patience. The eating is skilled and the skill is individual, not instinctive — each octopus develops its own techniques through trial and a process that is functionally experiment, though the octopus does not know it is experimenting. It tries things. It notices what works. It tries those things again. This is indistinguishable, from the outside, from learning. From the inside, if there is an inside — and there is, there is considerable evidence for something we might as well call an inside — it may feel like something. It may feel like something.

---

The sea turtles pass through. They are not residents but they are regulars, and the city knows them in the sense that nothing in the city responds to a sea turtle's arrival with alarm. A hawksbill making its way through the streets at depth, navigating the spaces between buildings with a swimmer's broad adjustment of trajectory, is simply part of the traffic. It is here for sponges, primarily — it has a beak designed for exactly that, for gouging into the sponge tissue that the city produces so abundantly — and it moves from one feeding site to the next with the directness of an animal following a mental map that has been refined over years of visiting. It has been coming here since before it was large enough to come alone. When it was smaller, it hugged the reef edges at shallower depth; as it grew, it found the deeper sites, and this city was one of them, and it has incorporated the city into its range with the easy permanence of a comfortable habit.

A turtle resting on the sediment between feeding bouts is almost architecturally still. It sets down with a small puff of displaced sediment and then simply ceases to move, the way only large things can cease to move — completely, with the commitment of something whose thermoregulation doesn't punish stillness. Its eyes remain open and its processing of the visual field does not stop; it is resting but not sleeping, in the way of animals that cannot afford to sleep in the open. It will hold that position for twenty minutes, thirty, before something internal shifts and it lifts from the sediment and returns to the work of the sponges, leaving behind a shallow depression that will persist until the next current event disturbs the floor.

---

And the things that have no name that seems right for them, the things at the edge of category.

Nudibranchs move across the encrusting organisms with the deliberate pace of something that has nowhere urgent to be because it is already where it is going. They are small — a few centimeters, most of them — and they should be invisible, and in practical terms they are invisible to most of what is looking, but they are the most vibrantly, almost aggressively colored things in the city, extravagant oranges and electric blues and crimsoned patterns that exist here in the near-dark where they serve no advertisement function visible to human eyes, and yet they persist, these colors, because the colors are warnings addressed to animals whose color perception extends into wavelengths not visible to us, or because the colors are what you get when you are made of the chemicals you eat, the chromatic accidents of a diet of toxic organisms accumulated in a body that cannot be bothered to hide them. A nudibranch crosses a wall like a sentence in an unknown language, slow and declarative, and arrives at the edge and keeps going over the lip and down the other side without any apparent acknowledgment that it has crossed a threshold, because it has not. For a nudibranch all surfaces are one surface.

The cleaning stations: certain spots in the city where a specific small shrimp has established a service that the city's larger residents use with something that looks remarkably like trust. The shrimp — banded, antennae long and constantly mobile, advertising its presence with a slight swaying dance when a fish approaches — will move across the surfaces of fish many times its size, across the gills, into the mouth, around the eyes, removing parasites and dead tissue with its small precise claws. The fish hold still for this. They adopt postures that expose the areas being cleaned, sometimes changing color slightly in a way that may indicate something like relaxation. The shrimp works methodically. When it is finished, or when it decides to stop, it disengages and the fish moves on, and another fish approaches, and the station continues its business. This has been happening here long enough that the location of the station is known to the city's residents, that fish navigate toward it when they need what it offers. The shrimp has become, in a real sense, an institution.

---

The city has one more resident that is not exactly a resident, not exactly alive in the usual sense.

Bacteria, in quantities that resist imagination, inhabit the warm stone and sediment, the thin biofilm on every surface, the interstitial water in the limestone itself. They have been here continuously since before the city was built — the stone was always bacteria-inhabited, the seabed before the buildings were quarried was bacteria-inhabited, and when the city sank the bacteria adapted to new conditions over generations too fast to count. They are performing the city's chemistry: cycling nitrogen, cycling sulfur, breaking down the organic matter that falls and drifts through the water column and settles, converting it into forms that the sponges and the worms and the small crustaceans can use. They are the city's metabolism. Without them, the city would be chemically dead within a timeframe so short it would seem catastrophic. With them — which is always — the city processes its own mortality continuously, recycling what dies into what can live, maintaining the conditions that make the fish and the octopuses and the slow pale crinoids possible.

The bacteria do not know they are doing this. There is no knowing here, only chemistry. But the chemistry is vast and ancient and competent and it runs underneath everything else in the city the way a foundation runs underneath a building, invisible and structural and utterly without interest in being noticed.

---

What the city holds, then, is not simply a collection of species but a collection of *relationships* — between the sponges and the water, between the damselfish and their walls, between the grouper and the geometry of corridors it has memorized, between the octopus and the concept of a good den, between the cleaning shrimp and the fish that approach it with something resembling the willingness to be helped. These relationships are older, most of them, than the individuals who are currently enacting them. They were worked out by predecessors and passed forward, not as knowledge exactly, not as culture exactly, but as the shape of how things are done here, as the available form of a life in this place.

The city once held a different set of relationships. People in doorways. Trade moving through the streets in a different direction. Children making noise. The noise is gone and the children are unimaginably gone and even the memory of the noise is gone, because memory requires a substrate and the substrate drowned.

But the form persists. The space between walls is still a street and it still organizes movement. The doorways still create thresholds and the thresholds still mark a transition between kinds of space. The rooms still shelter what needs shelter. The city still does what a city does — it concentrates life, it makes a certain density of relationship possible, it provides the architecture within which things can happen that could not happen as easily in open water with no walls and no corridors and no places for an octopus to make into a home.

The city does not know any of this. It is stone. But the city is full — warm, dark, and genuinely full — and the fullness is not accidental, and the stone remembers nothing but the stone made it possible, and in the warm darkness the work continues, patient and various and committed, because this is what is here, and this is what is here, and this is what is here.
