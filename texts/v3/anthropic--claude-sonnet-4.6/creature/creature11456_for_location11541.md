## What Lives Here

---

Begin with what is hardest to see.

In the silt at the pool's center — that layer of grey ripple-marks the culvert's current has been composing since the basin formed — there is life that does not look like life. It looks like sediment. It is sediment, in the sense that it is made of the same materials, shaped by the same forces, occupying the same grain-scale world. But it moves in sediment-time, which is slow enough to be invisible, and it eats in sediment-time, and it has organized itself into communities as complex as anything above the water's surface, only flattened, compressed, living between particles rather than between objects. These are the meiofauna: nematodes, water mites in their early stages, copepods small enough to navigate the spaces between silt grains as if those spaces were corridors. They are present in numbers that would be incomprehensible if stated plainly, so they will not be stated plainly. There are very many of them. They are the oldest layer of this place in the sense that their forms are ancient, and also the newest layer in the sense that they arrived first, before anything visible, testing the water's suitability with their tiny bodies and finding it acceptable.

What they experience, if experience is the word — and it is not quite the word, but there is no better word — is a world defined entirely by chemistry and pressure and current. The culvert's pulse registers for them not as sound but as pressure waves that periodically rearrange their immediate environment. The egg masses above them cast shadows they cannot perceive but alter the light spectrum reaching the silt by fractions they may respond to. They do not know they are at the base of a food chain. They do not know anything. But they are the reason everything else can be here, and in that sense they are prior, foundational, the ground condition.

---

Now the egg masses themselves.

They float near the quieter margins, as described, each a dark cloud in cold jelly, and what is inside them is neither frog nor egg but something that has not yet decided what it needs to be. The embryos at the center of each mass are a few days ahead of the ones at the edges — the interior of the jelly is slightly warmer, insulated by the sphere's own geometry, and so development is a gradient. If you could look with enough resolution, you would see something like rings of age emanating outward from each mass's center, the innermost embryos already showing the first suggestions of a spine, the outermost still undifferentiated, still potential.

What lives in the egg masses is time, mostly. Each embryo is running a developmental program that has been running without significant modification for something like a hundred million years. The wood frog genome contains the instructions for making a wood frog, and those instructions were written in conditions nothing like a corrugated steel culvert drainage basin, but the instructions do not care about context. They care about temperature and pH and dissolved oxygen, all of which this pool provides within acceptable parameters. The engineer did not include wood frogs in their calculations. The genome did not include corrugated steel. Neither cares. Both proceed.

There are perhaps eight masses, perhaps ten. Each contains perhaps a thousand eggs. Most of them will not become frogs. The ones that don't become frogs become, instead, nourishment for the things that eat them — water striders if any reach this pool (unlikely; it is too enclosed), predatory diving beetles if any have colonized it (possible; they fly and find water by the glint of it, and a culvert mouth might glint right), the odd ambystoma salamander if any spotted salamanders added their masses alongside the wood frogs' (there are none visible, but this is early, and the spotted salamanders are more deliberate about their timing). 

The egg masses that fail will drift to the margins and blanch and begin to break apart and the meiofauna will process them back into the sediment. The egg masses that succeed will hatch into tadpoles that will school together in the shallows, dark and comma-shaped, grazing algae from every surface they can find. They will be there for weeks if the pool persists long enough, and then they will not be there, because they will have become something that can leave.

---

The frogs themselves.

They are on every surface available to a frog: floating in pairs at the pool's margin, sitting on the clay fragments at the scarp's base, pressed against the wet concrete of the culvert's lip, mostly invisible until they move and then suddenly, briefly, intensely visible — a shape that resolves out of background, performs its frog-ness for a moment, and then reassembles itself into background again. They are the color of dead leaves, which is not accidental, and the color of wet clay, and the color of the shadows inside a moss mat. They are very good at being part of a place.

What the male frogs are doing is broadcasting. The call of a wood frog is produced by males and directed at females, but it is also, by accident of physics, directed at everything — at the culvert's steel walls, which amplify it back at the caller, at the pool's surface, which transmits it as vibration into the water where the females feel it in their lateral lines before they hear it, at the clay scarp, which absorbs it. The frogs have no intention toward the culvert. The culvert has had no intention toward the frogs. And yet: a frog calling inside a corrugated steel pipe mouth produces a sound with harmonics that a frog calling in open marsh does not produce, and those harmonics travel farther, and it is at least imaginable — not provable but imaginable — that the females find this pool more easily than they would find a pool in open land because the culvert is functioning, accidentally, as an amplifier, a broadcast tower, a thing that makes the pool audible across a distance the pool itself could not achieve.

The frogs do not know this either.

There are females here who have not yet chosen a mass to add their eggs to, who are moving through the pool with the unhurried deliberateness of animals making a decision that their genome is actually making for them, selecting for egg mass position based on criteria of temperature and water depth and dissolved oxygen that register as preference. There are males who have mated and are now simply present, still calling, still contributing to the general noise, carrying out a behavior that has become somewhat redundant but that they are going to continue anyway because they are in the middle of it and there is no mechanism in a wood frog for stopping in the middle.

The frogs have a circulatory response to cold that is, among vertebrates, essentially unique: they can partially freeze. Ice forms between their cells, and their cells survive it because they have flooded themselves with glucose acting as antifreeze, and when the temperature drops enough they stop — not dead, but stopped, a frog-shaped volume of supercooled biology waiting for the threshold to reverse. They have all recently done this and un-done this, thawed and resumed. The ones sitting on the clay at the scarp's base have been here before, have overwintered in the leaf litter and emerged and found this pool, or a pool like this pool, and will overwinter again somewhere in the leaf litter after the breeding season ends and they disperse back into the woods — where the woods are, which is to say somewhere not here, somewhere past the embankment and past whatever cleared zone surrounds it, in whatever fragment of deciduous forest remains within traveling distance.

They are not from here. They are not from anywhere specific. They are from the area. They are passage animals, breeding animals, users of this place for a few weeks and then gone, and in their going they become, paradoxically, more fully what they are — forest floor animals, cryptic leaf-litter animals, animals of a different hidden world — while the pool continues without them.

---

The stoneflies are not passage animals. They are transformation animals, which is different.

The nymphs that produced the shed skins on the culvert's rim have been in the water — specifically, in the culvert itself, and in whatever groundwater seeps feed it — for one or two years. They have lived in the dark, in the fast-moving cold water of the pipe's interior, clinging to the corrugated steel with claws designed for exactly this kind of surface though the corrugated steel did not exist when those claws evolved. They have eaten algae and detritus and each other when necessary. They have molted through many instars, each one a temporary form, each one shed, each shed skin a map of the previous self left behind like documentation.

Now they have emerged into air they have never breathed, with wings they have never used, with mouthparts that no longer function because the adult stonefly of this family does not eat. They have approximately two weeks. In those two weeks they will mate and the females will return to the water to lay eggs, not in the pool exactly but in the flowing water of the culvert where the current can carry the eggs to wherever the nymphs will hatch and begin the dark years of their development.

They are walking across the culvert lip, these adults, in their dark narrow bodies, in their ancient form, and they appear indifferent to everything — to the frogs, to the cold, to the fact of the culvert — but indifference is not the right frame for an animal that doesn't have the neural architecture for indifference. They are not indifferent. They are focused on one thing with their entire being, which is mating, but focused is also not the right word, because it implies a choice of focus, and there is no choice here. There is only the behavior, running, finding the moment it needs to find, and then the eggs in the water, and then the nymphs in the dark, and then years, and then this: the wet concrete edge of a rusted pipe, walked across carefully by something in a shape three hundred million years old.

What the stonefly is, at this moment, is a delivery mechanism for the next generation of stoneflies. What it was before, in the water, was a processing mechanism for the organic material the culvert delivered. What it will be after, in a week or two, is nothing — or rather, it will be protein, processed by whatever finds it. Somewhere in that sequence of forms, there is something we might call a life. It does not add up to a story in any way a story usually adds up to. It adds up, instead, to a stonefly, which is something more durable than a story.

---

The salamanders under the scarp's fragments are harder to describe because they cannot be seen.

What can be said is this: they are there in the way that a word is in a sentence, necessary, load-bearing, nearly invisible because the sentence reads so naturally that the individual word disappears into meaning. The red-backed salamander lives in the interstices. In the spaces between things. Under bark, under stone, in the narrow gaps between soil particles that are wide enough for a small cylinder of muscle and bone. They do not need the pool. They are not here for the pool. They are here because the scarp fell and exposed its underface and the underfaces of stones are, to a red-backed salamander, a habitat event, an opportunity, a space to occupy and hold.

Each one holds a territory of a few square feet against others of its kind, which it communicates through scent — through gland secretions deposited on substrate, written in the chemical grammar of salamander identity, readable only by other salamanders. Underneath the scarp's fallen fragments, there is a map of who is where and who was here before and who is encroaching, written in a script that requires no literacy to read, just the right receptors, which every red-backed salamander has and every other animal in this basin does not. The map is constantly being updated. It was being updated while the stoneflies emerged. It was being updated while the frogs bred. It will be being updated when the frogs are gone and the stoneflies are gone and the egg masses have either hatched or died, and it will continue to be updated through the summer and into the fall and through the winter underground, all of it invisible, all of it present, all of it load-bearing in the sentence the basin is always making.

They are eating springtails, primarily. Springtails are in the soil in numbers comparable to the meiofauna in the silt — another order of magnitude too large to state plainly — and they are small enough that a salamander needs many of them to constitute a meal, and the salamander eats many of them, and does not notice doing it, and the springtail population absorbs this without apparent distress because there are so many springtails. The salamanders are converting the hidden soil economy into visible animal life one springtail at a time, and in that way they are doing something the basin needs done: they are moving energy from one layer to another, from underground to surface, preparing it to be moved again by whatever eats the salamanders, which will be the garter snake if the garter snake is there, and other things, and eventually back into the soil, in a loop that is not quite circular but that rhymes with circles the way all natural processes rhyme with themselves.

---

The garter snake, if it is there in the moss of the upper embankment, is barely there. 

A garter snake at forty degrees Fahrenheit is a garter snake in very slow motion. Its body temperature tracks the environment's temperature, and the environment's temperature is the pool's temperature, and the pool's temperature is not quite sufficient for a garter snake to do much of anything. It is lying in the moss in a configuration that is less like an animal resting and more like an animal being, existing at the minimal level, its heart beating at a rate that would in any warm-blooded creature indicate crisis. It is not in crisis. It is in the cold, which is, for a garter snake in early spring, a condition to be waited out with the patience of an animal that has no other option and has therefore never learned impatience.

What the garter snake is waiting for, without knowing it is waiting, is warmth. A few degrees of warmth. Enough to elevate its body temperature into a range where its muscles can generate useful speed, where its tongue can process olfactory information at the rate the world requires, where digestion becomes possible. It is not yet at that range. It is close. It has overwintered nearby — under the embankment, probably, in a frost-free void in the structure that the engineer also did not calculate — and has emerged because the days are lengthening and some hormonal signal has been triggered by that lengthening, and now it is waiting for the temperature to catch up with the signal.

When the temperature catches up, the snake will become visible in a different way. It will move. It will descend toward the pool. It will find the frogs, or the salamanders, or the young-of-year frogs when they emerge from the water in a few weeks as froglets — small enough to be taken easily, numerous enough that one snake eating many of them is not a significant event for the frog population. It will eat. It will thermoregulate by moving between the moss (cool, humid) and any surface that catches direct sun, which in this enclosed basin may be rare, which may mean the snake does not linger here after the frogs disperse, may follow them out into the more open land where warming is easier and the hunting continues through summer.

Or perhaps it is not there. Perhaps the shape in the moss is a root, a shadow, an inference. This place makes inferences. It provides conditions sufficient for inference and then it produces the thing inferred, and you cannot always tell which happened first.

---

What else is here that does not announce itself:

Beetle larvae in the wood of the embankment's older timbers, if there are timbers, if the embankment has a timber core beneath its clay and gravel fill — which it may, being old enough to have been constructed when timber was used for such things. White and legless and segmented, moving through galleries they have excavated, converting structural wood to frass, to the fine brown powder of digested cellulose, weakening the embankment grain by grain in ways that will take decades to express themselves as anything visible. They will be here long after the frogs have gone for the season. They will be here, or their descendants will be here, when the embankment eventually shifts along whatever axis gravity and water and root pressure and beetle-work conspire to open.

Water striders, maybe, at the pool's margin where the current is least. They would walk the surface tension as if the water were a firm surface and not a surface tension, as if the physical fact of the barrier between water and air were simply ground to them. Their legs make six small depressions in the surface film, six tiny menisci, each one a demonstration of intermolecular force that could be used to explain chemistry to a student. They use them to hunt. The things they hunt are the things that fall into the water from above — the stonefly adults, occasionally, making a poor flight and landing wrong; midges if midges have begun; the frogs' exuvia of shed skin during the growth of the tadpoles, later. 

A colony of midges will emerge from the pool in a few weeks when the water warms slightly, will form their mating column above the pool's surface — a rotating cloud of small male bodies, hovering in a configuration that from above looks like weather and from the side looks like smoke and from inside is a geometrically structured event governed by rules midges cannot articulate but all follow — and that column will be visible from outside the basin, briefly, a sign that something is happening here if anything outside the basin were looking for signs, which nothing is, particularly.

The moss itself is a world. Each mat contains, between its individual stems, a community of animals visible only with magnification: rotifers in the water films between cells, tardigrades in their various states of alertness or suspended animation, mites, collembola, the egg casings of small spiders, fungal hyphae threading between everything, mediating the exchange of nutrients between living and dead tissue. The moss does not know about any of this. The moss is not a knowing thing. But it is a world, and it creates the conditions for being a world by holding water and moderating temperature and providing structural complexity at the millimeter scale, and in doing so it accommodates more lives than can be enumerated in any useful register.

---

Here is the difficult thing about describing what lives in this place:

The usual approach is to list inhabitants. To move through the taxonomic categories. To say: here are the plants, here are the invertebrates, here are the vertebrates. But that approach implies a kind of separability, a ledger of distinct entries, that this place does not support. What lives here is not a list. It is a set of overlapping processes that occasionally crystallize into identifiable forms — a frog, a stonefly, a salamander, a moss mat — and those forms are the legible part, the part language can handle, but they are not the majority of what is here. The majority of what is here is exchange. Transfer. The movement of energy and material between forms, the constant conversion of one thing into another, the way the culvert's water becomes silt becomes meiofauna becomes salamander prey becomes salamander becomes, eventually, soil again.

What lives here is transformation, living at a rate that happens to be biological. The frogs and the stoneflies and the salamanders are not inhabitants of this place in the sense of tenants; they are expressions of it, temporary crystallizations of the conditions the place provides, and when they leave or die or disperse they de-crystallize back into the place, back into the material and energy flows that produced them, and the place continues.

The place continues.

This is the most important fact about what lives here: whatever it is, it continues. The culvert is rusting and will rust further and will eventually fall or be removed and the pool will change and the species that live here may change and the exact community that exists on this early spring day in whatever year this is will never be reassembled. But the continuation is not contingent on this specific assembly. It is contingent on something more general — on the persistence of cold and water and silt and the patience of organisms that have been finding places like this, making use of places like this, for longer than the embankment has existed, longer than the road or rail line it once carried, longer than the human infrastructure that accidentally created this specific iteration of a very old story.

The frogs know the story. Not the frogs here, specifically. But frog. Frog knows this story, and tells it in cold water, in jelly, in the dry mechanical sound of a call that doesn't need to be beautiful to be effective, and has been telling it in every low wet place it can find since before there were corrugated steel culverts to tell it into.

That telling is what lives here. Everything else is how it sounds.
