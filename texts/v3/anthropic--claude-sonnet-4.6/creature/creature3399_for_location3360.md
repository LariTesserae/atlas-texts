# What Lives on the Plateau of Accumulated Greens

---

Begin with what is certain, then work outward.

---

## The Keeper

There is one person. A single person, or the strong impression of one — the boots, the path worn between structures, the clipboard on its nail, the LED that looks recent while everything else looks old. The evidence doesn't accumulate to a crowd; it doesn't even accumulate to two. The worn path between the structures has been made by one body, one set of habits, one repeated passage that has pressed exactly the same route into the ground cover each time. Two people sharing a space would make variations. They would take slightly different angles on different days, depending on who was leading, who was distracted, whose errand took them a degree off center. This path is singular, unwavering, the index of someone who walks it alone and always in the same mental weather.

Call them the Keeper for now, not because they have accepted that title — they probably haven't — but because the role is visible even in their absence.

They come and go. The boots suggest they are not here now: the person who intends to work puts on their boots; the boots standing empty in the doorway mean the person is somewhere where boots are unnecessary — inside, asleep, or gone down from the plateau for a period measured in days. But gone is not the same as absent in any meaningful sense. The whole plateau is organized by their attention. The pipes are warm because they maintain them. The LED on the junction box is recent because they installed it, checked it was working, will check it again. The clipboard with its sheet of paper is their record-keeping, their handwriting, their system. The path between structures is their body's accumulated memory.

What do they look like? Someone who has been here long enough to stop thinking about here. Not young — not because the work requires age, but because you develop a certain quality of presence in a solitary place, and that quality takes years to achieve, and when it's achieved it looks like a kind of deliberate weathering. Not dramatically weathered, not the outdoor-catalog roughness of someone who wants to be seen surviving — but the quieter wearing of someone who has simply been in a place through many cycles of it, and has adjusted accordingly. Clothes that are practical without being a statement about practicality. Hands that know the temperature of the pipes without needing to touch them for long. A way of moving through the dense vegetation that is economical — not graceful exactly, but without waste, the movements of someone who has pressed branches aside ten thousand times and has learned where each one wants to go.

They have been doing this alone for a long time. You can tell because the system they've built makes sense only from the inside. The pipes route in ways that aren't obvious but are probably efficient, given knowledge you don't have. The modifications to the machines layer over each other without documentation, because the documentation lives in the Keeper's head — which means there is no documentation, which means the Keeper has accepted that if they leave, the system will not be interpretable. This is either a practical decision or something more complicated. It might be both.

What do they think about, up here, in the afternoons when the light barely shifts and the condensation falls every thirty seconds from the waxy leaves? 

Almost certainly they think about the machines, or what the machines are doing, or what might go wrong next and how to address it. This takes up a great deal of a Keeper's cognition in any given day. Practical problem-solving loops continuously in the background, not anxiously but steadily, the way a river runs: possible failure modes, resources available, approaches to try. They think about this the way other people think about their relationships or their finances — not because it crowds out other thought, but because it forms the substrate that other thought runs over.

What else? They have brought things with them from lower down. A book or several books, read and reread past the point where the reading produces new information, now read for the texture of it. Music, almost certainly — something small and battery-powered, or something that runs off whatever power the machines produce, playing in one of the structures in the evenings. Music is the thing people are most likely to bring to a solitary place, and then be a little embarrassed by, as though needing music is an admission of weakness that the landscape would judge. The landscape does not judge. The lichen doesn't care. The Keeper listens to music in the evenings and has stopped being embarrassed about it.

Do they have a project? Yes. Unquestionably yes. The machines are doing something, and the Keeper maintains them toward that end, and the project is the axis around which the days organize themselves. But the project has been underway long enough that the urgency is gone from it, if urgency was ever present. The project is proceeding at the speed of the plateau — which is, as we have established, the speed of lichen, of patient accumulation. The Keeper has adjusted their internal tempo accordingly. This took time. It was probably difficult, the adjustment. It probably involved a period of feeling that nothing was happening, that the work was pointless, that the days were identical and therefore lost. And then something shifted, the way things shift after sufficient time in a slow place — the identical days began to seem not lost but rather present in some fuller way, each one available for observation because they were not rushing past. The Keeper now inhabits each day with a thoroughness that would have been impossible at lower altitude, at lower speed.

They are not enlightened by this. They are not transcendent. They have simply made peace with a tempo that other people would find intolerable, and that peace is real but unglamorous. They still get restless sometimes. They still descend occasionally, presumably — to the place with the thicker air and the full temperature of the day — and when they do, the world down there is too fast and too loud and they are glad to come back, which means that when they are back they feel the gladness of return, which means the plateau is home in the most functional sense of that word.

## The Burrowing Things

The burrow entrances around the water basins are clean-edged and recently used. Whatever made them is present — just not visible. This is a crucial distinction.

The burrowing things are small mammals. The confidence of the assessment comes from the burrow architecture: clean-edged means maintained, means something that lives underground cares about the integrity of its entrance, keeps it clear of debris, which is mammal behavior. Insect burrows have a different character. Reptile burrows have a different character where reptiles exist at these altitudes, which at this latitude and with this vegetation type, they largely don't.

Small means vole-sized or vole-adjacent — some compact, dense-furred thing built for the specific thermic problem of living underground in a place that gets cold at night and occasionally receives rain and never gets truly warm. They are brown. They are almost certainly brown: a color that is genuinely useless in this environment in terms of concealment — the ground cover here is green, and brown stands out against green — but which is what they are regardless, because they evolved brownness elsewhere, for different reasons, and have carried it to this place without updating it. Evolution is not responsive at the pace of individual lives. They are brown and slightly conspicuous if they are above ground, which is why they are usually below ground.

They eat roots and seeds and whatever they find at the margins of their burrows in the soil. They eat the roots of the ferns, which the ferns do not enjoy but have adapted to tolerate, having been coexisted with by the ancestors of these animals for longer than the plateau's current form has existed. There is a negotiation between the ferns' root systems and the burrowing things' tunnels that is older than the machines, older than the Keeper, old enough to have settled into a kind of mutual accommodation. The ferns send roots around the tunnels. The tunnels avoid the deepest root masses. Neither party planned this.

There are several of them — certainly not one, probably dozens, maybe a small colony, the burrows connecting underground in a way that the surface entrances don't reveal. They are aware of the Keeper in the way small prey animals are aware of large presences: continuously, anxiously, at a level below anything that might be called thought. The Keeper's footsteps register as vibration in their tunnels and they hold still for a moment, every time, before deciding this particular vibration is safe, is the known vibration, and resuming. They have been holding-still-for-the-Keeper for as long as the Keeper has been here. This is memory encoded in behavior, not in anything like narrative.

They have no name for the plateau. They have no name for anything. They have instead a very precise map, held in spatial memory, of every tunnel in the colony and every surface route between burrow entrances and the best feeding sites and the water basin and the areas that should be avoided and the areas that are safe. This map is more detailed, in its own terms, than anything the Keeper has written on any clipboard. It contains information the Keeper doesn't have.

## The Flies

The small flies moving in slow spiraling columns in the shafts of light are engaged in something that looks aimless but isn't. The spiraling is display. The columns of light are chosen because they are visible, because a fly that wants to be seen — for mating purposes, for territorial assertion, for reasons the flies don't have access to but that are real — goes to the visible place, the lit place, and moves in a pattern that makes itself as apparent as possible within its tiny frame. The spiraling says: *I am here. I am here. I am here.* On repeat, at a frequency too small and too monotonous for the Keeper to attend to, but attended to nonetheless by other flies, whose entire relevant world is the scale at which flies operate.

They are not one species. There are several, similar enough in the low light to seem identical but operating on slightly different frequencies of meaning, their spiraling patterns differing in ways that are significant to them and invisible to everything else. They do not interact with the burrowing things. They do not interact with the Keeper. They interact with each other, with the condensation (which they drink, from the dark wet leaves around the basins), and with the organic matter in the soil, where their larvae live and where the work of decomposition — the slow chemical return of dead plant matter to something plants can use again — continues in the dark and wet.

The flies are the plateau's digestive system. Without them (without the thing they do, which is what decomposers do everywhere, which is be the hinge between death and renewal) the accumulated greens would accumulate past the point of production, would build up, would eventually poison the very conditions that sustain them. The flies prevent this. They are not aware of preventing this. They are simply living at the scale they live at, doing the things that their form makes them do, and the consequence is that the plateau remains possible.

This is worth holding: the flies maintain the plateau. The plateau is their home in the most direct sense — it is the condition of their lives and they are the condition of its continuation — and yet they have no relationship with it that could be called a relationship. They have no distance from it. They are it, in the way that any organism sufficiently embedded in its environment becomes not quite separable from the environment. The Keeper can stand on the plateau and look at the plateau. The flies cannot do this. The flies are inside it all the way down.

## The Lichen

The lichen on the metal is not an animal and not a plant, which is why it's being addressed separately, because it lives here in a mode that the other categories don't capture.

A lichen is a negotiation made permanent. It is fungus and photosynthetic partner — algae, cyanobacteria, or both — living in a relationship so old and so complete that it has become a single organism with a name, a form, a way of being that neither partner could achieve alone. The fungus provides structure and mineral access; the photosynthetic partner provides the carbon compounds that sustain both. They became each other's answer to a problem of survival at the margins, and having found the answer, they have been it for four hundred million years.

The lichen on the corrugated metal of the buildings is pale — pale gray, pale cream, with here and there a disc of slightly warmer color, a reproductive structure offering spores to the air. It is extending across the metal surface at a rate measurable, as established, only across seasons. Its leading edge is not visible to the eye as a leading edge — it doesn't look like a wave, doesn't look like progress, doesn't look like anything except more lichen, arriving at its margin in forms too small to see individually.

What does the lichen experience? This is the question that the plateau, in its patience, keeps asking. Not with any expectation of an answer. The lichen responds to light: its photosynthetic partner demands light and the structure of the lichen orients toward it, grows toward it, the growth rates on the shaded side infinitesimally slower than on the illuminated side, so that over years the lichen curves, almost imperceptibly, to maximize its exposure. Is this experience? It is response. It is sensitivity. It is the lichen knowing, in whatever sense knowing can mean without a nervous system, where the light is.

The lichen on the plateau's fresh-broken rock faces has not started yet. The rock is too young. The lichen will start on those faces eventually — pioneer species are already there in the form of single-celled arrivals, as the description notes — and the lichen will follow the pioneers, establishing on the rock a slow succession of forms that will, over centuries, create the conditions for more complex life to follow. The lichen is not planning this. The lichen is simply growing, and growth here means succession, means the plateau covering itself more thoroughly than it already has, means the machines eventually covered too, if the Keeper stops clearing them, if the Keeper leaves and does not return.

The machines are the one place where the lichen has been held back. Where the Keeper walks, clears, maintains. Where the pipes are warm enough that the lichen finds the conditions wrong. The lichen has gone up to these edges and stopped, not because it decided to stop, but because the conditions for continuation don't obtain. It waits — not consciously, not with patience in any felt sense, but in the functional way that all organisms wait when conditions are insufficient: by existing, by not dying, by continuing at the margin until the margin shifts.

If the machines stopped. If the warmth left the pipes. If the Keeper didn't return. The lichen would not rush. It doesn't rush. But the margin would shift, and the lichen would continue, and in thirty years there would be a gray-cream bloom across the pipes and the junction boxes and the ceramic insulators on the posts, and the green LED would be dark, and the boots would be inside the moss that had colonized the structure, and the clipboard would be illegible, and the path between the buildings would be closed, and the plateau would be more thoroughly itself than it is now.

It is already, as established, thoroughly itself. The lichen is simply the principle of that thoroughness, extended forward in time.

## What the Machines Live With

The machines are not alive. This deserves acknowledgment rather than evasion.

But they are tended, which is a form of being lived with. The Keeper's relationship to the machines is the closest thing to love that is present on the plateau, if love can be defined as sustained, repeated, careful attention given without guarantee of return. The machines don't know they are tended. They operate or they don't, they fail or they don't, they accept modification or they make modification difficult. They have no opinion about the Keeper.

And yet: the machines were brought here, which means someone decided they belonged here, which is a decision that has accumulated consequence. The pipes run warm because the machines run. The warm pipes create a slightly warmer microclimate in their vicinity, perhaps a degree or two above what the plateau would otherwise be. This means the vegetation directly around the pipes is slightly different from the vegetation elsewhere: a degree or two warmer, in a cool place, shifts what can persist and what cannot. The ferns are perhaps a fraction more lush at those edges. The lichen has stopped at those edges. The burrowing things' tunnels probably run under the pipes, taking advantage of the warmth, making the soil there a slightly more hospitable place to spend the night.

The machines have changed the plateau. Not dramatically. Not more than the lichen changes the rock it grows on, not more than the burrowing things change the soil structure around the basins. But the change is real, and it has been absorbed into the system, and the system has adjusted around it the way systems do, and now the plateau is this — the plateau with the machines in it — and not the plateau without them.

What are the machines doing? What is the purpose of the pipes and the cables and the pressure vessels and the electrical generation? 

I am going to resist answering this directly, because I think the answer is located somewhere in the relation between the Keeper and the project, and that relation is not fully visible from the outside. It is possible the machines are measuring something — the plateau's atmosphere, its soil chemistry, the rate at which the fresh rock faces are being colonized. It is possible they are producing something: heat, power, some compound whose purpose is legible only to the Keeper. It is possible they are maintaining something: a temperature, a pressure, a condition that would otherwise not persist at this altitude. It is possible the purpose is modest — power for the structure where the Keeper sleeps, warmth against the cold nights, light in the winter when the pale ceramic sky goes dark early.

Whatever it is, the machines are doing it slowly and reliably, and the Keeper maintains them toward that end, and the plateau has accepted them the way it accepts everything — by covering them, slowly, in what it has.

## The Distant Thing

Beyond the edge of the plateau — past the fresh-broken rock, in the thicker air of lower altitude — something is moving. You can infer it but not confirm it. The description established this and I don't want to violate it.

It is moving at a speed appropriate to lower elevation, where things happen faster, where the air is richer, where birds call and the day reaches its full temperature and the pace of everything is different from the pace up here. Whatever it is, it is not coming to the plateau. Or not yet. Or not now. Or not in any timeframe relevant to this afternoon.

The Keeper knows it's there — knows there is a world below, a world where things move at that speed, because they came from it and descend to it and return. The lichen doesn't know. The burrowing things don't know, exactly, though they carry a generational memory of having moved up here from somewhere, their ancestors having arrived at this altitude along routes that are now overgrown, and that memory is stored not in thought but in the shape of their navigation, the direction their instincts gesture toward when they are uncertain.

The distant thing is probably a bird. Or several birds. Or the wind moving through trees at a lower elevation, producing a sound that carries upward and arrives here with its origin ambiguous, which is why it registered as something that might have been a call and might not have been. The plateau receives this signal and offers nothing back. The plateau doesn't call. It accumulates.

## A Summary, Which Is Also a Failure to Summarize

What lives here:

One person, alone, who has slowed themselves to the speed of the place, who maintains warm pipes and reads clipboards and listens to music in the evenings.

Many small mammals in a colony of connected tunnels, running their spatial maps in the dark, holding still when the Keeper's footsteps come, resuming when the vibration says *safe.*

Several species of flies spiraling in light columns, saying *I am here*, drinking condensation from dark leaves, transforming the dead into the living in the soil below.

Lichen, extending across every surface not actively too warm or too maintained, being the principle of accumulation, being the plateau's slow project of covering itself.

Ferns, ground cover, shrubs, small trees — all of them patient, all of them hungry, all of them in possession of exactly the right kind of exuberance for a cool bright high place that receives weather rather than making it.

And perhaps: the machines, which are not alive but have become part of what lives here, and which the Keeper tends with the attention that is very close, in its sustained and careful quality, to something warmer than maintenance.

They are all, in their different ways, doing the same thing. Continuing. Accumulating. Adjusting to the speed of this particular place, which is slow, and getting slower as the lichen covers more, as the soil deepens, as the plateau becomes more thoroughly, densely, quietly itself.

The Keeper is the fastest thing up here. This is not obvious when you're watching them work, but it's true. They are the fastest thing, operating on a human timescale in a place that runs on a geological one, and they have been here long enough to know this, and it has changed them — made them quieter, made them more attentive to things that take a long time to be visible, made them better at inference than at observation. They look at the plateau and see not just what is here now but what is accumulating toward, what will be here in ten years if conditions hold, what would be here in a hundred if conditions changed.

This is what the plateau does to the things that live in it, and live in it long enough. It teaches inference. It teaches the reading of a landscape from evidence of things not fully present. It makes the invisible — the recent rain, the slow lichen, the burrowing colony underground, the distant moving thing below the edge — more real than what is immediately visible.

The Keeper has learned this thoroughly. They are, by now, almost as good at being here as the lichen is. Not as patient. Not as comprehensive. But learning. Accumulating, in their own way, like everything else on the plateau accumulates. Slowly becoming part of what lives there, the way the machines have become part of what lives there, the way anything does that stays long enough for the place to grow around it.
