# Those Who Live in the Underloft

---

## The Roamers

They are not settled here. That is the first thing to understand about them.

They come down from above, through shafts in the root-vault that function as chimneys of a sort — narrow apertures, barely body-wide, that require the descent to be made in a particular sequence of positions, the body torqued and angled, arms preceding or following depending on the width at each point. The descents are long. Some take most of a day. The people who make them are lean in the specific way that comes not from scarcity but from the physical demands of moving in tight vertical spaces: the shoulder-width narrow, the arms disproportionately strong, the hips genuinely reduced. Children come down more easily than adults. This is noted and used.

They travel in groups too small to call bands. Three. Four, sometimes. Two adults and a child, sometimes, which is something other than a family and something other than not one. Whatever social structure exists aboveground, the underloft imposes its own accounting, and the accounting says: fewer. The empty space requires fewer. It does not support the social density of the surface the way a forest does, the way a shore does, the way a place with food in abundance and weather to shelter from does. The underloft is not a place of abundance. It is a place of something else, and the people who come here come for that something else, and the group-size reflects the purpose.

What they wear has been selected for the cold, which is real and cumulative and rises from the stone into the bones if you stop moving. Pelts, yes — the pale underloft fauna, small-furred and slow, worth the cost of catching — but also weavings, made above and carried down, that in texture and in the way they hold air between the fibers resemble nothing so much as compressed root-matter itself. Whether this is intentional or simply reflects what materials are available at the surface above the underloft is not determinable from looking. They move in it silently. The underloft has made them quiet; you don't speak at ordinary volume here, not because anything will hear you — there is nothing that would hear you as a threat — but because the acoustics of the space are strange enough that a raised voice does something troubling, reflects and scatters in ways that make it sound like more than one voice, or fewer, and this is not something you want to experience often. So they speak low when they speak at all. And they have learned, or they arrived knowing, that the underloft is a space for a different kind of attention than speech.

## Their Bodies

The ones who come regularly — who make the descent not once but seasonally, habitually, as part of the rhythm of their year — have bodies that show it.

The hands are the most visible record. Root-climbing leaves its marks: the base of each finger thickened where it meets the palm, the skin of the palm itself carrying a texture like old leather, the fingernails slightly abbreviated, worked down by the constant small negotiations with root-surface. A callus pattern unlike the patterns produced by digging or grinding or any tool-use, specific to the gripping of irregular rounded organic surfaces in the dark and cold. When you see hands like these on someone at the surface, you know where they go.

Their eyes are — not different, exactly, but used differently. The underloft's light is even and sourceless and there are no shadows to read, no directionality to interpret. People who spend time down here develop a habit of long focus, of looking across distances rather than reading the immediate ground, of using peripheral motion more than center-gaze. In conversation they sometimes look at the space slightly beside your face, which at the surface reads as inattention or evasion and in the underloft is simply calibration. Their blink rate is different. You notice it and then you can't stop noticing it.

The cold does something to posture over time. The shoulders come in slightly, not from timidity but from thermal conservation, a habitual gathering inward that becomes structural. They stand like people who are used to the air being expensive — used to each breath being a transaction rather than a given. Even aboveground, warmer, a person who has spent significant time in the underloft will sometimes stand briefly in this gathered posture, then release it, then forget they did either thing.

There is, among the long-term roamers, a low incidence of injury in the categories you might expect — no falls, no getting-lost, no hypothermia — and a somewhat elevated incidence of a specific kind of joint complaint in the upper spine, from the descents, from the years of contorting through the shafts. They carry this with them. It is the structural record of passage, written in cartilage.

## What They Do There

The marks on the stone at the base of the root-columns are the most legible evidence, and they tell this much: they are counting, yes, but what they count is not immediately apparent, and the counting is also something else.

There is a practice — pieced together from the marks, from the arrangement of the sleeping platforms, from the orientation of the carved bowls, from patterns that only become visible when you have seen several sites and begun to place them in relation to each other — of attending to the root-columns over time. Each column that has been visited carries marks from multiple visits, in multiple seasons, and the marks accumulate into what functions as a record of the column's growth. Its descent toward the floor. The rate at which its tip has extended in the months between visits. Whether it branches. Whether a branch arrests and another continues. Whether the column has begun to anchor in the stone, which happens rarely and slowly and, it seems, matters.

The bowl residue, analyzed as well as it can be from dried remains, contains plant material — surface-origin, processed, concentrated — applied to the marks in some visits. Not all. Perhaps when something significant has been observed. Perhaps when the visit itself is a different kind of visit. The pigment, if pigment it is, is the yellow-green of still-living root-flesh, which requires fresh material from near the surface and which would not survive the long descent in useful form without careful preservation. They bring it down anyway. This says something about what they consider necessary.

What they are watching for, in the growth of the columns, is not entirely clear. It may be navigational: the columns grow toward moisture, and their growth-direction over years maps the underlift's slow circulation of whatever water filters down. It may be something more anticipatory: the columns that are closest to the floor, that have been growing toward it for the longest time, will eventually anchor, and when they anchor the underloft changes in that zone. The column becomes a conduit rather than simply a root. Things will grow along it that couldn't before. The floor-world near an anchored column will be different from the floor-world away from one. Knowing where this will happen, and approximately when — in a generation, in three, in ten — is knowledge with uses that are not yet apparent but that feel, in the context of the people who gather it, like the kind of knowledge that is worth a life's returning.

Some of the marks are addressed to the column itself. This is the hard-to-avoid interpretation of certain sequences: not recording-marks, but something more directed, something with the grammar of statement or question or request. The people who make these marks are not confused about what root-columns are. They understand, at whatever level their knowledge operates, that roots are plant-tissue engaged in the slow work of finding water and structural purchase. And yet. They address them. Perhaps the category of "addressable entity" is wider in the underloft than it is at the surface, where entities are either quick and warm or clearly stone. The underloft presents something in between: slow, yes, and cold, and not obviously responding — but *processing*, unmistakably processing its environment, making decisions about where to grow, extending and halting and extending again in response to gradients that a person can learn to read. The marks may be part of a long negotiation whose terms will only be apparent in a century or five. The people making them accept this. They come back. They bring children down, when the children are old enough to manage the shafts, and the children learn the marks and add to them and return.

## The Children

They learn the descent in stages. First the short shafts, closer to the surface, that open into minor underloft cavities — not the great vault, but its tributaries, where the ceiling is lower and the scale is manageable. Here the child learns what the light does to the eye, learns the cold, learns the quiet that is physical. They sit. The adults do not perform instruction in any legible sense. They sit also. Something is transmitted by proximity and repetition that cannot be transmitted by explanation.

Later, the full descent. The first time entering the main underloft from above is — by all interpretive evidence, by the marks children leave near the shaft openings, which are different from adult marks in pressure and size but not in kind — significant in a way that does not pass quickly. Children who have done it once need some time before they are ready to look at the ceiling directly. They look at the floor first. They look at their own hands. They look at the adults, who are already looking across the empty air toward the nearest root-column, and they follow that gaze and eventually, naturally, look up.

The child marks near shaft openings are the most directly expressive marks in the underloft. They are not counting-marks. They are not directional. They are the marks of someone who needed to leave a record of having been here and finding it overwhelming and also finding it, somehow, okay. A child pressed their palm to the stone near a shaft mouth and traced it. The outline is still there. It will be there for longer than anyone now alive will live, in a space that does not weather, in stone that does not forget.

## The Pale Ones

This is a name applied retrospectively, by inference. It refers to the people who, at some earlier point — earlier by generations, by many generations, by a span of time during which the underloft itself has measurably changed, the columns have grown and some have anchored and the root-ceiling has dropped meters in certain zones — came down and did not return.

The evidence: a set of sites, five of them identified across the known portion of the underloft's floor, that are structurally different from the roamer camps. They are not single sleeping-platforms but arrangements suggesting longer occupation. Multiple platforms. Evidence of sustained use of the same bowl-hollows. A greater density of marks on the stones, covering more surface area, going higher on the root-columns than a person standing would naturally reach without deliberate effort. At one site, a section of floor stone has been smoothed — not by water, but by repeated passage across the same path, the stone's surface worn by feet over years. The only smoothed stone in the underloft that shows this pattern.

These people stayed. Whether by choice or by some failure of the return — shafts closing over, perhaps, as the ceiling continued its slow descent; illness; decision; the gradual attrition of the desire to go back to a surface world that must have grown theoretical and strange — they stayed.

Their bodies are not in the underloft. This is notable. People who stayed for years left no remains. The conclusion, tentative, is that they were carried out when they died, or that they went up at the end in a final effort, or — less satisfying but not dismissible — that something in the underloft's conditions removes organic remains before they fossilize. The cold, the aridity in certain zones, the fauna that consumes efficiently. The evidence is quiet on this point and will remain quiet.

What they learned, in their years of permanent residence, is preserved only in the marks. The marks at long-occupation sites are denser and more varied than at roamer sites, and some of them constitute a continuity — the same sequence, extended and extended again, visit by visit but without the gaps that roamer visits impose, the sequence continuing in what reads as daily additions until suddenly stopping. These are the closest thing the underloft has to a written record, and they are a record of attention paid without interruption to a root-column for what the sequence-length suggests was years. What they found, attending that closely that long, is encoded in marks that the current roamers can partially read — the portions that match the counting and directional systems they still use — and partially cannot.

The portions they cannot read they copy, carefully, on new stone surfaces, at each visit. Carrying the unknown forward. Keeping it in circulation until it becomes legible again, or until someone brings the knowledge to read it, or until the root-column itself anchors and something changes and the marks mean what they were waiting to mean.

## The One Who Came Alone

There is one site that doesn't fit either category.

It is not a camp and not a long-occupation. It is a single object: a piece of worked bone, not underloft-fauna bone but something larger, surface-origin, that has been carved into a shape that is not a tool. It is too small to be useful as anything structural or cutting or scraping. It is the size of a closed fist, and it has been worked into a form that suggests — suggests, can only suggest — the underside of the root-ceiling, the arches and descending columns, the vault. Miniaturized, abstracted, but recognizable in the way that models are recognizable to people who know the original. Someone made a model of the underloft and left it here, alone, on a flat section of stone, equidistant from several root-columns, positioned with its carved-ceiling side facing up toward the actual ceiling.

There is no sleeping-platform. No bowl. No marks on nearby stone other than one, which is not a counting-mark and not an addressing-mark but which has the character, in its pressure and its deliberateness and its position at eye-height on the nearest root-column, of a signature.

One person came down alone, placed a model of the space they were in inside the space they were in, marked the nearest column, and left. Or did not leave — there is no way to know. The bone is old, older than the current roamer practice, older than the long-occupation sites by the dating-by-root-growth-reference that the marks make possible. Old enough that whoever did this was an early visitor to the underloft, perhaps among the first, coming down into a space that had not yet developed the vocabulary of practices, the platforms and bowls and counting-marks, that later visitors brought. Coming down with only a made object and a stone tool and the impulse, apparently uncontrollable, to build a small version of the thing that was too large to fit inside a mind all at once.

The roamers do not camp near this site. They visit it. They look at the bone for a while. They do not touch it and they do not add marks near the one that is already there. Then they move on to their own root-columns, their own counting, their own long work of attending to a space that is still becoming itself.

## At Rest

When the roamers sleep, they sleep deeply. This is not a general statement about human sleep but a specific one about sleep in the underloft, where the cold accelerates the body's need for restoration, where the silence is so complete that there is nothing to wake for, where the even light does not shift as surface-light shifts and so the body has no cue to surface. They sleep wrapped in the pale pelts, on the platforms that hold them off the cold of the stone, and they sometimes sleep twelve or thirteen hours. They emerge from this sleep with a quality of presence that is different from the surface-waking quality: slower to establish the day's intention, longer in the state between sleep and full engagement, spending time simply being in the space before beginning the work of attending to it.

There are observers of the underloft — people from surface communities who have heard of the roamers, who have come to the shaft openings and looked down without descending — who believe the long sleep is the point. That the underloft is sought for what it does to consciousness in those hours of cold and silence and constant even light, that the practice of marking and attending is secondary to or derived from what happens in the sleep. That people come down to dream.

The roamers themselves, asked, do not confirm or deny this. They answer questions about the marks readily enough. They answer questions about the columns, about what they are watching for, about the children's learning. They are quiet when asked about the sleep, in the way they are quiet about many things — not evasive, but considering whether the question is one that language is suited to answer.

Usually they change the subject to the ceiling.

They always want to talk about the ceiling.
