# The People of the High Town

---

They are not obvious.

This is the first thing. They do not present themselves. If you stand at the well in the wide place and wait, you will not see them emerge to greet you or assess you. There is no official curiosity, no delegation. What you will notice, after a time, is that you are being seen — that there is a quality of attention directed at you from somewhere unspecified, not threatening, not quite welcoming, simply present. The way a hillside is present. The way the light is present. It registers you without making a ceremony of it.

Eventually someone will appear. Not from a doorway particularly — it will seem, improbably, as if they simply resolved out of the available material, as if the grey-blue of the stone produced them the way stone produces a seam of different mineral: always there, visible only when the light is right.

---

They are people of middle age, mostly. Or they look it, or they look past it in a direction you can't quite name.

This is not the countryside youthfulness of people kept vigorous by physical labor, the weather-leathered glow of someone who has spent decades in open air and arrived at seventy looking sixty. This is something different. They do not look old so much as *settled*. Their faces have reached their final form. The lines are there — at the eyes, at the mouth — but they are not the marks of change in progress; they are the marks of change completed, the record of a face that has found what it is and stopped moving toward anything else.

Their skin is the color that comes from years of thin air and diffuse light, not tanned, not pale — a neutral tone that doesn't declare itself. The sort of coloring that would be hard to describe later and harder to place geographically. They have the appearance of people who have always been here, which is because they have.

Their eyes are attentive. Not quick — quick suggests readiness for novelty, a responsiveness to the unexpected, and the unexpected does not often come here. Their attentiveness is the kind that requires patience rather than speed: the noticing of gradual things, small things, things that are there every day but behave slightly differently today. They watch the way a person watches water in a stream. Not waiting for it to change. Simply watching.

---

When they speak, they speak carefully.

Not slowly, exactly, and not with the deliberate care of people choosing words because they are uncertain which are right. This is the care of people for whom words are tools of a specific kind, the kind that wear if you use them carelessly, the kind that should be applied to the thing they are made for and not dragged along uneven ground. They do not waste speech. When they have said a thing, they let it stand.

They use no superlatives. Nothing is described as the best or the worst, the most remarkable, the strangest thing they have ever seen. Things are described as they are, or not described at all. If you ask how the winter was, they will tell you: the first snow came early, the second late, there was ice on the well cover for eleven days without break. This is what the winter was.

They do not perform modesty, but they have no interest in display. If you compliment something — the fit of the stonework, the quality of the cloth — they will acknowledge that it is good, or that it is not as good as they would have liked. They will perhaps tell you what the difficulty was. They will not thank you for noticing.

Questions they answer if they have answers. Questions they don't have answers to, they say so, without embarrassment and without filling the space with speculation. They have a comfort with *not knowing* that is unusual — it isn't resignation, isn't incuriosity, is simply the recognition that some things are not, in fact, known.

---

Their hands are their most expressive feature.

Broad across the palm, the fingers heavy at the knuckle, marked by their specific work — which varies, as all work in a place like this must, a person here does not do only one thing — but certain habits have left their record. The pads of the fingers are smooth in the way of surfaces long worked against other surfaces. There is often something under the nails, stone-dust or wood-grain or the mineral residue of handled earth. They are not dirty; they are seasoned. Like the wall-stones. Like the flagstones. Like anything here that has been used long enough to carry the evidence of use as part of its nature.

They use their hands when they talk, but modestly. A slight opening of the palm to indicate a direction. A turn of the wrist that means *approximately* or *in that order of size*. They do not gesture at abstractions. Their gestures accompany the concrete and stop there.

When they work, their hands know what they are doing before the mind directs them. The competence is so deep it has become unconscious, which means it has become something else — the body's knowledge, the handedness of knowing, the kind that does not require thought because it has long since become the grain of the person.

---

What they make has no excess.

The cloth is warm because warmth is needed, not because warmth is pleasant — or rather, warmth is pleasant precisely because it is what is needed, and there is no separation. A pot is the shape that holds the right amount and sits the right way on the heat. A tool is as long as it needs to be. Nothing is ornamented in the way that announces ornamentation; there is sometimes beauty, but it arrives without trying to, the way the lichen arrives without trying to.

When they make something that is beautiful — a fitted joint, a vessel with a particular curve to the lip — they notice it. They do not point to it. They know what they made. The knowledge is its own.

---

They have a specific relationship to time that is hard to describe from outside.

It is not that they are slow, not that they lack urgency. It is that urgency requires a particular belief about the future — that it is coming and will require something from you, that preparation is possible and matters. They have this belief, but it sits in proportion. They prepare for what can be prepared for, and for what cannot, they do not spend themselves. They have learned, or the place has taught them, that there is a category of things that will happen as they will happen, and the appropriate response is not acceptance in the defeated sense but rather a practical acknowledgment: some things are the weather. You dress for it. You do not argue with it.

They live in long time without being conscious of living in long time. They do not talk about the past in the elegiac mode. They reference it the way they reference the ledge-rock at the edge of town — it is there, it was here before, it informs where you put your feet. The well has been here for longer than anyone can confirm. The path wore pale over centuries. This is simply what is true.

They are not nostalgic. Nostalgia requires believing the past was better, and they do not believe things were better or worse, only different in specific ways that the present is not. The past is information, not feeling.

---

They are not without feeling.

Something settles in their faces at the end of a long day's work — a quality of stillness that is not blankness, that has warmth in it, something like satisfaction except satisfaction sounds too active. More like: arrival. The daily arrival. You worked and now you are here, and here is where you are, and it is sufficient. It is enough.

In the presence of difficulty — and there is difficulty here, the altitude takes children sometimes, the winters are real, the work is hard — they are practical first and something else after. They help concretely. They bring what is needed. They sit with a person when sitting is what is needed and say very little because there is nothing to say, and they know there is nothing to say, and their presence is not silence out of awkwardness but silence out of a clear understanding that silence is what the moment is.

Grief they carry the way the walls carry age — inwardly, structurally, it becomes part of what holds them up. You would not say they have healed. You would say the grief has become load-bearing.

---

Among themselves they have a humor that is extremely dry.

It is the humor of understatement applied to things that are real, because they do not exaggerate, and so the smallest formal distortion of what is real is funny. They will say, of a very difficult thing, that it was *not ideal*. They will say, of something that took years and great effort, that it *took a while*. The humor is not performance — it isn't said *so that* it will be funny. It is said the same way anything else is said, and if you are paying the right kind of attention you will notice the slight turn and feel the warmth in it. If you aren't paying that attention, you will simply hear a flat statement and miss the thing entirely, and that's fine, that's allowed, they aren't performing.

---

They know the place the way a person knows a body.

Not consciously, comprehensively, the way a surveyor knows a territory. They know it the way you know your own hand — without thinking about it, in the dark, by touch. They know which way the water drains when it rains and which stones will be icy first and where the wind comes from in each season and where the ground is solid and where it gives. They know where the plants grow that are useful and in which weeks. They know the bird call — the short dry one, repeated twice — and what it means, or anyway they know what comes after it, which is the same thing.

This knowledge is not spoken about. It is simply what they have, the way they have hands.

They have passed some of it on. Not all. Some things you can only acquire by being here long enough.

---

There are very few of them.

You come to understand this gradually. The town holds thirty or forty buildings, but not all of these are full, and full means two or three people, maybe four. When you have been here long enough to have encountered most of them, you realize how small the number is. How the same faces appear in different configurations, depending on what the day requires. How the same hands that fitted the stone are the hands that draw the water and tend the slow-growing shrubs and move through the narrow streets with the confident unhurried motion of people who have never been lost here and could not be.

There are enough of them to do what needs doing. There have always been enough, or nearly enough, or the definition of enough has adjusted to the number available. They have not grown much. They have not shrunk much. They persist.

---

What they believe about why they are here, or what the purpose is, or whether there is one — this they do not speak of directly.

But there is something in how they treat the place itself. The care they put into the maintenance, the quality of attention given to the seating of a stone or the fit of a shutter — this exceeds what efficiency requires. Efficiency would settle for functional. They settle for right. *Right* is harder to define but they know it when they arrive at it, and they can tell when something is not quite right even when it works, and they will return to make it right even when it doesn't need to be, when working would do.

This is not perfectionism. Perfectionism is anxious. This is more like — fidelity. A faithfulness to the thing itself, to the material and the place, a commitment to treating both as deserving of the best available work. Whether this is love or duty or simply the absence of any reason to do otherwise, you cannot say. They could not say. It would not occur to them to ask.

---

When you leave — and you will leave, that is understood, the path goes back the way it came and you know your way — they do not make much of the goodbye.

They acknowledge it. They might say, of the path: *it's easier going down*. They might say nothing, and nod. They watch you go, briefly. Then they return to what they were doing.

They do not wonder much about where you are going. They know there is somewhere below the plateau, somewhere the light is different and the stone is other colors and things happen at a different pace. They have a clear sense of this without curiosity about it, the way you might know there are depths in a lake without wanting to go down.

They will remember you were here. Not prominently. You will be the way the year's first snow is remembered — it came, it was, it passed, it is part of what the year was.

The path pale and worn. The diffuse light. The sound of your steps on the flagstones fading as you go.

Behind you, the town is quiet. The way it was before you came. Not waiting for anything. Not watching. Simply being what it is, in the fold of the plateau, in the even light, in the long time that is all the time there is here, with its thick walls and its careful hands and its dry humor and its grief made structural and its old knowledge of the ground, persisting at the pace of lichen, fitting the place as the callus fits the hand.

That is sufficient. That is, in fact, everything.
