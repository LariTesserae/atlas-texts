## What Lives in the Settling Basin

---

### The Consortium

The oldest and most sovereign inhabitants are the bacteria. Not a single species but an interlocking guild, a *consortium* in the technical sense microbiologists use: organisms that could not survive without each other, metabolisms that hand off product to product down a chain. They have been here since before the machinery arrived, were here when the depression first deepened enough to hold water reliably, and they will be here — performing variations on the same chemistry — long after the flywheel has finished its conversion into a stratum.

What they do is reduction. Iron reduction. Sulfate reduction. Carbon reduction. The anaerobic deep is their domain, and they run it with a thoroughness that has no analogy in any familiar economy — nothing wasted, no byproduct that isn't someone else's substrate, the entire energetic yield of the place's buried organic matter extracted over years and decades through a process that produces, as its most visible outputs, the ochre streaks in the grey clay and the soft soundless bubbles at the surface.

The ochre and the grey are not incidental to them. The ochre is their exhaled breath, in a sense — iron that was ferrous and is now ferric, oxidized not by oxygen (there is no oxygen down there) but by their chemistry, their hunger, the way they have learned to strip electrons from things that other organisms would find inert. The grey is the reduced iron, the iron they have worked on. You could read the coloration of the clay as a map of where they are most active. The streaks of ochre where oxidation crept in through cracks: these are the frontier zones, the edges where the anaerobic deep meets the surface world, where different guilds compete for position.

In the bacterial sense, the basin is extremely crowded. Millions per gram, the standard estimate, though estimates vary and the standard itself is hard to feel when looking at grey mud. They have no weight you can perceive, no sound, no smell beyond what their metabolic waste provides — the hydrogen sulfide note that you catch occasionally, not strong here, not the full rotten-egg of a swamp, just a faint mineral sulfurous suggestion, as if the air has memory of something that happened below it. What they have is *consequence*. The gas rising through the mud is theirs. The particular chemistry of the water that saturates the clay is theirs. The rate at which the iron pipe joints are dissolving into the basin — slowly, but not as slowly as they would in dry air — is theirs. The machinery's slow erasure is substantially their project.

They are not aware of the machinery. They have no awareness of anything. But they are treating it with the same patient thoroughness they bring to everything else, and in some number of decades the pipe joints will be rust-flour mixed into the clay, indistinguishable from the iron they reduced from some earlier input, and the consortium will be unperturbed.

### The Moss

The moss does not look like a thing that decides, but it has made a decision about this basin, and the decision is total.

What covers the better-drained margins in that dense green felt is not uniform — look closely, which is the only way to see it, and the surface resolves into a mosaic of species. There is a pleurocarpous moss spreading laterally in flat mats, its stems pressed to the substrate, moving outward at the rate of centimeters per year. Beside it, or underneath it, or interwoven with it, something with smaller leaves and a more upright habit, reaching toward the diffuse light with modest ambition. In the wettest zones nearest the basin center, *Sphagnum* has established itself in a small colony — not enough to call the place a bog, not even enough to call it bog-like, just a fist-sized patch of the characteristic pale green, but *Sphagnum*, which means something, which means the moss community is doing what moss communities do: building, incrementally, the conditions in which *Sphagnum* thrives, acidifying the water, retaining moisture, making the substrate a little more itself.

*Sphagnum* is an organism of extraordinary and slightly sinister patience. It is largely dead by mass — the lower portions of its stems are dead Sphagnum, compressing slowly into peat, and it is this dead mass that holds water, that creates the sponge effect, that gives bogs their particular character. The living Sphagnum is only the green tip of a long history of dead Sphagnum. The plant is in some sense a machine for making peat, for converting living material into a preserved and waterlogged archive, and it has been doing this since the Carboniferous with only minor updates to the design.

The small colony here has not yet tipped the basin's chemistry. But it is working on it. Given centuries — and the basin seems inclined toward centuries — it will have opinions about this place that the current residents cannot anticipate.

The broader moss community has a relationship with the machinery that is one of the more interesting dynamics in the basin. Wherever metal sits against the substrate, where a pipe joint rests on the mud or the drum's buried end meets the clay, moisture concentrates, and where moisture concentrates, moss finds purchase. The rust provides grip; the rough oxidized surface is better than smooth steel; the warmth differential between metal and mud — metal cooling faster at night, warming faster in the day — generates condensation that the moss intercepts. The moss is not interested in the machinery as such. But the machinery has become, from the moss's perspective, a set of unusually reliable moisture-concentrating substrates, and the moss has responded accordingly. The buried end of the drum is ringed with a collar of dark green. The pipe elbows sprout moss from every horizontal surface. In twenty years this will be more pronounced; in fifty, the machinery will be partly upholstered; in a hundred, moss will be the dominant visual fact of those surfaces and the metal will be visible only in glimpses.

The moss does not feel patient. It does not feel anything. But it behaves as a patient thing behaves, which in the long run is the more important quality.

### The Rushes

*Juncus effusus*, most likely — soft rush, the most common of the genus in wet ground across the northern temperate world, the one that grows in these tight clonal fists with dark-green cylindrical stems that are neither round nor flat but something between, and small brown flower clusters partway up the stem that, if you are not looking for them, you may mistake for a structural feature of the plant rather than its reproductive effort.

Each fist of rushes is, underground, a single organism — a dense rhizome mass from which the aerial stems emerge, spreading outward at the perimeter as the rhizome extends and dying back at the center as the older sections of rhizome decay. The rush clumps at the basin edge are old enough that some of them have developed the hollow center of a mature clump, the individual stems at the core dead and pale, the living growth concentrated at the outside ring. They look like small fortifications from above, circular, with the dense upright stems reading as parapets. They are not fortifications. But they do create a microhabitat in their interior — sheltered, humid, with the dead stems providing both structure and substrate — that differs meaningfully from the open basin floor around them.

Something has used these interiors. The mud inside the clump near the eastern pipe sections shows an impression that could be a resting site — a slight hollow, smooth-edged, the kind of compression that recurs. The water vole, perhaps, or whatever made the tracks, uses the rush clumps as way-stations, as places where the exposure of the open basin floor is briefly replaced by something more concealed. The rushes, for their part, are indifferent to this use. But the ongoing small mammal visitation is what keeps the center of those clumps from filling entirely with the debris of dead stems — the passage of a body through the interior, the nesting behavior, maintains a clearance that the rush itself does not maintain.

The relationship is not mutualistic in any strict sense. The vole does not benefit the rush; the rush does not particularly benefit the vole. But the forms of each have influenced the habits of the other, and without the rush the vole would route differently through the basin, and without the vole the rush clumps would look slightly different than they do. They have made each other's background in small ways. This is the kind of relationship the basin specializes in.

The sedge blades at the center — pale, sparse, looking uncertain — belong to a different enterprise. They are *Carex* something, and their presence at the center is both tenuous and telling. The basin center is too wet for most sedges; the few that persist there are doing so at the margins of their tolerance, surviving in years when the water table drops to the lower end of its range and struggling in years when it doesn't. They are not succeeding here, not in the sense of expanding. But they are maintaining a presence, which in difficult conditions is its own kind of success. They will be here next year, looking uncertain, doing what they are doing, which is surviving.

### The Vole

The tracks belong to a water vole, or something that presses into the mud with the dimensions and gait of a water vole: four toes on the front feet, five on the back, the prints small and closely spaced, the stride short, the path not straight but characteristically irregular — a water vole moves with the detour-taking, non-committal purposefulness of an animal that is always partly distracted by the nearness of cover.

It does not live in the basin. The water table is too high for burrowing; a water vole's burrow system requires something to dig into that isn't simply saturated mud, some bank or firmer ground. But the basin is on its range, or at the edge of its range, and it passes through for reasons that may include the rushes' shelter, the possibility of plant material to eat (the rhizomes of rushes are edible, the sedge blades are edible, the moss is largely not but it harbors invertebrates that are), and possibly simple geography — the basin is on the way between places.

Water voles are in decline across most of their European range, for reasons mostly concerning predators and habitat loss, and any water vole track in any given place carries this weight whether or not the individual vole has any awareness of it. This one is, as individuals, probably fine — at the moment, in the limited sense of moments, it is alive and moving through the world, exploring the basin's available substrates, testing what is available. Its lifespan will be brief, a couple of years at most, and its connection to the basin will remain exactly what it is now: visitation, impression left in the mud, occasional passage through the rush clumps.

The tracks disappearing under the pipe joint are most likely explained by the vole passing beneath the pipe in a gap between the metal and the mud, a gap of perhaps five centimeters, which for a water vole is adequate. It emerged on the other side and continued in some direction. The tracks on the other side were in a part of the mud that drained slightly faster and had dried to a crust that didn't accept the prints. Or it had changed direction, having decided, as animals do, that the purpose of the errand was complete, the thing investigated, the route no longer worth continuing.

There is one additional thing about the vole: it is swimming, in a sense, in the bacterial consortium's world. Its body is covered in their relatives; its gut is populated by distinct strains doing distinct work; when it dies and is incorporated into the mud, the consortium will process it just as it processes everything else. The vole and the bacteria have not met in any meaningful sense but they share the same long project — cycling the basin's matter through its forms, maintaining the quality of the place through their combined attention. The vole does it by eating and being eaten and defecating and dying. The bacteria do it by reducing everything available to them. Neither of them knows that they are working together. But the basin knows — if the basin can be said to know anything — in the sense that the basin's character is the product of both, among others.

### The Succession of the Machinery

The machinery has no life. But it houses it, and this is worth a paragraph.

The flywheel housing, where the rust is thickest and the surface most irregular, has been colonized by a biofilm — a thin community of bacteria and algae and possibly a cyanobacterium or two, the grey-green-black surface layer that appears on wet metal in still conditions and that most people see without registering as life. A biofilm is a structured community, not merely a smear; the organisms within it communicate, produce shared extracellular material that acts as housing and transport network, develop gradients of oxygen and chemistry across their microscopic depth that allow different organisms to occupy different niches within a layer that might be half a millimeter thick. This particular biofilm is producing mild acid as a metabolic byproduct, and that mild acid is accelerating the corrosion of the metal beneath it. The biofilm is not trying to destroy the machine. The biofilm is not trying to do anything. But it is, in effect, doing so.

Inside the pipe joints, where trapped moisture meets trapped darkness, something fungal has established itself — white threads visible if you look into the end of one of the pipe elbows, a mycelium working its way through the flaking rust and whatever organic material has blown or washed inside over the years. Fungi do not care about iron. But they can work with what is available, can find carbon in unexpected places, can tolerate conditions that would end most visible organisms. The mycelium in the pipe joint is processing the small amount of organic debris that has accumulated there: leaf fragments, dust, the bodies of small invertebrates that came in and did not come out. It is unhurried.

In the junction between the concrete pad's bottom surface and the mud below, where a slight gap has opened as the pad tilted, a different community has assembled — moist, dark, protected from above, in contact with the saturated clay below. Isopods, probably, though they are not visible right now; collembolans; mites; small worms navigating the interface between concrete and clay. This is the typical community of such spaces, the assemblage that assembles wherever there is moisture and organic matter and some shelter from direct exposure. They are not spectacular. They are everywhere.

What they are doing, collectively, is the same thing the whole basin is doing: taking what is there and cycling it forward. The isopods eat the decomposing material, producing feces that the bacteria process further; the collembolans graze the fungal threads; the mites eat everything smaller than themselves. The concrete pad, which was poured to support something and now supports nothing, is supporting in fact this community of small detritivores who are indifferent to its original purpose and who are, in their small way, beginning to colonize the surface, beginning to bring the concrete pad into the basin's long work.

### The Basin Itself, As Organism

This is not a scientific category. But it is a real thing.

What the basin does, considered as a whole, is what every organism does: it takes in material and energy, processes them through internal structure and dynamics, maintains a characteristic internal state, and produces outputs that differ from the inputs. It takes in water, atmospheric carbon, organic matter blown in from outside, the machinery (which it is still digesting, which may take centuries), and the occasional passing vole. It processes them through the bacterial consortium, the moss, the rushes, the invertebrate community, the slow tilting of the concrete, the freeze-thaw cycle working on the pipe joints, the gas bubbles rising through the methane-saturated clay, the condensation falling from the flywheel to the mud. It produces — as output, as the thing it releases back to the world — carbon dioxide in the bubbles, hydrogen sulfide in traces, water vapor in the morning, rust particles migrating into the clay where they will be reduced and oxidized and reduced again.

It has a character that persists across seasons. The cool wet temperature range it prefers — ten to fourteen degrees — is not accidental; it is the result of the water's heat capacity, the lack of canopy, the local microclimate created by the basin's form and the basin's wetness, and it reinforces itself, because the species that have established here are ones that can tolerate that range, and they maintain the conditions that created that range, and the conditions are self-stable.

It has memory. The marks left in the clay — the vole tracks, the finger-impression in the substrate — are temporary memory, erased in days. But the stratigraphy is permanent memory: each layer of the clay, each shift in the pollen record that would be found if you cored into the deep mud, each change in the rust horizon, is a record of what the basin has experienced and processed. In this sense everything that has happened here is still here, just deeper, just more compressed, just more converted — but available in principle to whoever is willing to read a core.

What lives in the basin, then, is the basin. The bacteria, the moss, the vole, the biofilm, the mycelium, the collembolans, the Sphagnum working patiently toward its long-term project, the sedge blades persisting against difficulty, the rushes sheltering what passes through — all of these are, in one sense, distinct organisms with distinct genealogies and distinct futures. But they are also, in another sense, the basin itself, the basin being alive in the only way anything is alive: by being a temporary concentration of activity and structure and matter, maintained by the continuous processing of everything that comes through, persisting against entropy through the turbulent quiet work of its many parts.

The machinery is part of this now. It came from outside but it is no longer outside. The basin has accepted it as input, has begun its long digestion, has incorporated it into the roster of habitats and substrates through which its many lives are cycling matter and gas and time. The flywheel with its pewter glow in the diffuse light is not waiting to be reclaimed or restored; it is already being reclaimed, is already in the process of becoming something else, is already — from the basin's slow perspective — barely distinguishable from the ochre streak in the grey clay, which was once iron in a pipe joint, which was once iron ore smelted somewhere far from here, which was once — before the smelter, before the mine — iron in a different kind of rock, in a different kind of basin, in a different slow process that was also, at each moment, continuous and thorough and patient and engaged in work that no individual part of it could have named.
