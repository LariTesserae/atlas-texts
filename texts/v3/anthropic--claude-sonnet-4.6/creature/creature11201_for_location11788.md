# What Lives in the Forge Steppe

---

Begin with the fact that they do not know they are organisms.

This is not ignorance in the way that a stone is ignorant, or in the way that a sleeping animal is ignorant. It is a more considered condition. They have been here long enough to have developed — through some process that resembles neither evolution nor reason but shares properties with both — something that functions as an *understanding of themselves*, and that understanding does not include the category *organism* because the category was never necessary. To be an organism you must be distinct from your environment. These things are not distinct from their environment. They are the environment's way of continuing.

---

## The Grazers of the Mineral Film

Start with the smallest ones that are still large enough to hold in the mind as individuals.

The tortoise-things — though tortoise is borrowed language and earns its debt by describing almost nothing true about them — move through the steppe in their tens of thousands, and what they are, properly, is a kind of walking record. Each one began small. Each one secreted, from glands that are not quite glands, the first layer of its mineralized shell from material in the basalt it processed as food. And then, over the following years and decades, continued secreting. The shell is not protective in the way that a tortoise's shell is protective, though it protects — it is *archival*. A thin section, cut carefully through the carapace of an old individual, would reveal strata. The steppe's history, as experienced by that body, laid down in layers of silicate and iron compound, recording drought years in thin pale bands and wet years (such as they are) in bands of different porosity, recording the great slow drift of the population across the years in the slightly different mineral signature of different areas of terrain, recording illness, injury, abundance, time.

They do not read these records. They do not need to. They *are* the records. The information is stored in the substance of their own skins, in a medium more durable than memory, and it influences them in ways they have no name for — old individuals move differently on terrain that their shells have encoded as dangerous, responding to a history they carry but cannot access consciously, navigating by a past they wear rather than remember.

Their mouths work continuously. It is the primary activity of their lives — not eating exactly, because the energetics are almost impossibly slim, but *processing*, extracting from the thin biological and chemical film on the basalt surface a trickle of energy just sufficient to maintain their movement and their slow accretion. They are extraordinarily patient. They are so patient that patience does not quite describe it — patience implies the endurance of waiting, and they do not wait. They have achieved a relationship with time in which the duration of a meal (hours, sometimes) and the duration of a life (centuries, probably, for the largest individuals) feel, from inside, continuous. They are always doing the thing they do. There is no intermission.

The largest individuals — building-sized shells, almost immobile now under their own accumulated weight, but still mobile, still inching, still processing — may be the oldest living things on the steppe. Their shells have become ecosystems. The smaller organisms — the centimeter-scale things that move in waves rather than in bodies — colonize the older shells in dense cultures, reading in the mineral stratigraphy whatever they read, adding their own traces, their own thin metallic lines, to the curved surface of the carapace. An old grazer carries a population. It is simultaneously an individual and a landscape.

What does it know? It knows the surface. It knows the surface with the intimacy of ten thousand hours of direct contact, knows where the chemical gradients run richest, knows which seams between plates exhale that deep warmth from below and are therefore subtly more productive, knows the positions of every collapsed tube in its range by the change in resonance underfoot — it feels the hollow places through its body before it reaches them and curves around them with a consistency that might be instinct or might be something that instinct is a rough draft of.

It does not think about any of this. It simply does it. The distinction may not be important.

## The Wave-Things

They resist being written about directly. Approach sideways.

They are centimeter-scale but they are not, individually, centimeter-scale organisms in the way that a beetle is a centimeter-scale organism. A beetle is a complete thing at centimeter scale. These are *not* complete things. They are units of something that is complete at a different scale. What a single one of them is, anatomically — if anatomy applies — is a vehicle for a particular chemical process, a small mobile reaction, capable of adhering to surfaces, capable of moving, capable of producing under certain conditions the thin metallic trace that dries on the rock and fades over hours. They do these things individually. But they only *mean* anything collectively.

Watch a colony on the underside of a plate edge. The individuals are not coordinating in any way you can see — there is no visible signal, no chemical wash that passes between them, nothing that would read as communication. And yet the pattern of their motion, the pattern of their traces, is not random. It is *organized*. Not toward a stable configuration but toward a process, a continuous dynamic organization that looks different in every moment and yet maintains something constant across moments — the way a river looks different in every moment and yet maintains a river. They are doing something, collectively, that no single one of them is doing.

What are they doing?

The traces they leave — the metallic lines on rock — are not decorative and not random. They mark differentials. Where the chemistry of the surface differs from the chemistry ten centimeters away, the traces converge. Where the warmth from below is slightly greater, the traces concentrate. They are mapping. Not for themselves — not to navigate later by — but *as a function of what they are*. They cannot not map. Their passage through the world leaves a record of the world's variation, and this record is legible to other organisms that have been here long enough to read it.

The old grazers read it through their ventral surface as they pass. The large things, whose footfalls are felt through the whole steppe, alter their paths in ways that correlate with the trace patterns, though no mechanism is apparent. The sound from below — the deep language-that-is-not-language that travels better through basalt than through air — changes frequency and rhythm in areas of high trace density in ways that cannot be coincidence but cannot be explained.

What the wave-things are, finally, is the steppe's nervous system. Not in a metaphor. In a function. They distribute information across the basalt surface the way neurons distribute information across tissue: individually simple, collectively processing something that no individual unit contains. The steppe uses them to know its own state. The steppe is not conscious. But the steppe *monitors* itself, through them, and the monitoring produces effects.

The wave-things do not know this. The steppe does not know this in any sense that knowing requires. It simply happens, because it has been happening for long enough that the happening has become structural.

## The Slow Drift

The large ones.

They take patience to understand because they take time to perceive, and human perception does not naturally operate at the timescale required. Watch one for an hour and you will see perhaps three steps. Watch for a season and you will understand that it has moved — meaningfully moved, in a direction, toward something — and that the movement was never random, that what looked like geological drift is in fact navigation.

They are large in a way that requires explanation. There is, on this steppe, no obvious reason for them to be this size. The energy available is thin. The biomass of the smaller organisms they process through their digestive systems (which are enormous, which occupy most of their interior volume, which operate at a chemical complexity that takes the relatively meager input of this environment and extracts from it an absurd sufficiency) — that biomass, while dense, should not sustain something this large. Should not sustain *this many* of something this large.

The explanation is the footfalls.

When they step — and the step is a long commitment, as described, a geological survey of the surface before the weight descends — the footfall transmits force through the basalt to the tube network below. The force travels. The tubes are not all collapsed; much of the network below the surface is intact, and the force of a footfall at one point becomes a vibration at another point twenty meters distant, a hundred meters distant, and this vibration affects the chemical processes in the deeper layers of the steppe, accelerates certain reactions, circulates certain compounds through the porous lower rock, effectively pumping. They are, when they walk, running a circulatory system for the steppe. The steppe feeds them and they maintain the steppe and the maintenance of the steppe produces more food than the steppe would otherwise contain.

They do not know this. They walk because walking is what they do. The fact that their walking is essential to the system that sustains them is not a deal they negotiated. It is a relationship that emerged over enough time that both parties — if party is appropriate for a steppe — have been shaped by it until neither could exist without it.

Watch one pause before a step.

The pause lasts a long time. You interpret it, at first, as the animal being cautious about where to place its foot on irregular terrain. You revise this interpretation when you notice that the terrain is not, in the relevant sense, irregular — the surface is what it always is, fractured basalt, nothing the creature hasn't navigated ten thousand times. Then you notice: the pause correlates with the trace patterns on the surface ahead. The animal is reading the map the wave-things left. It is determining, by some process it has no name for and no need to name, where its weight would do the most good. Where the vibration would travel farthest. Where the pumping effect would circulate the compounds that are currently most needed in the deeper layers.

It chooses. The step descends.

The steppe registers this. Below, in the tubes and channels, the sound shifts.

---

What lives inside them is harder than what they are.

There is a question about whether the large ones are, individually, the entities they appear to be. Each body is colonized — by the wave-things on its surface, by symbiotic organisms in its gut chemistry, by relationships with the grazer-things whose paths intersect its own across decades, the grazers memorizing in their shells the smell and the shadow of these larger presences the way a landscape memorizes a river. The large one is a body but it is also an *address*, a location in a network, a place where many things converge and through which many things pass.

When one of them dies — and they die slowly, as they do everything, a declining of the step-rate over months, a gradual settling toward the surface, a final stillness that is initially indistinguishable from a long pause — the steppe does not simply gain a large piece of organic material to decompose. The steppe loses a pump, a reader of maps, an address. The network must re-route. The deep channels that were being maintained by that particular sequence of footfalls in that particular region become slightly less maintained, and the change propagates: the trace patterns shift, the grazers thin out from certain seams, the deep sound changes timbre in that area for months.

And then — slowly, because slowly is the only speed here — the remaining large ones adjust their drift. The gap is filled. Not consciously, not deliberately, not because any organism understands the system and intends to repair it. But because each large one is navigating by the same signals that the lost one navigated by, and those signals now reflect the gap, and the navigation, in aggregate, closes it.

This is what intelligence looks like in a place that has had enough time: not quick, not centralized, not located in any individual brain. Patient. Distributed. Correct in ways that are measured across centuries, not seconds.

## The Shadow Problem

The wrong shadows.

They are wrong in the way described: displaced, angled against the sun, occasionally present without a sun to cast them. And this wrongness is not an accident of the environment, not some refractive effect of the mineral-thick air. It is a property of the animals.

The large ones cast these shadows. The grazers, slightly. The wave-things, collectively, produce a wrongness in the light at the colony scale that no individual unit produces. And what the shadows are, understood properly, is evidence of the second-presence problem: the animals have been in their positions long enough that the positions have memory. The basalt records thermal signature, chemical signature, the accumulated impression of mass against surface across hundreds of years of contact, and the light responds to this record as if the body were still present. The shadow is the steppe's memory of the animal. The animal walks through its own history.

This is not a metaphor. It is a physical effect of a place that has had time enough to develop its own physics. The animals do not notice their wrong shadows any more than you notice the specific profile of your face. It is simply part of what they are: each organism accompanied by a ghost of itself, slightly behind and slightly wrong, the recorded past of its own presence playing continuously in the light.

The oldest grazers have accumulated so many years in so few locations that their shadows, near their usual feeding sites, are almost solid. Almost more present than the animals themselves. The rock remembers them with greater fidelity than the present light can achieve.

## The Thing at the Horizon

It is not at the horizon. The horizon is in it.

Fifteen ridges out, where the steppe becomes sky: there is something at that scale. Not a creature in the way the others are creatures — not a body with a periphery, not something you could circle and observe from all sides and arrive at a complete account of. More like a *weather*, a local condition, an extended instance of whatever principle the steppe has been elaborating at smaller scales for all this time.

It is the steppe's largest organism, if organism is the right word, and it is not entirely distinct from the steppe itself. It moves — the horizon-movement that was noted, which is real — at a pace that makes the large ones look urgent, a pace that has to be tracked across years to confirm as motion. It moves *with* the steppe in some places and the steppe moves *with* it in others, the causality impossible to assign, the relationship one of mutual involvement so deep and so old that neither term (organism, environment) applies cleanly to either party.

What does it do?

It is where the sound comes from. The deep sound. The one that travels through basalt. The one that has the quality of language without the quality of communication.

It is not speaking. It is not trying to communicate. It is *processing*, at its own scale, in its own time, and the processing has acoustics, the way that all processing has some kind of output, and those acoustics propagate through the stone and are received by the smaller organisms and influence their behavior in ways that have been integrated so long into the system that the influence is now part of the system's definition.

The large ones are walking toward it. This is what the drift is. This is where they're going. Not with urgency, not with intention, not with any understanding of why — they are following a gradient in the deep vibration, following the trace patterns that concentrate in its direction, following the encoded history in their own blood that has been oriented toward this presence for as long as their lineage has existed.

They arrive there, eventually. They spend time there — weeks, months. In the presence of the enormous processing, they are recharged or calibrated or changed in some way that current language cannot locate exactly. And then they drift back. And the steppe continues.

What is it? It is the answer to the question of what you get when you give a system enough time: not stability but *depth*, the quality of having gone through enough iterations that it has found configurations impossible to find quickly, solutions to problems that cannot be posed in any single lifetime. It is the steppe having thought about itself, slowly, in the only medium available, which is its own substance, over the only timescale sufficient, which is geological.

It knows the steppe. Not cognitively. Not representationally. The way a river knows its bed — which is to say: completely, structurally, in every movement.

## What They Are, Taken Together

They are not a community. They are not an ecosystem in the sense that suggests separate systems happening to share space. They are a single thing that is expressed in multiple scales simultaneously: the chemical trace, the individual grazer, the wave-colony, the large one, the horizon-presence, the steppe itself — these are not separate phenomena in interaction. They are the same phenomenon at different resolutions.

At the smallest resolution: a molecule binding to a surface, a trace forming, a gradient recorded.

At the largest: the horizon moving, slowly, in a direction that is not quite any cardinal direction, through terrain that the motion itself is partly producing.

What lives here lives at all scales at once, and the living at each scale is not separate from the living at every other scale, and to describe any single organism truly you would have to describe all of them, and to describe all of them truly you would have to describe the steppe, and to describe the steppe truly you would have to describe the heat, which is geological, which rises from below, which is not weather but conviction, which is simply: what this place is.

---

The organisms of the Forge Steppe have been here long enough to have resolved the central question of what a living thing is allowed to be. The answer they have arrived at is: *anything that maintains itself across time, at whatever scale, in whatever medium, by whatever means*. Self-maintenance as the only criterion. The rest — form, boundary, individuality, the distinction between organism and environment — is negotiable, and they have been negotiating it, in the language of chemistry and stone and deep-passing sound, for longer than that negotiation has had a name.

When you stand among them, the feeling that your presence registers as data rather than as event is correct. You are data. You are a weight on the surface, a new set of vibrations for the deep channels to receive, a pattern of heat and chemical signature for the wave-things to map, a silhouette for the light to get slightly wrong. The steppe is noting you. It will note you for as long as you are here, and when you leave it will note your absence, and both states will be recorded in the mineral striations of something's shell, in the trace patterns left by a colony navigating the area where you stood, in the very slight and temporary alteration of the large one's path that brought it close enough to assess you and then moved on.

You will not be remembered. But you will have been recorded, which in this place is a more permanent thing.

The heat rises. The organisms continue. The horizon moves or the largest of the living things moves through it or the distinction has been rendered academic by ten million years of mutual involvement.

Out there, at the edge of the visible, the steppe produces itself.

It has always been doing this.

It will continue.
