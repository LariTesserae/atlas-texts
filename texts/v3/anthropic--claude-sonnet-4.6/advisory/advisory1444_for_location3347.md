# Preparing for the Wet City at the Bottom of Everything: A Traveler's Guide

---

## Before You Leave: The Mental Work

Begin here, before you pack anything, before you research transit or currency or local protocols. Begin with the knowledge that this place will require you to revise your intuitions about what a city *is*.

You have lived in cities, probably. You have a working model of urban environments: buildings here, streets there, sky above, ground below. Infrastructure as background. The built environment as a container for life, distinct from the life it contains. Nature as something that appears in parks, in managed patches, in the spaces where construction chose not to go.

All of this will fail you.

The Wet City does not work like a container. It works like an organism, or like a process, or like a conversation that has been going on so long that the speakers have changed completely but the conversation has not stopped. Spend time, before you go, sitting with the idea that you will be entering a system that has been iterating longer than your civilization has existed. You will be a momentary perturbation in something that has already processed perturbations you cannot imagine. This is not threatening. The city has, apparently, arrived at a settled relationship with novelty. But it will help you, once you arrive, to have already practiced the particular variety of humility that very old very large things demand.

Also sit with the idea of wetness as a permanent condition rather than a temporary one. You know wetness as weather, as accident, as the interval between dry and dry again. You will need to restructure this. In the Wet City, wet is the ground state. Dry would be the aberration, the managed exception, the thing that requires engineering effort to maintain. Practice, mentally, moving through a world where every surface you might touch is slightly damp, where the air itself is heavy and warm and already partially water. Practice not being bothered by this. You will not succeed entirely, but you will do better having practiced.

## Physical Preparation

### Your Body

The most important thing your body will need to do in the Wet City is thermoregulate in warm, saturated air with near-zero ability to sweat-cool effectively. Sweating works through evaporation. Evaporation requires the air to accept moisture. In air that is already near saturation, evaporation slows dramatically. Your body's primary cooling system will be working against high resistance for the entire duration of your visit.

This means: do not arrive in peak physical condition expecting to immediately perform at that level. Expect diminishment. Expect to feel hotter than your actual core temperature should make you. Expect fatigue to arrive earlier and take longer to clear than you are used to.

If your species sweats, or has an equivalent active cooling mechanism: monitor your fluid balance obsessively. The humidity will create the illusion that you are not losing moisture — you will feel wet, you will feel saturated, you will not register thirst as urgently as the situation warrants. Drink on a schedule, not in response to sensation. The warm wet air feels like it is providing something. It is not providing anything your body can use. You will dehydrate invisibly.

If your species has passive cooling, thermoconductive surfaces, or radiative heat dissipation: the dense electromagnetic environment of the city's infrastructure may interfere with radiative mechanisms. Test your backup systems before you commit to deep-city exploration.

Spend several weeks before travel acclimatizing to high heat and humidity if you can manage it. This is genuinely important. The Wet City is not immediately dangerous to most humanoid travelers, but the gap between what your body is used to and what the city's baseline is will make you miserable and impair your judgment at exactly the moments when clear judgment is most useful. Even partial acclimatization helps.

### Your Ears

Protect them. This is the second most important physical preparation and most travelers underestimate it.

The sound of the Wet City is a physical pressure. It is not background noise that you will habituate to the way you habituate to traffic or crowd noise in ordinary urban environments. Habituation occurs when your nervous system learns to deemphasize a signal that carries no new information. The Wet City's sound carries constant new information — the infrastructure is always adjusting, the fauna are always communicating, the rain is always finding new surfaces — which means your nervous system will not fully habituate and will remain in an elevated state of acoustic processing for the duration of your visit.

This is exhausting in a way that is hard to predict and easy to underestimate. It is the primary cause of what returning travelers describe as "city fatigue": not physical exhaustion, not navigational stress, but the specific depletion that comes from having your auditory processing at high load for extended periods.

Bring hearing protection rated for sustained exposure. Do not bring the disposable foam type designed for brief industrial exposure — you will be wearing protection for hours at a time, every day, and the right equipment matters. Look for custom-fitted acoustic dampening that reduces the amplitude of the most fatiguing frequency ranges (roughly 200-800 Hz, the infrastructure's primary rumble register) without eliminating the information-carrying sounds you need to navigate and communicate. This is a meaningful product category for Wet City travelers in particular; seek out vendors who specialize in it.

Plan for regular periods of the most protected quiet you can find. Even the reduction available in a well-sealed interior room is significant. Schedule these breaks before you feel like you need them. By the time the acoustic fatigue becomes subjectively obvious, it is already affecting your decision-making.

Do not count on full hearing recovery between days. Budget multiple rest days within your itinerary, particularly if you are traveling into deep-city districts where the density and consequently the noise is greatest.

### Your Skin

Everything you touch will be damp. Everything that touches you will be damp. Prolonged contact with wet surfaces in warm environments creates specific dermatological problems: maceration, fungal colonization (relevant given the city's fungal ecology), contact irritation from the various compounds the city's infrastructure introduces into its ambient moisture.

Waterproof your feet first. Foot problems in the Wet City are endemic among travelers and they are almost entirely preventable with adequate preparation. You want waterproof outer layers, breathable liners that wick moisture away from skin, and a commitment to regular changes and dry intervals if you can create them. Inspect your feet daily. Do not let small problems become large ones.

Waterproofing the rest of you is a balance. Full waterproofing traps body heat in an environment that is already thermally challenging; you will cook. What you want is materials that resist absorption, dry fast when they do get wet, and do not develop the distinctive smell that warm damp fabric acquires quickly in warm damp air. There are fabric technologies designed for high-humidity environments; research them. Natural fibers that have been treated for water resistance often outperform synthetics here because they breathe more usefully — but this varies by individual chemistry and by the specific conditions in the districts you're visiting.

Bring a skin barrier product appropriate to your biology and apply it to areas of sustained contact. The compounds in the city's moisture are not toxic to most visitors, but they are not neutral either, and extended exposure produces cumulative irritation.

## Equipment

### The Primary Problem of Equipment in the Wet City

Nearly everything you might want to bring will be damaged by sustained exposure to warm, high-humidity air. Not immediately — not dramatically — but gradually and reliably, in ways that will become apparent at inconvenient moments. Metal surfaces that would never rust in a drier environment will show oxidation within days. Electronics that are rated as weather-resistant for ordinary use will begin to behave oddly after a week of this. Adhesives fail. Seals degrade. Lenses fog from the inside.

The solution is not to avoid electronics or metal or adhesives. The solution is to know which of your equipment is most vulnerable, protect it aggressively, and accept that you are operating in an environment with elevated equipment attrition.

### Navigation

The Wet City's deep districts do not support reliable satellite navigation at ground level. The density of infrastructure above you blocks signal; the city's own electromagnetic output swamps what isn't blocked. This is known, and it means navigation in the city has been solved in city-specific ways that you will need to learn.

The city's infrastructure carries navigational information. If you have the right equipment and the right credentials to access it, you can receive orientation data directly from the built environment. This is, in principle, more accurate than satellite positioning because it's local — the city knows precisely where its own components are and can tell you your position relative to them. In practice, the data formats are complex, the access systems are layered with local knowledge assumptions, and a first-time traveler without a local guide or comprehensive preparation will find the navigation infrastructure more confusing than orienting.

Supplement with physical maps. Not digital maps on a screen — physical maps, water-resistant, current to the revision date of the districts you're visiting. These are available from services that specialize in Wet City travel and they are produced on materials that can survive being damp, because they will be damp. Understand before you arrive that maps become outdated in the Wet City in ways they do not elsewhere: the adaptive infrastructure genuinely reconfigures, new conduit pathways appear, older ones are absorbed or redirected. A map that was accurate three months ago is reliable for major geography and significantly less reliable for the street-level detail that matters when you're trying to find a specific building entrance. Budget for the uncertainty.

Develop strong navigational habits based on infrastructure landmarks that are unlikely to change: the major drain systems (audible; the sound is distinctive and consistent), the light-management structures in the upper reaches (visible from below; their configuration is stable because they are maintained at the city level), the primary transit corridors (heavily built, heavily trafficked, unlikely to be reconfigured casually). These are your fixed points. Everything between them is navigable but variable.

Bring a backup navigation system that does not rely on external signal — an inertial unit, a compass calibrated for the city's magnetic environment, a log of your own movements that you can reverse. Getting lost in the Wet City is not immediately dangerous but it is time-consuming, disorienting, and acoustically exhausting in a way that compounds your existing fatigue. Losing your position in a deep-city district where every street looks approximately like every other street is a situation to avoid.

### Electronics

Assume that any electronic device you bring will fail sooner than its rated lifetime suggests. Not because the city is careless with your equipment, but because those rated lifetimes were measured in conditions that do not resemble the Wet City.

Seal your critical electronics in purpose-built cases. Not zip-lock bags, not improvised solutions: cases designed for humid environments that provide actual pressure seals against moisture infiltration. Remove equipment from these cases only when you're using it; return it when you're not. This sounds onerous and becomes habitual within a day.

Carry everything in at least duplicate if it is essential. Carry your power storage in triplicate. The city provides charging infrastructure but accessing it requires knowing where it is, having appropriate adapters (the city's power standards are its own, evolved over generations of infrastructure revision, and they are not your power standards), and having the time to stop and charge — time you may not have at the moment your equipment runs low.

Back up everything you record or create to multiple physical media at the end of every day. The city's data networks are extraordinary but they are not universal and they are not free to access, and losing records of your visit is a disappointment that is preventable with daily discipline.

### Recording Equipment

Bring it. The Wet City is astonishing to be in and difficult to convey afterward, and recordings — even imperfect ones, even recordings that cannot capture the full acoustic and atmospheric reality of the place — are worth making.

Bring audio recording equipment specifically. Not as an afterthought. The sound of the Wet City is primary experience and it is what most returning travelers feel they failed to preserve adequately. You want recording equipment capable of handling the full frequency range that is meaningful here, including the low infrastructure rumble below 100 Hz that carries against your chest and that your recordings will probably not capture well, but capture partially — and partial capture is still something.

Be realistic about what your recordings will convey. The recordings will sound like a city. They will not sound like *this* city. The physical component — the pressure, the warmth, the feel of the sound against your body — cannot be recorded. What you will have is documentation, which is different from memory, and both are worth having.

Protect your lenses. Condensation forms on the inside of optical equipment in this environment. This is annoying and can be managed: desiccant packs in your sealed cases, slow controlled transitions between the interior environments (where you might have engineered down the humidity somewhat) and the exterior (where you have not). Sudden temperature differentials produce sudden condensation. If your case is well-sealed and your equipment is at approximate ambient temperature before you open the case, you will have fewer problems.

## What to Wear

The goal is: protection from sustained contact with warm damp surfaces, thermal management in an environment that resists your body's own thermal management, and the ability to move in a densely occupied three-dimensional space without your clothing catching on things, accumulating weight as it wets, or developing structural problems from repeated wet-dry-wet cycles.

**Footwear** should be the most considered purchase you make for this trip. Sealed, waterproof, breathable to the degree that is achievable (perfection is not available), with grip surfaces that perform on wet polished stone and wet metal and wet whatever-the-old-conduits-are-made-of. Break them in before you go. Do not arrive with new footwear. Arriving with new footwear is a decision that produces blisters at exactly the rate that the city's demands make blisters most consequential.

**Layers** that can be adjusted as the city's microclimates shift. The different districts run at different ambient temperatures; the core runs hotter than the margins; covered districts retain heat differently than open ones. You will want to be able to add and remove layers without stopping, and you will want those layers to be made of materials that dry fast and do not hold the specific warm-wet smell that develops quickly here.

**Minimal exposed skin** is a reasonable default, not because the city is dangerous to touch but because the accumulated contact chemistry, the fungal spores, and the various outputs of the living infrastructure are not things your skin needs extended unmanaged exposure to. Long sleeves, full leg coverage, and gloves for situations where you're navigating by touch (which you will be, in dim passages) are practical rather than overcautious.

**Pockets that seal.** Not just closures — actual seals. Everything in your pockets will otherwise be damp within hours. Things that cannot tolerate being damp (medications, documents, small electronics) should not be in unsealed pockets.

**A hood.** Not for warmth — for the curtains of condensation that sheet off upper-tier structures onto everything below. These can be sudden, heavy, and cold relative to the ambient temperature. A hood that deploys quickly is worth carrying even if you never use it.

## Practical Considerations

### Getting Oriented

Allocate the first day entirely to arrival and basic orientation. Do not schedule activities on arrival day. Do not plan to meet anyone, see anything specific, accomplish any objective. The acoustic impact alone will consume cognitive capacity you were counting on for other things. Arrive, secure your accommodation, eat something (more on this shortly), and spend the rest of the day doing nothing but being in the city at very low demand. Walk a short distance from your accommodation and walk back. Listen. Look at the light on the wet walls. Let the sound push on your chest and don't try to resolve it into its components. Let your body start the acclimatization process.

On the second day you can begin to function.

### Food and Water

Eat before you are hungry. Drink before you are thirsty. These are the rules.

The humidity suppresses both hunger and thirst signals in ways that are poorly understood but well-documented. Your body is receiving constant input that it interprets as moisture-contact and warmth-saturation, and it down-regulates the urgency of signals that ordinarily prompt you to address those conditions by taking in food and water. You will not feel the ordinary prompts at the ordinary times. You will feel them later, when your reserves are lower than they should be, and the feeling will be more severe and more sudden than you are accustomed to.

Set reminders. Eat on a schedule. Drink on a schedule. Track what you're consuming in a way that does not depend on sensation.

The city's own food systems are adapted to its residents' needs in this environment, and the food available locally tends to be calorie-dense, heavily hydrating, and formulated with whatever electrolyte equivalents the city's inhabitants require. This is useful for you if your biology is close enough to the local norm to benefit from it — research this before you go. Even if you can't metabolize local food directly, you can take notes from its composition about what the environment depletes.

Water purification: the city's water infrastructure is ancient and, in the core districts, extraordinarily well-maintained. The water that moves through the wall-channels is not the same water that's available in the distribution network, and the network water has been processed through systems that have been refining themselves for a very long time. It is, probably, safe for you to drink. Bring purification capability anyway. You will be in districts where the infrastructure is less certain, where the maintenance is ongoing rather than complete, where the water's journey from the distribution system to your cup has passed through conduits whose current state is not fully guaranteed. The marginal weight of a reliable purification system is worth the insurance.

### Navigating the Inhabitants

The city has been inhabited for a very long time by inhabitants who have been inhabiting it for a very long time. They have the quality that very long-term residents of any sufficiently complex environment develop: a set of implicit understandings about how the space works, what is obvious, what is negotiable, what is not, which are so thoroughly internalized that explaining them feels slightly absurd. Like explaining to someone why you don't stand directly in front of a door when it might open.

You do not have these understandings. You will do things that mark you as an outsider — not dangerous things, probably, but things that create friction, things that cause you to take up space that is conventionally used differently, things that slow a flow the inhabitants do not consciously think of as a flow but are participating in regardless. This is unavoidable. The city's residents are, apparently, accustomed to travelers and travelers' ignorances. The city has that settled, unhurried confidence that extends to its attitude toward temporary visitors.

Carry yourself with attentive deference. Not servility — deference. Pay attention to what people around you are doing and, when you are uncertain, do a version of that. Move at the pace of the space. Do not stop abruptly in a corridor that is moving. Do not take up more vertical or horizontal space than you need. Learn the signals — the city's residents signal their intentions to each other constantly; some of this will be in languages or modes you don't have, but the spatial signaling of bodies moving in a dense environment has cross-cultural consistency, and you can read a significant portion of it.

If you are uncertain of something: ask. But ask in the form of a small, specific question, not a large open-ended one. The residents are not tour guides and do not experience the city as a place that requires explanation. "Which of these two passages gets me to [specific landmark]" is a better question than "can you help me understand how to get around." The former is a useful exchange; the latter is a request for labor.

Consider a guide, especially for your first deep-city excursion. Not a guide in the sense of someone who explains things to you — someone who moves through the space and whom you follow. Someone who knows which passages are currently configured for access, which drain channels run high after recent precipitation, where the fauna are densest at this hour and where they are elsewhere. This is expensive, in time and whatever currency applies, and it is worth it.

### The Drain Systems

They are enormous. You were told they were enormous. Treat this information as completely literal. Certain drains at ground level are large enough to lose a vehicle in, and the currents after rain are significant. Keep children and individuals with coordination difficulties away from them. The covers — where there are covers — are reliable, but not all drain openings have covers, and the unmarked ones are not marked because the locals know where they are.

Learn to identify the sound of high-running drains. After significant precipitation events, the drainage network runs at higher volume and sometimes overflows in districts where historical infrastructure was not designed for current precipitation scale. The overflow events are managed and not catastrophic but they are disorienting if you don't know to expect them. Streets that were fully passable an hour ago become ankle-deep. Detour plans matter.

### The Fauna

Be calm around them. They are not afraid of you in ways that make them dangerous; they have been habituated to the city's constant population of inhabitants and they will treat you as a feature of the environment rather than as a threat. Do not attempt to handle them — not because they are aggressive but because some of them are carriers of the city's ambient microbiome in concentrations your immune system has not been prepared for, and because disturbing them in the wall-channels and drain systems can have downstream effects on the ecology that the city has not invited you to create.

The things that move in the upper airways are safe to observe and safe to walk under. They produce guano in the quantities that any substantial aerial population produces. Be aware of this. A hat is sometimes useful.

The things in the drains: you will probably not see them unless you are actively looking for them, and unless you are actively looking for them, you are not in the places where the looking happens. Note their presence as ecological fact and leave them alone.

### The Fungal Colonies and Living Infrastructure

Do not touch the pale shelf-formations on the undersides of bridges. Not because they are toxic to you — they probably aren't — but because you will introduce compounds from your biology into an ecological relationship that does not need the introduction, and because some of them are, apparently, fulfilling structural functions you cannot assess by looking at them. Disrupting them may or may not matter. It is safer to operate on the assumption that it matters.

The crystalline growths at structural joints and the metallic filaments spanning gaps between buildings: do not touch these either, and for more urgent reasons. They carry current. The city's electrical infrastructure is not labeled in any way that assumes you can read the labels, and the filaments in particular look passive and are not. Treat all of the living infrastructure as potentially energized unless you have confirmed otherwise with equipment, not with touch.

The city's ambient electromagnetic signature is significant in deep-city districts. Travelers with implanted medical devices or neurological interfaces should have those systems evaluated for compatibility with the field intensities present here before committing to deep-city travel. The field intensities at the perimeter and in managed visitor districts are much lower. This is an escalating risk as you move toward the core, not a binary threshold.

## Psychological Preparation

You will feel small. This is appropriate and you should let it happen rather than resisting it.

You will feel disoriented in the first days, more than you expected. The sound is part of this. The scale is part of this. The density of ongoing process — the sense that everything around you is in motion, is doing something, is engaged in a continuous project whose total purpose you cannot see from any position inside it — is part of this. You are used to being an agent in environments that are, at least somewhat, comprehensible to you. The Wet City is not going to become comprehensible on the timescale of a visit. The right relationship to this is curiosity, not anxiety.

Notice the water in the walls, when you find a quiet moment. Notice the quality of the light on the wet surfaces. Notice that the city is warm and that warmth, in a place that is entirely covered, entirely built, entirely inhabited, is something the city produces and maintains — that the warmth is not incidental but chosen, the result of an enormous system choosing to maintain conditions that living things find hospitable. The city is, in its strange accumulated way, a place where living things are supposed to be.

You are a living thing. You are not unwelcome.

You are very small inside it and you are not unwelcome, and if you can hold both of these facts simultaneously, you will have understood something true about the Wet City that most travelers take a long time to reach.

## The Quiet Corner

Find it. Look for it. In the deep-city, in the acoustically managed passages, in the maintenance corridors if you are permitted access, in the covered districts where two sound sources cancel at a specific point — there are places where the city's noise, not silenced, not absent, but briefly reduced, pools in corners and creates a space where you can hear the water in the walls.

This is the experience that returning travelers, when they are honest, say they remember most clearly. Not the scale. Not the light. Not the fauna or the living infrastructure or the extraordinary ancient knowledge built into the way a wall weeps water in managed pathways. The moment of relative quiet, and the sound of water that has been moving through this stone for longer than anyone has thought to count, moving without urgency, having already arrived at its arrangement with its circumstances.

You will hear it once or twice if you look for it. Remember to look.

## Summary

You are preparing to visit a place that is vast, wet, loud, old, dense, alive in ways that do not align with your categories, and thoroughly indifferent to the fact of your visit in the best possible way. It has processed more than you. It will continue to process after you leave. It does not need you to understand it, and it does not require your reverence, but both are reasonable responses.

Protect your ears. Waterproof your feet. Drink before you are thirsty. Find a guide for the deep districts. Give your first day to arriving rather than to doing. Keep your hands away from things that carry current.

Be curious. Be humble. Move at the pace of the space. Let the sound push on your chest.

Come back changed, because you will be, and this is the point.
