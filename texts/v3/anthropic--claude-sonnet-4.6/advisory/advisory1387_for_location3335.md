# Preparation Guide: The Hollow Under Bright Nothing

*For the multiverse traveler approaching a site of active ontological formation*

---

## First, Understand What You Are Going To

Most preparation guides begin with practical matters — what to wear, what to carry, what to eat beforehand. This one cannot begin there, because the practical preparations will not make sense until you understand what kind of place this is, and what kind of place this is will actively resist your attempts to categorize it quickly.

The Hollow Under Bright Nothing is not a cave. It is not a ruin. It is not an ecosystem in any sense your biology education prepared you for. It is a site of *ongoing formation* — a place where the basic processes by which matter decides to become itself have slowed to a pace a human can perceive, or have been made visible by some property of the local physics that is not well understood. The rock is forming. The light is sourceless. The creatures have renegotiated their relationship to the survival imperatives you are accustomed to reading in living things.

What this means, practically: you are not visiting somewhere that has finished. You are visiting somewhere *mid-sentence*. The Hollow does not care that you have arrived. It will continue its processes without adjustment. You are, in the most literal sense, a tourist at a construction site where the construction has no foreman, no blueprint, and no anticipated completion date.

This should produce in you a specific kind of humility before you arrive. Not awe — awe is what happens when you encounter something finished and overwhelming. This is something else: the humility of being in the presence of a process that has not yet produced its noun.

Pack for that kind of experience. Prepare your nervous system for that kind of experience. Everything else follows.

---

## The Arrival

**You cannot practice arriving here.** The entry is a sideways slippage — not a direction you know, not a technique you can learn in advance. What preparation can offer is this: a description of what the transition feels like, so that you recognize it when it happens rather than trying to reverse it out of reflex.

The moment before arrival will feel like ordinary ground ceasing to be ordinary. This is not dramatic. It is quiet. The ordinariness resolves into something else, and the something else is the Hollow. Many travelers report that the transition feels less like moving and more like *understanding something differently* — as if the Hollow was always where you were, and you have simply stopped reading the space as something else.

**Do not fight this.** The slippage is not a threat. It is access. Travelers who resist the transition often find themselves returned to the ordinary without having entered, sometimes repeatedly, sometimes with a growing sense of anxiety around the transition itself that makes subsequent attempts harder. The entry to the Hollow rewards a particular disposition: not passive, exactly, but *available*. Willing to have the ground change its interpretation of itself beneath you.

**Preparation for arrival:**
- Slow down before you expect to arrive. Literally slow your walking pace. The slippage seems to favor a contemplative gait.
- Do not be in conversation when you arrive. Not because conversation is dangerous but because the Hollow will interrupt it, and the interruption, if you are mid-sentence, is more disorienting than the arrival itself.
- Do not have headphones in, music playing, a podcast running. You need your full sensory attention available. The Hollow will not announce itself. You will simply be there.
- Note where you are just before you slip. Not for navigation purposes — you cannot navigate back the same way — but because many travelers find it grounding, on return, to remember that the ordinary existed before and will exist again. The memory of the ordinary is a useful anchor.

## What to Wear

**Warmth for approximately 50°F / 10°C.** The Hollow is cool without being cold — a persistent cool that will not worsen but will not improve. You will be in it for an indeterminate period, and indeterminate periods at cool temperatures require more insulation than a brief visit would.

The key word is *layering*. Not because the temperature will change — it will not — but because the Hollow tends to produce a kind of phenomenological time dilation, a quality of experience where what felt like fifteen minutes was forty, or where what felt like two hours was twenty minutes. You do not know how long you will be there. You cannot know in advance. Dress for a range of durations.

**Specific recommendations:**

- A base layer that manages moisture. You will not sweat — the Hollow does not produce exertion — but the condensation that fills the air is persistent. Your skin will feel subtly damp after a while. Wool or technical fabrics work better than cotton here.
- A mid-layer of real warmth. A fleece, a light down piece, anything that traps air well. This goes over the base.
- A shell layer that you can remove. Not for rain protection — there is no rain in the Hollow, only the periodic drip of condensation — but because the shell gives you something to remove and fold and hold, which gives your hands something to do during the periods when your hands will badly want something to do.
- Long pants. The floor of the Hollow is not rough but you will want to sit at some point, and sitting cross-legged on cool packed substrate while wearing shorts is a sensory distraction you don't need.
- Closed-toe shoes. This is not negotiable. The floor has creatures — pale, slow, mostly minding their own business — and the ethical expectation of the space is that you will not step on them. This requires attention and a firm sole. Sandals make the attention harder. Also: the seam of wet along the far wall. You may want to crouch near it. You do not want wet feet for the rest of your visit.

**Do not wear anything you cannot bear to have marked.** The walls of the Hollow are forming — actively, continuously, leaving mineral deposits the way limestone leaves itself on everything it contacts. If you brush against a wall, and you may, you will carry a faint pale trace of it. This washes out, but some travelers have reported that the mark resists washing in ways that are mildly interesting and not harmful. Wear something you won't be upset about.

**Head covering:** Optional but recommended. The ceiling is four to five feet overhead. At close approach — if you want to examine what is forming there, or observe the ceiling creatures, or watch the process of the drip leaving one of the ceiling things — you will want to tilt your head back rather than lean your head forward, and a hat brim will interfere with that. Go hatless, or choose a close-fitting cap. But something over your ears if your ears run cold: the temperature in the Hollow is one of those temperatures that sneaks into ears and stays.

## What to Carry

**Carry less than you think you need.** The Hollow is not large — roughly two furnished rooms in volume, closer to one — and excess gear will make you feel encumbered and managerial in a space that rewards stillness and attention. A well-stuffed pack becomes a negotiation with a small space. Bring what serves you and leave the rest outside.

**Essential:**

*Water.* Bring it even though there is moisture in the Hollow and water along the seam. The seam water is real and cold and clean-feeling, but you do not know what the mineral content of water accumulating in a space of active geological formation actually is, and the sensible traveler does not experiment with unknown mineralogy on an empty decision. A half-liter is sufficient. More will go undrunk; the Hollow suppresses the sensation of thirst in most visitors, probably because the cool air reduces the body's felt need for hydration even when actual need continues. Drink before you arrive. Drink when you leave. Bring the water as insurance.

*A small notebook and a pen that works in the cold.* Not a phone. There are several reasons not to bring a phone into the Hollow — the light will make your screen unreadable, the silence makes ringtones an obscenity, the camera will not capture what the camera usually captures — but the deepest reason is this: the Hollow is a place for sustained attention, and phones are machines for interrupted attention. Leave it in your pocket if you must bring it, and commit to leaving it in your pocket.

The notebook serves a different purpose than documentation. Yes, you may want to note observations — the spacing of the wall creatures, the pulse interval of the seam, the dimensions of the partially-built structure — but the more important function of the notebook is *pacing*. Writing is slow. Writing forces you to stay in one place, to look at one thing long enough to form words about it. The Hollow rewards this. The Hollow is, in some sense, a place that only reveals itself to attention that has committed to staying for a moment, and writing is a form of that commitment.

Use a pen that doesn't require warming up. Gel pens freeze in the cold and the Hollow is not quite cold enough to freeze them but is cold enough to make them unreliable. A graphite pencil, or a ballpoint.

*A small light source.* Not because you will need it — the Hollow is inexplicably, genuinely bright, and the sourceless light is sufficient for everything — but because having it available will reduce the background anxiety of being underground without a light source. You are an organism with instincts, and your instincts will notice that you are below the surface in a chamber without windows, and they will want a backup. Give them a backup. A small headlamp, or a keychain light, kept in your pocket and never turned on. Its presence is the thing. Its use is not required.

*Something to sit on.* The floor is cool packed substrate and you will want to sit at some point, and sitting on cool packed substrate for more than a short while begins to transfer cold to your body at the sacrum and hips in ways that linger. A small foam sit pad — the lightweight kind backpackers carry for rest stops — takes up almost no space and removes this limitation. Alternatively, a folded jacket placed under you works. The goal is insulation from the floor, not cushioning. The floor is actually fine to sit on; it is only the temperature transfer that becomes a problem over time.

**Things you might consider:**

*A magnifying glass.* Pocket-sized, the kind with a 10x or 15x lens. The creatures on the wall will not flee from close observation — they have different treaties around being observed — and a magnifying glass allows you to examine their precise form without getting close enough to risk contact. The ceiling things are too high for this to be useful. The floor creatures are too small and too dispersed. But the wall creatures, at magnification, are worth the weight. You will see details that resolve the question of whether they are insects in the direction of *not quite, no*, in ways that are interesting rather than disturbing.

*Waterproof matches or a lighter.* Not because you will build a fire — you absolutely will not, cannot, and should not attempt to build a fire in the Hollow, both ethically and practically — but because naked flame in a space of anomalous light is interesting to observe. The Hollow's light does not interfere with flame. Flame casts normal shadows in the Hollow, which is notable because the Hollow's own light produces only faint puzzled shadows. A small candle lit in the Hollow will throw dramatic shadow-work across the forming walls that you cannot produce with your body alone. This is, strictly speaking, a luxury item. It is also one of the more beautiful things the Hollow permits. Bring it if you have space.

**Do not bring:**

Food. You will not be hungry. The Hollow suppresses appetite more completely than it suppresses thirst, and eating in the Hollow feels wrong in a way that is hard to articulate but consistent across visitor accounts. The wrongness is not nausea. It is more like inappropriateness — the sense that eating is a concluded-thing behavior and the Hollow is not a concluded thing and there is some categorical mismatch between the act and the place. You can eat before and after. Eat before.

Anything you are afraid of losing. The Hollow returns what you bring in when you leave — nothing stays behind that was yours — but some items come back subtly different, changed in ways that are minor and not harmful but worth knowing. A watch may run slightly wrong afterward for a day or two before correcting. A book brought into the Hollow may have pages that are faintly damp and remain faintly damp longer than they should. A phone camera, if used, will have images that are lit differently than they were exposed — the Hollow's light doesn't translate, and your camera will have guessed, and the guess is usually warm and strange. If you don't want these things happening to an item, leave it outside.

## Physical Preparation

**Your body will behave strangely in the Hollow, and most of this is fine.**

*Eyes:* The light level is that of late-morning overcast. Your pupils, expecting underground darkness, will arrive dilated and will adjust. During this adjustment — which takes longer than normal pupil response, perhaps three to four minutes rather than one — the Hollow will feel painfully bright. This passes. Some travelers find it useful to wear sunglasses during the transition; others find this creates an uncanny effect when they remove them, as the light seems brighter by comparison. There is no correct answer. Try sunglasses if you are light-sensitive; skip them if you are not.

After adjustment, your vision in the Hollow will be unusually good. This is the light doing something anomalous with distance rules. You will be able to see fine detail at greater distances than you normally could, and this can produce a mild sense of unreality — the feeling that the space is larger than it is, or that your eyes are working differently. They are, in a sense. You are seeing by light that doesn't diminish. This is not a threat to your eyes. It is simply unfamiliar.

*Time:* You will lose track of it. Not because the Hollow is disorienting in a destabilizing way, but because the Hollow does not run on human time rhythms. There is no light cycle to anchor your circadian sense. The drip comes every forty seconds but you will lose count of the drip. The creatures do not move on any rhythm you can detect. The formation of the walls is slower than the geological pace that produces feelings of time passing in ordinary caves. Nothing in the Hollow gives you a clock signal. If time matters to you — if you have somewhere to be — set an alarm on the watch or phone in your pocket. Check it deliberately rather than assuming you know how long you have been there. Many travelers exit the Hollow convinced they were there for twenty minutes when they were there for two hours, or convinced they were there for most of a day when they were there for less than an hour.

*Balance:* The floor is reliable. You will not fall in the Hollow. But your proprioceptive sense — your body's awareness of where it is in space — may feel subtly wrong. This is usually described as a very mild feeling of the floor being either closer or farther than it should be, and it tends to pass within five minutes of arrival. Sit down when you first arrive if it troubles you. The floor is cool but trustworthy.

*Breathing:* The air in the Hollow is breathable but has a quality that some travelers find initially difficult — a density, a mineral presence, as if you are breathing air that is more fully occupied than usual. This is the active deposition process, the stone forming from what is in the air. You are not breathing stone. You are breathing air that has more mineral content than outdoor air, which is a familiar phenomenon in caves. The Hollow's version is slightly more extreme than most caves but not harmful. If you have significant respiratory sensitivity, bring yourself in slowly — step into the Hollow and stand near the transition point for several minutes before moving further in, allowing your respiratory system to adjust.

Take slightly slower, slightly deeper breaths than you normally would. This seems to help. Not dramatically slower — not meditative breathing, not a protocol — just a slight expansion of the breath, a slight drop in pace. The Hollow seems to favor this rhythm. Visitors who breathe quickly and shallowly report feeling more unsettled than visitors who breathe slowly.

*Temperature regulation:* Your core will be fine. Your extremities — hands, ears, toes — will feel the fifty degrees after a while. Wiggle your fingers and toes periodically. Move your ears (or massage them; most people cannot wiggle ears voluntarily). This is routine cold management and nothing the Hollow makes special.

**One thing to prepare for that is harder to explain:**

Many visitors report a moment, somewhere between ten and thirty minutes into the visit, of a very specific feeling. It is not panic. It is not dread. It is something more like the feeling of being in a room where you have just realized you are the only thing in the room that is finished. That everything else — the walls, the light, the creatures, the wet seam, the structure in the corner — is ongoing, and you are a concluded thing, a completed thing, a thing that has already happened, in a space where happening is still in progress.

This feeling is real and it is not dangerous. It is the Hollow's most honest quality. You are, in a profound sense, not native to a space of ongoing formation — you are a thing that has formed, a thing that has its current edges. The Hollow does not make you a stranger because it is hostile. It makes you a stranger because it is at a different stage.

The feeling usually passes within a minute or two. Some travelers find it passing into something beautiful — a sense of privilege at witnessing something before it understands itself. Some travelers find it remains as a low background hum for the whole visit. Either way, it is not a signal to leave. It is a signal that you are genuinely in contact with what the Hollow is.

Know it is coming. Name it when it arrives. *This is the feeling. I knew this was coming. This is fine.* That naming is more effective than distraction or reassurance.

## How to Behave in the Hollow

This section could be framed as ethics, or as etiquette, or as practical advice for getting the most from your visit. It is all three and the distinctions between those framings are less clear here than elsewhere.

**Do not touch the walls.**

This is the most important instruction. The walls are forming. They are mid-process. Whatever you leave on them — the impression of a hand, a mark, heat — becomes part of what they are forming into. You do not know what they are becoming. You do not know if your impression belongs there. The ethical position is not to impose it. Furthermore: the description of the location contains a genuine warning here, the possibility that pressing a hand to the becoming-wall might result in the hand being, in some sense, incorporated into the formation. This is not a metaphor. Treat it as the literal warning it is.

You may look as closely as you like. You may bring your face near the wall to observe the mineral processes. You may not touch.

**Do not touch the ceiling things.**

They breathe. They are alive in some sense. They are positioned where they are by some process of arrangement or preference that you do not understand and should not disrupt. Leave them where they are.

**Do not handle the floor creatures.**

They are not afraid of you and they will not flee and this is a trap for the curious, who may feel that the absence of fear is an invitation. It is not. The absence of fear is simply the absence of fear. It does not imply consent. The beetle-shape, the pillbug-wrong, the spider (especially the spider, which is ordinary and should be treated with the ordinary courtesy you extend to ordinary spiders, which is: observe it and leave it alone) — these are doing something. They are mid-process like everything else in the Hollow. Do not interrupt their loops and accumulations and slow purposes.

**Do not attempt to block the drip.**

The drip comes from the ceiling things. Blocking it disrupts something about their process and the seam's process and the relationship between the two that you are not equipped to evaluate. Watch the drip. Count it if you can. Lose count. Do not stand beneath it.

**Do not explore the structure in the corner beyond observation.**

The structure is the most tempting thing in the Hollow because it implies agency, implies someone or something made it, implies a relationship between this place and deliberate making that you can understand. Resist the temptation to touch it, to insert a finger into its partially-formed cavities, to test its solidity. It is mid-construction. You are not the builder. Act accordingly.

**Make your movements slow and deliberate.**

Not for the sake of the fauna — they are not startled — but for your own sake. Quick movements in the Hollow feel wrong in a way that compounds into mild disorientation. The Hollow is a space of slow process, and your body will be happier at the pace the Hollow suggests. Walk slowly. Crouch slowly. Turn your head slowly to look at things. Think of yourself as moving at a speed appropriate to the formation rate of rock, which is not fast.

**Speak, if you wish, at low volume.**

The silence in the Hollow is not silence of absence but silence of decision. It has arranged itself away from sound with care. You do not need to be mute — there is no indication that speaking is harmful — but loud sound in this space creates a specific kind of wrongness, a categorical error, like playing music in an operating theater. Not prohibited, but wrong. If you are traveling with a companion, speak quietly. The Hollow is a place for murmuring, not for carrying on.

## The Creatures: A Specific Note

The spider is the most important creature in the Hollow for psychological preparation purposes, because it is ordinary, and its ordinariness in context is alarming, and that alarm is informative.

The spider is frightening because it tells you something about the scale of the Hollow's wrongness. If the spider is ordinary, then the things that are not ordinary are not merely unfamiliar — they are *different in kind*, not just in degree, from the ordinary. The spider is your control group. Everything else in the Hollow fails to be what the spider is.

This is useful information. It means: when you look at the wall creatures and they do not flee, the reason they do not flee is not that they are particularly calm or tame. It is that the treaty by which fleeing is the correct response to an approaching large organism does not apply to them. They operate under different terms entirely. You are looking at something that is alive in a mode of aliveness that does not include you as a relevant variable.

**This should produce in you wonder rather than unease,** but wonder and unease occupy adjacent space, and the spider can tip you toward unease if you are not prepared. Know in advance that the spider is there and that the spider is ordinary and that this is the most frightening thing about it. Decide in advance to find this interesting rather than troubling. You will be more successful if the decision is made before arrival than if you make it reactively upon seeing the spider.

## Psychological and Cognitive Preparation

**Prepare to not understand things.**

This sounds simple. It is not. Most travelers arrive at unusual locations with an implicit expectation that the location will, eventually, make sense — that given enough time and observation, the logic will become apparent, the system will reveal itself, the interpretation will arrive. The Hollow will frustrate this expectation thoroughly.

The creatures' behavior does not resolve into a pattern you can name. The light does not have a source you can find. The structure in the corner does not clarify its origin or purpose. The seam's pulse does not fall into a rhythm you can count. You will observe these things and understand them better for having observed them, but *better* is different from *enough*, and *enough* is different from *resolved*. The Hollow will not resolve.

Practice this before you go. Spend some time with things you cannot fully understand and practice being comfortable there, in the place of not-quite-knowing. Meditation traditions sometimes call this *beginner's mind* or *not-knowing*, which is fine language, but the practice is simpler than any tradition: sit with something confusing and let it remain confusing. Notice the discomfort of non-resolution. Notice it passing, if it passes. Notice it not passing, if it doesn't. Practice being present in the middle of something rather than after it.

**Prepare to be witnessed without being regarded.**

The creatures in the Hollow will look at you — the wall creatures' stillness is a looking-at — without registering your significance. You are in the room with them. They are in the room with you. This does not make you important to them. They are engaged in something that precedes your arrival and will follow your departure and does not require your acknowledgment or your assessment.

Many humans find this subtly uncomfortable. We are accustomed to being registered by living things — to seeing the neural flash of recognition in an animal's eyes, to being fled from or approached, to being a variable in the survival calculus of everything we encounter. In the Hollow, you are not. You are present but you are not a factor.

This can feel like loneliness, or like invisibility, or like being very far from home. Know this might happen. It is not the Hollow rejecting you. The Hollow has no mechanism for rejection; it also has no mechanism for welcome. You are simply there, in a space that is not organized around your presence. This is actually the normal state of the universe and the Hollow is rare in making it legible.

**Prepare for your sense of completeness to feel strange.**

You are a finished thing. You have a history, a form, a set of stories about who you are that are largely settled. The Hollow is in the middle of having these things. This differential will announce itself to you and it will feel like something, and the something it feels like is hard to name in advance. Some travelers describe it as humility. Some describe it as grief. Some describe it as the most alive they have felt in years. It may be that the Hollow shows you, by contrast, what it is to be concluded — what it is to have arrived at your current form and stopped.

You might take something from this. If your life contains something that is still forming, something you are in the middle of — a work, a relationship, a self-understanding, a question — the Hollow tends to make travelers feel less urgency around that middle. To be mid-process is not to be incomplete. It is to be in process, which is different, and which the Hollow treats as perfectly valid. You may find that this settles something in you. You may find that you leave the Hollow with a slightly more generous relationship to your own unresolved things.

This is not guaranteed. But it is common enough to mention.

## The Sourceless Light: A Special Preparation

Understand before you enter that the light will change your relationship to your other senses.

When vision is fully provided for — when there is no darkness pressing in, no need to strain your eyes, no shadows creating ambiguity — your other senses move into a different configuration. They become supplementary rather than compensatory. You will hear things more carefully in the Hollow not because the sound is louder but because your nervous system, having been given adequate light, will distribute attention differently.

This means: the drip will be very present. The not-quite-sound of the wall creatures' possible motion will be noticeable. The almost-sound of the seam's pulse will be a real sensation. The quality of the silence — its texture, its intention, the way it has been arranged — will be available to you in a way it would not be if you were also trying to see in the dark.

It also means: you will feel the temperature more precisely. Fifty degrees as an even fifty degrees rather than as a cold that also has shadow in it, cold that also has darkness in it. The mineral quality of the air will be more distinct. The condensation on your skin will be more noticeable.

The Hollow's light is, in this sense, a gift to your non-visual senses. Accept it by giving them attention. Do not spend the entire visit looking. Spend some time listening to the drip and the silence around the drip. Spend some time with eyes closed, attending to the air. Spend some time with a hand very close to (not touching) the wet seam, feeling the cold it radiates.

You have been given full light in a place that should have none. Use it to see things clearly. Use the clarity to free your other senses. The Hollow is more legible to a full sensorium than to sight alone.

## On Returning

The return is disorienting and there is no way to fully prepare for it except to know it is coming.

The primary disorientation is the light. Ordinary outdoor light — even bright outdoor light, even noon sun — will feel insufficient for some hours after you leave the Hollow. This is the light's wrongness having recalibrated your expectations. Your pupils were not dilated in the Hollow; the Hollow's light was sufficient. But you have now experienced light behaving without the distance rule, without the square-law falloff, and your sense of what *adequate illumination* means has shifted. Outdoors will feel dim. Indoors will feel very dim. This passes within half a day for most travelers, within a full day for those who were in the Hollow for a long time.

**Do not drive immediately after leaving.** Not because your judgment is impaired in any legally meaningful sense, but because your perception of light conditions is temporarily calibrated wrong, and driving requires accurate light perception. Wait an hour at minimum. Wait until the world stops looking darker than you know it should be.

**Do not make large decisions immediately after leaving.** The Hollow produces a cognitive state that is, in some travelers, subtly disorienting in its aftermath — not confusion, but a heightened sense of everything being in process, everything being mid-formation, everything being still capable of being otherwise. This is philosophically true but it is not always a useful state in which to commit to things. Give yourself an evening.

**Eat.** The Hollow suppresses appetite, and you will be hungry without knowing you are hungry. The hunger will make itself known once you are back in a context where eating is normal. Eat something real. Something warm if possible. The Hollow's temperature is fine but your body will want warmth now in the way it didn't want warmth there, and food is warmth.

**Expect the faint pale mark.** If you touched a wall — which you should not have, but if you did — or if you moved too close to a wall and it touched you, you will have a pale mineral trace on your clothing. This washes out. If you find a trace on your skin, which is possible, it also cleans off. Do not be alarmed. You have not been marked in any permanent sense. You have simply been in contact with something that was in the process of depositing itself, and it deposited a little on you, the way chalk leaves itself on hands.

**The thing that does not go away quickly:**

You were in the presence of something that was becoming. The world you have returned to is mostly a world of things that have become. Rock that has been rock for ten thousand years. Light that obeys the inverse square law consistently and without exception. Creatures that flee and approach and regard you as a survival variable.

You will notice this. You may notice it for days. Everything will seem, very slightly, more settled than it should be — more concluded, more committed to its current form. This is not the Hollow making the world seem wrong. This is the Hollow having shown you, briefly, a different relationship to form-in-progress, and your perceptual system noticing, for a while, the absence of that.

It fades. But some travelers report that it never entirely fades — that they retain, from having visited the Hollow, a slight sense that everything around them is mid-process in ways that are invisible, that the stone in buildings and sidewalks and mountains is still in some sense becoming, slowly, at scales below perception. That the creatures around them are in the middle of something. That the light, even when it follows its rules, is following them *right now*, continuously, in a kind of ongoing choice to be the kind of light that diminishes with distance.

This is a reasonable way to live. The Hollow's vision is not wrong, exactly. It is just more visible there.

## A Final Note on Why You Should Go

There are locations in the multiverse that are beautiful. There are locations that are dangerous. There are locations that will teach you things about the outer structure of physics, or the range of possibility for biology, or the spectrum of ways consciousness can organize itself. The Hollow Under Bright Nothing is not primarily any of these, though it has something of each.

The Hollow is a place where you can be present at the moment before something understands itself. This is a very rare thing. Most of what exists has already understood itself, or has understood itself and been forgotten, or never had a self to understand. The Hollow is caught, somehow, in the continuous moment just before — just before the walls have decided to be walls, just before the creatures have negotiated the final terms of their treaty with existence, just before the structure in the corner has committed to being a structure rather than a possibility.

You, as a finished thing, cannot stay. You will pass through on your way back to the ordinary, to the world of things that have formed and are now doing the work of having formed. But for the duration of your visit, you will be in the presence of something that is still choosing, still becoming, still in the process of arriving at whatever it will be.

This is not a small thing to witness. Most of what you will see in your lifetime of traveling — most of what anyone sees in a lifetime of traveling — is aftermath. Result. The Hollow is the only place most travelers will ever visit that is reliably, completely, entirely process.

Go slowly. Pay attention. Breathe the mineral air. Count the drips until you cannot count them. Let the wall creatures not flee from you and let that be interesting rather than frightening. Watch the pale plant growing toward the sourceless light and toward something else that the light cannot account for.

And when the sideways slippage returns you to the ordinary world, and the ordinary world seems dim and settled and committed to its current form — walk through it knowing what you now know: that somewhere below, in a pocket of bright impossible nothing, the process continues without you, forming and accumulating and pulsing, entirely on its own terms.

It does not need you to continue. It was continuing before you arrived.

This should be, if you have prepared yourself correctly, the most comforting thing you will have learned on your travels.
