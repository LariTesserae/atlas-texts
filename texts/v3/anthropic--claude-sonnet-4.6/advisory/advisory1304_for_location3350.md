# Preparing to Visit the Lacunate City: A Traveler's Guide

---

## Before You Go: Fundamental Reorientation

The first and most important preparation for the Lacunate City is not logistical. It is cognitive. Most human multiverse travelers arrive having unconsciously prepared themselves for *more* — more stimulus, more color, more noise, more explanation, more legibility — or for *threat*, because threat is at least a known category of experience. The Lacunate City offers neither more nor threat. It offers a kind of structured withholding that your mind will initially attempt to process as deprivation or wrongness.

You must prepare yourself, in the weeks before travel, to practice finding adequacy in understimulation. This sounds simple and is not. The practical exercises that help most: spend deliberate time in genuinely quiet rooms without filling them with music or ambient sound or the comfort of a device. Notice water moving slowly. Sit with fog. Train yourself to find the moment when patience tips from uncomfortable into productive, because in the Lacunate City, nearly everything meaningful happens just past that threshold.

If you are someone who experiences anxiety when your questions go unanswered, or when you cannot map a space to some known template, or when you sense something is happening that you lack the perceptual tools to fully perceive — then you should do significant preparation around anxiety management before attempting this visit. Not because the city is threatening, but because it will generate these feelings continuously and in volume, and you will need to be able to be present through them rather than dominated by them. Consider working with a good meditative practice, or with whatever your personal equivalent is. The goal is the ability to notice a question arising and let it rest unanswered without that rest feeling like emergency.

This is not a city that will explain itself to you. This is perhaps the most important thing you can know.

## Physical Preparation

### Climate and Comfort

The city presents conditions that are cool but not cold — a persistent ambient temperature in what Earthly travelers typically classify as the low-to-mid fifties Fahrenheit, give or take variance from the warm-air thermal events that pass through with some regularity. The humidity is high without being tropical or oppressive; this is the humidity of an English autumn morning, the kind that makes itself known on skin and in hair without ever becoming the subject of complaint. You will not be cold if you dress for a cool damp day. You will not be hot under any circumstances so far documented.

**Recommended clothing:**

- Layering is your best strategy. A base layer, a mid-layer, something windproof but not heavy for the exterior. The warm-air events that move through can raise the local temperature enough that you'll want to be able to remove a layer without ceremony.
- Waterproofing is important not for rain — rain here is rare and brief — but for incidental contact. You will brush against vegetation that carries condensation. You will crouch to examine ground-level features and find the ground is perpetually slightly damp. You will touch a filament, or be unable to stop yourself from touching one, and your hand will come away feeling as though you touched mist that held its shape.
- Footwear should be comfortable for extended slow walking and should not have hard, resonant soles. The ground absorbs sound, yes, but footwear with a clear acoustic signature feels wrong in the Lacunate City in a way that is difficult to articulate and easy to feel. Soft-soled boots. The kind that make you quiet.
- Nothing that rustles. Waterproof nylon that crackles with movement is a mistake you will regret within the first hour. The acoustic texture of the city is quiet in a way that makes your own noise highly legible to you, and to whatever inhabits the periphery of the city's life. Bring gear made from materials that can be damp without becoming loud.
- Colors should be muted. Not because anything will react to bright colors — nothing is documented as reacting to anything you do — but because you will feel conspicuous in a way that interferes with the quality of attention the city rewards. Slate, moss green, deep charcoal, the gray-brown of bark. If you want to feel like a visitor rather than an intrusion, dress like the city's existing palette and it will help your mind settle.

### The Nose and Its Discomfort

The near-complete absence of smell is well-documented and reliably unsettling. Humans use olfactory information to calibrate aliveness — the smell of a living city, a forest, a meadow, another creature — and the Lacunate City strips this away almost entirely. There is no smell to the water. The vegetation has a faint, very faint, green-chlorophyll scent when you crush a leaf, but only then, only if you commit to destroying some of it, which seems inadvisable. The structures exhale warm vapor that carries no scent you can register.

Many travelers find this produces a low-grade dissociation, a feeling that the world is somehow not quite real, which is not a useful conclusion in the Lacunate City. You should know this is coming and prepare yourself for it. What helps, for some people: bring something with a meaningful personal scent — a small cloth with a familiar essential oil, something from home, something anchoring — not to use constantly, but to use if the dissociation tips toward distress. One breath of something known can reset the nervous system without taking you out of the experience.

Do not bring anything with a strong artificial scent. Perfume is inappropriate here in a way that goes beyond mere preference.

### Physical Stamina

The city does not demand athletic performance. The terrain is flat and the surface is walkable without technical skill. However, the kind of attention the city requires is physically tiring in ways that are easy to underestimate. You will be doing sustained low-intensity observation for hours. You will be crouching to look at things, standing to look at other things, stopping frequently, adjusting your focus. This is not strenuous but it is continuous, and it draws on a kind of concentrated stillness that exhausts different muscles than a hike would.

Plan for fewer hours of walking than you might in a city of comparable geographic spread. Plan for more stopping. Bring more food than you think you need, because whatever metabolic engine powers sustained attention will be working harder than usual. The food should be quiet — not in the acoustic sense but in the sensory sense. Simple things. Things that don't produce a lot of smell or take a lot of attention to eat. An apple is fine. A complicated meal wrapped in packaging that crunches is wrong.

Bring water. The water in the city's channels is visually clear and seems pure, but there is something about it that most travelers are reluctant to consume, and this reluctance has an intuitive wisdom to it that should be respected. Bring your own.

## What to Bring

### Documentation Equipment

The question of how to document the Lacunate City requires thought before you arrive, because what you will want to document — the slow shifts in surface translucence, the formation of new filaments, the quality of light-change across an hour — resists most standard documentation approaches.

**Photography:** Cameras work, and photographs are taken, but they consistently fail to convey what the place is actually like. The sourceless low light renders the structures beautifully but strips the sense of their aliveness. You will have photographs of tall gray things in gray light and they will look like architectural renders from a firm that has grown too fond of melancholy. They will not be wrong. They will not be sufficient. Take them anyway, because sometimes documenting the insufficiency of documentation is itself informative.

**Video:** Marginally better, but the pace at which anything happens is too slow for video to render it legible. If you are going to shoot video, commit to extremely long takes — minutes-long, unedited, with the camera absolutely still on a tripod — because what you're trying to capture requires more than a few seconds to become visible. Most travelers who bring video return with footage that shows a tall gray structure and then forty minutes of nothing apparently happening, and then, if they watch it through, a change in one translucent patch that required forty minutes to resolve. This is accurate footage. It will not be popular at your debriefings.

**Audio recording:** This is the most useful documentation tool, and it is worth investing in the best microphone you can bring. The sonic texture of the Lacunate City — the structures' almost-inaudible exhalations, the filament-tones when wind moves, the barely-perceptible machinery-cycling from within — registers more meaningfully in high-quality audio capture than it does in any other recording medium. The pauses between the cycling are worth capturing. Whatever is in those pauses may be the most important thing you come home with.

**Handwritten notes and drawings:** Bring them. Paper and a mechanical pencil. The act of writing in the Lacunate City produces a different quality of observation than digital note-taking, possibly because it is slower and requires you to commit to what you've noticed rather than flagging everything for later consideration. Draw things you're not sure you can describe. Draw the proportion of a structure against a filament. Draw the moss edge against the ground-material. The drawings don't need to be good — they need to be the record of your attention.

### Navigation Equipment

The city has no center, no landmark toward which everything else can be oriented, no visible edge in most directions. It does not produce the spatial experience that would allow you to navigate by landmarks in any conventional sense. The structures are individually distinguishable, if you look closely enough — they are not identical, each has its own arrangement of translucent patches and filaments — but cataloguing them as navigation aids requires extraordinary prior presence of mind.

Bring a compass. It works. The city exists on a physical plane where terrestrial orientation remains operative. Track your heading from arrival and leave yourself a reliable way back to your insertion point, because the city does not have the social infrastructure — no residents to ask, no maps posted — that allows you to recover from getting turned around in a human city. If you become uncertain of direction and your compass is not accessible, your options diminish very quickly to *keep walking and hope*, which is not a strategy.

Some travelers bring drones for overhead mapping. This has mixed results. The drones function, but the aerial perspective of the city is disorienting rather than clarifying: from above, the distribution of structures looks less like a city than like something that grew, which is accurate, and the pattern logic that you spent time trying to read from ground level becomes even less legible from the air. The drone footage is interesting. It does not help you navigate.

Bring a high-quality compass and a physical journal in which you record headings and pacing from known waypoints. Old-fashioned dead reckoning is what works best here.

## Mental and Emotional Preparation

### On the Questions the City Generates

You should expect to leave the Lacunate City with more unresolved questions than you arrived with. Not about the city specifically, though there will be those — what are the filaments doing, what is the cycling sound, what logic governs the distribution, what came through the vegetation and where did it go. These are legitimate unanswered questions and you should write them all down and not expect to resolve them.

But the Lacunate City has a secondary effect that travelers consistently report: it surfaces older, more personal unresolved questions. The quality of attention the city demands strips away the usual cognitive noise that keeps certain questions at a comfortable distance. You will find yourself, in one of the long corridors between structures, with nothing demanding your attention except the felt sky and the slow structures and your own mind, and your own mind will produce content you have not had the conditions to encounter in some time.

This is not necessarily unpleasant. It is sometimes profoundly useful. But you should know it is coming and not be surprised when you are thinking about your mother, or about a choice you made fifteen years ago, or about whether you have been honest about something important, in the middle of what is nominally a professional exploratory visit to an alien settlement.

Travelers who have a journaling practice find the Lacunate City significantly more productive than those who do not, because the journal gives the surfaced material somewhere to go without needing to be resolved on the spot. Bring the journal. Use it for both documentation and for whatever else needs to be written.

### On Loneliness

The absence of fauna sound — that undertone of living-rustling that your nervous system reads as *inhabited* — will register as a form of loneliness in many travelers, regardless of whether they consider themselves particularly social. This is not a character flaw. It is your nervous system doing what nervous systems do: reading environmental cues about safety and available support.

The Lacunate City does not provide those cues, because it is operating on a definition of inhabited life that your nervous system did not evolve to recognize. The structures are almost certainly alive in some meaningful sense. The filaments carry something. The surfaces record or relay or think. There is something in the bases of the structures that moves through walls without disrupting them. The city is not empty. It is just inhabited in ways that your alarm-systems cannot easily classify as company.

Accepting this distinction — *not empty* versus *companioned in any way that feels meaningful to you* — is important. The city is not lonely. You are lonely in the city. This is different. The city is not doing something to you; you are doing something to yourself in an environment that does not provide your usual correctives.

What helps: the warm-air thermal events. For reasons that are not well understood, the passage of warmer air through the city corridors reliably produces in travelers a sense of being welcomed, or noticed, or included in something, even though no one has ever documented a clear causal link between the thermal events and any intentional activity by the city. Nevertheless, this is where you will feel least alone, in those few minutes when the air shifts and the mosses move on their stems and the filaments change their frequency. Allow yourself to receive this, whatever it is.

### On the Filaments

Experienced Lacunate City visitors consistently advise new travelers to think carefully, in advance, about their relationship to the question of consciousness and what it requires.

Many people have never had to practically confront the question of whether something that does not resemble any familiar category of life — something that does not have a face, does not make sound, does not respond to you, does not even acknowledge your presence — might nonetheless be aware. The Lacunate City will force this confrontation at close range. The filaments that continue their pulse without acknowledging your touch. The surface that continues its slow thinking regardless of what you do in front of it. The structure-material that continues advancing into the earth at its geological pace without reference to you.

If you have strong prior commitments about what does and does not constitute awareness, the city will pressure-test them. If you are philosophically flexible on this subject, the city will be philosophically interesting. If you have never thought about it, the city will introduce the question abruptly and without anesthetic.

The preparation here is simply: think about it before you go. Sit with the question of what it would mean to be in the presence of something aware and not perceiving you. How would you behave? What would you want to do, and why? What does it mean to be polite to something that cannot receive your courtesy? Is politeness still meaningful then, and for whose benefit?

You don't need to resolve these questions before going. You just need to have encountered them before going, so that they are familiar terrain when the city opens them up in full.

## Practical Protocols

### On Touching Things

Touch the filaments if you must — and you probably must, because the description of their damp pulse will be irresistible and you will be standing next to one, and your hand will go out before you've fully deliberated. This is fine. The filaments do not respond. They will not recoil. Nothing appears to happen. You will pull your hand back with a feeling you may not be able to immediately name, which is roughly the feeling of having been fully ignored by something you suspected might be important.

The structures themselves: touching their surfaces is safe and produces a slight warmth, the warmth of something that maintains its own temperature, and occasionally, if your hand is still for long enough, you may feel something that might be the very edge of a structural exhalation, the almost-nothing of vapor escaping through a surface that does it very slowly. This is worth taking time for.

The vegetation can be touched without apparent harm to the vegetation. It does not respond either, but this is less unsettling, because plants do not respond in ways you expected them to.

Do not put your hand into the channels of water. This is not danger — there is no documented danger — but it is wrong in the way that defacing a work of art is wrong, even if the work of art will not protest. The channels are precise. They have corners. They were scored by something with intention. Do not muddy them.

Do not enter the structures. The material of their bases clearly can be entered — you will witness something enter, probably — but you cannot. There is no door. There is no moment where the material presents itself as passable by you. The thing that went through it had some quality you do not have. Attempting to push through the surface will result in pressing against something that has approximately the resistance of a wall, which is exactly what it is for you.

### On Timing and Duration

The city does not have a day in any normal sense — the light is constant in its dimness, varying only by cloud thickness rather than by hour. However, most travelers report that the city becomes most *itself*, most legible on whatever terms it offers legibility, in the second or third hour of sustained presence. The first hour is spent in nervous adjustment, in the initial over-monitoring that any new environment produces. By the second hour, if you have not filled the space with too much activity, you begin to perceive at the right scale for the city.

Plan to visit for a minimum of half a day. Less than three hours produces a traveler who has experienced novelty but not anything deeper. More than eight hours is probably beyond the concentration most travelers can sustain productively; the city will still be doing what it does, but you will be running on diminishing returns of attention.

Do not visit multiple times in quick succession. The Lacunate City requires processing time. It deposits something in you that has not finished settling when you leave, and returning too soon, before the first visit has resolved in your mind, produces visits that are cognitively muddled in ways that are hard to correct for. Give yourself at least a week between visits.

### Arrival and Insertion Point

Mark your insertion point with something physical, visible, and durable. Not a flag — nothing that will move with wind and thereby confuse you. A cairn of several small stones brought from outside the city is appropriate; the dark ground material does not provide adequate contrast for anything scratched into it, and you should not mark the structures or filaments. The cairn will feel faintly absurd, and it will save you.

Arrive having already eaten and used whatever facilities your home location provides. The city does not have these things, and although the expedition is manageable without them for a day, anticipating the need is preferable to discovering it.

## A Note on What You Will Not Understand

You will not understand the city's distribution logic. You will not understand what the filaments carry or whether it is information or something for which information is too thin a word. You will not understand what moved through the base of the structure. You will not understand what the translucent patches are recording, or whether it is recording at all versus something better described by a different verb, one you do not have.

You will not understand what the pauses in the cycling sound contain.

You will go home with all of these open. You should be at peace with this before you arrive, because the alternative — frustrated attempts to force legibility onto a place that is not withholding its logic out of hostility but simply operating in registers your instruments and cognitive frameworks were not built for — is the thing that makes Lacunate City visits feel like failure, when nothing about them is.

The filaments will be there in your thoughts for years. This is not a problem. This is what you brought back from a place that makes gifts of questions.

Prepare to receive them. That is, in the end, the primary preparation.

Pack the soft boots. Bring the microphone. Write in the journal. Mark where you arrived so you can leave. Let the quiet be enough.

---

*Be patient with yourself. The city has had much longer to practice patience than you have, and it will not notice if you struggle. But you will, eventually, find the pace. Most people do, somewhere in the second hour, when the felt sky has pressed its gentle weight long enough that you stop resisting it and begin, instead, to breathe.*
