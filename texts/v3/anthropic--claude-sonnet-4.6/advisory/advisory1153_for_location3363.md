# Preparing to Visit the Roaring Plateau

## Before You Leave: Reframing Your Expectations

The first and most important preparation for the Roaring Plateau is conceptual, and it needs to happen before you pack a single item. You need to dismantle a particular assumption that most travelers carry without knowing they carry it — the assumption that a landscape is a backdrop, a setting for human experience, something that exists in relationship to your presence.

The Roaring Plateau does not exist in relationship to your presence. It existed before you arrived and it will exist after you leave in exactly the same configuration, performing the same operations, making the same sound. You will not change it. It will not remember you. This is not a hostile fact. It is simply the condition of the place, and travelers who arrive expecting the landscape to respond to them — to offer drama, to shift, to acknowledge — will find themselves in a low, grinding psychological difficulty that is hard to name and harder to escape, because the place doesn't feel wrong, exactly, just utterly indifferent in a way that slowly becomes oppressive if you've staked your experience on being noticed.

The travelers who do well here are the ones who arrive having already made their peace with irrelevance. Not resignation — peace. There is a difference. Resignation is the mood of someone who expected more. Peace is the mood of someone who decided in advance that witnessing is enough, that being briefly present in a place that has no interest in you can itself be a form of sufficiency. Prepare for that mood deliberately. It won't arrive on its own.

The second reframing: abandon any notion of the Roaring Plateau as a place of dramatic revelation. It is not that kind of place. Its strangeness is cumulative and quiet. Things will accrete. You will notice them gradually — the finch flocks turning for no reason, the cushion plants' peculiar spacing, the sound that never drops below its threshold. If you arrive looking for a moment, a peak experience, a single image that explains everything, you will miss what the plateau actually offers, which is a slow, patient accumulation of observations that eventually cross some threshold and become something different from the sum of their parts. That threshold crossing may happen on your second day. It may happen on your fifth. It may happen the night after you leave, when you're somewhere else entirely and you suddenly understand something you saw on the plateau without knowing you were seeing it.

Plan for a stay long enough that the accreting can happen. Anything less than four days is probably insufficient. Seven to ten days is better. The plateau rewards duration.

## Dealing With the Sound

This requires its own section because most visitors are not prepared for it and because the consequences of underpreparedness here are significant.

The sound is continuous. It does not habituate the way most continuous sounds do. Most sounds — traffic, a fan, air conditioning — fade from consciousness within an hour of exposure as your auditory system learns to treat them as irrelevant background. The Roaring Plateau's sound does not behave this way. The current theory among those who have studied it (insofar as anyone has studied it) is that the layered complexity of the sound — the constant micro-variation in which layers are prominent, the way individual bird calls surface from and sink back into the collective roar — is precisely varied enough to prevent full habituation while being too constant to be experienced as a series of distinct events. Your auditory system cannot categorize it as background because it keeps changing. It cannot categorize it as foreground events because no individual element lasts long enough. It stays, therefore, in a kind of permanent intermediate status: not quite heard and not quite filtered, demanding low-level attention continuously.

By the end of your first day this is exhausting in a way that is difficult to attribute to anything you can point at. You won't feel *loud*, exactly. You will just feel subtly depleted and you may not immediately connect it to the sound.

**What to bring:**

Good foam earplugs. Not the cheap disposable kind — the kind designed for consistent industrial noise reduction with high NRR ratings. You will need them not because the sound is dangerous to your hearing (it almost certainly is not, being bird calls rather than machinery) but because you will need to sleep, and you will need to think, and you will occasionally need to simply rest your auditory system. Use them strategically: not constantly, which would deprive you of the experience of the place, but during sleep, during rest periods in your shelter, and whenever you feel the specific depletion that the sound creates.

Test your earplugs before you leave for the plateau. Know how to insert them properly. In the cold, your hands may be clumsy.

You should also practice, before arriving, something that might be called deliberate listening — the intentional act of picking one layer of a complex sound and following it to the exclusion of others. This is a skill that takes practice and that will be extremely useful on the plateau. When you can hold your attention on the finch-flock chitter and let the cliff colony roar become background, or vice versa, you have the auditory equivalent of being able to focus your eyes. You will hear more, and the complexity that would otherwise be overwhelming becomes instead interesting. Musicians and sound engineers and ornithologists tend to do better on the plateau than others, for this reason. You can develop a version of this skill before you arrive by practicing with complex music — not as entertainment but as an exercise, deliberately tracking individual instruments through dense orchestral passages.

There is also a reported phenomenon — not universal, but common enough to mention — of a period of temporary silence-deafness that affects some visitors after leaving the plateau. For a day or two, quiet environments feel wrong, lack something, are experienced as incomplete rather than restful. This passes. It is not a sign of damage. It is your auditory system adjusting back to a world with less in it.

## Physical Preparation: Altitude

The altitude is not optional to prepare for. This is not the kind of thing that some people can ignore.

At the extreme elevation of the plateau, the partial pressure of oxygen in the air is significantly reduced — low enough that your body cannot simply adjust to it the way it might adjust to a day at moderate altitude. The effects are real and they are physical, not psychological, though they will affect your thinking as well as your body.

**Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS):** This is the name for the cluster of symptoms that typically affects people in the first twenty-four to seventy-two hours at high altitude. Headache is the most common symptom — not a mild headache, but the specific drilling pressure of altitude headache that ibuprofen touches only incompletely. Nausea. Disrupted sleep, characterized by a particular pattern of alternating fast and slow breathing that jolts you awake repeatedly. Fatigue disproportionate to exertion. Difficulty concentrating. A sense of mild cognitive fog.

Most people experience at least some of these symptoms on the plateau. The severity varies based on your rate of ascent, your individual physiology, and how fit you are (fitness helps but does not protect you — fit people get AMS too).

**Acclimatize before you arrive.** If possible, spend two to three days at an intermediate altitude — somewhere above your home altitude but below the plateau — before making the final ascent. This is not optional if you are coming from sea level or low elevation. The body needs time to begin producing more red blood cells and to make cardiovascular adjustments. Arriving at the plateau in a single day from low elevation is asking for severe AMS and a first several days of the plateau that you will spend suffering rather than observing.

**Ascend slowly on the final approach.** The final ridge climb to the plateau lip should not be rushed. The instinct on a long approach is to push faster as the destination nears. Resist this. The faster you gain altitude in the final hours, the more likely you are to trigger or worsen AMS symptoms immediately on arrival.

**Hydrate more aggressively than you think necessary.** The air at altitude is very dry, your breathing is deeper and faster than at sea level, and you lose moisture rapidly through respiration and skin in ways that are much faster than at lower elevations. Dehydration worsens AMS substantially. The plateau's water sources — those dark still pools, possible snowmelt — should be treated before drinking (see below), but they are water, and you should use them. A general rule: drink until your urine is pale straw colored. At altitude this requires more fluid intake than you are used to.

**Do not drink alcohol for at least the first three days.** Alcohol impairs the body's acclimatization process, disrupts sleep (which is already disrupted at altitude), and worsens dehydration. The pleasure of a warming drink in cold conditions is real, but the cost on the plateau is high enough to make it inadvisable until you are solidly acclimatized.

**Diamox (acetazolamide):** This is a prescription medication that accelerates acclimatization and reduces the severity of AMS. It is worth discussing with a physician before your trip. It is not a substitute for proper acclimatization, and it has side effects (it makes carbonated drinks taste flat, causes increased urination, and in a small number of people causes tingling in the extremities), but for most people who take it the tradeoff is favorable. Get the prescription before you leave. You cannot obtain it on the plateau.

**HACE and HAPE:** These are high-altitude cerebral edema and high-altitude pulmonary edema, respectively — serious, life-threatening conditions that can develop from AMS. Know the warning signs. HACE: confusion, inability to walk a straight line, extreme headache, drowsiness, vomiting. HAPE: severe shortness of breath at rest, coughing (possibly with pink frothy sputum), inability to walk without extreme breathlessness. Both require immediate descent. The treatment for both is: go down, right now, as far as you can, as fast as you can. The plateau's remoteness means that a HACE or HAPE emergency is a genuine survival situation. Travel with a companion who knows these warning signs. Brief your companion before you arrive, not after.

## Physical Preparation: Cold

The temperature on the plateau sits in what the description calls a narrow cold band — consistently cool at midday, genuinely cold at night, and capable of rapid shifts when the cloud shadows cross and when the thin air loses whatever warmth the direct sun provided.

The danger here is not dramatic. It is the slow, grinding cold that takes advantage of inadequate insulation in layers you didn't think to protect — the cold that finds the gap between your glove and your sleeve, the cold that conducts from the rock you've been sitting on for an hour into your core without you feeling it happening. Hypothermia at altitude is a real risk not primarily because of extreme temperatures but because of the combination of physical exertion, altitude-driven caloric demands, wind, and the tendency of people to misjudge how cold they are when they're also tired and cognitively affected by altitude.

**The layering system:** You know this, probably. But knowing and doing are different things at the plateau's extreme elevation, where the margin for error is smaller.

- A moisture-wicking base layer against your skin. Not cotton. Cotton holds moisture and will make you cold. Merino wool or synthetic.
- A mid-layer for insulation. Down is the best warmth-to-weight ratio but loses insulating capacity when wet. Synthetic fill is heavier but functions when damp.
- A windproof and waterproof outer shell. The plateau's thin but present wind is enough, over hours, to strip warmth through any non-windproof layer.
- Insulated pants or the ability to add insulation over base layer pants.
- A hat that covers your ears. You will want it even in midday.
- Gloves with a liner. The liner allows you to do fine tasks (operating equipment, tending to gear) without removing your outer glove.
- Insulated, waterproof boots. Your feet will be in contact with stone and potentially wet ground. Boots that are warm in controlled conditions may not be warm enough when you're crossing a boulder field in morning shadow.

**The wind:** The description says the wind is barely present — a low fundamental hum across the stone surfaces rather than a gale. Do not mistake this for safety from wind. The thin air at altitude means that even a light wind removes heat much faster than the same wind at sea level. And wind at altitude changes. Carry your full cold-weather gear regardless of what the morning shows you.

**Caloric needs:** You will burn significantly more calories at this altitude than at sea level — more from the effort of moving in thinner air, more from the work of keeping warm, more from the increased metabolic demands of altitude itself. Bring more food than you think you need. Bring foods that provide dense caloric value without requiring cooking. Bring foods you find palatable when you're not hungry (altitude suppresses appetite, which is one of the more dangerous aspects of AMS — you need calories but don't want them). Nuts, cheese, chocolate, dried fruit, dense grain bars. Hot food is morale as well as sustenance; a thermos of something hot to drink or a hot breakfast can change the character of a difficult morning.

## Water

The plateau's moisture is, as described, a rumor. There is water — the small dark pools in the basins, possible snowmelt in north-facing shadows, condensation on rock surfaces — but you cannot rely on finding water at any given point, and the pools, while they look clean (and may be), are still open water at extreme altitude where bird colonies are producing large quantities of guano. Treat everything.

Carry a reliable water treatment method and a backup.

**Primary:** A filter with a high-quality ceramic or hollow-fiber element rated for giardia and cryptosporidium. Many quality filters also remove bacteria. The cold will affect some filters — at near-freezing temperatures, hollow-fiber filters can freeze and crack, rendering them non-functional and making them appear to function while actually passing untreated water. Store your filter in a warm place at night (inside your sleeping bag works) and check it before use.

**Secondary:** Chemical treatment tablets as a backup. Iodine or chlorine dioxide. Chlorine dioxide is more effective against cryptosporidium. These work slowly in cold water — allow more time than the instructions say when the water is near freezing.

**Carry capacity:** The pools and melt sources are intermittent and may not be where you need them when you need them. Carry enough capacity to hold a day's water supply. At altitude, with the increased respiration and dry air, you should plan on at least three to four liters per day, more if you're doing substantial physical work.

## Navigation and Emergency

There is no one here. There are no structures, no cairns, no paths. This is not a place where you can navigate by human artifacts.

The plateau's topography — ridges and basins, cols and broad shoulders, all at roughly the same elevation — creates a specific navigation challenge: from within it, it looks the same in every direction. Distinctive rock formations become your landmarks, but in the flat, hard light of midday or in overcast conditions or in any fog, even formations you think you know can look different. People get turned around on featureless high plateaus with surprising ease and surprising speed.

**Navigation tools:**
- A detailed topographic map of the plateau and surrounding terrain, printed on waterproof paper or laminated. Digital maps fail when batteries fail, which batteries do more quickly in cold. Have the physical map.
- A compass and the knowledge to use it with the map. Not GPS reliance with a compass as a backup. Compass proficiency as a baseline, GPS as a supplement.
- A GPS device with fresh batteries, spare batteries, and the plateau loaded as a saved map before you left your last location with data connectivity. Download the maps. Do not assume connectivity.
- Waypoints saved before your trip: the plateau lip where you entered, your camp location, any significant water sources you identified on the map. Know how to navigate back to these without being able to see them.

**The descent issue:** The plateau lip is described as requiring pulling yourself over a final ridge — which means the approach is steep enough to be non-trivial. Know where your descent route is at all times. Mark it on your GPS. In deteriorating weather or fog, the ability to navigate directly to your descent point rather than searching for it along the rim is not a luxury.

**Communication:**
- A satellite communicator (Garmin inReach or similar). Not a satellite phone, which is larger and less reliable; a two-way satellite messenger that allows you to send and receive messages and, critically, to trigger an SOS that transmits your GPS coordinates to emergency services. Leave the plateau's general area and your planned itinerary with someone at home who knows to initiate a search if they haven't heard from you by a specified time.
- Know what emergency response in this region looks like. Know the nearest location from which helicopter rescue might be possible (if it is possible — at extreme altitude, helicopter performance degrades significantly and some elevations are above effective helicopter ceiling). Know the nearest road. Know how long the walk out takes. This information should be researched and known before you arrive, not looked up on the plateau where there is no connectivity.

## Shelter and Sleep

You will be sleeping on the plateau. The question is whether your shelter and sleeping system are adequate to the conditions.

**Shelter:** A four-season tent, not a three-season tent. The difference matters less in summer at this latitude during calm conditions, but it matters enormously if the weather changes — and weather at extreme altitude changes faster than forecasts account for. A four-season tent is built to handle wind load and snow accumulation that would damage or collapse a three-season shelter. It is heavier. Bring it anyway.

Choose your campsite with care. Look for the natural wind-sheltering that the plateau offers: the south-facing sheltered angles between large boulders where the cushion plants grow deepest. These spots exist for a reason — they are the warmest, most protected microhabitats on the plateau, as millions of years of plant survival have demonstrated. What works for cushion plants works for you. Do not camp in the open on a boulder shoulder. Do not camp in a basin bottom where cold air pools at night. Do not camp where birds nest — not for their protection alone, but because the noise at a nesting colony at dawn is something beyond what earplugs can address, and because nesting birds, disturbed, create both ecological problems and occasionally defensive behavior.

The cushion plants themselves: do not camp on them, do not disturb them, do not use them for any purpose. They are decades to centuries old. The ones that have taken a human body's weight of impact do not recover from it on any timescale that is meaningful to you.

**Sleeping system:** Rated for temperatures well below the plateau's midday temperature. Sleeping at altitude is difficult — the AMS-induced breathing pattern, the cold that comes after the sun drops, the sound — and an inadequate sleeping bag will make it worse. A bag rated to -15°C is not excessive. Know how to use it with a quality sleeping pad (both for insulation from the ground and for cushion on stone) and a shelter floor that prevents ground moisture wicking.

## What to Observe, and How

This section matters because the plateau's rewards are observational and the quality of your observation determines the quality of your experience.

**Keep a written log.** Not a journal of your feelings, though those matter too, but a log of observations: what you noticed at what time of day, the behavior of the finch flocks (when they moved and in what direction and what preceded the movement), the state of the pools at different times of day, the behavior of the raptors (what time they began flying, what altitude they worked at, what the apparent trigger was for their stoops). The plateau's strangeness, as noted, is cumulative. You cannot hold enough observations in working memory to see the pattern. The written log gives you a second memory that doesn't degrade, that you can read back across days and begin to see things that your in-the-moment mind was not able to connect.

**Carry a notebook rated for outdoor use.** Rite in the Rain makes notebooks that work in cold and wet. Regular paper notebooks become unusable when damp. Standard pens stop working in cold. Carry pencils as a backup, and a pen that works in cold temperatures (Fisher Space Pens do; test yours at home in your freezer).

**Binoculars.** The cliff colonies and the raptors require optics to observe properly. A compact 8x42 or 10x42 binocular is worth the weight. At altitude the extra physical mass matters less than the additional information. Practice using binoculars with cold hands and gloves before you arrive — it is harder than it looks.

**The light:** The midday hallucinatory quality of the light at this altitude is real and documented. Photography at midday is technically difficult — the contrast ratio between the blazing pale rock faces and the deep shadow in the fissures exceeds what most camera sensors can capture in a single exposure. Early morning and late afternoon light, when the sun is lower, is more photographable and also, arguably, more beautiful — the warm colors that the plateau rock contains become visible in low-angle light in ways that the bleaching midday sun suppresses. Plan your observation time accordingly: the middle of the day is for resting your eyes, reviewing your notes, tending to camp. The hours just after dawn and before dusk are when the plateau shows you what it looks like.

**The cushion plants' spacing:** Worth spending deliberate time with a measuring tape or simply your hands. Document the distances between the larger cushion plant mounds in several different areas of the plateau. This is not necessary for your enjoyment of the place, but it is the kind of observation that might cross the threshold into something stranger than coincidence if the pattern is what it appears to be.

## Psychological Preparation: Extended Solitude

There is no one here. For the duration of your stay, in all probability, there will be no one here. You will not encounter another human being. You will not have a conversation. You will not receive any social information — no news from the world below, no indication of what is happening to the people you know, no feedback from any other person about how you are doing.

This is fine for some people. It is genuinely destabilizing for others. The destabilization doesn't announce itself as loneliness, exactly — it shows up as a low, background difficulty in trusting your own perceptions, a slight sense that if no one else is here to confirm what you're seeing, it might not be real. At the plateau's particular combination of altitude (which affects cognition) and the overwhelming constant sound (which affects perception) and the complete absence of social reference points, this tendency can become pronounced.

Prepare by knowing which type of person you are. If you have not spent extended time alone in remote environments before, the Roaring Plateau is a challenging first experience. If you have, calibrate your expectations based on your previous experience while accounting for the additional stressors the plateau adds.

**If you are vulnerable to extended-solitude difficulty:**
- Maintain a journal not just of observations but of your internal state. Writing processes things that would otherwise loop.
- Keep a daily routine. Eat at the same times. Check your equipment at the same time each morning. Have a set waking time. Routine in the absence of external structure maintains a kind of social scaffolding that the mind uses to feel located in time.
- Give yourself permission to use your satellite communicator to send non-emergency messages to someone at home. This is what the two-way messaging capability is for.
- Know the warning signs of acute psychological decompensation in isolation: intrusive paranoid thoughts about the landscape's intentionality, an inability to distinguish between your own internal voice and an external sound, a strong and irrational conviction that you need to leave immediately for reasons you cannot articulate. These are rare but real. If they occur, take them seriously. Descend.

**On the subject of the plateau's apparent intentionality:** The sense that the place has preferences, that its equilibrium is decided rather than accidental, is an observed effect reported by many visitors. It is difficult to know what to do with this. The scientifically cautious interpretation is that it is a product of the mind seeking agency in an overwhelming environment — pareidolia of intentionality, the same cognitive tendency that sees faces in clouds, applied to a complex landscape pattern. The scientifically incautious interpretation is more interesting and less helpful for preparation purposes. What matters practically is that you will likely experience this feeling and that you should not immediately either dismiss it or act on it. Notice it. Write it down. See what it points you toward. The cushion plants' spacing, the finch flock behavior, the sound's constant floor — these are real observations about the plateau regardless of what explains them. Let the observations be prior to the interpretation.

## A Note on What the Plateau Might Want

This is not a normal section to find in a preparation guide. Include it anyway.

The plateau has been here long enough to have preferences, the description says, and something in that deserves practical consideration. Not supernatural consideration — you don't need to perform rituals or make offerings or treat the place as a sentient being. But there is a form of ethical orientation to the place that the practical traveler might consider.

The plateau is doing something. It has been doing it for a very long time. What it is doing is maintaining this configuration — these species in these locations making this sound in this light — and what it wants from you, insofar as that word applies at all, is that you not interfere with that. This means the cushion plants, obviously, but it means more than that. It means not disturbing the bird colonies during nesting. It means leaving the pools exactly as you found them. It means not moving rocks, not piling cairns, not leaving any artifact of your passing that would make the next observer's experience different from yours. It means taking everything out that you brought in, including waste, because at this altitude and in these cold dry conditions nothing decomposes quickly and what you leave will be there, in some form, for a very long time.

This is leave-no-trace ethics, which you already know, applied to a place where it matters more than usual because the plateau is more fragile than it appears and because its completeness — the thing the description keeps returning to, the sense that it has arrived — is precisely the quality that your presence could most easily disrupt.

The goal is to pass through having left the configuration intact. You will do this better if you think of it as an obligation rather than a courtesy. The plateau's indifference to you should not translate to your indifference to it.

## Summary Packing List

This is not complete — your general backcountry kit should be assumed — but these are the plateau-specific items worth confirming:

**For the sound:**
- High-quality foam earplugs, multiple pairs
- Practice in layered-audio listening before departure

**For altitude:**
- Diamox (discuss with physician, obtain prescription)
- Additional hydration capacity beyond what you'd carry at lower elevation
- Altitude sickness awareness briefing for your travel companion
- Knowledge of HACE/HAPE warning signs for both of you

**For cold:**
- Full four-season clothing system including down or synthetic midlayer
- Gloves with liners
- Four-season tent
- Sleeping bag rated to -15°C minimum
- High-quality insulating sleeping pad

**For water:**
- Quality hollow-fiber filter with cold-storage protocol
- Chemical backup treatment (chlorine dioxide)
- Sufficient carry capacity for a full day's supply

**For navigation:**
- Printed waterproof topographic map
- Compass (and proficiency in its use)
- GPS with maps preloaded and waypoints saved
- Confirmed knowledge of descent route

**For emergency:**
- Satellite communicator with SOS capability
- Filed trip plan with contact at home
- Knowledge of regional emergency response capability

**For observation:**
- Binoculars (8x42 or 10x42)
- Waterproof notebook and pencils plus cold-capable pen
- Measurement tool (even a simple string marked in centimeters)
- Camera with understanding of the contrast challenges of plateau light

**For psychology:**
- Realistic expectations of solitude duration
- Daily routine plan
- Journal practice established before departure

---

The Roaring Plateau does not need you. It does not want you. It will not change because you came, and it will not miss you when you leave. The birds will keep making their foundational roar, the cushion plants will advance a few millimeters outward at their rims, the raptors will ride thermals you cannot feel, the pools will hold the cold sky in their dark still surfaces.

You can, if you are prepared and patient and willing to be irrelevant, witness all of this. You can be briefly present in a place that has been itself for longer than anything human has existed, and you can carry that back with you, and it will have been worth the considerable effort.

Go prepared. Go slowly. Listen more than you speak, and notice more than you conclude. The plateau will not explain itself. It will only continue being what it is, and that, if you let it, will be sufficient.
