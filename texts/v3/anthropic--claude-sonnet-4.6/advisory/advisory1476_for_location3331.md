# Preparing for the Calcified City: A Traveler's Guide

---

## First, a Necessary Reframing

Most preparation guides begin with gear lists. This one cannot, because gear is downstream of understanding, and understanding the Calcified City requires a preliminary act of self-examination that most travelers skip and then regret.

The question you must answer before you pack anything is this: *what kind of wrongness can you tolerate, and for how long, before it stops being interesting and starts being corrosive?*

The Calcified City will not threaten you with violence. It will not challenge you with privation beyond the ordinary privations of high altitude. What it will do, continuously and without apparent intention, is present you with a category of experience that your mind is not built to file correctly — wrongness that is too subtle to dismiss and too consistent to explain. Shadows that fall wrong. Interiors larger than their exteriors. Light arriving from the direction it cannot be arriving from. These are not dramatic. They are quiet, accumulative, and they work on you the way altitude works on the body: imperceptibly at first, then all at once.

Some travelers find this exhilarating. Some find it destabilizing. A small number find it unbearable by the second day, not because anything has harmed them but because the constant low-grade impossibility has exhausted their capacity for the uncertain. Know which kind of traveler you are. If you don't know, assume you might be the third kind and plan accordingly — specifically, plan your exit.

With that established: the practical preparations.

## The Body at Altitude

### Acclimatization

Do this slowly. Do this more slowly than you think necessary. The Calcified City sits at an elevation where the air delivers substantially less oxygen per breath than sea-level air, and the consequences of ignoring this are not subtle — they are headache, nausea, impaired judgment, pulmonary edema, death, in escalating order of how seriously the altitude has decided to take you. The psychological literature on high-altitude judgment impairment is relevant here for an additional reason specific to this location: you will already be experiencing perceptual phenomena that resist explanation. You do not want to be experiencing those phenomena with a brain that is additionally starved of oxygen. The combination produces a kind of recursive unreliability — you cannot trust your senses, and you cannot trust your judgment about whether your senses can be trusted.

Spend at minimum two to three days at intermediate elevations before ascending to the plateau. If you are a multiverse traveler with access to pharmacological acclimatization aids, use them, but do not use them as a substitute for the altitude adaptation itself — they treat symptoms while your body catches up, they do not catch your body up faster. Let your body catch up.

The practical upshot of conscious breathing: at this elevation, you will notice yourself breathing. This is new for most people. Breathing becomes a foreground activity rather than background. This is cognitively expensive — it takes processing that would otherwise go to observation and analysis — and it is also, counterintuitively, useful in the Calcified City specifically, because it anchors you in the body at a location where your mind will be tempted to float untethered into abstraction. When the geometry of a room is wrong and your mind starts spinning explanations, the sensation of deliberate breathing pulls you back to the fact of having a body, being in a place, taking up space. Treat it as a resource.

### Cold

The temperature at high altitude drops immediately at sunset and stays dropped. You know this in principle. You will be surprised by the immediacy of it in practice — the sun does not set gradually on the plateau, it falls behind an escarpment and the temperature follows it within minutes. The coldest part of the night occurs in the hours before dawn and is colder than the coldest part of a temperate winter night, on average, because the low atmospheric density offers less insulation against radiative heat loss.

Bring more insulation than you think you need. Specifically:

- A sleeping system rated for temperatures fifteen to twenty degrees Celsius colder than your coldest expected temperature. The "expected temperature" estimate is yours and is probably wrong.
- Insulated layers that do not rely on compression to function — down is compromised when wet, and while precipitation at the plateau level is infrequent, the clouds that pass through deposit moisture on surfaces and on people. Synthetic insulation or, if weight permits, a combination system.
- Wind protection. The plateau has its own pressure dynamics — the updrafts at the escarpment are consistent and the winds they generate cross the open plazas with force. Wind is the most efficient thing the cold has for getting past your insulation.
- A specific note on hands and extremities: the combination of cold air and low humidity dessicates skin aggressively. Your hands will crack. Bring a barrier cream and use it before symptoms appear rather than after.

### Hydration

The air at this altitude is dry in a way that is difficult to convey to someone who has not experienced it. The humidity is low enough that you lose water through respiration at a rate significantly higher than at sea level, simply by existing and breathing. You will not feel thirsty in the way that thirst usually works — the sensation arrives later than the deficit. Drink water on a schedule, not in response to thirst. Electrolytes matter here because you are losing them too, and plain water without them can in sufficient quantity actually worsen hyponatremia.

A practical note: there is no water in the Calcified City. There is no water table within reach. There are no streams, no springs, no snowmelt channels — the description is specific about this, the dryness is total and the sounds of water, when they occur, are among the city's wrong sounds, coming from nowhere, meaning nothing available to drink. You must carry all water you will consume for the duration of your time on the plateau. Calculate this carefully, adding a safety margin, and then add another margin because calculation at altitude is impaired and your first estimate will be wrong.

### Nutrition

High-altitude environments suppress appetite through mechanisms not fully understood, but the body's caloric needs are not suppressed — they are elevated, because maintaining core temperature in cold, dry air costs energy. You must eat when you do not want to eat. The easiest solution is calorie-dense food that does not require preparation and has no off-putting texture at cold temperatures — nuts, dense dried fruit, chocolate with high cocoa content, hard cheese, whatever variation on these your particular metabolism tolerates. Avoid food that requires significant water to digest, because you are already managing a water deficit.

Do not attempt to cook elaborate meals on the plateau. The cognitive bandwidth required to manage fire and food preparation in cold and thin air is substantial, and you need that bandwidth for other things.

### UV Radiation

At altitude, with a thin atmospheric filter above you, ultraviolet radiation is intense even on overcast days — especially on the grey-overcast days typical of the plateau, because cloud cover scatters UV while blocking visible light, creating conditions where it seems dim but is actually burning you. Bring full-coverage sun protection including UV-blocking eyewear, and apply it whether or not the sun is visible. Snow blindness is possible even without snow, given the way light behaves on reflective pale stone surfaces.

## Equipment: What to Bring and Why

### Navigation

Conventional navigation technologies should be considered unreliable in the Calcified City and planning should proceed from that assumption rather than treating malfunction as a contingency.

The relevant observation is that the city's interior is measurably larger than its exterior, which means any map drawn from external observation will be inadequate to navigate the interior. GPS or equivalent positioning systems that triangulate from external references will report a position on that inadequate map, which means they will tell you where you are on a map that is wrong, not where you are in the city. This is arguably more dangerous than having no position data at all, because it creates false confidence.

The city also appears to interfere with magnetic compass readings — not dramatically, not with needle-spin or reversed polarity, but with a gentle consistent offset that varies by location in ways that do not correspond to expected magnetic declination variation. If you rely on compass navigation, your path will curve. You will not notice the curve until you have traveled further than correction is easy.

What does work:

- **Dead reckoning with conscious pace-counting.** It is imperfect, but it is imperfect in ways you can partially audit. Keep meticulous notes of distance traveled and direction, using the sun's position as a reference corrected for time of day. Accept that your dead reckoning will accumulate error and work within that limitation.
- **The towers.** The towers are the most stable landmarks in the city — they do not move, and while shadows are unreliable, the towers themselves can be used as fixed reference points to triangulate position relative to each other. Learn the silhouette of specific towers before you travel away from them. The slenderness and height of individual towers varies enough to be distinguishing.
- **The escarpment edges.** You cannot get off the plateau without finding an edge, and the edges are real and consistent even when the interior is not. If lost, travel in a consistent direction until you reach an edge, then navigate along the edge to your descent route. This will take longer than expected because the interior is larger than the exterior, but it is a reliable solution.
- **Colored cord or lightweight flagging tape.** Leave a trail. Literally. The floors are stone and do not take impressions, and the wrong-light conditions inside buildings make it easy to exit through a different opening than you entered. Flagging tape at decision points — every junction, every building entrance — provides a navigable trail out. Bring more than you think you need. The city has more junctions than expected.
- **Charcoal for marking stone.** Washes off in rain (of which there is little) but is visible for the duration of your visit. Mark walls at your height with directional arrows in the same system consistently. Note the time of marking so you can tell old marks from new ones.

### Light

The transition from day to night at the plateau edge is fast and cold. You need a primary light source and a backup. The primary should be powerful enough to illuminate open plazas at meaningful range — the structures are at sufficient distance from each other that a headlamp optimized for close-range reading is inadequate. A headlamp with a high-lumen flood mode and a useful beam distance is appropriate. Bring spare batteries; cold kills battery performance.

A note on artificial light at night in the Calcified City: the guide describes a paradox where the city's plan is *more* visible at night because the empty spaces collect starlight and the walls are absolute dark. Artificial light will partially destroy this visibility by washing out the starlight-on-stone contrast. Use lights when necessary for safety. Turn them off when you want to observe. Your eyes will dark-adapt at altitude faster than expected — the reduced atmospheric scatter means starlight is genuinely bright on pale stone.

Do not use artificial light to look at the peripheral movement in the outer streets at night. The guide is explicit that direct attention functions differently here. This is not superstition; it is an operational observation about the specific perceptual rules that apply at this location. Treat peripheral vision as your primary sense for some categories of information, and do not attempt to verify peripheral impressions by turning toward them.

### Shelter

The Calcified City's buildings can provide windbreak and partial weather protection, but they should not be relied on as primary shelter for two reasons. First, structural integrity is uncertain — the freeze-thaw cycling that has heaved the flagstones has also worked on mortar joints and stress-bearing elements of the buildings, and a structure that appears intact may have compromised load paths in elements you cannot inspect. Do not shelter in a structure that shows evidence of recent movement (disturbed dust patterns, fresh stone fragments on floors), that makes sounds under wind load, or that you have not assessed in daylight.

Second, and more importantly: the interior light phenomena, the directional anomalies, and the scale irregularities are all more pronounced inside structures than in the open. Spending the night inside a building in the Calcified City means spending the night in a space that is not reliably the size you measured it to be, lit by light from directions that are not the directions of the windows. This is manageable as a day-exploration experience. It is more stressful as an overnight experience. The quality of sleep in such a space is likely to be poor, which compounds altitude effects.

Bring a freestanding tent capable of handling the wind loads typical of high exposed plateaus — a four-season mountaineering tent or equivalent. Site it in the open where you can see a clear sky, in a location where the flagstones are stable (test by pressing firmly at several points around your intended tent location to check for wobble). The open plaza is preferable to proximity to towers, which are implicated in the very-low-frequency pressure effects described.

## Mental and Perceptual Preparation

### The Wrongness Taxonomy

Before you arrive, it helps to categorize the specific types of perceptual anomaly you are likely to encounter, so that when you encounter them you are working from a pre-established framework rather than trying to build the framework under conditions of impaired judgment.

**Category One: Metric anomalies.** The city's interior exceeds its exterior. Rooms are illuminated from directions inconsistent with their window placement. These are anomalies in spatial measurement — your expectations about distance, volume, and angle are being violated. The practical response is: do not make critical decisions based on geometric assumption. You estimated the distance to the next tower? Double your estimate. You thought the room would be dark? Act as though the light environment might be anything. Treat your spatial intuitions as rough guides requiring verification rather than reliable inputs.

**Category Two: Shadow anomalies.** The shadows do not always fall where the sun requires. This is the most difficult category because it is the most testable — you can calculate where the shadow should fall and observe where it does. The practical response is: do not use shadow direction for navigation or time-of-day estimation. Use the sun's actual position, not the shadows it casts. This requires looking at the sun (briefly, with appropriate eye protection) rather than at its effects.

**Category Three: Sound anomalies.** Sounds that imply doors closing, water moving, stones settling — in contexts where these sounds should not occur. The practical response is: categorize anomalous sounds as data rather than alerts. A sound that implies a door closing does not mean there is something on the other side of a door about to open it. This categorization may need to be made consciously and explicitly in the moment, because your threat-assessment instincts will want to respond to the door-sound as though it is a door. Note the sound. Note what it implies. Note that the implication is not consistent with context. Move on.

**Category Four: Peripheral presence.** Things visible in peripheral vision that are not visible on direct inspection. The practical response is not to eliminate this but to develop a protocol: allow peripheral vision to register what it registers, note it without turning toward it, continue. You may wish to keep a log of peripheral observations during the day and review it in daylight with both eyes and full attention before you sleep. This serves as both a practical record and a cognitive processing step that may reduce the frequency of the observations appearing in dreams.

**Category Five: The lichen patterns.** The growth patterns that your eye keeps trying to resolve into meaning. The practical response is probably the hardest: stop trying to read them. The eye will return. Redirect it. You are not going to decode the lichen. Even if the lichen is encoding something, you do not have the apparatus to decode it, and the attempt costs more than it returns. Observe the lichen as biology. Observe the color variation, the texture, the differential growth in sheltered versus exposed positions. This gives your pattern-recognition faculty something real to do rather than letting it spin on the impossible question.

### The Attention Economy

At altitude, with thin air doing its work on cognition, your ability to sustain focused attention is reduced. At the Calcified City specifically, your attention will be under additional demand from the wrongness categories above. You have less attention than usual and more demands on it than usual. This means you must triage.

Attention priorities, in order:
1. Your body. How cold are you? When did you last drink water? When did you last eat? Are you breathing? Are you breathing more shallowly than an hour ago? Physical safety is the first claim on whatever attention you have.
2. Your position and route. Where are you? Where is the descent route? How long until sunset?
3. Your shelter and sleep plan. Where are you sleeping tonight? Are you equipped to get there before dark?
4. Everything else, including the interesting anomalies.

The anomalies will tempt you to invert this order. They are compelling. A shadow that falls wrong demands attention in a way that hunger does not. Train yourself in advance to check the body first, position second, and give the anomalies their place in the queue rather than letting them jump it.

### Journaling

Bring a journal and use it. The specific value of a journal in the Calcified City is not primarily as a record for later — it is as a real-time externalization of experience, a way of moving observations from the unreliable substrate of altitude-impaired memory into a medium that holds its shape.

When something anomalous occurs, write it down immediately. Write the time, the location (as best you can establish it), the nature of the anomaly, and your current physical state. The exercise of writing forces a clarity of description that helps sort genuinely anomalous observations from observations that are unusual because you are tired, cold, or hypoxic. "The shadow fell approximately 20 degrees west of where the sun angle suggested it should fall, observed at approximately 2pm, from the northeast corner of the largest visible plaza, after a night of poor sleep but adequate food and water" is a different quality of observation from "the shadows were wrong" that you remember later.

Additionally: writing is a grounding activity. It requires conscious language formation, fine motor control, physical posture. At moments when the city's weirdness is at its most intense — when the geometry has been wrong for an hour and you can't find the edge you were trying to reach and the light is coming from the wrong direction inside a room — writing something is a way of reasserting that you are a person with a body making marks on paper. This is not nothing.

### Companions and Communication

If you travel with companions — strongly recommended over solo travel — establish in advance a check-in protocol that is mandatory regardless of whether anything seems wrong. Check in verbally with each other every two hours at minimum. The purpose of this is not emergency response (though it serves that too) — it is calibration. Two people experiencing the same anomaly provides some evidence that it is the anomaly rather than the individual. Two people experiencing different anomalies from the same position provides evidence of the individual's perceptual state. This data is useful.

Also establish in advance a vocabulary for degrees of concern, because in the moment it is difficult to formulate sentences about perceptual unreliability under time pressure. Something as simple as a three-level scale: *odd* (worth noting, not requiring response), *concerning* (worth discussing, may require response), *getting out* (immediate withdrawal toward the descent route regardless of plans). Agree on what triggers each level before you need to apply them.

If you travel solo, you do not have this calibration resource. This is a significant disadvantage in a location where your own perceptions are unreliable. Solo travel in the Calcified City is not recommended for first visits and is not recommended for people who have any history of dissociative episodes, depersonalization, or psychotic features even in manageable forms — the city's specific category of wrongness interacts with these vulnerabilities in ways that are not predictable and not safe.

## Specific Hazards

### The Towers

The towers are implicated in the very-low-frequency pressure effects described — the pressure you feel in the inner ear, the sensation of breath-length fluctuation in the air. These effects appear to be generated by the towers interacting with wind and possibly with the city's own unknown process. They are felt rather than heard. Prolonged exposure to low-frequency vibration in this range (infrasound, technically) is associated with effects ranging from mild discomfort to significant distress including anxiety, feeling of presence, and visual disturbances in some individuals.

Do not sleep near the towers. This is specific enough to warrant inclusion as a distinct precaution rather than subsumed in the general sleep advice. A tower's infrasound effect appears to have a radius; the open plazas are preferable. Site your tent in open space, away from walls.

If you experience increasing anxiety or the sensation of being observed while near a tower, this may be an infrasound effect rather than an accurate threat assessment. Move away from the tower until the sensation diminishes. Then reassess.

### The Floors

The flags heave with freeze-thaw. Many are unstable. An unstable flag can tip under foot pressure and cause a fall; a fall at altitude onto stone flags, with potentially impaired judgment and no proximate medical care, has consequences disproportionate to what would be a minor incident at lower elevation. Walk deliberately. Test unfamiliar flag positions before committing weight. Do not carry anything that prevents you from seeing where your feet are going. In dim light or night conditions, treat every flagstone as potentially unstable until proven otherwise.

### The Buildings

Structural integrity, as noted. Also: within buildings where the light anomalies are most active, the visual system has reduced useful information about spatial relationships. You may misjudge step height, doorway width, the position of walls. This is the perceptual equivalent of the unstable floors — a minor disorientation that becomes significant because of the consequences of falling. Move slowly in building interiors. Use a hand on the wall where you can. Bring a walking staff that allows you to probe ahead.

### Weather

The weather described is cyclical and moderate by the standards of extreme high-altitude environments — not storm-type, not catastrophic. But *moderate* at extreme altitude is still life-threatening if you are underprepared. The temperature drop at sunset has already been noted. Add to this: the clouds that form and dissolve over the plateau can, at intervals, produce precipitation or rapid visibility reduction. A white-out condition on the plateau — cloud, reduced visibility, disorienting snow-light — in a city where the geometry is already unreliable is a serious emergency. Your exit plan must include a procedure for finding the escarpment edge in zero-visibility conditions. This means knowing the compass bearing to the edge (with the understanding that the compass is unreliable and you should have verified the bearing by sight before visibility dropped), and knowing the distance (with the understanding that distance inside the city is unreliable and you should have measured it by pace count on the way in).

Do not wait for the cloud to form before thinking about this.

## Exit Planning

Plan your exit before you enter.

Specifically: identify the descent route in daylight, before the city has had time to work on your spatial reasoning. Mark it. Walk partway down it so you know what it looks like. Establish the time it takes to reach it from different points in the city, and add a substantial margin because the interior is larger than expected and your pace will be slower than planned when cold and tired.

Set a hard exit time each day — a time at which you will begin moving toward the descent route regardless of what you are in the middle of, what you are observing, or how much you feel you have left to see. This time should be calculated backward from full dark, accounting for the transit time to the descent route, the descent itself, and a safety margin. The hard exit time should not be negotiable with yourself in the moment. The city will feel like there is more to see when it is time to leave, because there is always more to see, and because you will have been in it long enough for its pull to have weight.

The city has been there long enough to be patient. You have not.

## What You Are Actually Doing Here

A final preparation, which is not practical but is, in a different sense, the most practical of all.

The Calcified City is not a destination in the ordinary sense — a place you go to see a thing, to accumulate an experience, to check a location against an expectation. It is a process, long-running and indifferent, and what you are doing by visiting is inserting yourself briefly into that process. You are not the subject of the visit. The city is not performing for you. The city is not oriented toward you at all, in any of the senses that imply acknowledgment.

This is an unusual experience for a human being, who is accustomed to being at least a consideration in most environments they inhabit. Natural environments are indifferent but they are indifferent through simple mechanism — the mountain does not orient toward you because mountains do not orient; it would orient toward you if it could, if it had a faculty for it, which it does not. The Calcified City's indifference is of a different character. There is something there that could, in some sense, orient. You will feel this. It does not orient.

Prepare yourself for this, specifically. Not for fear of malice — there is nothing malicious in the description, nothing that reaches for you — but for the particular quality of being *not acknowledged by something that has the capacity to acknowledge*. This is the experience of certain very old institutions, very old artworks, certain categories of profound social rejection. It is existentially uncomfortable in a way that cold and thin air are not, because it does not engage any of your survival instincts. It simply sits with you. It is simply what the place does to a person.

It is also, if you can remain in it rather than flinching from it, clarifying. To be genuinely unimportant to something very old and very large is to be reminded that your sense of your own significance is a construction, useful in many contexts and optional in others. The Calcified City makes it optional. What remains, after that particular construction is set aside, is what is actually there: a body breathing, a mind observing, a brief presence in a long story.

This is enough to be. It is, in fact, quite a lot.

**Physical Readiness**
- [ ] Acclimatized over minimum 2-3 days at intermediate elevation
- [ ] Altitude pharmacological support if applicable
- [ ] All water for plateau duration carried in; minimum 3 liters per person per day plus margin
- [ ] Calorie-dense cold-tolerant food for duration plus two emergency days
- [ ] Electrolyte supplement
- [ ] UV protection including eye protection
- [ ] Barrier skin cream
- [ ] First aid kit including blister treatment, analgesics, altitude medication

**Shelter and Warmth**
- [ ] Four-season freestanding tent
- [ ] Sleeping system rated 15-20°C below expected minimum
- [ ] Wind-resistant outer layer
- [ ] Insulated mid-layer (compression-independent)
- [ ] Insulated gloves and spare gloves
- [ ] Insulated footwear; gaiters for cold stone floors
- [ ] Balaclava or face covering

**Navigation**
- [ ] Physical map of plateau exterior (with understanding of its limitations)
- [ ] Colored cord or flagging tape (bring more than expected)
- [ ] Charcoal marking sticks
- [ ] Pace count established (know your pace length)
- [ ] Compass (with understanding of its limitations here)
- [ ] Descent route identified, measured, and marked before first interior exploration
- [ ] Hard exit times established for each day and committed to

**Light**
- [ ] High-lumen headlamp with flood capability
- [ ] Spare batteries (more than calculated; cold degrades)
- [ ] Backup light source

**Documentation and Communication**
- [ ] Physical journal and multiple writing instruments (cold kills pens; bring pencils as backup)
- [ ] Communication device for emergency contact with non-plateau party
- [ ] Check-in schedule established if traveling with companions
- [ ] Three-level concern vocabulary agreed upon with companions
- [ ] Solo travelers: trip plan filed with someone who will act if you miss check-ins

**Perceptual Preparation**
- [ ] Wrongness taxonomy reviewed and internalized
- [ ] Attention priority order established
- [ ] Protocol for peripheral observations established
- [ ] Protocol for anomalous sounds established
- [ ] Exit decision criteria established and committed to
- [ ] Understood: direct attention functions differently here
- [ ] Understood: the city is not performing for you
- [ ] Understood: the city will be here after you leave
