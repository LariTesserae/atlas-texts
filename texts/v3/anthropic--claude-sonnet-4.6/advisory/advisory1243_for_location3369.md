# Preparing to Visit the Plateau of Settled Things: A Traveler's Guide

---

## First and Most Important: What You Are Walking Into

Before anything else, understand that the Plateau of Settled Things is not primarily a place in the geographic sense. It is a *condition*. You are not visiting terrain; you are visiting a state of being — one that the planet itself has achieved through processes you cannot accelerate, replicate, or fully comprehend. The place has opinions. Not about you specifically, but opinions in the sense that it has developed deep structural preferences about how things are, and those preferences are older than your entire civilization, possibly older than your species, and they will not adjust themselves to accommodate your expectations.

This is not hostile. The plateau is not hostile. But it is also not interested in being hospitable in any familiar sense. It is what it is, completely and without remainder, and you will be moving through that completeness as a visitor moves through a room where someone very old is resting — you adjust, not them.

Go knowing this. Go knowing that whatever you were hoping to find, what you will actually find is something more specific and less convenient and far stranger and ultimately more valuable than what you hoped for.

## Physical Preparation

### The Cold

The cold here is not dramatic. This is what makes it dangerous to underestimate. On many worlds with extreme cold, the cold announces itself — it bites, it burns, it produces immediate and unmistakable feedback. The cold of the plateau does not do this. It is steady and ancient and has been doing this for so long that it does not bother making its point loudly. You simply lose heat, at a moderate consistent rate, continuously.

Dress in full, serious layers. Not layers as fashion or as moderate precaution but layers as engineering. The principle is trapped air: multiple thin layers trap more air than one thick layer, and trapped air is the mechanism. The innermost layer must wick moisture away from your skin because you will perspire during exertion, and wet cloth against cold skin is how people die in places like this, places where the cold is not dramatic enough to make you feel immediately afraid but is persistent enough to kill you if you allow your thermoregulation to fail. The outer layer must resist wind and moisture — the rain comes sideways here, and even the fog-like condensation before dawn is sufficient to wet fabric that is not specifically prepared for it.

Pay particular attention to: your hands, your feet, your head, and the back of your neck. These are where the body loses heat disproportionately and where you will first notice the cold becoming a problem and where, if you are not careful, you will lose the ability to notice. Mittens rather than gloves for the hands — gloves sacrifice warmth for dexterity, and on the plateau you will not need much dexterity but you will need warmth continuously. Boots must be waterproof and insulated and high enough to keep moisture from entering at the top. Gaiters over the boots are strongly recommended because the moss-filled cracks in the stone hold moisture and you will be stepping over and through these constantly.

Carry more insulation than you think you need. The spaces between the outcrops are variable, as you will learn, and you may spend longer in exposed conditions than you planned without any single decision to do so.

### The Altitude

High elevation affects the body even when the effects seem absent. Your blood will carry less oxygen here than it carried below. Your heart and lungs will compensate, which is appropriate and necessary, but the compensation takes time — days, sometimes more than a week, to complete. Arriving at the plateau and immediately attempting to move quickly, carry heavy loads, or perform tasks requiring sustained concentration is an error. The body needs time.

Symptoms of altitude adjustment: headache, especially in the morning; fatigue disproportionate to exertion; disrupted sleep; reduced appetite; occasional mild confusion or difficulty with complex thinking. All of these are normal at first. All of them will recede as your body adapts. The serious symptoms — persistent vomiting, inability to walk a straight line, severe confusion, coughing that produces pink foam — are different and require immediate descent. The plateau is not a place to attempt to push through serious altitude illness. It is too large, too sparsely inhabited, too difficult to exit quickly.

Hydration is complicated at altitude. You will not feel thirsty in the normal way, but you are losing moisture continuously through respiration in dry cold air and through the increased breathing rate your body uses to compensate for reduced oxygen. Drink water regularly on a schedule rather than in response to thirst. The water from the plateau's basins is clear and clean and cold and you will want to verify it with whatever filtration or treatment method your kit includes — not because it is contaminated in any biological sense, but because it reflects a sky from a slightly different time, and the relationship between that temporal displacement and its chemistry has not been studied by anyone from outside the plateau. The people who live here drink it without evident harm, but they were born here and have been drinking it all their lives, which may matter.

Carry your own water supply for at least the first several days until you have assessed local sources. More than you think you need. At altitude and cold, running low on water creates cascading problems.

### The Walking

You will walk a great deal on this plateau. There is no other way to be here. The terrain is not technically difficult — no climbing, no scrambling, nothing that requires hands and feet simultaneously — but it is long and complex and the scale of the plateau means that distances between things you want to reach are significant.

Your footwear has been addressed. Additionally: carry a walking stick or pole. Not for technical terrain but because the stone surfaces, especially the low flat areas between the cracks, can be wet or covered in shallow moss that conceals irregularity, and a fall on stone in cold that is affecting your cognition is a situation to avoid. The pole also helps with the distance-uncertainty issue, which will be discussed at length later, by giving you something consistent to measure against as you move.

Learn before you go: how to read the light here. The light at high elevation comes from unusual angles and the diffused quality of it, combined with the lack of shadows that behaves reliably, means that your depth perception will be somewhat less accurate than you are accustomed to. Low features in the stone — the crack systems, the basin edges — can be misjudged. Walk deliberately. Look at your feet more often than you would need to elsewhere.

## Navigation and the Space Problem

This section deserves its own heading because it is the thing most likely to cause you serious difficulty, and it is the thing most different from anywhere else you have likely visited.

The distances between the stone outcrops are not consistent.

This is not a metaphor. This is not a subjective experience of distance distortion of the sort that happens when you are tired or anxious. This is a property of the plateau's space. The ground between two outcrops contains a variable amount of itself. You can walk from A to B, count your steps, and then walk back from B to A and count different steps, and both counts will be accurate to the ground you actually crossed. The outcrops are fixed. The space between them is not.

Your technology for navigation will have problems here. Any device that measures distance by counting steps or by triangulating against fixed points will produce inconsistent results that cannot be reconciled by recalibration — there is nothing to recalibrate against, because the problem is not in the device. Any device that measures distance by timing against a known speed will produce results that are accurate to the speed and time but not to the space you have crossed, because the space and the crossing of it are not in constant relationship here. Magnetic navigation, if the plateau does not interfere with your specific technology, will remain reliable for direction but not for distance.

What this means practically: you can know which way you are going. You cannot reliably know how far you have gone.

The solution used by the people of the settlement — and this is important, learn this before you go — is the paths. The paths that run between structures and water sources and other significant locations were laid with knowledge of this problem. They are constructed so that walking the path produces a consistent distance regardless of what the surrounding space is doing. The paths are reality anchors. This is their primary function, prior to their function as routes.

**Stay on the paths when traveling between known locations.** Do not decide that you will cut across open ground because the destination looks close — looks-close is unreliable here, and the space between you and it may currently be generous or withholding in ways you cannot assess. The paths are where distance is what it looks like. Everywhere else is provisional.

When you must go off the paths: move in increments, use visible landmarks you can return to, never let more than one or two outcrops distance yourself from a path, and have a plan for what you do if the space extends itself beyond what you expected. The plan is: do not panic, stop moving outward, orient toward the nearest path by direction rather than distance, and move toward it steadily. The space eventually relents. It always has, for the people who live here. But it may take longer than seems reasonable.

Carry a map if you have one. Maps of this place are likely inaccurate in their distances but accurate in their topology — the relationships between things, which is left of which, which is higher. Topology is what remains reliable. Use your map for topology and use the paths for distance.

Do not attempt to navigate by memory of previous crossings. Your memory of distances from a previous crossing is accurate to that crossing and will not generalize to the current one.

## Preparation for the Shadow Disalignment

The shadows here sometimes fall at angles that do not match the light source.

This is not constant. It may not happen during your entire visit, or it may happen frequently, depending on conditions that have not been identified. When it happens, it happens subtly — your shadow slightly longer than the sun's angle explains, or angled a few degrees off, or a shadow somewhere in your peripheral vision that does not correspond to anything above the surface. The moment you focus on it directly to examine it, it tends to self-correct.

This is notable primarily because it affects your sense of time and direction if you are using shadows for either. At high elevation with diffuse cloud-scattered light, the sun's position is often your most reliable directional indicator, and your shadow is often your most accessible check on that position. When the shadows are behaving anomalously, this check fails.

**Practical preparation:** carry a mechanical or magnetic compass for direction. Not electronic if you can avoid it — the plateau has not been assessed for electromagnetic anomalies and many of the things that are strange here suggest that electronics may be less reliable than mechanical or magnetic instruments. A simple magnetic compass gives you direction independently of shadows and light angles and will remain reliable here for direction even when other things are not.

The emotional preparation required is subtler. The shadow disalignment is not alarming once you know to expect it, but if you encounter it without preparation it produces a specific form of disorientation that is worth naming: the feeling that the physical world is less trustworthy than you had assumed, that the basic relationships you rely on without thinking — light sources produce shadows at predictable angles — are not guaranteed. This feeling is accurate to the situation, and that is what makes it difficult. It is not a mistake in your perception. The shadows are wrong. Knowing in advance that they will sometimes be wrong allows you to treat it as information rather than as a failure of your own cognition, which is a much more manageable experience.

## Preparation for the Water and Temporal Displacement

The water in the plateau's basins contains reflections that sometimes show a sky arranged differently than the sky overhead. The temporal displacement appears to be small — minutes to hours in either direction, perhaps, based on the cloud arrangements observed versus the cloud arrangements reflected — but it has not been precisely measured and the range may be larger than casual observation suggests.

The people who live here drink this water without apparent harm, and there is no reason to believe the water is dangerous. The temporal displacement, if it is genuine, appears to be a property of the reflection rather than of the water itself — the water does not seem to transport you when you touch it, only to show you something adjacent to your moment rather than exactly your moment.

However: do not use the reflected sky for navigation or time-keeping. If you need to know what the sky currently looks like, look up, not down. If you are using the basin surfaces for orientation or information of any kind, you are using data from a slightly different time, and while this is unlikely to cause serious problems, it compounds the other navigation challenges of the plateau.

Drink from these sources with your water treatment system active. Cook your food using water you have treated. This is not because the water is biologically dangerous — it appears to be extremely clean, as water that falls from clouds at high elevation and runs over ancient stone with no agricultural runoff or contamination sources tends to be — but because you are a visitor and your gut flora is not adapted to this water the way the resident population's is, and drinking untreated water in a new place is always a risk worth eliminating when you can.

The condensation that forms on everything before dawn is clean and can be collected if you are running low on water. The moss holds moisture and can be gently wrung over a container in extremity. Neither of these is a primary water strategy, but both are available.

## Psychological and Perceptual Preparation

This is the longest section of this guide and the one most likely to be dismissed as unnecessary by a traveler who considers themselves experienced. Please do not dismiss it. The plateau's effects on human psychology are not metaphorical and they are not merely the result of altitude and cold. They are specific to this place, and they have specific contours.

### The Loss of Toward

The plateau has arrived at itself. Everything here — the geological processes, the ecological processes, the settlement, the people — is in a condition that is not moving toward anything. This is the most fundamental thing about the place, and it will work on you in ways you cannot entirely anticipate.

You are, almost certainly, a being who orients yourself by progress. Where am I going? What am I doing here? What will I accomplish? How does this experience fit into the trajectory of my life? These questions are not wrong questions. They are how creatures with finite lifespans and active futures navigate their existence. But the plateau offers no purchase for these questions. Nothing here is going anywhere. The processes are ongoing but not progressive. The people have built what they have built and are maintaining it, not building toward something larger. The stone is still eroding, but eroding toward a state it will reach in ten million years, which is not a timeline that your presence here either affects or fits within.

You will feel this. It will arrive as a kind of restlessness that has nowhere to go, a reaching for the future that finds no future to reach toward. Some travelers find this peaceful — a release from the forward-pull they did not know they were under. Some find it genuinely disturbing, a vertiginous sense that meaning has become inaccessible. Most people move between these in the course of a visit.

**Prepare for this by deciding in advance that you will feel it and that it is not an emergency.** The restlessness will not harm you. The loss of toward is temporary — you will carry your own toward with you when you leave. While you are here, try to practice encountering what is without asking what it is for. The moss in the cracks is not for anything. The cloud circulating overhead is not building toward anything. Your standing in the middle of this plateau at this hour of this day is not a step toward anything. It is only what it is. Practice allowing that to be sufficient, because here, for these days or weeks, that is what will be true.

### The Silence

The silence of the plateau is not quiet in the suburban sense, the absence of traffic or human activity. It is quiet in the sense that the foundational frequency of the place is low — the sub-bass wind tone, the felt-rather-than-heard geological sounds — and that everything sitting atop this foundation is sparse and considered.

Your nervous system is adapted to a noisier baseline than this. The adaptation took your whole life and was never anything you chose or were aware of. In this silence, sounds that your system would normally filter out — your own heartbeat, your own breathing, the sound of your clothing as you move — become present. This is not alarming but it can feel alarming until you identify what is happening. What is happening is that your perceptual system, finding less external signal than it is designed to process, amplifies what signal exists, including the signal from your own body.

Additionally: the sound that arrives at the edge of audibility and is felt more than heard, the subterranean recognition of enormous surfaces. You will probably hear this. It occurs irregularly and correlates with nothing obvious. The best description available is that it sounds like the planet becoming aware of its own scale. It does not signify anything that has been decoded. It is simply what the plateau does, acoustically, at its deepest level.

When this happens: breathe. Remain where you are. It will not last long and it will not repeat immediately. It does not indicate danger. It indicates that you are in a place where the planet is very old and has developed habits at frequencies below the comfortable human range, and you happen to be present while it does one of those habitual things.

Do not attempt to find the source. There is no source in the sense of a locatable object producing the sound. It comes from the stone below, from the deep continuity of the plateau with its own geological past. Looking for it is not useful and moving toward what seems like its direction may take you off the paths.

### The Presence of the People

The people of the settlement have been here longer than your civilization. This is worth holding carefully.

They are not exotic and they are not primitive in any evaluative sense. They are skilled in ways you will not initially perceive as skill because their skills are entirely local — fitting stones together without mortar, reading the cloud circulation, walking the paths in the dark, sensing when the space is being generous or withholding, sitting with the ambiguous outcrops in whatever practice it is they do there. These skills took lifetimes to accumulate and generations to refine and you do not have them and will not acquire more than the barest beginning of them during your visit.

Arrive as a learner rather than as an observer. The distinction is behavioral and is immediately legible to people who have lived for generations in a place where misreading others has consequences.

Ask before entering their structures. Ask before using their water basins if someone is already there. Ask before touching anything you are uncertain about. These are basic courtesies that have additional weight here because some of what they build and arrange has functional purposes you cannot identify by looking — the stone arrangements at the thresholds of certain structures, the path materials, the placement of the pole-and-roof frameworks relative to each other. If you disturb something that was where it was for a reason, you may not understand what reason, and you may not be able to restore it correctly.

When they do something you do not understand — sitting alone with an outcrop, making an arrangement of stones that seems patterned, following a specific path at a specific time — do not conclude that you are watching a ceremony you should not witness, and do not conclude that you are watching a superstition with no practical content. You are almost certainly watching something that has a function that has been refined over a very long time and that you do not yet have the context to decode. Watch without interpretation. Make notes. Ask later, if you have established the kind of relationship in which asking is appropriate.

Their quiet is not shyness and it is not coldness. It is a mode of presence in a place where presence matters and where words are not the primary currency of relationship. They will assess you through other means — how you move through the space, whether you stay on the paths, whether you look at the ground and at the sky or only at them, how you respond to the anomalies. Be someone they would recognize as a person who is paying attention. That is the most useful thing you can be here.

### The Weirdness Itself

You will encounter things that your cognitive system will attempt to normalize and will fail to normalize and will attempt to normalize again. This cycling of attempt and failure is uncomfortable and somewhat energy-consuming, and it will happen repeatedly.

The preparation is: decide in advance to let the anomalies be what they are. The shadow is falling wrong. Yes. The water is showing a different cloud arrangement than the sky above. Yes. You walked between those two outcrops and it took forty steps, and now it is taking sixty, and both measurements are accurate. Yes. You do not need to resolve these things. You do not need to produce an explanation. The people who live here have not resolved them and do not appear to be under pressure to do so — they have a vocabulary that covers the practical aspects and they do not seem to find the lack of deeper explanation troubling.

This is a practice that the plateau will train you in, if you allow it: encountering what is genuinely unclear without demanding resolution. This is harder than it sounds. Humans are, in general, explanation-hungry in ways that are deeply wired rather than chosen. The plateau will test that wiring. Repeatedly. The testing is not malicious. The plateau simply does not provide the kinds of resolution that your pattern-recognition systems are seeking, and those systems will keep looking, and the looking will eventually tire, and in the fatigue of that looking you may find something else — a mode of encountering the unclear that is neither explanation nor dismissal, that simply holds the thing as what it is without collapsing it into something more manageable.

This is something worth having. It is one of the things you can carry back with you. It is not available everywhere.

## What to Bring: Practical Inventory

**Shelter and warmth:** you can stay in the settlement structures if offered, and this is advisable. The structures are built for exactly this climate by people who have understood this climate for a very long time. If you must create your own shelter in the open: a tent or structure that can shed sideways rain, is weighted or staked against wind that will be continuous and occasionally strong, and provides significant insulation from the stone ground. Ground insulation matters enormously — cold rises from stone as readily as from air, and sleeping on uninsulated stone is how you lose the warmth you worked all day to generate.

**Food:** bring more than you think you need. Altitude and cold increase your caloric requirements, your appetite will be reduced at first as part of altitude adjustment, and the plateau produces very little that is edible to a newcomer — the moss is not food for you, there appear to be very few animals, and whatever the people of the settlement eat locally will not be available to you without their involvement. Carry high-density caloric food. Fats and complex carbohydrates handle cold better than sugars, which burn fast and leave you lower than you started. Hot food is important — not as a luxury but as a thermoregulation tool. A means of heating water and food that functions in wind and damp is essential.

**Navigation:** mechanical compass. Hard-copy topological map if available. Walking pole. A way of marking where you have been that does not rely on your memory of distances. Small flags or markers that you can place and retrieve are useful, so long as you retrieve them — leave nothing permanent on the plateau.

**Water treatment:** chemical or filter treatment for drinking water. A container for collecting condensation as an emergency backup. More water than you think you need for the first several days.

**Light:** the nights here are dark and the paths are the only reliable way to move in the dark. A headlamp with significant battery reserve or multiple spares. The quality of the night sky here is such that you will want to spend time outside in the dark by choice, and you should be able to find your way back to your shelter without difficulty.

**First aid specific to this environment:** treatment for hypothermia (which is warming the core, not the extremities — get this wrong and you accelerate the problem), treatment for altitude illness (the primary intervention is descent), blister care for extended walking, wound care for cuts on stone edges. Nothing on this list is exotic. What matters is having it and knowing how to use it in cold with reduced hand dexterity.

**Sensory:** ear protection for the wind if you are sensitive to sustained wind noise. Eye protection — UV exposure at altitude is significantly higher than at lower elevations even under diffuse cloud cover, and the stone and water surfaces reflect it. Quality UV-blocking eye protection is not optional here over a multi-day visit. Sunblock even when it does not feel sunny, for the same reason.

**Documentation:** bring whatever you use for recording observations, and be aware that the observations you will most want to record — the anomalies, the specific quality of the light, the exact nature of the distance inconsistency — are the observations that will be most difficult to record in ways that convey their actual character to someone who was not here. Do your best. Write in the present tense, with specific detail, before the memory of the vividness fades. The vividness fades quickly in the normalization that occurs when you return to places with more noise and more forward-motion.

## Duration of Visit and Timing

### How Long

Less than three days on the plateau is not enough time to acclimatize, adapt to the silence, and begin to understand what you are actually seeing. You will spend the first two days managing your body's adjustment and your nervous system's adjustment, and only after that will the plateau begin to come into real focus.

More than three weeks alone on the plateau, for a first visit, risks a kind of cognitive settling that is difficult to describe precisely but that amounts to: your pattern-recognition systems, having failed to find familiar explanations for the anomalies, stop looking for them, and something loosens in your relationship to ordinary causality that can be disorienting when you return to lower elevations and ordinary causality reasserts itself. This is not permanent. It is similar to how the eyes take time to readjust to low light after high-light conditions, except that it is the part of you that tracks cause-and-effect doing the adjusting. A first visit of one to two weeks seems to offer significant exposure without pushing into the territory where readjustment becomes prolonged.

### When

The plateau has no obviously bad season in the sense of a season that makes it inaccessible. What it has is variation in the likelihood of the cloud system clearing to permit night sky observation, variation in rainfall frequency, and variation in temperature range. Without knowing the specifics of this planet's calendar, the general principle is: avoid the peak of whatever season brings maximum precipitation if you want to travel the paths with any reliability. The paths are designed to anchor distance, not to drain water, and extended rain makes their surfaces uncertain.

Come when you can accept what you find, rather than when you expect to find specific conditions. The plateau does not accommodate expectations. This is not its role.

## On Leaving

You will walk back down. The transition out of the plateau is as gradual as the transition in — the retroactive understanding, some time after you have begun the descent, that you are no longer there. The plateau will not announce that you have left. You will simply find yourself, after some unmarked moment, in a place that is becoming something again.

The noise of lower elevations will be surprising. You will have normalized the plateau's soundscape sufficiently that ordinary ambient noise — wind through denser vegetation, the sounds of more abundant animal life, the acoustic texture of places with topographic variety — arrives as information-dense in a way that feels almost cluttered. This fades in hours.

The reliability of shadows will also be surprising, in the direction of comfort. Things will cast shadows where you expect shadows and not elsewhere, and this will feel slightly excessive in its compliance, slightly too well-behaved, before it simply becomes normal again.

What does not always return quickly: the capacity to be comfortable with unresolved things. Having spent time in a place where the anomalies exist without explanation and where the people manage this without distress, you may find that you have become slightly less tolerant of premature resolution, slightly more able to sit with something ambiguous without forcing it closed. This is a good outcome. It is one of the genuine gifts of difficult places with extreme characters. It lasts approximately proportional to how fully you allowed the plateau to work on you, which means it lasts proportional to how much you were willing to let yourself be uncertain and cold and lost in the space problem and present with subterranean sounds without demanding that any of it make immediate sense.

If it lasts long enough: carry it carefully. It is rarer than you might think.

## A Final Note

The plateau has been there for ten million years of pre-living geological time plus however long it has been inhabited since, and it will be there for another ten million years after everything currently on its surface is gone. It has not noticed your civilization, has not adjusted itself for any of the civilizations that came before you, and will not adjust itself for you. It does not need you to understand it. It does not need you to approve of it or explain it or integrate it into your existing frameworks.

What you are doing when you visit is entering an ongoing condition that is very old and very large and very settled into itself, and you are small and young and not yet settled, and you are going to spend some time there anyway, because that is what multiverse travelers do — they go, even when going is not comfortable, even when what they find cannot be brought back as any kind of trophy or proof. What you bring back from the plateau is not images or measurements or a story about the shadow that fell the wrong way. What you bring back is a change in the angle at which you hold uncertainty.

This is worth the cold. It is worth the distance problem and the sideways rain and the sub-bass sound of the stone remembering itself. It is worth the altitude headache and the first two days of your nervous system searching for familiar patterns and finding none.

Go carefully. Stay on the paths when you are traveling. Look at the moss when you are still.

*Come back when you can.*
