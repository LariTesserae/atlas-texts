# Preparing for the Condensation Cities: A Traveler's Guide

---

## Before You Leave: What You Are Actually Deciding

The first thing to understand is that you are not preparing for a trip. You are preparing for a *submersion*. Most destinations a multiverse traveler encounters can be managed through a series of adjustments — the right clothing, the right documentation, the right cultural knowledge, the right pharmaceutical preparation. The Condensation Cities require all of those things and then require something else, something harder to acquire at a gear shop or through a briefing: a fundamental renegotiation of what you expect your experience of being somewhere to *feel like*.

You will return from this place, if you return, changed in a way that is not dramatic. It will not be the dramatic change of someone who survived something. It will be the quiet change of someone who spent a long time in a place that operated by different rules, until the different rules stopped feeling like rules and started feeling like the way things simply are. This is worth knowing in advance. It is worth deciding, before you go, whether that is something you want.

If you are the kind of traveler who measures a destination by its landmarks, its cuisine, its social infrastructure, its opportunities for the kind of experience that produces good stories for the kind of story you already know how to tell — consider carefully. There are landmarks here in the technical sense, but they are landmarks in the way that a particular water-carved canyon is a landmark: they are the result of processes, not the products of intention directed at you. There is no cuisine. The social infrastructure is not yours to enter. The stories you bring back will be difficult to tell because they will be about duration and texture and a quality of attention that does not compress into narrative.

But if you are the kind of traveler for whom the above paragraph is not a warning but a description of exactly what you came for — read on carefully. This place will give you what you're looking for, and it will take more than you expect.

## The Cold: Understanding What You Are Up Against

The cold of the Condensation Cities is not a temperature to be managed. It is an environment to be inhabited, which is a different problem.

Standard elevation cold is a straightforward adversary: it pulls heat from exposed surfaces, you cover those surfaces, the problem is solved or at least negotiated. The cold here is something different in character because it works through moisture, and the moisture is *pervasive* — not weather that will change, not a rain that will end, but the atmosphere itself in its normal state. The air at the Condensation Cities is chronically supersaturated. Water is not falling through the air; water is becoming the air; water is becoming *you*.

This distinction matters enormously for equipment choices. A parka that would keep you warm and dry in an Arctic wind will, within a few hours here, have wet-through its insulation from the outside — not from rain but from simple molecular contact with the air. Standard waterproofing, which works by preventing liquid water from penetrating a membrane, is adequate against drops but less adequate against an atmosphere that is simply depositing itself on everything. Your outer layer must be vapor-barrier capable or you will slowly lose the battle.

The practical hierarchy of priorities for warmth is:

**First, your core.** You will lose heat from the inside out if the cold gets ahead of you, and once it gets ahead of you it is very difficult to recover in an environment where there is no warm space to retreat to and no sun to sit in. Wool against the skin is the classic choice because it retains some warming capacity when wet; some synthetic insulators will serve better depending on the specific construction. Whatever you choose, it should be capable of maintaining function when saturated, because it *will* become saturated.

**Second, your extremities.** Hands are the critical issue and require more thought than most travelers give them. You will need to use your hands — to steady yourself, to handle equipment, to take notes, to do the small tasks that travel requires — but standard gloves, even good ones, become wet and then useless over the course of hours. Consider a layered glove system: a thin inner glove for when you need dexterity, a substantial outer covering that can be shed quickly, and critically, a *backup pair* kept in a dry-bag inside your main layer so that you always have something dry to go back to. Wet hands in this cold are a medical issue within thirty minutes. Do not be casual about this.

**Third, your head and face.** The mist condenses on every exposed surface including the skin of your face. After several hours of exposure, your cheeks and forehead will be continuously wet. This is not dangerous on its own but compounds everything else; moisture on the face accelerates the loss of core temperature. A balaclava or face covering that you can raise and lower as needed, that works when wet, is not optional.

**Fourth, your feet.** The ground — where there is ground, which is not always — is wet stone, and wet stone at this temperature becomes cold-transfer device of remarkable efficiency. Insulated, waterproof boots are the obvious answer, but pay careful attention to insulation ratings under wet conditions rather than dry, which is where most manufacturers are publishing their numbers. Bring vapor-barrier socks as an inner layer if you plan to be here longer than a day. Gaiters are worth wearing from the moment you arrive.

**A note on sleeping:** There is no comfortable way to sleep in the Condensation Cities. There is no warm building to retreat to, no reliable shelter, no campfire that will stay lit in this moisture. Sleeping outdoors here is a technical challenge equal to or greater than high-altitude mountaineering, and in some ways harder because you cannot simply dig a snow cave. You are on wet rock. Your shelter must be free-standing, double-walled, vapor-barrier capable, and weighted to resist the structural components of the city that will, slowly, press against it if you stay in one place long enough. Some travelers recommend against sleeping here at all and simply visiting in extended day-trips from a more hospitable anchor point. If you must sleep on-site, practice your camp setup until you can do it quickly, in the dark, with gloves on, because those will be the conditions.

## The Dark: Navigating Without Landmarks

The light of the Condensation Cities is real but it is not sufficient for navigation. The bioluminescent secondary emission from the organisms and the structure-junction glow will allow you to see that shapes exist at the distances where shapes exist, but not to read those shapes well enough to make confident movement decisions. You will need light.

This creates an immediate conflict: the organisms respond to certain wavelengths of light more strongly than others, and you do not, before arriving, know which wavelengths those are or what "respond" means in organisms that operate on geological timescales. There is some evidence that standard white LED light in the range of 5000K to 6500K produces detectable behavioral changes in organisms within a radius of several meters over a period of hours — changes that are too slow to observe in real time but are visible in documentation taken at intervals. Whether those changes are harmful to the organisms, neutral, or in some sense positive is unknown. What is known is that they are changes, and that the Condensation Cities are precisely the kind of place where producing undocumented changes in the existing system is the kind of thing a thoughtful traveler thinks carefully about.

If you want to minimize your impact, carry red-spectrum lights and use them for safety-critical navigation only. Red light in the 620-750nm range appears to produce fewer observable secondary effects, and your eyes will adapt to it sufficiently for careful, slow movement. Bring several — one as primary, one as backup, one as emergency — all rated for cold-weather battery performance, which is to say: rated for at least twenty degrees below the temperature at which you expect to operate, because batteries fail in cold, and fail faster when wet.

Accept that you will not be able to move quickly. This is not a problem with the right orientation. The city does not reward quick movement. The city rewards the opposite.

Do not rely on electronic compass systems. The structural materials of the city, particularly the old deep-rooted components, produce magnetic field distortions that are inconsistent and not corrected for in standard cartographic equipment. The city exists on young rock that contains mineral seams of unusual composition, some of which are affecting to navigation instruments. Paper maps — if maps of this location exist, which is inconsistent and varies by access point — are more reliable than digital navigation for general orientation but are not reliable at the detail level; the city changes on the timescale of decades, which means a map more than a few decades old represents something meaningfully different from what is there now.

Your best navigation tool is a combination of altitude sense and careful observation of water movement. Gravity is operating normally. Water moves down. The city is layered on a vertical face. By keeping careful track of which direction water is running and paying attention to your altitude relative to known reference points, you can maintain a general sense of where you are without requiring accurate magnetic north. Develop this habit early.

## The Sound: Protecting Your Hearing and Your Mind

The noise of the Condensation Cities is extraordinary in volume, and it is a specific kind of volume that hearing protection calculators are not designed to handle. Standard industrial hearing protection is calibrated against intermittent loud sounds or consistent loud sounds at specific frequencies. The sound here is consistent, omnidirectional, layered across a very wide frequency spectrum, and partially conducted through the ground and the structures themselves rather than through air alone.

Bring good earplugs. Use them when the noise is at its most intense — which seems to vary, though the mechanism of variation is not well understood, possibly tied to the activity levels of the organisms at their own slow rhythm. Do not use them so consistently that you deprive yourself of the texture of the sound, which is information. The sound is the way the city communicates with itself and you are inside that communication; eliminating it entirely is like visiting a foreign city with your eyes shut.

More important than the physical protection is the psychological preparation. The sound does something to temporal perception after extended exposure. This is reported consistently enough by travelers to be treated as a reliable effect rather than an individual quirk. The mechanism is unknown, but the experience is: after some period of continuous immersion in the sound — which varies by individual but seems to average between six and fourteen hours — you begin to lose the ordinary sense of time passing that you rely on for grounding. You do not lose consciousness. You do not hallucinate. You simply cease to have reliable access to duration. A period of twenty minutes may feel like two hours or like five. You continue to be functional but you stop being able to trust your intuition about when things happened.

This is manageable. The way to manage it is to set and keep regular external time cues before the effect begins, so that the habit is established: set a timer for every thirty minutes, and when it goes off, write in a notebook what you observe. Not just what you see but what you feel and what time it is. This creates an external record that your unreliable internal clock can be checked against. Many travelers find that the act of writing at intervals is additionally useful for the reason that it keeps them in contact with language, with the practice of describing things, which is a form of cognitive anchoring.

After returning from an extended visit, your temporal perception returns to normal within a few hours. There are no known long-term effects. The disorientation is specific to the environment and does not persist outside it.

What can persist is something harder to name: a kind of patience that you acquire by necessity in a place where everything moves slowly, which can make the ordinary pace of life elsewhere feel anxious and rushed by comparison, for some days or weeks after return. This is generally experienced as an adjustment period and resolves on its own. Some travelers report that they miss it.

## The Organisms: Behavior and Protocol

The fauna of the Condensation Cities are large. This is the most important practical fact about them and it is consistently underweighted by travelers who have read descriptions and still not truly internalized it until they are looking at something whose passage takes several minutes to complete and which, at its largest cross-section, would block a doorway.

They are not aggressive. This is established through enough direct observation to be stated as a general principle rather than a hopeful guess. They do not exhibit what we would recognize as predatory behavior toward humans or toward objects humans bring. They do not appear to perceive humans as either threats or resources in any way that registers in their behavior at the timescales we can observe. This does not mean they are safe to be near in all circumstances, because their movement at slow speed is still movement at significant mass, and something large moving slowly can do significant mechanical damage to something smaller and slower to detect the problem. Pay attention to any large organism that is moving in any direction, even a direction that seems unlikely to intersect yours. Unlikely intersections over the timescale of minutes are more possible than they look.

Do not touch them. This is both a safety guideline and an ethical position. We do not know what our touch communicates to them on their timescale or what, if anything, it means in the chemical or vibrational registers they appear to use. The organisms are not visitors to this place. This is their place in the most fundamental sense: they are woven into it, it is partially constructed from their activity over deep time, they are not guests. You are the guest. Conduct yourself accordingly.

Do not touch the structures either, more than necessary for safety. This is less rigorously enforceable — you will, at times, need to steady yourself on something, and the city offers structures as the most available steadying surface — but default to minimal contact. The organisms interact with the structure surfaces in ways that are chemically mediated, which means that introducing your skin chemistry to those surfaces is potentially introducing something into those interactions. The effect is probably small. The compounding effect of many travelers introducing their skin chemistry over time is less small.

Do not bring food into the city. This is more important than it might sound. The chemistry of the Condensation Cities is calibrated to processes that are not based on organic decomposition, because nothing decomposes here — the cold and the organism-ecology together prevent it. Any organic material that you drop, leave, or lose will persist and will be a chemical anomaly in a system that has been running for geological time without that particular anomaly. The organisms will eventually interact with it, over their timescale, and what that interaction produces is unknown. Keep your food in sealed containers. Eat before you arrive or after you leave. If you must eat while inside the city, seal and remove every wrapper, every core, every crumb with the same attention you would give to radioactive material, because the timescale of the effects, while not radioactive, are in the same category of *will outlast you.*

## Equipment: The Practical List

Starting from the assumption that you have the thermal and lighting gear discussed above, these are the remaining essential categories:

**Documentation.** Whatever format you use, it should function when wet and in the dark. Paper notebooks work; carry more than you think you need, in waterproof cases, with multiple pens because pens fail in the cold. Cameras work if they are sealed against moisture; record audio separately from video because the sound is half the experience and most integrated microphones will compress it into unrecognizable noise. Write things down even if you are also recording them, because the physical act of writing keeps you connected to ordinary time in the way that pressing record does not.

**Medical.** Hypothermia is your primary medical risk. Carry chemical hand warmers as emergency tools. Carry a full change of clothing in a dry bag for emergency use. Know the signs of hypothermia onset in yourself, which are specifically: paradoxical warmth (suddenly feeling too warm when you have not changed your situation), slurred or difficult thinking, reduced coordination, cessation of shivering (which is late-stage and serious). Have a plan for what you do if any of those appear — which plan must not involve thinking you can resolve the situation where you are, because you cannot; it must involve exiting the city.

**Structural security.** You will at some point need to move across surfaces that drop away on one side. The city's geometry is complex and occasionally presents you with situations where the next position you need to be in is across a gap or above a void that you would prefer not to fall into. Carry a harness and a length of dynamic rope and know how to use them. If you have not climbed before, this is a reason to learn some basics before you go — not because the city is a climbing destination, but because the ability to make yourself safe on vertical rock is a prerequisite for moving through this environment without avoidable incident.

**Navigation.** Paper map if available. Altimeter. Watch with alarm function. Writing materials as discussed. Red-spectrum light. Do not rely on GPS or electronic compass for fine navigation; use them only for broad orientation if available.

**Communication.** Standard radio communication is inconsistent inside the city due to the interference generated by the structural materials. If you have a partner or a team, establish a protocol of periodic check-ins at fixed locations and fixed times, and establish clearly what the response to a missed check-in is *before you are inside the city,* because trying to communicate the plan when you are already in the sound and the dark and potentially experiencing temporal disorientation is not the time to be deciding it.

## Mental Preparation: The Long Work Before the Trip

Everything above is equipment and protocol. This section is different.

The Condensation Cities will test your capacity to be present in a place that is not designed for you, in any sense, to be present in. Not hostile to you — the city is not hostile — but *indifferent* in the fullest sense of the word, which is to say: the city is oriented toward purposes that have nothing to do with you, that were established before you or anything like you existed, that will continue after you are gone, and that do not intersect with your presence in any way that the city or its inhabitants will find meaningful.

Most places a traveler visits are, to some degree, shaped by the presence of travelers. Even wild places are shaped by human presence; even remote ecosystems are ecosystems with humans somewhere in them. The Condensation Cities are not. The city was not built for visitors. The organisms have no category for visitors in any behavioral sense we can detect. The water moves through channels that were directed by purposes operating on geological time. Your presence is, in the most precise sense, irrelevant to the place you are visiting.

This is either deeply uncomfortable or deeply liberating, depending on your orientation. Either way, it requires preparation, because our ordinary experience of being in a place involves some degree of the place being, in some sense, aware of our being there — even a forest responds to our footsteps, even a desert shows us the marks of our passing. Here, you will leave marks that the slow work of water and organisms will erase on their timescale, and the city will not care that you were there, and the organisms will not remember that you moved among them, and the rock beneath you will continue becoming what it is regardless of what you do or how long you stay.

Some travelers find this devastating. Some find it clarifying. You should find out, before you go, which you are likely to be — not because the devastating experience is a reason not to go, but because you should enter it with your eyes open. The way to find out is to practice: find a place in your ordinary life where you can spend several hours in an environment that is operating completely independently of your presence and wishes, and practice not needing it to be otherwise. A forest will do this. An ocean will do this. Sit somewhere that has its own concerns, attend carefully to those concerns, and observe your response to the fact that the concerns are not about you. That response is information.

Practice also the specific skill of slow attention: taking something you can see and watching it for longer than you want to. The Condensation Cities offer nothing that resolves quickly. If you are habituated to experience that offers regular moments of resolution — scenes that complete themselves, actions that conclude, interactions that have outcomes — you will find the city exhausting in a way that is not physical. Everything here is process, never product. You can develop a tolerance for process, and it is worth developing before you go.

## A Last Note, and What It Might Mean

The city is in the process of becoming itself. You can read that as description or you can let it stay in your mind as something more unsettling.

Every city is, in some sense, an answer to the question of what a particular form of collective intelligence produces when it has enough time and enough stability. The Condensation Cities are an answer to that question from a form of intelligence — or process, or collaboration between many forms — that operates in slow time, on wet rock, in permanent darkness lit by the artifacts of living. The answer is, by any measure: vast, complex, durable, intricate, and absolutely indifferent to whether you can understand it.

You are going to a place that represents a kind of success — biological, architectural, whatever the right word is — that succeeded on completely different terms than anything that built the civilization that built you. The terms it succeeded on are patience, duration, integration, the willingness of individual elements to become deeply entangled with the substrate and with each other over periods of time that make the concept of individual accomplishment meaningless.

You cannot extract anything from this place. You cannot bring a piece of it with you in any meaningful sense. What you can do is spend time inside it and return having been, for a period, in contact with something ancient and sure of itself and completely uninterested in your opinion. This is, for many travelers, the most important thing the multiverse has to offer.

Dress warmly. Protect your hands. Write things down. Move slowly. Do not touch the organisms.

Do not expect it to be for you.

Let it be what it is.
