# Preparing for the Fabricated Plateau: A Traveler's Guide

---

## First, Adjust Your Expectations About What "Preparation" Means Here

Most locations reward preparation that is practical — the right gear, the right maps, the right immunizations. The Fabricated Plateau rewards a different kind of preparation, one that is more psychological than logistical, more perceptual than material. This is not to say the logistical doesn't matter. It matters. But if you arrive here having only packed well and not having thought carefully about how your mind is going to fail you, the packing will not save you. Prepare your equipment and then, more importantly, prepare your epistemology. You are going somewhere that will systematically mislead your senses, and most of what you'll need to survive it is an honest reckoning with that fact before you get there.

---

## Understanding the Altitude Before You Arrive

The plateau is at sustained elevation, and sustained elevation is a different physiological problem than peak elevation. With peak climbing, your body knows something is wrong immediately — the deprivation is acute. With plateau altitude, the deprivation is chronic and mild and easy to misattribute. You will feel slightly slower than usual, slightly more tired, slightly less sharp, and you will explain this to yourself as mood, as nerves, as the strangeness of the place affecting you emotionally, and you will be partly right but mostly wrong. It's your blood oxygen.

Prepare for this honestly. If you have access to altitude acclimatization before arrival — a few days at intermediate elevation — take it. If you don't, at minimum give yourself a day of low activity after arrival before you attempt any navigation or problem-solving. The first day, just observe. Sit near the machines. Let your body find its footing. The plateau is not going anywhere, and the decisions you make at 80% cognitive capacity on day one will be worse than the decisions you make at full capacity on day two.

Pack acetazolamide if you have access to it and no contraindications. Drink more water than you think you need. The air is cool and the light is bright and the diffuse luminosity creates no sense of heat, so you will not feel like you're losing water, and you will be losing water. The dryness of the altitude combines with the false mild humidity of the plateau air to produce a dehydration that sneaks up on you over hours. Your first sign of trouble will not be thirst. It will be a slight worsening of the depth perception problems the place already creates, a slight acceleration of confusion that you'll want to attribute to the plateau's strangeness and should instead treat by drinking water immediately.

## Clothing and Thermal Strategy

The plateau is cold in the specific way that high elevated places with sourceless bright light are cold — which is to say, your eye says warm and your skin says otherwise, and the disconnect between these inputs is itself mildly destabilizing. The brightness without shadows reads as noon heat even when the temperature is twelve degrees Celsius. Dress in layers you can add and remove, and develop the discipline to add a layer before you feel cold rather than after. By the time you feel cold here, you've been cold for longer than you know.

The moisture situation is subtle but important. The dew, the occasional light rain, the ambient humidity below conscious perception — these will work on you gradually. Nothing dramatic. But synthetics that wick are better than cotton that holds. Your base layers should move moisture away from your skin. The tacky-cold quality of metal surfaces on the plateau is your indicator that condensation is happening constantly, and the same process is happening to your clothing at a slower rate.

Footwear requires specific consideration. The hexagonal plates are proud at their edges — uplifted, sharp, unchamfered — and the gaps between them are two to five centimeters wide and dark. These gaps are a sprain risk and a debris trap. You want boots with ankle support and soles stiff enough that they don't flex down into the gap if your foot lands across one. Vibram-type soles. Nothing with a toe box that tapers into a point. The edges of the plates where they've lifted are described as geometrically new, meaning sharp and unforgiving. Low-clearance footwear will catch on these. Also: the hollow-drum quality of the plates suggests there's air underneath, which means sound carries through the surface differently than through earth. This matters for tracking, which matters for the fauna question. Learn to move quietly, and your footwear should cooperate.

Gloves or at minimum hand awareness. The machines are warm to the touch in some cases and cold in others, and the tacky-cold metal of the plateau is everywhere. You will want to touch things, because the place is strange and touching is how humans investigate strange things, and you should touch things, but do it with awareness that some of the machines are processing material that exits through vents and the nature of that material is uncertain.

Bring a hat that covers your ears. The sourceless brightness will not burn your skin the way directional sun would, but the UV at altitude is real regardless of whether you can identify a sun in the sky. And your ears will get cold before you expect them to.

## The Lighting Problem and What to Do About It

The light on the plateau is your most significant practical problem. Sourceless, diffuse, total, shadowless — this is not just aesthetically strange. It is functionally disabling in ways that cascade through every other activity you'll attempt.

Depth perception depends on shadow. Shadow tells your visual system where the surface is relative to the object, where the object is relative to other objects, how far away the far object is. Remove shadow and the visual system improvises using other cues — relative size, atmospheric haze, texture gradient, overlap. The plateau removes or degrades most of these too. The consistent surface texture (mineralized plate, plate, plate, plate) destroys texture gradient. The machines are too varied in size for relative size to work reliably. The atmosphere, slightly damp but not dramatically so, provides minimal haze for distance estimation. The machines overlap each other in the clusters and the visual system reads this as depth information but the depth information is wrong.

You need to compensate externally for what your visual system cannot do internally.

Bring a rangefinder. This sounds like overkill and it is not. A simple laser rangefinder, the kind used by hunters and golfers, lets you point at the far edge of the plateau or at a machine cluster or at a specific machine and get an actual number. Use it constantly. Develop the habit of ranging anything you're about to navigate toward before you start moving. The subjective distance and the actual distance will diverge regularly and dramatically, and the only cure is to know the actual distance before your legs start believing the subjective one.

Bring bright-colored physical markers — flagging tape, tent pegs painted vivid orange, anything highly chromatic and portable. The plateau's palette is gray-white-rust, and anything saturated will stand out violently against it. When you establish a camp or a base point, mark it visibly and abundantly. When you move through the machine clusters, leave markers at intervals. The scale problem means that "retracing your steps" is not a reliable way to return to your origin point; the place you came from looks far away even when you're close to it. You need to be able to see your anchor point from a distance, which means your anchor point needs to be visible against the gray.

A compass. Not GPS — GPS will tell you coordinates, but coordinates are not useful when the navigational problem is that sixty meters takes ten minutes of walking. A compass gives you direction, which is what you actually need. Decide on a heading when you leave your camp and hold it consistently. The machines will force deviations, but maintain your heading awareness and return to it. Establish a bearing toward the plateau's edge, note it, walk it, and range it regularly to track whether you're actually approaching.

A note about the sun, or its absence: the sourceless light means you cannot use the sun to navigate, cannot use shadows to estimate time, cannot use the sky's gradient to tell east from west. This is disorientating in a way you cannot fully appreciate until you're experiencing it. The sky is a uniform pale plate in every direction. Bring a watch. Track time consciously, because your internal clock will decouple from any environmental cue and drift.

## The Acoustic Environment and Your Relationship to It

The engineered quiet is one of the plateau's genuinely remarkable phenomena — hundreds of small sounds overlapping into a textured silence — and it is also a safety hazard.

The problem is signal. In normal environments, you hear things coming. Footsteps, breathing, the rustle of movement through vegetation. On the plateau, the background acoustic texture is fine enough and omnidirectional enough that locating a new sound within it is difficult. The animals that leave warm nests and tracks with a stride length longer than you'd prefer — they exist in this environment, and they move within it, and the collective mechanical hum may or may not mask their approach.

Wear nothing over your ears. No noise-canceling headphones. No music. Keep your hearing free and active, and begin immediately to learn the specific acoustic texture of the plateau so you can recognize departures from it. This takes time. Spend the first hours of your visit simply listening — not searching for anything specific, but calibrating. What does the normal sound of this place feel like as a felt sense in your body? The subsonic vibrations that register in your teeth are part of this. Memorize the baseline. Departures from baseline are information.

The teeth-vibration is worth specific attention. Subsonic sound at plateau levels is produced by large things — large mechanical systems, large animals, large structures settling. When something new begins contributing to the subsonics, you may not hear it consciously. You will feel it in your body. Trust your body's reporting on this. If something changes in how the plateau vibrates and you can't immediately account for it, become more alert rather than explaining it away.

## Water and Food

The plateau is not a foraging environment. There is nothing to eat there in any recognizable sense. Bring all food you will need for the duration of your visit, with surplus for delays. The scale problem means that trips you plan as short may run long, and you should have more food than your optimistic schedule requires.

Water is more complex. The dew and condensation exist, and in principle could be collected, but the surfaces they condense on are decades-mineralized concrete-analog plates and machine housings with unknown processing histories. Drinking water that has run across these surfaces and into the gaps is drinking water with an uncertain history. If you must collect water from condensation, collect it from the highest point of a plate's surface, before it runs toward the gap, and filter and treat it. The rust-brown staining of ferric process on the machines tells you that metal is leaching into the water cycle here. Bring a filter capable of removing heavy metals, not just biological contaminants. A ceramic filter will handle biological material; you need something with an activated carbon stage for the dissolved minerals.

The light rain events — twenty minutes, infrequent — are cleaner water than the condensation. A collection tarp or rain catchment of some kind is worth its weight. But the unpredictability of the rain makes it a supplement rather than a plan.

Bring more water than you think you need. I said this under altitude and I am saying it again. The plateau will not feel like a place that dehydrates you and it is a place that dehydrates you.

## The Fauna Problem: How to Think About What You Cannot See

The animals on the plateau are present and invisible, which is the most destabilizing thing about the place. It is one thing to know there are animals and not see them because they're hidden. It's another to have the distinct impression they're offset — occupying the same space in a register you can't access. The second experience is harder to metabolize psychologically. Your threat-detection systems evolved for visible dangers and they do not handle gracefully the idea of danger-that-is-present-but-not-accessible. Expect low-grade continuous alertness and understand that this alertness will be tiring.

What you know about the fauna:

The small animals are small — appliance-sized or smaller, based on the scale of nesting material and the register of their feces. Their invisibility is likely behavioral (extreme flight distance, nocturnal, or genuinely fast) rather than supernatural. They are not your primary concern.

The nest-builders are intelligent in at least one sense — they read the machine's interior geometry and worked with it. This is a level of problem-solving that suggests a complex nervous system. They are large enough to use the open-framed machines as housing. They are not present when you look at the nests, but the nests are warm. This means they left recently and may return. Do not sleep inside a machine, open-framed or otherwise. Your camp should be in one of the lanes between clusters, with clear sightlines in multiple directions and with your markers set. If you hear or feel the acoustic texture shift in your camp area at night, light should be your first tool — bright, omnidirectional if possible. You are not trying to be threatening. You are trying to be legible. Animals that are nest-building problem-solvers are likely responsive to information about your presence.

The large thing. The thing whose stride length you would prefer shorter. Its sign is recent. You do not know its nature. What you know is that it is heavy enough to disturb mineral particulate sequentially across multiple plates and that its stride is long. In the absence of more specific information, the only rational posture is: don't be interesting to it. This means: process your food waste completely (carry it out or bury it deep), don't leave attractants on your camp surface, sleep off the ground if possible, and recognize that on a plateau with scale distortion, "far away" means nothing reliable. The large thing could be very close. Your rangefinder and your compass are tools for making space legible; use them.

The aerial things are likely not a direct concern. They move fast, they're navigating thermals, they don't call. They're doing their own thing. Observe them if you can; they may be reading the surface in ways that give you information about what's below.

## Navigation: A Practical System for Not Getting Lost in a Place That Wants You Lost

The plateau is not hostile, exactly — it is indifferent, and its indifference produces conditions that your navigation systems will fail in. Here is a system that accounts for that failure:

**Establish a home base.** Mark it lavishly. Multiple flags, visible from multiple directions, as tall as you can make them. The gray-white palette of the plateau means that anything chromatic stands out at a distance; use this.

**Before every excursion, range your destination.** Know the actual distance in meters before you start walking. Write it down.

**Count your steps.** This sounds tedious and it is. Do it anyway. An average adult walking pace is about 1.5 meters per step. If your rangefinder said 120 meters, you should arrive in about 80 steps. If you've taken 200 steps and haven't arrived, you have a problem you need to address immediately rather than continuing. Stop, re-range, recalibrate.

**Leave a physical trail.** Every twenty meters, place a marker. This is not for navigation out — the compass handles that — but for psychological anchoring. You need to be able to turn around and see where you came from. The scale problem removes this capability without artificial assistance.

**Set time limits and honor them.** Decide before you leave camp that you will turn back at a specific time. The plateau will eat time — you'll be absorbed in observing a machine cluster, you'll be recalibrating your rangefinder, you'll be watching the aerial things cross the bleached sky — and if you have no external time commitment, you will find yourself farther from camp with less light than intended. The light here doesn't change in a way that warns you. There's no reddening of the western sky, no lengthening shadows. The change, when it comes, may come differently than you expect. Set alarms.

**The edge.** You may be walking toward it indefinitely. This is the plateau's central navigational deception, and it is not fully explained by the false depth cues — there is something additional happening, something that the description names but doesn't fully account for. My recommendation: don't make reaching the edge your goal on any given excursion. Make a specific machine cluster your goal, or a specific distance from camp, or a specific duration. The edge as a target may be permanently unattainable, and fixating on it may cause you to over-extend. If the edge matters to you, approach it from a different angle on a different day and see if the behavior changes. Make notes. Treat the scale distortion as an empirical phenomenon to study rather than an obstacle to overcome.

## Mental Preparation: The Harder Work

Everything above is practical. This section is for the part of you that is going to be uncomfortable in ways that gear cannot address.

The Fabricated Plateau is not a place where you will understand what is happening. The machines are processing materials for purposes you cannot deduce. The animals occupy a register you cannot access. The scale does not resolve. The light provides no temporal information. The engineering is purposeful and the purpose is opaque. If you are a person who needs to understand what you're seeing — and most humans need this, it's not a weakness, it's a feature of the pattern-recognition system that keeps you alive — you will find the plateau continuously, quietly distressing in the way that things that resist categorization are distressing. Your mind will reach for an explanation and close on nothing and reach again.

Prepare for this by practicing, before you arrive, a specific mental posture: *observation without resolution.* You will note something. You will not know what it means. You will file it without interpreting it. This is harder than it sounds. We note things by interpreting them — we see a nest and immediately think "something sleeps here" and "something made this" and "is it coming back?" Useful thoughts, but on the plateau they cascade quickly into anxiety because the answers don't arrive. Practice stopping at the observation. A nest is here. It is warm. It is made of these materials. I don't know more than this yet. Leave the file open. Move on.

The low-grade attentiveness — what the description calls a category question your body is asking — will not resolve. The plateau will not clarify whether it is safe. You will be in that state of extended alertness for the duration of your visit. This is physiologically expensive. It accelerates fatigue, disrupts sleep, impairs decision-making over time. Build explicit rest periods into your schedule — times when you stop navigating, stop observing, stop trying to make sense of the place, and simply exist in it. Sit with your back to a machine, drink water, eat something, let your eyes unfocus. This is maintenance, not laziness.

The warm empty nests and the invisible animals and the recent large tracks and the things that move in your peripheral vision and are gone — all of this is designed, by the nature of the place, to make you feel watched. You probably are watched. The fauna is present even when it's not visible. Accommodate this rather than resisting it. You are a visitor here, and the permanent residents, whatever their register of occupation, have tenure you don't. The appropriate posture is one of respectful acknowledgment: you know something is here, you can't see it, you're not trying to disturb it, you're passing through.

## What to Bring: Consolidated List

**Navigation:**
Laser rangefinder; compass; mechanical watch or watch with altimeter; bright chromatic flagging tape (50+ meters); tent pegs in high-visibility color; notebook and pencil (not pen — cold and moisture will fail ink) for recording distances, headings, and observations

**Visibility and Safety:**
High-lumen headlamp with red-light mode; spare batteries; a second flashlight independent of the first; personal locator beacon if your multiverse travel modality supports such technology

**Environmental Protection:**
Layered synthetic or wool clothing (no cotton in contact layers); waterproof outer shell; insulated hat covering ears; waterproof gloves; ankle-supporting boots with stiff Vibram-type sole; UV-protective eye covering (glacier glasses or quality sunglasses — the sourceless bright light is real UV even without a visible sun); sunscreen for exposed skin, applied whether or not you feel like you need it

**Water and Food:**
All food for duration plus two additional days; minimum 3L water capacity per person; water filter with both biological and heavy metals capability; rain collection tarp; electrolyte supplements (altitude and exertion both deplete electrolytes)

**Health:**
Acetazolamide if appropriate and accessible; ibuprofen (for altitude headache); blister treatment (the plate edges will catch feet unprepared); first aid kit with emphasis on wound cleaning (the mineral environment means abrasions pick up contaminants); pulse oximeter to monitor blood oxygen

**For the Fauna Situation:**
Bear canister or equivalent for food storage; bear spray or equivalent deterrent (check whether it's legal and applicable to fauna with unknown biology); bright light source that can be pointed omnidirectionally; something that makes noise you can deploy without reaching for (a whistle around your neck)

## A Final Note on What Kind of Visitor to Be

The plateau is old enough to have developed its own ecology, young enough that the ecology is still finding its form. The machines have been here for decades; the animals have adapted to them, built with them, nested in their interiors. The rust is the machines' autobiography, and the mineral deposits on the plates are the water's autobiography, and the nests are the animals' autobiography, and none of these stories is finished.

You are arriving in the middle of an ongoing process whose beginning you missed and whose end you won't see. This is true of most places, but most places have accumulated enough legibility that you can at least read the chapter you're in. The plateau makes this harder. The legibility it offers is oblique — you read it through tideline rings and disturbed particulate and warm nests, through the mild suction of intake vents and the taste of metal in the water cycle and the stride length you measure across disrupted plates.

Read slowly. Take notes. Don't try to conclude.

The far edge is sixty meters away.
It has been sixty meters away for a while now.
Keep walking toward it, and remember that the walking is the thing, not the arriving.
