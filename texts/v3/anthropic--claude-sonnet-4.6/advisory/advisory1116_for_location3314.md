# Preparing for the Hollow City: A Traveler's Guide

---

## Before You Go: Honest Assessment of the Destination

The Hollow City is not hostile to human visitors. This distinction matters. There is no predation, no active danger, no malice in the environment. The city is not trying to kill you. It is simply engaged in its own process, and that process has parameters that differ substantially from the parameters human bodies evolved to handle. The danger is not attack. The danger is accumulated physiological stress compounding quietly until it becomes a crisis, combined with a sensory and psychological environment that is unusual enough to degrade judgment in ways you will not initially recognize.

Go in knowing this: the Hollow City is an endurance environment. The challenge is not dramatic. The challenge is metabolic, cognitive, and existential — the slow grinding pressure of sustained heat, desiccation, sensory strangeness, and the peculiar mental difficulty of existing in a place that is neither hostile nor welcoming but simply indifferent in a way that is more total than you are accustomed to. Most visitors underestimate this because there is nothing obviously dangerous. This is a classic failure mode. Prepare accordingly.

## The Primary Threat: Heat and Desiccation

Fifty degrees Celsius of dry heat. Let that number sit for a moment. Human body temperature is approximately 37°C. The air of the Hollow City is thirteen degrees above that. Your body is used to losing heat to the surrounding environment through radiation and convection. In the Hollow City, the surrounding environment is hotter than you are. You cannot lose heat to it. You can only gain it.

The body's primary remaining cooling mechanism in these conditions is evaporative cooling — sweat. But the air is described as phenomenally dry, so dry that exhaled breath vanishes before it can drift. This means sweat evaporates almost instantaneously from your skin, which is actually efficient cooling, but it means your fluid loss rate is catastrophic and largely invisible to you. You will not feel wet. You will feel, initially, almost nothing — dry, perhaps, a slight warmth. The sweat is gone before it accumulates. You are being desiccated at speed while feeling almost comfortable. This is the trap.

Standard dangerous heat exposure timelines collapse in these conditions. A fit adult in moderate shade at 50°C with zero humidity can experience dangerous heat stress in under an hour without intervention. Water is not available in the environment. The vegetation yields no moisture. The stone will pull at your tongue. The channels breathe dry. There is nothing to drink in the Hollow City.

**What this means for preparation:**

You must bring all the water you will need and then bring more. This is not a situation for estimating based on how thirsty you feel. By the time you are thirsty in extreme dry heat, you are already meaningfully dehydrated. Establish a drinking schedule before you enter and adhere to it mechanically, by timer if necessary, regardless of subjective thirst. In 50°C dry conditions, a resting adult loses fluid at roughly a liter per hour through respiration and skin transpiration alone. Activity doubles or triples this. Calculate conservatively.

If you are staying for any significant period, electrolyte replacement is not optional. You are losing sodium, potassium, and magnesium along with the water. Pure water replacement without electrolyte replacement in high-sweat situations causes hyponatremia — dilution of blood sodium — which presents as nausea, headache, confusion, and in serious cases, seizures. The irony is that drinking water without electrolytes in these circumstances can kill you while dehydration is also killing you, and you will not immediately know which direction the danger is coming from. Bring electrolyte supplements. Use them on a schedule.

**Cooling equipment:** Passive clothing choices matter enormously. The intuitive response to heat is to remove clothing, but in extreme dry heat this is wrong. Loose, light-colored, breathable fabric — natural fibers like linen or light wool, which handle moisture-wicking differently than synthetics — creates a microclimate against the skin that reduces the rate of radiative heat gain from the environment. The Bedouin wearing robes in desert heat are not doing it wrong. You want your skin shielded from the air, not exposed to it. Cover your extremities. A loose, light wrap for the head and neck. The goal is to slow heat gain, not achieve comfort — comfort is not available here.

Bring a small, densely packable cooling vest or phase-change cooling panels if you can manage the weight. In the absence of active cooling, even a vest soaked in water and allowed to evaporate will help — though you will need to account for the water it contains in your supply calculation. A battery-powered personal fan is of limited use in still 50°C air (you are moving hot air over yourself) but has some value if it helps accelerate evaporation from damp cooling fabric.

**Do not bring heat-generating equipment you do not need.** Headlamps are necessary; choose those with good thermal management. Extra batteries generate no heat in storage but will in poorly managed electronics. Be sparing.

## Breathing: The Dry Air Problem

You exhale moisture. In the Hollow City, that moisture is stripped from the air immediately and does not return to you. Your respiratory tract — the mucous membranes of the nose, throat, and upper lungs — are designed to work in air with at least some humidity. Extended breathing of extremely dry air at high temperature causes progressive drying of these surfaces, which first reduces their function (they filter, warm, and humidify incoming air for the lungs) and then causes cracking, bleeding, and increased infection risk.

**Preparation:** A lightweight respirator or dampened mask serves two purposes here. First, it partially humidifies the air before it reaches your throat and lungs by trapping some exhaled moisture at the mask surface. Second — of significant relevance in a very old sealed space with fine mineral dust on every horizontal surface — it reduces the particulate load you are breathing. That pale powder on the flagstones has been accumulating for centuries. It is almost certainly not biologically dangerous, but fine mineral dust in quantity is mechanically irritating to lungs regardless of chemistry, and in a place where thermal channels actively create air currents that redistribute this dust, it will become airborne. A simple particle-filtering mask significantly reduces the burden.

If you have never breathed very dry hot air for extended periods, be prepared for headaches associated with this. They are distinct from dehydration headaches and from heat headaches, though all three can overlap unpleasantly. The sensation is often located in the sinus cavities and behind the eyes. Staying hydrated and using a mask both help. So does slow, deliberate, deep breathing rather than the shallow rapid breathing that tends to accompany heat stress.

## Navigation and Light

The amber light of the Hollow City fades objects to inference at ten meters. Navigation in a structured urban space where you can clearly see only ten meters in any direction is not impossible, but it requires discipline.

**Mapping.** Bring materials to sketch your path. Not because you will necessarily get hopelessly lost — the city is a city, not a cave system, and has structured geometry — but because spatial memory degrades under heat stress. What seems like a route you will certainly remember becomes uncertain after an hour of sustained heat exposure, and in a uniform amber dimness where all the stone looks similar and the vegetation has colonized all the distinctive carved markers, you will find your confidence in your own recall dropping. Do not trust your memory. Externalize it. Sketch as you go.

Bring thread or cord if you are going deep — the old system is reliable precisely because it requires no batteries. The ability to follow your own path back is worth more than it seems when you are tired and hot and slightly cognitively impaired by heat stress and you no longer entirely trust your sketch.

**Lighting equipment.** You need a headlamp that functions reliably at high temperatures. Many LED lights perform fine at 50°C; most batteries do not, or rather they drain faster. Lithium batteries handle temperature extremes better than alkaline. Bring more batteries than you think you need. Bring a backup light source. Bring a third. Losing light in the Hollow City is not immediately fatal but it escalates the difficulty of every other challenge substantially and very quickly.

The amber light of the environment itself — seeping from walls or plants or some combination — is real and helpful for orientation, but do not rely on it for task lighting or close examination. It is atmospheric, not functional. Think of it as the visual equivalent of the hum: it establishes the place, it allows you to exist in the space, it does not allow you to do detailed work.

**Trail marking.** Because the vegetation has consumed most visible architectural distinction in the lower and mid levels, and because heat-stressed spatial memory is unreliable, consider bringing small, lightweight, highly visible markers — colored tape, glow tabs, anything that will read as distinct from the ambient amber — to place at decision points in your route. Remove them when you leave. The city has been in its current state for a very long time and is maintaining its own equilibrium; there is no call to add to its substrate.

## Sound Management and Cognitive Load

The Hollow City is loud in ways that accumulate. This requires serious consideration.

The subsonic grinding you feel in your back teeth more than hear is particularly interesting to prepare for because it is below conscious hearing threshold for most people but above the threshold for physiological response. Extended exposure to infrasound and near-infrasound has documented psychological effects: unease, mild paranoia, a feeling of being watched or of presence, disorientation, and in some cases visual disturbances at the edge of vision. These effects are real, physiological, and not a sign that something is wrong with you — they are a sign that the environment is working on you in a frequency range your brain processes without being able to consciously identify the source. Knowing about this in advance substantially reduces its psychological impact, because the experience of unease without identifiable cause is significantly more distressing than the experience of unease with a known physical explanation.

The thermal channel hum, which is audible and which changes pitch and character as you move through the space, is likely to produce sustained acoustic fatigue over several hours. This is a specific phenomenon: continuous complex sound that your brain attempts to parse and categorize, consuming attentional resources. The acoustic environment of the Hollow City — the hum, the crackle, the deep periodic boom, the background creak of the column-plants, the slow low grinding — constitutes an unusually high cognitive load for auditory processing, especially because it is complex enough to be interesting without being interpretable, which keeps your brain engaged with it even when you are trying to focus on other things.

**Preparation:** Ear protection that attenuates the high-frequency crackles and the channel hum while preserving enough awareness to hear significant changes in the sonic environment is worth considering. Standard foam earplugs attenuate too broadly; electronic ear protection (the kind worn at ranges, which compresses loud sounds while passing quiet ones) would be well-suited but is another piece of electronics to manage in the heat. At minimum, bring standard earplugs and use them for rest periods. The ability to dial the acoustic environment down even partially, even briefly, substantially reduces cumulative fatigue.

**The periodic boom.** It will startle you the first three or four times. It will not stop being startling entirely, but you will integrate it into your sensory background. Be aware that each startle response has a physiological cost — cortisol, adrenaline, cardiovascular spike — and in sustained heat these responses compound. There is no method to prevent this except prior knowledge. Knowing that it is coming, knowing what causes it (geometry plus thermal current plus resonant stone), allows you to recategorize it from threat-signal to environmental-feature, which reduces but does not eliminate the startle. This is another case where information is your best protective equipment.

## The Plant-Life: Contact and Caution

The vegetation of the Hollow City is alien in its biochemistry. It runs on something other than water. Its leaves yield a metallic, warm smell when pressed. It pulses with slow light.

The instinct of any curious traveler will be to touch things. Here is the calibrated caution:

Touch the stone freely. The stone is ancient and stable. Touch the lichen-like pulsing organism with gloves for your first interactions and observe for any skin response before touching it bare-handed. Touch the leaves of the column-plants cautiously the first time and wait. The alien biochemistry that allows these plants to function without water and in 50°C dry heat in sealed stone is not automatically dangerous to humans, but it is not automatically benign, and your immune system has no established response to these compounds because it has never encountered them. The smell of copper warming in a palm is interesting; it is also an indication of metallic compounds in the plant chemistry that could be irritating or toxic in sufficient dose.

**Do not eat anything.** This should not require emphasis but: do not eat anything. This is not a food source. It is not nutritionally familiar. The caloric return cannot be assessed and the toxicological risk cannot be assessed and the Hollow City is hot enough that gut problems compounding heat stress compounding dehydration is a cascade you genuinely do not want to experience.

Do not attempt to break or cut the plants from their substrate. The relationship between root-system and stonework is load-bearing and structural. You cannot assess what you might destabilize. Move through the spaces they have left without rearranging the architecture.

**The pulsing organisms:** if they respond to your presence — if your proximity changes the pattern or rate of the light-pulse in observable ways — note it and step back. The root-network appears to conduct signal across the whole settlement. You may be legible to the system in ways you cannot fully evaluate. Legibility is not danger; it is a reason for thoughtful behavior. If the system is signaling something throughout the city in response to you, that is information and the appropriate response is calm observation, not alarm.

## Duration and Exit Strategy

This is not a place for long stays.

The heat stress is cumulative and the recovery curve is slow. Sustained exposure to 50°C, even with adequate hydration, produces a physiological debt that does not resolve when you exit — it resolves over hours or days afterward, depending on total exposure time. Plan your visit in terms of heat-hours, not clock-hours. A two-hour visit at 50°C with full preparation is a significant physiological event. A four-hour visit is a serious one. An eight-hour visit with current preparation technology is at the outer edge of what a healthy, acclimatized human can survive safely, and survival is not the standard you want.

**Acclimatization:** If at all possible, spend several days before your visit in progressively hotter environments — hot but not dangerous, 35°C, then 40°C, then 45°C if achievable, with adequate hydration. The body's heat acclimatization response is real, documented, and substantial: plasma volume expands, sweat rate increases, sweat composition improves, cardiovascular response to heat improves. A well-acclimatized person tolerates the Hollow City significantly better than an unacclimatized one. This is not optional fine-tuning; it is the difference between a difficult experience and a dangerous one.

**Exit triggers.** Establish your exit conditions before you enter, write them down, and commit to them. The psychological pull of an unusual environment tends to override physiological warning signals, especially when the cognitive changes associated with heat stress are already affecting your judgment. Make the decision about when you will leave before your judgment is compromised.

Exit triggers should include: headache that is worsening rather than stable; cessation of sweating in an environment where you should be sweating (this is a medical emergency — anhidrosis in extreme heat indicates imminent heat stroke); any visual disturbances that are not explainable by the ambient light conditions; confusion about your route that is not resolving with reference to your map; consuming more than three-quarters of your water supply without clear path to exit; and a simple time limit you have established in advance.

The city will still be here. The second visit, properly spaced and with the benefit of knowing what the first visit felt like, will be better than the first.

## The Psychological Preparation

This is the preparation most travelers skip, because it seems soft relative to the practical logistics. It is not soft. The Hollow City presents specific psychological challenges that logistics cannot entirely address.

**The deep present-tense of the place.** The Hollow City is not oriented toward human time. It has been doing what it does for long enough that human timescales are negligible. The vegetation has colonized centuries of stonework. The thermal channels have run for longer than recorded history. The city has arrived at itself, and your arrival and departure are events of no consequence to it. This is a disorienting quality to inhabit for any sustained period. Most environments that humans visit have human scale — even wilderness has responded to human presence enough to feel, in some sense, relational. The Hollow City is not relational. It is not threatening. It is simply other, in a total way, and this can produce a specific flavor of existential unease that is distinct from fear.

Name this in advance. The feeling, if it arrives, is not a warning signal. It is the accurate perception of what the place is. The city will not acknowledge your presence beyond whatever biochemical notation the root-network makes of it. This is fine. You are a visitor to a process that has found its equilibrium, and your equanimity in the face of that fact is good preparation.

**The sound as presence.** The acoustic environment of the Hollow City, particularly the infrasound and the resonant boom, will produce, for many visitors, a persistent sensation of presence — of something else being there, attending, watching. Intellectually you know there are no animals, no people. The sensation does not care about what you know. The physical cause is documented and real: infrasound at certain frequencies activates threat-detection circuitry in mammalian brains, and the acoustic geometry of the city means this is sustained exposure, not intermittent.

The preparation is: have this conversation with yourself before you enter, not while you are already in the city and already experiencing the sensation. The experience of *I knew this would happen and I know what is causing it* is radically different from the experience of *something is watching me and I don't know why I think so.* Both experiences involve the same physiology. Only one of them is manageable.

**Solitude versus company.** The Hollow City is probably better experienced with at least one other person, not for safety reasons (though there are safety reasons), but for cognitive anchoring. Heat stress and infrasound and sustained sensory strangeness erode the certainty of your own perceptions. Having another person to check against — did you hear that, what do you think that plant is doing, can you find the corridor we came through — is not weakness. It is appropriate epistemic humility about the limits of individual perception in unusual environments. That said, a companion who panics in unusual environments is worse than no companion. Choose for equanimity.

**What you will probably feel when you leave.** Most visitors to intense, self-contained environments report a significant reorientation discomfort upon exit — the reverse of culture shock. You have been inside a complete system with its own parameters, and emerging from it into ordinary space will feel wrong before it feels right. The ordinary air will seem thin, too moist, too variable. The sounds of the outside world — wind, birds, the variability of an open environment — will seem chaotic after the organized complexity of the channel-hum. You will probably feel, for some hours afterward, a peculiar pull toward the place you left, which is not sentimentality but a recalibration artifact, a brief period while your nervous system resets its baseline.

Allow for this. Do not make significant decisions in the immediate aftermath of a visit. Drink water. Eat something. Sit in shade that is genuinely cool rather than merely less hot. Let the recalibration complete before you attempt to assess what you experienced.

## Supply List: Practical Summary

**Water:** More than you calculate. Then more. At minimum two liters per hour of planned stay, double for active movement, plus a reserve. In heavy containers this is the primary weight consideration of the trip. Do not compromise on it.

**Electrolytes:** Salt tablets or oral rehydration salts or commercial electrolyte packets. Minimum one dose per hour of stay, taken with water, not instead of it.

**Cooling equipment:** Loose, light, covering clothing; phase-change or evaporative cooling vest or panels; small spray bottle of water (this is weight, balance against benefit); any cooling cloth that does not require large volumes of water to activate.

**Respiratory protection:** A well-fitting particle-filtering mask, dampened at the interior to help humidify incoming air if possible. Multiple spares.

**Lighting:** Primary headlamp (lithium batteries); secondary headlamp; tertiary light source (small keychain light is enough, the goal is redundancy). Extra batteries for all. No piece of lighting should be unredundant.

**Navigation:** Paper and pencil (which tolerate heat better than electronics or pens, which can bleed and dry at extreme temperatures). Compass if the geology permits accurate readings. Thread or cord in quantity. Small, removable trail markers — colored tape or folding glow tabs. A rubberized sketch of your planned rough route to orient your drawings against, made from whatever information is available before entry.

**Sound management:** Earplugs, at minimum two pairs. Electronic ear protection if available and manageable.

**Medical:** Oral rehydration salts beyond your planned electrolyte intake as emergency reserve. Emergency thermal blanket (counterintuitive but useful if you need to rest on the hot stone, as a barrier). Antihistamines in case of contact reaction to the plant chemistry. Analgesics for the heat and acoustic headaches you will probably experience. Aloe or equivalent for any skin irritation from the alkaline dry air. A treatment for blisters — the hot stone and fine dust will stress feet over any significant walk.

**Skin:** Cover it. Full coverage. If you have skin that burns in high UV environments, this habit transfers well here even without UV; the dry heat and mineral atmosphere are their own kind of abrasion. Lips will crack. Bring balm.

**Handwear:** Light gloves for initial plant contact. Can be removed for fine work once you have assessed the contact safety.

**Timer or alarm:** Mechanical preferred (no battery failure, no heat sensitivity). Set it to your drinking schedule and to your hard exit time. Obey it.

## The Final Preparation

Spend time, before you go, simply thinking about what the place is.

Not the logistics. The place.

It is a city that has arrived at itself. Its builders have been gone long enough that their purposes are subsumed into what the place became without them. The vegetation that should not exist has found a way to exist, and it has made the city part of itself, and the city and the vegetation are now a single system in a long, slow equilibrium that has been stable for longer than any recorded civilization. The thermal channels the builders built for habitation still run, carrying their breath through the old channels as they have always done, making music that was not intended. The stone hums. The roots signal. The light pulses in patterns that carry meaning you cannot read.

You are going to spend some hours in the presence of something that does not need you and is not aimed at you and will not be changed by your visit. This is rarer than it sounds. Most places humans go have been changed by human presence, are organized partly in response to human presence, carry the history of human interaction in their surfaces. The Hollow City has had long enough without us that it has stopped being, in any functional sense, a human place. It is something else now. You will visit it as a guest visits a phenomenon — not as a destination created for arrivals, but as something that simply is, and that you are, briefly, permitted to observe.

Go in knowing this. Go in with adequate water and a working light and a way home. Go in having told someone where you are.

And then go slowly, and let the heat press around you, and stand still long enough to feel the channel-breath move past you, and watch the light pulse through the root-network from plant to plant to plant across the amber dark, and try not to name what you are seeing as anything other than what it is.

It is not a mystery. It is not a warning. It is not waiting for you.

It is just occurring, as it has occurred, as it will occur.

You are there for as long as you are there, and that is everything and nothing, and the city will not notice when you leave.

Come back anyway.
