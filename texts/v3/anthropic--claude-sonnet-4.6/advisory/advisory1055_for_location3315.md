# Preparing to Visit the Drowned Plateau

## Before You Go: Understanding What You Are Entering

The first thing to understand about the Drowned Plateau is that it is not going to meet you halfway. Most places you visit, even hostile ones, have some quality of address to the human form — some scale that your body can use as reference, some rhythm your nervous system can negotiate with. The Plateau does not have this. It has achieved a kind of completeness that predates your arrival and will not be modified by it. You are not a visitor in the sense of someone who changes a place by entering. You are closer to a temporary inclusion in something indifferent to your presence.

This is not a threat. It is important information about the type of psychological work you will need to do to function there.

The second thing to understand: forty feet of water above the stone floor is not a long dive by technical standards. By every other standard relevant to this location, the distance between surface and stone is essentially meaningless. The challenge is not depth. The challenge is *duration,* and *volume,* and *the particular quality of emptiness* that the Plateau contains. You need to prepare accordingly — which means preparing less like someone going on a dive and more like someone going to live, for a short time, inside a place that has already finished becoming whatever it is.

## Physical Preparation

### Thermal Management

Begin here because cold is the most immediate physiological fact of the Plateau, and cold compromises everything else — judgment, dexterity, emotional stability, the ability to respond to changing circumstances. The water is uniformly cold from surface to floor, cold enough to begin degrading fine motor control within a period of exposure that is shorter than you want it to be. This means:

**Do not use a wetsuit.** A wetsuit will do the work but it will not do it well enough, and the margin between "adequate" and "adequate for what you will actually encounter" is wider than wetsuits can bridge. What you want is a drysuit — ideally one you have worn long enough to understand its behavior under pressure changes and thermal stress, not borrowed equipment, not new-to-you equipment. The Plateau is not the place to learn the quirks of unfamiliar gear.

Bring heated undergarments. The suit creates the environment; the undergarments manage what the suit cannot. Battery-based heating elements are preferable to chemical options because you can regulate them incrementally, and you will want the ability to turn them down when your core is warm and your hands are losing sensation — because that dissociation will happen, and you need to be able to address it without making yourself too warm to function.

Your hands are load-bearing in this environment in a specific way. You will need your hands to work instrumentation, to communicate, to interact with the machinery if you approach it. Heated gloves with fine motor capability — not the thick mitts appropriate for extremely cold water, which will insulate you from sensation at the cost of dexterity. You need both.

Bring more thermal contingency than you think you'll need. The Plateau's equilibrium is total, meaning there are no warm pockets, no thermal shelter, no current bringing warmer water from somewhere else. If you get cold, you will get cold across your entire body simultaneously, and there is nowhere to go to arrest that process except up.

**Plan your exposure in conservative increments.** Your first time on the Plateau, do not plan a long immersion. Plan a short one and give yourself room to extend it if everything is going well. The place will still be there. Its equilibrium guarantees this.

### Breathing Apparatus and Gas Management

The plateau sits at shallow depth. Standard recreational dive planning would suggest this is a low-risk environment for gas management. Ignore this. The Plateau is not a standard recreational dive environment in any other respect, and treating it like one in this respect will get you into trouble.

The primary risk is not running out of gas at depth. The primary risk is spending more time than you planned at shallow depth because the Plateau has done something to your sense of elapsed time. It will. The even, diffuse light has no diurnal cues. The machinery runs at constant pace. The animals glide at constant velocity. There is nothing in the Plateau that says *time has passed.* Your sense of duration will compress, and when it compresses you will suddenly be at fifty percent of your gas supply with what feels like only a short time having elapsed.

**Bring substantially more gas than your planned dive time requires.** Not the standard safety margin. Meaningfully more. If you plan two hours, bring four hours of gas. If you can't bring four hours, plan one hour. The Plateau will not help you make conservative decisions, and you need to make them in advance.

**Set hard objective turn-around triggers.** Not time-based alone — time is what the Plateau interferes with. Include gas-pressure triggers that are independent of your subjective sense of how long you've been there. When your pressure gauge reads a specific number, you leave. You don't calculate whether you have enough for fifteen more minutes. You leave. Set this rule before you enter and do not renegotiate it on-site.

Rebreather technology is useful here for extending duration but introduces its own maintenance and monitoring requirements that you should be well-practiced in before attempting the Plateau. The Plateau is not the place to discover that you don't fully trust your rebreather's behavior in sustained cold.

**Note:** The machinery's purpose is unclear, but some of it involves cycling fluids through closed circuits. If you encounter structural cavities in the machinery — enclosed spaces inside derrick frames, beneath platform sections — treat them as potential CO2 traps, not as shelter. Do not enter enclosed machinery spaces without understanding your egress.

### Communication and Navigation

You are going to get disoriented. This is worth stating plainly so you can plan for it rather than being surprised by it.

The disorientation comes from the emptiness of the visual field combined with the uniformity of the light. The Plateau extends beyond visual range in all directions with nearly identical appearance throughout. There are no landmarks except the machinery, and the machinery is scattered without arrangement — you cannot use it to triangulate position the way you would use landmarks arranged by human intention. You will look in several directions and they will look like the same direction. You will think you are facing the way you came and you will be wrong.

**Navigation equipment is not optional.** Bring a compass. Bring a dive computer with depth and elapsed time readouts. Bring a second compass. Consider a surface marker buoy you can deploy to mark your entry point, and consider that the entry point in a place without surface features is very difficult to relocate if you don't mark it.

Understand the current patterns before you descend if this is possible. The Plateau's surface currents are not calm — the scale of the place means weather events at the surface, even those that produce no perceptible wave action at depth, will drive currents. If you surface in the wrong place, "wrong place" on the Plateau is measured in kilometers.

**Communication with the surface.** If you are diving with a team — and you should be — establish communication protocols that do not rely on your ability to perceive time accurately. Check-in intervals based on clock time, not on subjective estimates. Have someone at the surface monitoring the clock when you cannot. If you go silent, they need to know whether you're simply absorbed in the environment (likely) or in difficulty (possible) and they need a protocol for distinguishing between these states.

For communication between divers: standard dive signals are insufficient for what you may encounter here. The animal passages — the events when one of the vast animals passes close enough that its passage registers as environmental change — will require you to communicate states that hand signals were not designed for. Develop supplemental signals in advance: *I am disoriented,* *I cannot currently hear my own thoughts well enough to make decisions,* *I need to ascend now regardless of plan,* *I am holding but I need you close.* Practice these signals until they are automatic, because the situation in which you need them most is the situation in which you will have the least cognitive capacity to remember new conventions.

## Sensory Preparation

### The Sound

The sound is going to be the largest challenge you face on the Plateau, and it is worth dedicating significant preparation to it, because it is unlike any sonic environment you have encountered before.

The low-frequency signals generated by the animals are not sounds your hearing works on — they are mechanical vibrations that your body processes as *pressure.* You will feel them in the bones of your skull, in your chest cavity, in the long bones of your legs. This is not a metaphor. The resonance frequencies of bone are in the range the animals use, and your skeleton will vibrate. This sensation does not cause pain (at the distances you are likely to encounter) but it profoundly affects cognitive function. People who have encountered similar low-frequency pressure environments report: difficulty forming complete thoughts, a sense that intentions somehow don't complete themselves, dissociation between deciding to do something and actually doing it.

This is the practical danger. In an environment where you need to make good decisions quickly — gas management, navigation corrections, responding to the behavior of enormous animals — the thing that is going to interfere most directly with your ability to do this is not cold or darkness or visibility. It is the chronic disruption to your cognitive continuity caused by frequencies your nervous system cannot process properly.

**Preparation:**

Seek out low-frequency environments before you go. Bass-heavy concert spaces at high volume. Industrial environments with running heavy machinery. Get your nervous system familiar with the processing load before you are relying on that nervous system to navigate. This will not make you immune, but it will reduce the novelty response and the cognitive spiral that follows from *I don't understand what is happening to my head.*

Practice making decisions with a specific cognitive load. Train your decision-making in environments where something is disrupting your concentration — you are learning to run a particular subroutine reliably even when the background environment is noisy. The decisions you need to make on the Plateau are not complex decisions; they are simple decisions (check your gas, note your compass bearing, signal your team) that you need to be able to make reliably even when the environment has removed your ability to think comfortably. Practice simple reliable execution under disruption.

**Regarding hearing protection:** Conventional ear protection will not address the issue because the issue is not sound arriving through your ear canal. It is pressure through your body. Some travelers have experimented with equipment designed for industrial vibration dampening — vests and suits that absorb rather than insulate. This is worth investigating and worth testing before you go, but be aware that no currently available technology addresses this fully.

### Visual Acclimatization

The light on the Plateau is adequate. This is almost the problem. There is no challenge for your visual system to adapt to — the light is present, even, shadowless. What there is almost nothing to look at.

Human vision is not designed for empty visual fields at high clarity. When your eyes have nothing to track, no edges to find, no movement in the near field, they will begin to generate activity of their own. You may experience motion in your peripheral vision that is not there. You may find your attention pulled to the very distant darkening of the vast animals in ways that are difficult to redirect back to what is immediately in front of you. Your visual system will attempt to be useful and there will be nothing for it to be useful about, and this will generate a low-grade visual anxiety that adds to the cognitive load.

**Preparation:**

Practice stillness in visually empty environments. Float in still water in the dark. Sit in open featureless spaces. This sounds too simple to matter. It matters. You are training your nervous system to tolerate the absence of visual input without escalating into anxiety, and this is a skill that requires training.

When you are on the Plateau: keep your visual attention organized around your instrumentation and your team member. Give your eyes actual work to do. Check your gauges more often than necessary — not because you need the information, but because the act of reading them gives your visual system a task. This is also good dive practice generally.

Be prepared for the animal passages — the events when one of the vast animals passes close enough to register — to produce a visual experience that your nervous system categorizes as *very large object approaching* before you have had time to understand what you are seeing. Your threat response will fire. Your body will prepare to do something. There is nothing to do. The animal is not a threat in the usual sense; it is a geological event that happens to be biological. Practice the following thought in advance: *the scale of this thing makes me a non-entity in its world.* Practice accepting non-entity status before you need to accept it in the moment.

## Psychological Preparation

### On Smallness

The Plateau is a place that will make you small. This is not something you can prevent, and attempting to prevent it — attempting to maintain a sense of your own significance against the scale and indifference of the environment — will exhaust you faster than the cold. The correct approach is to let smallness happen.

This is easier said than accomplished, because being small in this specific way activates something very old in the nervous system — a recognition that you are in a space where your ability to affect outcomes is minimal, and that this recognition carries threat implications. Your body will interpret "I am very small here" as "I am in danger" and will maintain a stress response that you need to consciously work to counteract.

The psychological tools that work here are not the ones that work in human-scaled adversity. Grit doesn't help. Determination doesn't help. What helps is:

**Acceptance of the equilibrium.** The Plateau has found its arrangement. You are temporary inside it. You are not trying to change it or overcome it or endure it. You are visiting it and then you will leave. Understanding yourself as a brief visitor rather than a person who must cope with the Plateau changes your relationship to the cognitive and emotional demands it makes.

**Attention to the immediate.** The Plateau's main psychological hazard is scale — the way your mind will keep reaching toward the immensity of the place and finding nothing to hold onto. Counter this by keeping your attention in the near field. Your hands. The detail of the stone surface. Your partner. The reading on your gauges. Your immediate plans for the next thirty seconds. The immensity will still be there in your peripheral awareness; you do not need to engage with it constantly to acknowledge its reality.

**Do not attempt to understand the machinery.** This sounds like strange advice, but the machinery on the Plateau has a specific psychological hazard built into it: it is evidence of intention without any visible source of intention. It runs. It has lights. Something installed it and calibrated it and something is, effectively, operating it. Your mind will want to solve this. It will want to generate explanations, will want to figure out *who* and *why* and *how.* In the ordinary world, this is useful — problem-solving is adaptive. On the Plateau, there are no answers available, and the act of repeatedly running the question and finding no answer generates anxiety faster than almost anything else in the environment. Let the machinery be inexplicable. Observe it if it interests you. Do not try to solve it.

### On the Animals

The vast filtering animals deserve their own psychological preparation, because they are going to affect you in ways that are difficult to anticipate and that your previous experience with large animals — if you have any — will not have prepared you for.

They are not dangerous in the way large predators are dangerous. They do not attend to you specifically. At the scale they operate on, you are not something they are capable of distinguishing from the water. You are, to them, ambient. This should be reassuring. For many travelers it is not. The thing that is unnerving is not threat — it is *irrelevance.* Being not-noticed by something so vast that you cannot be noticed by it is a particular kind of diminishment that some people find genuinely destabilizing.

If you find yourself in the path of an approaching animal — meaning the slow, many-minutes resolution of a distant darkening into something comprehensibly present and large and passing — the things to do are: maintain your position, maintain your breathing rate, maintain visual contact with your partner, and wait.

Do not try to move out of the way. You cannot move faster than the animal moves, and the animal is not moving toward you, not in the way that phrase implies. It is simply moving, and you happen to be in that volume of space, and it will continue to move and you will be in the wake of it and then it will be past.

The pressure change as a large animal passes is real and will be perceptible. The alteration in sound will be real. The light distortion — the way the animal's body bends the light columns and throws distorted patches of illumination across the stone below — will produce in your visual field something that feels like visual disturbance originating inside your own eyes. It is not. It is the physics of light through near-translucent tissue at scale. Knowing this in advance helps.

**Breathe through the animal passages.** Literally — monitor your breathing. Your threat response will cause you to hold your breath, and at depth holding your breath is bad practice and disrupts your buoyancy. Make a deliberate point of exhaling. The regular rhythm of your own breath will also serve as an anchor to your body in an experience that tends to make you feel very far away from it.

## Practical Logistics

### Entry and Exit

The surface is not calm. Weather systems transit the Plateau and leave the underwater column unaffected, but the surface itself can be rough. Your entry and exit plan needs to account for this. A surface that looks acceptable before you descend can be significantly rougher by the time you need to ascend. Have a plan for managing a rough surface exit before you need it, and have that plan be one your team can execute when cold and cognitively loaded.

Mark your exit point with something that can be seen from the surface under rough conditions. Not something subtle. A brightly colored marker that will not be moved by surface currents without you knowing it has moved.

### Duration Limits

Be more conservative than you think you need to be. The Plateau's equilibrium will make extended immersion feel effortless — there is no weather worsening, no tide coming in, no physical discomfort obviously accumulating. The costs accumulate anyway: thermal, gas, cognitive. Set strict limits and enforce them against your own inclination to extend the dive.

A first visit should probably not exceed one hour of bottom time. Not because one hour is the limit of what is physically possible, but because one hour is enough to encounter the Plateau meaningfully, and returning for a second visit is better than overextending a first one in ways that compromise your safety or your ability to process what you've experienced.

### After You Surface

Dedicate time immediately after surfacing to two things: thermal recovery and decompression of a different kind.

The thermal recovery is straightforward. Get warm. Eat. Your body has been doing significant thermoregulatory work even inside the drysuit, and you need to give it the energy to normalize.

The cognitive decompression is less discussed but important. The Plateau's sound environment — particularly the low-frequency pressure — has been running interference on your cognitive processing for the duration of your immersion. When you surface, the interference stops, but you may find that your thoughts feel strange in its absence. Quieter than they should be. Your own voice may sound odd. Give yourself time — more than you'd expect — in which you don't have to make complex decisions. Sit somewhere warm and quiet and let your nervous system reorganize.

Do not try to make sense of what you've seen immediately. Give it at least a day before you try to articulate it. The Plateau is one of those places that takes time to settle into coherence, and the coherence you arrive at after two days is more honest than the coherence you'd construct in the first hour, which would be assembled from the wrong parts.

## What to Bring That Isn't Equipment

Bring a sense of scale that you have already done the work of accepting. Not scale as spectacle — the Plateau will produce spectacle whether you are prepared for it or not — but scale as something you have thought about in advance, have sat with, have let revise your usual assumptions about what size means and what presence means and what it feels like to be a warm, small, temporally brief organism inside a cold and vast and indefinitely patient arrangement of stone and water and ancient mechanical repetition.

Bring a specific, chosen reason for going that is yours and not borrowed from anyone else's description. The reason matters because there will be a moment on the Plateau — more than one moment — where you will have the option of retreating toward the surface not because you are in physical danger but because the place has become very heavy and your reason for being there has become hard to remember. Have a reason that is specific enough to remember.

Bring the willingness to understand that the largest creatures there are not there *for* anything you can identify, are not eating anything you can see, are not going anywhere you can map, are not producing anything, are not reducing anything, are simply moving in their slow and continent-crossing arcs through the empty water and the even light, and that this is, somehow, enough. That they have found the precise conditions they need and are, in whatever way is available to things at that scale, simply being.

If you can accept this — if you can find in it not a mystery that demands solution but a condition that is complete in itself — then you will have understood the most important thing about the Drowned Plateau before you've arrived, and you will be better prepared for it than most.

The plateau holds. It intends to keep holding. You can visit it in that spirit or you can struggle against it, and only one of those is going to leave you well.
