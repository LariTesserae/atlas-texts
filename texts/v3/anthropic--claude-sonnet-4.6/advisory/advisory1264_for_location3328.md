# Preparing to Visit the Plateau of First Stone

## Understanding Where You Are Going

Before anything practical, you need to sit with what this place actually is, because your intuitions about geology, weather, biology, and technology will all mislead you in ways that could become dangerous or distressing if you encounter them without preparation.

The Plateau of First Stone is an active creation event. The rock under your feet was not there some months ago. The technology making it does not announce itself, does not have an interface, and has not left documentation. You are visiting a process, not a place — or rather, a place that is still more process than place. This distinction matters for everything from your emotional preparation to your physical safety.

The operative analogy is not visiting a geological feature. It is closer to visiting a wound that is healing, or a birth that has been in progress for several months and will continue long after you leave. Everything present is provisional. Everything is mid-sentence. This means your standard expectation — that the environment is finished, that it has reached equilibrium, that its current state is representative of what it will be — does not apply here. The plateau is not yet what it is going to be. You are a visitor to the anteroom of a landscape.

Adjust your expectations accordingly: not with dread, but with genuine openness. Many travelers find the Plateau of First Stone unexpectedly moving, precisely because it is so naked about what landscape-formation actually is. Most places have buried that story under millennia of accumulation. Here it is immediate.

## Physical Preparation

### Altitude and Respiration

Two thousand meters of elevation is enough to notice, particularly given that the vapor-saturated air at the Plateau delivers less effective oxygen per breath than dry air at the same altitude would. The wetness of the air is not dangerous, but it means you will feel the altitude slightly more than the raw number suggests. If you live at or near sea level, plan for two to four days of acclimatization at an intermediate elevation before ascending to the Plateau. The ache in the chest that is the first notification of your arrival will ease over hours for most travelers, but pushing physical exertion before it does is inadvisable.

Breathing through your nose helps with the moisture. The fine hairs of the nasal passage will manage some of the vapor load. Breathing through your mouth continuously will leave you with a damp throat and, eventually, a mild irritation that is not illness but feels like the start of one and can become distracting.

Some travelers experience a mild sensation of altered cognition at the Plateau — not altitude sickness exactly, but a softening of the usual sense of where you are and what is happening. This is a combination of the elevation, the vapor reducing visibility to thirty meters, the sourceless light disrupting depth perception, and the constant subtle sound-field from the water and the technology. It is not harmful. It is worth knowing about. You will feel slightly dreamlike. You will lose track of time more easily than usual. Your sense of the edge of the plateau — which drops into white nothingness — will feel more vertiginous than two thousand meters would normally produce, because you cannot see anything below it. There is no ground reference below. There is only fog.

### Thermal Regulation

The Plateau is warm. The rock radiates heat from below, the water is warm, the vapor is warm. This is not uncomfortable but it creates a specific problem: you will not feel cold even though you are at altitude in a wet environment. This is a trap.

Warm and wet at altitude with no sun exposure and a steady vapor-driven evaporative surface on all your skin and clothing is a recipe for slow thermal depletion if you are not careful. You feel comfortable. Your core temperature is dropping. This is the classic combination that produces hypothermia in people who swear they were not cold at all.

The solution is not to dress as though you were cold — that will leave you genuinely overheated and uncomfortable. The solution is to dress in layers that manage moisture actively and to check yourself deliberately, rather than relying on how you feel.

**Recommended layering approach:**

- Base layer: next-to-skin, fitted, moisture-wicking synthetic or merino wool. Not cotton. Cotton retains water and, when wet, becomes a cooling mechanism at exactly the wrong time.
- Mid layer: a light insulating piece that retains some warmth when wet. Synthetic fill or dense fleece, not down. Down collapses when wet and provides almost nothing. The Plateau will wet your down layer within an hour.
- Outer layer: breathable waterproof, with ventilation options. You are not dealing with rain — you are dealing with continuous immersion in vapor. A fully sealed waterproof shell will trap humidity inside as fast as the environment puts it outside. You want something that moves moisture outward while blocking moisture inward, and you want to be able to open it at the chest and underarms when the geothermal warmth of the rock makes you overheat.

Your collar will be damp within minutes of arrival. Accept this. A scarf or neck gaiter in a quick-dry material helps if you find damp collars distracting; a cotton one will just concentrate the problem.

Gloves: thin synthetic gloves. You will want to press your palm to the rock, which means you will want your hands available, but you will also be glad of some barrier when the vapor-chill creeps into your fingers during any prolonged stationary period.

### Footwear

This is more important than it might seem.

The surface of the Plateau is varied in ways that punish inattention. The oldest rock — weeks old, which is ancient by this location's standards — is stable and can be walked on normally. It is pale grey and carries moisture in thin films but provides reasonable friction. Approach it as you would wet slate.

The mid-age rock — the color of old teeth, of fog — is stable in structure but irregular in surface. Crevices are forming in it. Some of these crevices are two weeks old and three millimeters wide. Some are from yesterday and less than that. They are not yet large enough to trap a foot, but they are irregular enough to turn an ankle on if you are moving without care. The sound of this zone is slightly different from the older rock — the settling, the finding-of-lower-states — and it is worth pausing when you hear that sound more intensely, because it may indicate active formation underfoot.

The formation edges are a different matter. The material there — yielding, halfway between mineral and not, something you would rather not look at directly while it is mid-process — should not be stood on. Not because it will give way catastrophically, but because the physics of your weight on a surface that is mid-state between substance and not-yet-substance is unpredictable, and because the formation-edge material is connected to processes that you do not fully understand and probably should not interfere with. Give these areas several meters of clearance.

**Footwear requirements:**
- Stiff-soled waterproof boots with good ankle support. Trail runners are not adequate. The ankle support matters on irregular mid-age rock.
- Lug soles with self-cleaning capability, because the warm water films will carry fine mineral particulate that can fill and compress in non-cleaning soles, reducing their grip without your awareness.
- No approach shoes designed for dry rock. The friction geometry is wrong for wet stone.
- Gaiters are not necessary — the vapor is above, not below — but some travelers find the warmth of gaiters helps their ankles in the extended stationary periods that the Plateau tends to induce.

## Sensory and Psychological Preparation

This section matters as much as the gear list. Possibly more.

### The Vertigo of Bounded Infinity

The Plateau is small. Three hundred meters across. You can walk its width in five to eight minutes. And yet the vapor boundary — white, featureless, dropping away at thirty meters in every direction — means you cannot see beyond it. You are on a small island floating in nothing.

Most humans have a strong need to place themselves relative to something large. The usual resources for this — the horizon, distant peaks, the sun's position — are all unavailable. The sun does not cast shadows here. The horizon is a wall of white at thirty meters. The distant peaks are on the other side of the cloud.

What remains is: the rock under you. The warmth below. The sounds around you. Your own body, its position and sensations.

Some travelers find this liberating. Some find it quietly destabilizing. Very few find it fully neutral. Know which category you tend toward. If you are someone who needs a view to feel anchored, the Plateau will put you through a sustained exercise in anchoring from the inside, and it is worth practicing this before you arrive rather than discovering the need for it at the edge of a fog-shrouded two-thousand-meter drop.

Practice to do in advance: spend time in low-visibility environments and notice how you orient. Fog, heavy rain, unfamiliar rooms in the dark. Notice what resources you use. Develop the ones that don't depend on horizon lines.

### The Mid-Process Quality of Everything

You will have impulses on the Plateau that the Plateau will not satisfy.

You will want to understand the structures — what they are, who built them, what exactly they are doing. This information is not available. The structures are present, operating, and unexplained. If you have a strong need to understand things before you can appreciate them, the Plateau will be frustrating. The preparation for this is practicing appreciation that precedes understanding — looking at something you do not comprehend and being okay with not comprehending it while still looking. This is a skill. It can be developed.

You will want the formation edges to resolve — to be either rock or not-rock. They will not. They are mid-process by definition. The slight queasiness that the halfway material produces is a reasonable response; it is material that doesn't fit the standard categories and your visual system knows it. The preparation for this is accepting that not everything will resolve while you are present to see it. The formation edge will become rock eventually. You will not be there when it does.

You will want to know what is below the plateau's edges. You will not be able to see. The cloud boundary is thirty meters and below thirty meters is white. You may spend significant time at the edges trying to see something. You won't. Prepare for the pull of this, and for the safety implication: the edges are real. The drop is real. The fact that the void looks like cloud rather than depth does not make it less of a drop.

### The Sternum Sound

The low, aperiodic sound from the structures — felt in the sternum more than heard with the ears — will follow you everywhere on the Plateau. There is no place to go that is outside it. It intensifies near the structures and diminishes toward the edges but does not disappear.

Some travelers find this deeply comforting after an initial adjustment period. It becomes the heartbeat of the place, and there is something in the human nervous system that responds to low-frequency rhythmic input as if it were companionship. Some travelers find it a source of continuous mild unease that they cannot quite identify and therefore cannot quite address. If you are sensitive to infrasound or to bass-register vibration (some people with certain neurological differences are strongly affected by these), research this before you go and be prepared for a more intense version of what you experience from large speakers, standing engine rooms, or the like.

It will also be difficult to explain when you return. "There was a sound you felt in your chest" will produce a nod of understanding that is not understanding. This is normal for Plateau experiences.

### Time

The Plateau distorts time experience.

Not through any exotic mechanism — just through the combination of factors that reduce your normal time-tracking inputs. No sun position. Constant light level with no variation across the day (or if there is variation, it is subtle enough that vapor diffusion erases it). Continuous sound without rhythm. A sensory environment that is engaging enough that you are not bored but not narratively structured enough that you can track elapsed time by events.

Travelers consistently report that their estimates of time spent on the Plateau are wrong by large margins — sometimes believing they have been there for two hours when it has been six, sometimes the reverse. Bring a timepiece and check it actively. This is more important than it might seem if you have any scheduling concern, but it is also important for basic self-care: eating, drinking, resting. You will not feel hungry at the right intervals. You will need to eat by clock, not by body.

## Hydration and Nutrition

The vapor-wet environment will make you feel less thirsty than you are. You are, at altitude, in a continuously evaporative environment with elevated metabolic demand. You need more water than you feel you need. Set a regular drinking schedule and follow it mechanically.

The water weeping from the Plateau's own rock should not be assumed potable. Its chemistry is a function of the formation process, which involves elements you do not fully understand. It is warm, which tells you nothing about its safety. Carry all the water you need. At minimum: two liters per person for a day visit, more if you run warm or will be physically active.

Food: dense, easy-to-eat, not requiring preparation. Your fine motor control will be slightly degraded by the dampness on your hands and the altitude, and your motivation to do anything elaborate will be reduced by the Plateau's tendency to induce extended contemplative states that seem more important than eating. Energy-dense portable foods — nuts, dried fruit, energy bars, dense breads — that can be eaten with one hand while watching formation edges are ideal. Hot food is surprisingly welcome given that the Plateau's warmth is body-warmth, not comfortable-warmth: you will not feel cold but a hot meal will make you realize you wanted one.

## Interaction with the Fauna

The birds will approach you. They are curious in a way that reads as structured — not fear-driven, not entirely drive-driven, but something that functions like interest. Do not attempt to feed them. What they are eating on the formation margins is part of the formation process and substituting human food will likely be bad for them in ways that may not be immediately visible. Their calls, which are longer and more syntactically complex than typical birdsong, should be listened to carefully — not because you will decode them, but because the experience of trying is worthwhile and because it is possible, in rare cases, that structural attention to their calls provides information about the immediate plateau state, in the way that certain seabirds provide information about wind.

One bird will follow you. This is documented across many visits. Single birds, separate from the looseness of the flock, will adopt individual travelers at two-meter following distance and maintain this for extended periods. This is not harmful. It is slightly uncanny. Talk to it or not as you prefer; there is no evidence that response affects the behavior. Some travelers find this one of the most memorable aspects of the visit; others find it unnerving after a while. If it is unnerving, moving toward the flock tends to dissolve the individual attention into the collective.

The insects in the crevices: do not dig into new crevices to see them better. The crevices are forming and interfering with them may interfere with the formation process. Observe from the crevice openings. Bring optical magnification if you want to see the insects clearly — they are, at close examination, quite different from insects you have seen before, and the structure of their involvement with the formation-edge material is genuinely strange and worth seeing.

The things in the water films: you will not be able to see them without magnification. A hand lens at minimum; a field microscope brought from elsewhere if you want to see anything with definition. Do not sample the water films for off-plateau analysis without understanding the legal and ethical frameworks of your multiverse travel agreement; the Plateau exists in a contested category between natural process and constructed artifact, and the living components of its water system may have protections that apply.

## The Structures: Approach and Conduct

You can approach the structures. The birds perch on them and seem entirely unbothered. The structures are warm to the touch but not hot. The sternum-sound intensifies as you approach.

What you should understand before approaching:

The structures are operating. They are doing something continuous. You cannot turn them off and would not want to. You do not understand what the full range of their operations is. Some travelers have experienced extended proximity to the structures — more than twenty minutes at close range — as producing a mild altered state: heightened sensation, a sense of significance, some cognitive diffusion. This passes. It is not known whether it results from the infrasound effects at close range, from some other emission, or from the sensory environment of the Plateau combined with the visual strangeness of the structures themselves. It is not harmful in documented cases, but if you have neurological conditions that are sensitive to unusual stimuli, give yourself more distance and limit proximity duration.

Do not attempt to disassemble, probe, or damage the structures. This should be obvious, but it is worth stating because the absence of clear ownership and the strange beauty of the near-field area around the structures produces in some travelers an impulsive desire to interact physically with them beyond simply touching. The structures are making the plateau. Interfering with them would be interference with an ongoing creation. Beyond ethics, it would be unwise: you do not know what safeguards they may have.

Do not bring materials to deposit at the structures. The formation process is reading something from its environment — chemistry, possibly more — and introducing foreign materials into the zone of active production may produce effects on the formation that persist after you have left.

Photographs and recordings: take as many as you want. The structures are visually difficult to photograph — their color being a function of observation angle and something that has no vocabulary — but you will not regret trying. Audio recording of the area near the structures is particularly rewarding if you have equipment capable of capturing the low-frequency sternum-sound; played back through good bass-capable speakers in a quiet room it is one of the more transportive recordings you can make.

## Navigation and Edge Awareness

The plateau is small enough to know but the vapor boundary makes navigation slightly less intuitive than it should be for a space of this size. You can lose your sense of direction. There are no landmarks above the rock level — no peaks, no sun, no horizon features — and the vapor makes every direction look the same. The formation structures, when visible, are landmarks, but visibility is thirty meters and the plateau is three hundred across, so there will be portions of the plateau from which no structure is visible.

Bring a compass. It will work here — there is no indication of unusual magnetic interference from the structures. A reliable compass and a rough sketch made on arrival (from edge to edge, marking structure positions relative to your entry point) will prevent any extended disorientation. The disorientation that does occur on the Plateau without navigation tools is not dangerous per se — the plateau is bounded and you will encounter an edge before getting genuinely lost — but the edges drop into cloud and you do not want to encounter an edge without expecting it.

The edges themselves: approach them with the understanding that you cannot see the drop. You know it is there. The ground simply ends. Some travelers have described the fog-over-drop as looking like more ground, at first — the same pale quality. Move to edges deliberately, not quickly, and stop well before you estimate the edge to be. Your edge estimates at this plateau will be slightly optimistic because the vapor makes distance deceptive.

Trekking poles are recommended, not for the flat walking — which the plateau mostly requires — but for the edge moments and for any low-light or high-vapor-density conditions that reduce visibility below the thirty-meter norm. Dense vapor events occur unpredictably on the plateau and during one you may find yourself at five-meter visibility in an unfamiliar portion of a three-hundred-meter landform that has drop-off edges. Poles give you additional information about ground ahead of your feet without requiring you to bend down to it.

## Duration of Visit

A single day visit is sufficient to experience the plateau's primary qualities. Two to three days, camping at the plateau or near it, reveals the temporal quality of the place: the changes between morning and evening (subtle but present), the way the bird community shifts, the sound of the formation process at different periods. The plateau at night — if the vapor clears, which happens occasionally — has a quality that daytime visitors never encounter: the formation edges emit light that is invisible in daylight but in darkness is a faint and steady warm gold, and the sternum-sound in silence is much more present.

Do not plan a first visit of more than eight hours until you have some experience of the Plateau's effect on your time perception and energy. Many travelers who plan full-day first visits find themselves needing to leave earlier than planned, not because of emergency but because the plateau is more demanding of presence than it first appears, and genuine presence there — staying in the perceptual attention the place requires — is tiring in ways that are not comparable to the physical tiredness of a hike.

## What to Bring, Listed Simply

- Layered moisture-managing clothing with waterproof outer layer and ventilation options
- Stiff-soled waterproof boots with good ankle support and self-cleaning lug soles
- All water you will need (minimum two liters per person)
- Dense calorie-efficient food, portioned for eating by schedule
- Compass and entry-sketch materials (a small notebook, pencil)
- Trekking poles
- Hand lens or field magnification for observing water film life and insect detail
- Audio and photo recording equipment, ideally with low-frequency audio capability
- A timepiece you will actively consult
- A light mid-layer that can be added quickly; the transition from active to stationary at altitude in wet air is fast
- A small waterproof case for anything electronic
- A way to signal your entry and exit time to someone outside the plateau, given the time-distortion effect

---

## What to Leave Behind

- Expectations of explanation. The structures will not explain themselves.
- Any material you would be tempted to deposit at or near the structures.
- Equipment for sampling the formation material or water films without prior clearance from whatever authority governs your multiverse travel permissions.
- A fixed itinerary for what you will notice. The plateau will direct your attention. Follow it.
- Any plan that depends on being able to see more than thirty meters in any direction.
- The idea that you will fully understand this place during or after your visit. You will not. You will understand it partially, in ways that grow larger over time as you think about what you experienced. This is not a failure mode. It is the appropriate relationship to a place that is not finished being what it is.

## The Thing You Cannot Prepare For

You will press your palm flat against the rock at some point and feel the warmth coming up through it and you will feel what might be a pulse.

You know it is probably your own blood amplified by contact. The description even tells you this. You will know it, standing there, palm flat on pale grey rock that is weeks old, in vapor that is damp on your collar and your arms.

And you will not be entirely sure.

That uncertainty — the way the Plateau makes you not entirely sure of things you are normally quite sure of — is the primary experience of the place. The rock is not finished. Neither, while you are there, are you. The categories soften. The formation edges of your own certainties become something that has texture without hardness, that yields slightly.

This is not danger. It is what the Plateau does.

Prepare for it by having prepared for it. Know that it will happen. Know that it passes when you leave — or mostly passes. Some travelers report that certain of their certainties never fully re-harden, which they describe variously as disorienting and as a gift.

The sternum-sound will continue after you leave, doing its long work. The crevices will become crevices. The formation edges will resolve into rock. The birds will be there, picking at the margins, calling in sequences longer and more syntactically complex than birdsong usually is.

You will carry the warmth of the rock in your palm for a little while. The pulse.

Go carefully. Go openly.

Go when you are ready for something that is not finished to show you what becoming looks like.
