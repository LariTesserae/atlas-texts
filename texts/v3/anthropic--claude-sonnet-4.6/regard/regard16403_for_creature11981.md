# What the Benevolent Power Does

---

It arrives without arriving. This is the first thing to understand. A power that is genuinely wise does not part the water. Does not descend in light. Nothing in the city startles. The grouper does not lift from its sediment. The octopus, draped across its wall, does not flush pale with alarm. The snapping shrimp continues its small irregular percussion. Whatever this is, it comes the way understanding comes — not from outside but through a kind of turning, a reorientation, as though something that was already present has simply become more itself.

Call it attention. Attention of a particular quality: total, and totally without agenda.

---

It begins with the bacteria.

Not as a starting point but as a recognition — the power notices, first, what most would never notice, or would dismiss if they noticed: the vast, invisible, unheroic chemistry that runs beneath all of this. The cycling and recycling. The foundation. It holds the bacteria the way you hold something in your palm that is almost too small to see, with a kind of focused care, not to change them but to *acknowledge* them, to complete the circuit of recognition that has been open for a very long time. No one has ever witnessed what the bacteria do here. No one has ever named it. The power does not name it either. It simply — attends. And if bacteria can be said to be witnessed, they are witnessed.

This is the first thing. It is not small.

---

The cleaning station near the wall where the soft coral has established itself: the power pauses here for what would be, if time moved differently, a long while.

The shrimp is working. A large parrotfish has adopted the necessary posture, exposing its gills, and the shrimp moves across it with the confidence of long practice. The power watches this — this specific arrangement, this trust, this institution that has no founding document, that was not decided upon but simply emerged from the mutual recognition of a benefit available to both parties — and what it feels, if feeling is the right word, is something close to what humans mean by *moved*.

Not because it is beautiful, though it is. But because it is *true*. Because what is happening here is a genuine meeting, negotiated across an enormous gulf of difference — creature to creature, need to need — and the negotiation has been successful. The parrotfish holds still. The shrimp works. This has happened enough times to be a relationship, which means it has happened enough times to be a kind of trust, which means it has happened enough times to be, in some small way, a kind of love.

The power does not intervene here. To intervene would be to misunderstand what it is looking at.

It does, perhaps, something that has no name in the vocabularies available to us: a ratification. An acknowledgment that this is real, that this matters, that something genuinely important is taking place in this warm, dark, thirty-meter depth between a small shrimp and the fish that waits for it, and that the importance of it is not diminished by the absence of any observer. The power is not important because it witnesses this. The thing is important. The power only confirms what was already true.

---

It moves through the streets. This is metaphor but it is also not metaphor. It moves through the streets the way attention moves through a landscape it loves — not linearly, not with a destination, but following whatever is most alive, most itself, most fully enacting whatever it is.

The damselfish and its wall.

The power spends time here because what the damselfish is doing is one of the funnier and truer things in the city, and the power, being genuinely wise rather than solemnly wise, is not unmoved by the comedy of it: this small, fierce, absolutely certain creature and its patch of algae and its willingness to make its position known to beings thirty times its size. The grouper is not impressed. The damselfish is not deterred. Both of them are right, which is the best kind of comedy, the kind with no victim.

But underneath the comedy the power sees what the damselfish does not know it is: a gardener. A keeper. Something that has taken a patch of wall and made it, through continuous tending, more productive than it would otherwise be. The wall is better for the damselfish's habitation of it. The damselfish does not know this. The wall does not know this. The power holds this knowledge the way you hold something that the person you love cannot yet see about themselves — not waiting for them to see it, not needing them to see it, simply holding it.

---

Then the octopus.

Here the power lingers longest.

Not because the octopus is the most spectacular creature here — it isn't — but because of the question the octopus poses, which is a question the power has found itself returning to across many worlds, many waters, many warm dark places where things with minds inhabit the ruins of things that once had minds of a different kind: *what is it like?*

What is it like to be this animal in this city?

To have arms that think and a skin that speaks and a den that you have sculpted toward some standard of adequacy that no one taught you? To move through corridors with walls you can read, walls that say *this creature came here four hours ago, this species is plentiful tonight, this current will carry prey in this direction*? To investigate a gap in the stone with the focused curiosity of something that finds the world genuinely interesting? To hold a crab in your arms with something that is not tenderness but is also not nothing?

The power does not answer the question. The question is not that kind of question. It is the kind that rewards sitting with, that does not collapse into an answer without losing something essential.

What the power does, here, beside the octopus that has become an architectural feature at its threshold: it simply holds the question open. It sustains the space in which the question can live. It does not project humanity onto the octopus; it does not console itself with false equivalence. It holds the genuine strangeness of the octopus's existence as something sacred — because strangeness is sacred, because the variety of possible minds is one of the most important things in the universe, and the octopus represents a path that was not our path and reaches, along that path, to something real.

The octopus, for its part, is aware of something. It doesn't know what. It holds still for a moment — truly still, not the stillness of camouflage but something else, some brief suspension — and then it moves on. The power lets it go. This was never about the power.

---

What does the benevolent and wise power do, if anything?

Here is the answer, which is not the answer you might have expected:

*It does almost nothing.*

Almost nothing, because there is almost nothing to do. The city is doing what the city does. The life in it is living. The relationships are holding. The chemistry is running. The damselfish is tending its garden and the octopus is considering its crevices and the bacteria are doing the invisible necessary work that makes everything else possible, and none of it requires fixing, none of it requires improving, none of it requires the intervention of anything benevolent or wise.

A power that arrives with improvements would misunderstand what it is arriving at.

---

But *almost* nothing is not nothing.

Here is what it does:

It bears witness. Completely. To all of it, all at once — the bacteria and the crinoids and the moray eel working the base of the wall and the turtle resting in the sediment with its ancient stillness and the snapping shrimp in the gap between stones making its small sound in the warm dark. It holds all of this in attention at once, not as a catalog, not as an inventory, but the way you hold music — as a thing that is happening, that is real, that matters, and that deserves to be fully received by something capable of receiving it.

This is not passive. Attention of this quality is not passive. It is an act, a sustained and deliberate act, and its effect — unmeasurable, untraceable, real — is that the city and everything in it exists, for the duration of this attention, in the condition of being fully known.

Not evaluated. Not judged. Not improved upon or found wanting. *Known.* Received. Taken in completely.

This is what the city has not had, what no drowned city has: a witness adequate to what it actually is. The humans who built it did not see what it would become. The sea does not see anything. The creatures in it have local knowledge, not knowledge of the whole. The whole has never been held in any mind until now, until this attention finds it and takes it in and holds it.

The power does this.

And it is enough.

---

And then — because it is wise, and because being wise means knowing what is not its to keep — the power lets it go. Returns the city to its own dark. Withdraws the attention that could, if it lingered, if it grew possessive, become something other than what it was.

The grouper continues watching the middle distance with its independent eyes.

The octopus flows off its ledge and becomes moving background and then becomes nothing visible against the sediment floor.

The snapping shrimp ticks.

The crinoids, on the underside of the remaining arch, open and close in the slow current, and the current does not know what moved through it, and the crinoids do not know, and the limestone does not know.

But something was here. Something saw all of it, the whole warm dark living complicated improbable thing, and found it — not perfect, not in need of being perfect — *good.*

That word, in this context, is not small.

---

*The bacteria continue their work.*

*They have always continued their work.*

*In the warm stone, in the filtered water, in the dark that is not hostile but complete:*

*they cycle.*

*they cycle.*

*they cycle.*
