# What the Power Does

---

It arrives the way attention arrives — not with displacement of air, not with light, not with any of the traditional signals. It simply becomes present in the way that understanding becomes present: you were not having it, and then you are having it, and the transition has no detectable boundary.

The isopods glow a little brighter for a moment. Not dramatically. Three degrees warmer on the spectrum, perhaps. The Warden, sitting in the chair facing the unlit stove, notices and files it. The rats, two of them in the east passage, pause and then continue. The fish make no adjustment. They are already in contact with everything that touches the water, and something has touched the water very slightly — a change in the room above, some infinitesimal shift in the air pressure — and they have already compensated.

The power looks at everything. This takes no time, or a very long time; these are the same thing for a power of this kind.

---

It sees the Warden first, because consciousness is a kind of brightness, even quiet consciousness, even the specifically muted brightness of a person who has chosen eleven years of deliberate dimness. She is sitting in the chair with her hands on her knees and she is counting drops without choosing to count them, and her face in the faint isopod-light is the face of someone who is, on balance, all right. Not happy exactly. Not unhappy. Calibrated. She is, the power understands, approximately where she has placed herself, doing approximately what she intended to do, in a condition that is as accurate a representation of her inner state as any external life can be.

She is lonely in the way the pool is still. The power sees this clearly. It sees that she knows this about herself. It sees that she has considered it carefully and has not yet reached a conclusion about whether it is a problem, and that the not-reaching has become its own position, and that the position is, for now, stable.

The power does not intervene in this. It considers doing something — a thought, or what passes for a thought in an entity for whom thought is more like weather — and the thought is: *she does not need to be less lonely, she needs the loneliness to be the right loneliness, and it is currently the right loneliness.* This assessment is accompanied by something that, in a human being, would be recognized as a kind of ache: not for her suffering, because her suffering is not acute, but for the difficulty of being a person who has made peace with a condition that is still a condition. The power holds this feeling for what would be, to the Warden, about twelve drops.

It does not heal her of solitude. It does not send someone down the access stairs. It does not arrange circumstances.

What it does — and this is so small it will never be noticed — is find, in the particular structure of the stone above the chair, a tiny modification, a slight realignment of material too small to measure, such that the drop falling in the main room falls now every four seconds instead of every four to five seconds. More regular. By a small increment, more reliable. She will notice within the week that the interval has settled. She will not know why. She will add a notation: *the rhythm is more consistent lately; this may indicate pressure change in the upper feeds, worth monitoring.* She will monitor it. The monitoring will give her something new to attend to for a while. This is what the power can offer her: not comfort, but something to tend that has not been tended in quite that way before.

It is a very small gift. The power is not embarrassed by its smallness.

---

It turns to the rats.

The rats are more difficult, because the power is accustomed to consciousness and the rats are at the edge of what that word describes, the fringe where consciousness becomes something else — not less, exactly, but differently distributed, spread across more immediacy, less burdened by the abstract. The one that sits at the pool's edge is sitting there now. In the dark, without the single bulb, the isopods provide just enough light to see the shape of it: still, long-tailed, the large eyes reflecting back a cold blue-white.

The power looks at what the rat is doing.

The rat is listening. Not to sound — the acoustic information of the dropping water is already parsed and filed, background, beneath the rat's attention. It is listening to the water itself: the pressure of the system above, the chemical information coming through the wall seeps, the difference between the pool's current state and the pool's state several hours ago. The rat's lateral awareness of the space is more extensive than the Warden's, different from the fish's, and the power sees that the rat is performing a function that no one assigned and no one knows about: a kind of monitoring, imprecise but genuine, attending to the system in the way that attention attends without theory. The rat does not understand what it is doing. This does not mean it is not doing it.

The power considers the rat for a long time. Longer than it spent with the Warden, though the Warden is vastly more complex; the power spends longer because the rat requires a different kind of looking. You cannot understand what the rat is doing with the same faculties you use to understand the Warden. You have to adjust.

When it adjusts, it sees something remarkable, which is that the rat at the pool's edge and the fish in the pool's depth are, functionally, attending to the same thing from different positions. The fish feel the water from inside. The rat feels the water from the edge. The water-tender reads the gauges, and the gauges measure things adjacent to what the fish and the rat are already sensing more directly. The three of them — the Warden, the rat, the fish — are a distributed system of attention. They are monitoring the cistern together. None of them knows they are doing it together.

The power sees this and is moved. This is the word: moved. The way water is moved. A small displacement.

What it does for the rats is almost nothing, but it is accurate: it adjusts something in the isopod-light, very marginally increases the visibility in the passage between the main chamber and the east corridor, so that on the nights when the large rat sits by the pool there will be just enough light that, if the Warden is awake and moving through the east passage, she might see it. Not certainly. Not reliably. But possibly. Maybe twice in the next year she will round the corner and see it there, still, by the black water, and she will stop, and they will be in the same darkness for a moment.

The power does not arrange for them to understand each other. They already do, in the way that things understand each other when they have shared a space long enough. It simply makes the shared space more visible, fractionally, on some of the nights.

---

The fish are where the power spends what might be called the most time, though time is not quite what it spends.

The fish are the most purely themselves of anything in the cistern quarter. The Warden is herself plus her history plus her choices. The rats are themselves plus the structure they have learned to read. The fish are only themselves, entirely, without remainder. They have no gap between what they are and what they do. They drift and process and sense and the drifting and processing and sensing is the totality of being a fish in the settling pool of the cistern quarter.

The power looks at this for a long time and feels something that resists naming. It is not envy — it has no gap of its own to envy the fish for not having. It is something closer to reverence. The fish have been in the pool for long enough that they are, in a genuine way, the pool's consciousness, if consciousness can be distributed across twenty slow colorless bodies in black water. They know the pool. They are the knowing.

The power does not intervene with the fish at all. Not because they don't matter — they matter, they matter enormously, the power is surprised by how much they matter — but because there is nothing to correct, nothing to tend. They are in a condition of complete adequacy to their situation. This is rarer than it sounds. Across everything the power has attended to, genuine adequacy is rare.

What the power does, for the fish, is simply: it looks. It pays attention. It lets the attention be the action. The fish drift. The power watches. For a moment that is not quite a moment, a thing that knows everything and a thing that knows only water are both attending to the same water from different positions.

Then the fish drift slightly, feeling a pressure change they cannot name, and the moment passes.

---

The isopods.

The power finds the isopods the most interesting and is genuinely uncertain what to do, which is not a state it occupies often. The uncertainty is not distress. It is the good uncertainty of a genuinely open question.

The isopods have made light. They have solved their own problem. No one told them darkness was a problem to be solved; they are not solving it on behalf of anyone; they glow for whatever reasons a thing glows when it has been in the dark long enough and something in its chemistry finds a use for making light. And yet: the light they make illuminates the chair and the absent person and the unlit stove and the Warden when she sits in the chair, which she does not know is being illuminated. They have made a lamp for someone who does not know she is being lit.

This is not intentional. This is what the power keeps coming back to: the complete absence of intention in a gesture that is, structurally, indistinguishable from care.

It decides, finally, that this does not require a distinction. Care that is unintentional is still care, in the way that the pool is still the pool whether or not it knows it is. The isopods glow above the door. The Warden is illuminated. The intention is not the point. The light is the point. The cold blue-white on the face of a woman sitting in a chair she did not build, in a place that has been tending itself for longer than she has been alive.

For the isopods, the power does one thing. There is, in the west wall of the chamber with the chair and stove, a section of pipe that has been slowly corroding for two years. It will fail in eight months, probably, if nothing is done; it will fail slowly, a seep that becomes a drip that becomes a wet patch that becomes a problem the Warden will have to address with materials she doesn't have, rerouting through the north passage, significant work. The power adjusts the corrosion rate. Not stops it — stops it would be too much, would be the wrong kind of help, would be managing rather than tending. It slows the corrosion by about a third. The pipe will now fail in a little over a year. The Warden will notice the seep while it is still a seep. She will have time to get the materials, to plan the rerouting, to fix it properly rather than urgently. She will never know the timing was a gift.

The isopods will continue to cluster on the pipe above the door. Their light will still be cold and blue-white and unreliable and precisely sufficient.

---

Now the place itself.

The power spends the longest here, attending to the structure the way the structure attends to the water: pervasively, patiently, as a condition rather than an act. It looks at the walls with their accumulated renders, the doors that open onto brick, the gauge whose needle points to something no one can interpret anymore. It looks at the calcite formations, the mineral deposits, the specific pattern of the Warden's worn path through the wet sections.

What it sees is: a place that is working.

Not functioning in the engineering sense, though it is also functioning in the engineering sense. Working in the larger sense, the sense that includes the rats and the fish and the isopods and the Warden and the chair with its eleven years of a particular body's weight and the ledger and the hook at the right height and the drop falling into the basin every four seconds now, more regularly. Working in the sense that all of these things are doing what they do and the doing is in relationship to each other and the relationship is, overall, sustaining. The water comes down from the city and the city sends it without knowing where it goes and the cistern quarter receives it and processes it and sends it on, and the fish and the rats and the Warden and the isopods attend to it in their different ways, and the place contains all of this attendance in its stone and brick and render and the very old water moving very slowly in the channels.

This is not an accident. It is also not a design. It is what happens when the same thing is tended for long enough by enough different kinds of tending.

The power does not add to this. Adding would be hubris of a particular kind — the assumption that more is needed when what is needed is continuation. What is needed is not improvement. What is needed is simply: for this to go on for a while longer, in the way that things that are working should be allowed to go on.

So the power does the most fundamental thing available to it, which is: it witnesses. Fully, carefully, without any agenda of alteration. It sees the Cistern Quarter completely — the Warden and her loneliness that is the right loneliness, the rats and their unassigned monitoring, the fish and their complete adequacy, the isopods and their unintentional gift of light, the doors that open onto brick, the sign whose caution cannot see what it cautions, the pool that looks ceremonial because it has been treated with care long enough to have taken on the quality of something sacred, the slow water, the dark.

It sees all of it.

This is rarer than intervention. This is harder than adjustment. Adjustment is easy; adjustment takes a moment and a small redirection and a slowing of corrosion and a regularizing of a drop. Witnessing is the long work. Witnessing is attending completely, without flinching from the loneliness or the limitation or the eventual failure of the pipe or the body in the chair or the generations of rats who came before and left nothing but paths worn in the stone.

The power witnesses the cistern quarter for what would be, to the Warden, approximately one hour. One hour of her careful tending, her counting without choosing to count, her sitting in the chair she didn't build facing the stove she doesn't light.

Then it releases its attention, which is like releasing a breath, and the cistern quarter continues without it, as it continued before.

---

The Warden gets up from the chair. Her knees are what they are at her age, in this cold. She picks up the hand lens and the ledger from the stove's surface, where she left them. She makes her way through the passage toward the main room to check the settling pool before sleep, if sleep is what the hours of lying on the cot have become.

In the east passage, there is a rat. The large one, she thinks. It is sitting at the channel's edge and it does not flee. She stops. In the faint blue-white of the isopods on the wall above them, she can see the shape of it: still, patient, already aware of her and not concerned.

She stands there for what feels like a long time, watching the rat, the rat watching the water. She is being illuminated by organisms that do not know they are illuminating her. She is being accompanied by an animal that does not know it is accompanying her. She is in a place that has been tending itself since before she arrived and will continue after she leaves.

She is, in this moment, not lonely.

She does not examine this too closely. She is a practical woman.

She notes the rat's location in the ledger — *east passage, channel edge, the usual one* — and moves on to check the pool.

The drop falls every four seconds, more reliably than it used to.

The water moves in the channels, always slightly.

The fish drift in their depth, attending to the water from within it.

Above the city, it is raining.

The rain finds the streets and the streets find the drains and the drains find the culverts and the culverts find the cistern quarter, and the cistern quarter receives it, as it has always received it, as it will go on receiving it, patient and dark and full of cold light.
