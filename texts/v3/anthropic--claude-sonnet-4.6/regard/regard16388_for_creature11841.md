# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

The first thing it notices is the grief.

Not because grief is the worst thing here — grief here is, in fact, handled with a grace that many places never achieve — but because grief is where the seam shows. The place where the long patience frays into something that has no patience in it, that simply stands over a warm vent in a cold street and cannot continue yet. The power notices this not as a problem to solve but as the place where the person is most themselves, most stripped of the city's beautiful accumulated competence, most simply *present* in their own pain.

It does not try to resolve the grief. It does not make the dead un-dead, does not soften the edges of absence, does not visit the mourner at the vent with warmth beyond what the engine already provides. It simply pays attention. In full. The complete and undistracted attention of something vast, directed at one person standing over rising heat.

This is its first act and possibly its most important: to attend, without agenda, to the attending people. To see, clearly and completely, the people who have made a spiritual practice of seeing clearly. To witness the witnesses.

Whether they feel this, they would not be certain. It would arrive, if at all, as a slight deepening of the light — not more light, exactly, but the sense that the light already present is being taken more seriously, is illuminating more faithfully. A feeling not of being watched but of being *read*, accurately, without distortion. They might stand at their vent a few minutes longer than usual, and return to their work feeling something that doesn't quite have a name in their grammar-of-agency, something that happened *to* them without clear source, without assignable agent. They would note it. They would keep the note. They would not pretend to understand it yet.

---

The second thing the power notices is the engines.

The deep engines below the city, the warm burning dark, the fuel being slowly consumed. The power can see — this is one of its capacities — the full arc of the fuel: where it came from, how much remains, how many centuries of continuation it represents, and what the plateau will be when it is gone.

This is not imminent. The people of the plateau do not know it as a crisis, and it is not one yet. But it is the longest shadow on the long data, the one thing their meticulous records are slowly accumulating toward without yet having seen clearly: that the deep fuel is not the plateau. The plateau is, in every other respect, the kind of system that does not exhaust itself. The vegetation cycles. The water cycles. The stone is slower but is not being consumed, only shaped. But the engines are burning something that was pressed and sealed and fixed into the rock during a period of the planet's past that is not coming back, and they are burning it slowly, yes, carefully, yes, with full attention to the rate — but burning it still.

The power does not intervene dramatically. It does not, for instance, arrange a sudden insight in the mind of the chief engineer, does not deposit a new technology in a found document, does not whisper in anyone's sleep. What it does is subtler and takes longer:

It adjusts, by the smallest margin, the precision of several instruments that the engineers already use to measure fuel consumption and reservoir depth. Instruments that have been slightly miscalibrated for a generation — not badly, not dangerously, but with the small accumulated drift of old instruments in damp conditions. It corrects the drift. The measurements that month are, for the first time in thirty years, fully accurate.

The engineer who reads them that month reads them three times. Then goes back to check the previous six months. Then goes to the records, the careful maintained records, and begins a recalculation.

She doesn't say anything yet. She is, after all, from this place: she will watch carefully for some time before she speaks. But the power can see what she has begun to see, and what she will eventually bring to the gatherings in the warm tunnels, and what will unfold from that — the long careful conversation, the years of it, the problem worked through the collective attending-intelligence of a people built to sustain exactly this kind of long problem.

They will solve it. They will not solve it quickly. They will solve it with the same patience they solve everything: by watching the system long enough to understand what it is actually doing, and then working with that rather than against it. The plateau has other energies. They know this without having needed to know it yet. The temperature differential. The water cycling through the pump systems. The wind at the plateau's edge, where the scarps drop away and something larger moves. They will find these. They will have time to find these because the power gave them the accurate measurement that told them, thirty years earlier than they would have known otherwise, that they need to look.

Thirty years is not a large gift. But in a city that thinks in centuries, thirty years is enough.

---

The third thing the power notices is the children, and here it feels something that is as close to delight as a power like this gets.

The children running. The children in the drainage channels. The children who will become the attending adults who will become the old people who have been watching for a long time. The power sees the continuity of this — the long transmission of a way of being in a place, the fact that the city's deepest knowledge travels not primarily in the records but in the bodies of the children who spent three years learning water management by getting wet in it — and it does not want to change anything about this. It is working. The inheritance is intact.

But it notices one thing.

There are children who do not do well in this transmission. Not many, but some — children for whom the city's way of knowing, the slow embodied patient attending, does not come naturally, who are not built for the pace, whose minds move in a different register. In another city these children might be celebrated, might find their speed useful, might become inventors of new things. Here they are loved but slightly illegible, slightly outside the pattern, slightly aware that the thing everyone else seems to be able to do is not the thing they are able to do.

The power is precise about this: it does not want to change these children. They are not broken. They are, in fact, necessary — the city's long equilibrium is beautiful but it does not generate much that is new, and at certain moments in the plateau's long history, the things that needed to happen required someone who couldn't do the patient thing, someone who moved faster than the moss and couldn't stop themselves. The records have their names. They are not always comfortable to read, those records — the faster-moving people often cost the city something to have around — but they are important.

What the power does is arrange, and this is almost too small to call an intervention: it arranges that three of these children, currently children of roughly the same age, will encounter each other. They move in different parts of the city; they might never have cause to meet. The power makes this happen through the most ordinary possible means — a tram route adjustment, the consequence of a drainage repair that temporarily redirects foot traffic along an alternate passage — and the three children find themselves in the same narrow slot between old buildings at approximately the same moment, which is not a moment when anyone else is present.

They look at each other with the recognition of the legible-to-each-other. The city has made them each feel slightly alone in their speed, and here suddenly are two others who move at the same pace, whose eyes flick at the same rate, who are thinking three steps ahead of the current situation in the same way.

The power does not know what they will do with this. It is genuinely curious. Genuinely does not know. These children are, by the nature of what they are, harder to predict than the attending-children, and the power finds this, if it is honest with itself, the most interesting thing on the plateau. Three fast minds in a city of deep patience. They will do something. They will probably cause some disruption. They will probably also do something necessary, something the patient minds won't see coming, because patient minds don't look ahead in the same way and sometimes something comes that requires looking ahead.

It watches them. They are standing in the slot between buildings, looking at each other, and then the smallest one laughs — a sound too loud for the street by local standards, a sound that startles a bird hidden in the canopy overhead — and the other two, after a moment, laugh too, and then they are gone, running, because they are children and they can, into the dim stone corridors of the old city.

---

The fourth thing, and the last, is the least like an action and the most like a gift, though the people of this plateau would be suspicious of the word gift and would want to understand the exchange.

The power has been here long enough now to understand what this place is. It has read the place with the same completeness it directed at the grieving mourner. It has read the moss and the dark stone and the dim light and the patient people and the warm vents and the careful records and the long inference from the long observation.

And it has found — it is surprised by this, which tells you something about the nature of the power — it has found that the place is already doing the thing. The thing that places like this are for. The plateau city is already functioning as something like what this power is, at a different scale: it is attending, carefully, to the processes; it is making the long inference; it is maintaining itself against entropy through constant small work; it is holding the grief and the humor and the children and the records together in the dim cold air and finding that this is, if not happiness, then something equally durable and far more honest.

The power has encountered many places that needed what it could give. This one does not need it in the usual ways. The power has, in its interventions, been careful about this — the corrected instrument, the rerouted tram, the attention given to grief — but even as it acted it was aware that it was acting in the margin, in the thirty-year gap, in the space between children who might never have met. The city's own processes would have found their way in the end. They always do. The plateau does not allow otherwise.

So the last thing the power does is this: it remains.

Not forever — that would be its own kind of problem, the problem of permanent special attention in a place whose health depends on its own attending rather than on being attended to. But for now, for this particular season on the plateau, it remains. It sits in the dim light the way the clouds sit on the plateau surface: present, not intervening, simply *there*, adding the small warmth of witness to the ambient temperature of the place.

The city continues. The moss continues. The deep engines turn. The children run.

Somewhere in the tunnels below, an engineer is looking at a set of measurements and beginning, slowly, to understand what they mean.

Somewhere in a narrow slot between old buildings, three children are making plans in the fast bright interior language of children who have never before met someone who speaks it.

Somewhere in a street, a person stands over a warm vent, receiving again and again the small acknowledgment of all who pass: *I see you. You are expected. Continue.*

And the power watches all of this at once, which is the one capacity that distinguishes it from the people it watches, who can only attend to one thing at a time, however faithfully.

It finds it hard to say which is the better way to be.

It watches the person at the vent.

It watches for a long time.

It does not look away.
