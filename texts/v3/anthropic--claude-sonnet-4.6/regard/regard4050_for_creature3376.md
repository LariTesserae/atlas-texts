# What the Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do

---

The first thing is: it would stop.

Not stop moving — it might have no movement to stop. But it would stop the forward press of intention, the readiness to act, the assumption that to care about something is immediately to do something to it. It would stop in the way that a person who has walked into a room and suddenly understood that something important is happening stops — not freezing, but *attending*. Going quiet inside. Making room for what is already occurring.

It would look.

It would look the way you look when you are not looking *for* anything but simply receiving — the wide soft focus that takes in the whole field rather than selecting a subject. The raptors tilting in the gray. The braided meltwater going where it will. The dim pale gold of the sedge stems, the only warmth in the whole palette, marking the places where transformation is happening fastest. The ibex, already nearly past, their hooves finding the wet rock with the accumulated wisdom of ten thousand generations of feet.

It would take a long time doing this. Much longer than most powers, benevolent or otherwise, would take.

It would not immediately perceive the glacier and think: *this is retreating too fast, the climate is wrong, I should fix the climate.* It would perceive the glacier and think: *here is a thing that has been doing what it does since before I arrived, and it knows something I don't, and I should understand what I'm looking at before I begin to believe I understand what's missing.*

---

The being who lives here — let us say the one who lives here most fully, most permanently, the one whose claim on this place is oldest — is the cushion plant.

This is not a metaphor or a condescension. The cushion plant is the being whose tenure is longest, whose shaping of the place is deepest, whose relationship to the specific chemistry and temperature and moisture of this ground is most intimate. Everything else here passes through or depends on what the cushion plant has already done. The raptors hunt above what the cushion plant made possible. The pikas live between boulders in a climate the cushion plant helped create. The soil, such as it is, such as it is becoming, carries the chemical memory of the cushion plant's centuries.

The benevolent and wise power, in its long attention, would find the cushion plant.

It would look at it for a very long time.

It would feel — powers like this feel things, it seems important to maintain — it would feel something that is not exactly reverence but is adjacent to it. The feeling of being in the presence of something that has done an enormous amount of work quietly, without acknowledgment, without anyone to acknowledge it, and done it well. The cushion plant has been here, possibly, since before the ice came this time. It survived the glacier because of an accident of crack placement and has spent the subsequent centuries building warmth out of its own body, making shelter for things that don't know it's sheltering them, dying slowly on the inside and insulating with its own dead, living on the outside and photosynthesizing what thin light the diffuse sky offers.

This is a very particular kind of life. The power would recognize it.

It would not want to disturb it. It would want to, in some sense that isn't easily nameable, *honor* it — but honor is a human word for a human transaction, and the cushion plant has no use for honor, no capacity to receive it. What the cushion plant needs is not honor but continuation. What it needs is what it has always needed: the particular cold and wet and mineral-richness of this specific ground, the slow recession of ice that creates new possibility ahead of it, the freedom to do its work without being removed or replaced or improved.

The power would recognize that the greatest thing it could do for the cushion plant would very likely be nothing. Or nearly nothing.

---

The nearly-nothing is worth saying carefully.

If the benevolent and wise power has genuine wisdom — not the wisdom of the powerful who believe they understand the systems they govern, but the harder wisdom of recognizing the limits of understanding — then it would feel the weight of that word *before*. The glacier before it has decided what it is. This place is in the middle of a process that has been running, with interruptions, for longer than most powers have existed. The interruptions are written in the rock. There have been ice ages and warm periods and extinctions and colonizations and everything now present here is present because it survived them, or because it came from something that survived them, and the coming of a benevolent and wise power is its own kind of interruption, however gentle.

The wise part of benevolent-and-wise knows this.

So the nearly-nothing. The almost-action. The things it might do that are so close to not-doing that the difference is difficult to articulate but real.

---

**The first nearly-nothing: witnessing.**

The power would witness. Deliberately, carefully, with full attention. This is an act — a genuine act, not the absence of one — because genuine witnessing changes the witness and may, in ways that are not fully physical, change the witnessed. A place that has been truly seen is different from a place that has only been looked at. The cushion plant living its centuries in this basin would, after the power's witnessing, be a cushion plant that has been known. Not famous. Not protected by decree. Not written into any ledger. Simply: known. Held in a consciousness that cares about its being there.

This matters. It matters even if we can't say exactly how.

The power would do this for the cushion plant. It would do it for the midge larvae in their cold pools, cycling the glacier's released past into the plateau's future. It would do it for the pikas with their hay stores and their sharp calls and their farmer's relationship to margin. It would do it for the voles' millimeter-scale world. It would do it for the ibex-herd's collective intelligence of wet rock. It would do it for the weasel-like thing in its brown flashes, the node in the network, the reshaper of other creatures' alertness.

It would do this fully and without hierarchy — understanding that the cushion plant's claim on witnessing is neither lesser nor greater than the raptor's, that the midge larva's existence is neither more nor less worthy of being known than the herd's.

This would take a long time. That would be all right. The power would have time.

---

**The second nearly-nothing: a small adjustment to one thing that is genuinely wrong.**

Here I have to be careful, because benevolent-and-wise is not the same as benevolent-and-certain, and I said the power would stop the forward press of intention. But wisdom that is only restraint is not wisdom; it is timidity dressed up. Genuine wisdom can tell the difference between a system running as it should — which should not be intervened in — and a specific damage that is genuinely not part of the system's natural process, which may warrant a response.

There is, on this plateau, a particular meltwater pool that was, until recently, full of midge larvae and small crustaceans and the first colonizing aquatic insects doing their work. Two seasons ago, a warming event — an anomaly, not part of any historical pattern, part of the long human disruption whose consequences are reaching even here — caused the pool to warm enough, briefly, to allow a bacterium to bloom that would not have been present in this pool otherwise. The bloom consumed the oxygen. The larvae died. The pool is now almost empty, still cold again, but empty.

This is damage. Specific, unnecessary damage. The pool is not learning from it. The system is not strengthened by it. The pikas that relied on catching the emerging adult midges from this pool are having a harder summer and one family that was marginal anyway has failed to raise young that will survive winter. The raptor pair whose territory centered on this part of the plateau is ranging further now and the female is thinner than she should be.

The cascade from one damaged pool is small but real.

The power would fix the pool.

Not restore it to some ideal historical condition. Not stock it with the optimum number and variety of organisms according to some model of what ought to be here. But take the specific, non-natural damage — the bacterium that would not have been here without the warming event, the oxygen depletion, the loss of the colonizers — and quietly reverse it. Return the pool to approximately what it would have been if the anomaly had not occurred. Restock it with the organisms that would have colonized it on their own if the bloom had not killed the first wave.

Do this invisibly, without signal. Not because the power is hiding, but because what the pool needs is its ordinary function, not the knowledge that it has been helped. The midge larvae do not need to know a power fixed their pool. They need the pool to be fixed.

The pika family downstream of the pool will have a better autumn. The raptor will find more prey. These outcomes will look, to anyone watching, like good luck, which they are. All help is luck, from the inside of a life.

---

**The third nearly-nothing: being available.**

The power would remain present — not imposing, not surveilling, but available — in the way that something becomes available when it is genuinely attentive and genuinely responsive and has made itself known to a place through the act of witnessing.

What does this mean practically? It means that if the bacterium blooms again — if the warming continues, as it likely will, and the pool faces the same damage, or different damage, or something unforeseen — the power would be there. Not with a plan. With attention and capacity and the willingness to make, again, the careful judgment between a process that should be allowed to run and a damage that should be mended.

It means that the power would keep watching the plateau across time: the long time of the cushion plant, the shorter time of the raptor pair's successive seasons, the generational time of the ibex-herd's changing routes. It would hold the record of what was here, not in any archive but in the way that deep attention holds things — impressionistically, fully, with feeling for what matters.

It would not intervene in the lives of the animals. The pika that the weasel-like thing catches — the power would not prevent this. The raptor that stoops on the vole — the power would not prevent this. These are the lives of the place, and the place requires them, and the power's goodness would not be sentimental about death any more than the plateau is sentimental about winter.

But if a human expedition came to survey the mineral potential of the plateau — if the people with their equipment and their assessment reports arrived, doing the work the power sensed was coming when it first looked at the beauty of unextracted resources in new stone — the power would make itself felt in the surveyors in a way that is not easy to describe. Not a vision. Not a voice. Something more like: the thing that happens when you are in a place and you become, slowly, unable to be purely instrumental about it. When the ground starts to be ground rather than substrate. When the cold starts to be the cold rather than a logistical challenge. When you find yourself, for a moment, watching the raptor and forgetting entirely what you came for.

The power would make that moment more likely. More available. Longer, if the person could stand to let it be longer.

It would not force anything. People have to find their own way to places, to understanding, to the choices that preservation requires. The power would make available the experience of the place itself — the experience the place has always been offering, to anyone attentive enough to receive it — and trust that the experience, honestly encountered, does its own work.

This is the deepest thing the power could do, and the most uncertain, and the one that requires the most faith in the capacity of lives — any lives, even human lives in their current condition of amnesia about what they are part of — to be reached by reality when it is allowed to present itself clearly.

---

The power would leave no mark on the plateau. It would carry the plateau, for a long time, and then perhaps always, as the kind of knowledge that changes how you understand scale and patience and the nature of process. It would understand something, from the cushion plant's centuries and the midge larvae's short fierce adult lives and the ibex-herd's collective wisdom of wet rock, about what it means to inhabit a becoming rather than a fixed thing. About the specific dignity of creatures and places that are still deciding what they are.

It would return, probably. Not to intervene but to witness again, to take the long measure of the place across the time it needed to become itself.

It would hope for the tundra, in ten thousand years. For the soil that the sedges are building and the cushion plants have been building before them. For the pikas whose descendants will have more to store, when there is more to cut. For the pool full of larvae in the cold water, cycling the ancient organic material forward through the biosphere, one short fierce generation at a time.

It would not know if the tundra would come. The climate is not settled. The future is not settled. The place is still deciding what it is.

That would be all right. The power would have learned, from the plateau, that undecided is not unworthy. That a stub in the long index of the world's places is still a place, complete at every moment in what it currently is, asking its question of what can I be with genuine and patient openness.

It would love the plateau the way the plateau deserves to be loved: fully, and without demanding that it hurry up and become.
