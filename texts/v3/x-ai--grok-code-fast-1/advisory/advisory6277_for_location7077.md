### Physical Preparation: Gear for Muddy Immersion
You'll be wading through waterlogged dirt, so your body (and kit) needs to handle perpetual dampness. - Base layers: Moisture-wicking fabrics (merino wool or synthetic blends) in long sleeves and pants—cotton rots fast here. Tuck pants into socks or boots to block mosquitoes.
- Outerwear: Waterproof jacket and pants (e.g., Gore-Tex) for fog or drizzle, but lightweight to avoid overheating. No heavy rain gear; it's more mist than downpour.

- Headgear: Wide-brimmed hat for sun/fog visibility, bug net for face and neck (mosquitoes swarm relentlessly).
- Accessories: Sunglasses for bright haze, gloves for handling slimy rocks or plants, and a neck gaiter as a multi-use scarf/bandana.
- Pro tip: Pack extras of everything—humidity degrades fabrics. Test gear on a humid hike back home.

**Shelter and Sleeping:**
- Tent: A lightweight, waterproof one (e.g., a tarp setup or dome with bug net). Pitch on raised berms (look for drier spots) to avoid flooding. Intuitively, sleeping here feels cozy at first—the warmth and sounds lull you—but nights get chilly near water, with dew soaking everything.
- Sleeping bag: Synthetic, rated for 50-60°F (warm climates), but bring a mylar blanket for damp cold snaps. Sleep off the ground on a thermarest pad to prevent hypothermia from the cool mud.

**Mobility and Navigation:**
- Backpack: Sturdy, waterproof duffel (or dry bag) to keep everything mud-free. Use a trekking pole for balance in slippery terrains—mud can hide holes.
- Nav tools: Compass and GPS (multiversal anomalies might scramble signals, so have a backup map or landmarks like distant "humps" of rock). The land's flatness makes getting lost easy; use waterways as guides, but rivers shift imperceptibly over visits.
