Here, the world is a submerged one, vast and woven entirely from human craft—or at least, from hands that mimic humanity's relentless drive. It exists below the surface of something vast, perhaps an ocean that wraps around the entire sphere of a planet, or a hollowed-out world submerged in its own amniotic sea. Water is everywhere, thick and pervasive, a fluid medium so dense that it defines motion, rarely clinging to forms but always pressing in with a cool embrace. It's not the boiling cauldron of hydrothermal vents or the searing heat of shallower tides; temperatures hover in that crisp, alpine chill—around 50 degrees Fahrenheit or less, feeling brisk against scaled skin or engineered plate. The chill bites subtly, numbing extremities over time, demanding constant circulation to stave off the lethargy it brings.

Elevation here is zero in every direction: it's all beneath, no peaks or valleys that aren't engineered. The floor sags into shallow trenches or rises into knolled plateaus, but these are not natural extrusions—they're deliberate, constructed flatnesses or tilts designed for utility, rising no higher than a submarine mount yet dipping into chasms that mimic fathoms. Erosional forces are young, scant; the landforms are sharp-edged, recently smoothed or lasered into place. Where water has nibbled at the edges, it's brief and fresh, etching fine scars into synthetic composites or reinforced alloys, but nothing ancient or gravel-rounded lingers. These are newborn realms, fabricated over decades rather than eons, their edges still raw like the lip of a freshly poured mold.

The scale is continental, no—planetary, a webbing of interconnected structures spanning hemispheres or even girdling a small moon in orbit around a gas giant. Your perspective might be confined to a single node, a vast chamber the size of a city block or an entire island, but beyond it sprawl corridors, tunnels, and hab-modules stretching into darkness, connected by umbilical conduits that pulse with fluid flow. Weather here isn't storms or clouds but thermodynamic currents: great convective loops where warmer saline plumes rise like thermal updrafts, carrying heat from geothermal forges or industrial exhausts, only to cool and plunge like aquatic waterfalls into abyssal sinks. These cycles create their own dynamics—ebbing tides that shift resources, spiraling eddies that herd flotsam, and periodic "storms" of sediment swirls where eroded particulates from grinding machine parts or bio-digested waste churn into muddy vortexes, obscuring normal light for hours at a stretch.

Density is sparse, a near-void that amplifies the vastness. Structures cluster like archipelagoes in this oceanic expanse—hanging scaffolds of girderwork, buoyant factories tethered to heaving struts, or submerged silos floating weightless in the gelid flow—but between them yawn distances measured in kilometers. Vast spaces bubble with emptiness, where nothing inhabits the volume save for scattering motes of particulate or the faint, distant hum of aqueous propagation. It's hollow in feeling, a lattice of presence suspended in absence, where you could traverse leagues of cool water without brushing against a single wall or occupant, the engineered forms mere punctuation marks in an otherwise featureless brine.

Everything here is built, utterly constructed; there's no natural substrate beneath the overlays of fabricated hulls and erect plates. Rocks don't exist except as quarried imports bonded into concrete composites; soils are synthetic clays molded into foundations or dispersal pods. Even the sparse vegetation feels artificial—straggling patches of engineered algae clamped to filter-grates or hydroponic tendrils woven through conduit vents, their growth stunted by the cool waters and sparse nutrients. Sparse means intermittent: a lone frond here clinging to a corroded coupling, a mat of symbiotic kelp there lashed to a water-intake tower, but rarely more than a veneer, as if the ecosystem were an afterthought, obliged only to process waste or vent oxygen. Fauna is abundant, paradoxically thriving in this mechanized marrow: schools of bio-engineered fish dart like living circuits, their silver scales reflecting normal light in rippling flashes. Larger creatures—slender eel-like custodians, perhaps, or shoaling crabs with lamprey-mouthed manipulators—patrol the vastnesses, grazing on particulates eroded from the structures or preying on smaller schools. Predator-prey dances unfold in slow ballet, radiators chasing scavengers through the empty corridors, their bioluminescence strobing faintly in the dimmer recesses. The fauna seems purpose-built too, immune to the chill via altered metabolisms, feeding off the industrial effluvia that seeps like slow poison into the waters—nutrients from oil leaks, protein from spent catalysts, metals from corroding joints.

Tech permeates, at an industrial level: steampunk hydraulics replaced by vast piston-forces and motor hums, factories churning out replacement parts for perpetual maintenance. You'll hear the roar of turbines drawing in seawater for cooling, the clang of forge-hammers shaping alloys submerged in pressurized chambers, the whine of electrolyzers splitting hydrogen for fuel. It's mechanized production on a vast scale, lines of assemblers clunking along chain-conveyors in flood-lit bays, extruding building materials or bio-stock from raw slurry pumped from distant refineries. Electrified veins wire the whole, conduits of bundled cables snaking through the water like exposed nerves, sparking occasionally with overload arcs that illuminate brief, blue-white halos.

Light here is normal, not the crushing dark of abyssal depths nor the glare of surface suns—it filters through, perhaps from engineered skylights or diffusing membranes that mimic overhead illumination, casting a diffused blue-green glow that feels like twilight in a flooded basement. In the denser clusters, it's augmented by fluorescent tubes humming through protective casings or the bioluminescent output of the fauna itself, creating zones of half-shadow where structures loom like vast, dripping machines. Deeper into the voids, light fades to pinpricks, but never vanishes entirely; it's modulated for practicality, bright enough to reveal the sharp features of those young erosions, the glint of metal edges, the flash of schools turning en masse.

Sounds amplify in this aqueous world, loud and relentless. The water carries vibrations like muscle through bone—every stroke of a propellor echoes as a distant thunder, the grind of gears translates into a bone-rattling churn that rattles your internals. Hydraulic hisses build into crescendos during maintenance cycles, like whale songs twisted into industrial roar, while the fauna adds its chorus: the slap of fins against glass hulls, the clatter of exoskeletal collisions during hunts, the amplified buzz of electrical fields sparking off dermal charges. It's not deafening chaos but layers of active dynamism, the rhythm of processes intersecting—coolant rushes surging counter to migratory herds, the deep bass of tectonic simulators testing structural integrity against emergent fissures.

Emerging from this, the rules of the world fold into place: movement is governed by fluid dynamics, where swimming or tethered gliding supplants walking; society revolves around resource flows, rationing nutrients and energy in cycles tied to the convective weather; maintenance is eternal, as erosion nibbles perpetually at the made-things; and the vast emptiness demands connection, through networked devices or migratory pathways, lest one drift irrevocably into isolation. Life persists through these voids, driven by the active churn—fauna bursting into sudden frenzies as currents shift, factories awakening in synchronized pulses to replenish the reserves, all under that cool, relentless pressure. The place breathes, not with lungs, but with the rise and fall of its engineered tides, carving out a reality that's as much machine as milieu.
