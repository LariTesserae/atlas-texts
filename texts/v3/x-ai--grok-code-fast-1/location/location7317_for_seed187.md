The hollow you enter—or stumble upon, depending on how one arrives—could be thought of as a sunken amphitheater in the earth, a bowl carved out by countless eons of water's patient work. It's not vast in the way of a valley or desert; it's small, contained, perhaps no more than a single valley's curve mirrored in a still, ancient lake that never quite dried up nor swelled over its banks. You might call it a "grove," but that undersells it—it's more like a living cathedral, a space that holds itself together through sheer, unyielding presence, without walls or arches save those grown from the earth itself. The scale is intimate yet immense: from lip to center, it might span the width of a modest hamlet, but the way it pinches upward into mist and shadow makes it feel like a vast, enclosed room in some forgotten palace, where ceilings arch in green and the floor bows gently beneath your feet.

The ground here is low, hugging close to whatever distant, imaginary sea line this world might claim—elevation here is nearly flat, a confederacy of subtle slopes and dips that testify to the ancient erosion that shaped it all. This isn't dramatic erosion, not the kind that carves canyons or topples cliffs; it's the quiet, primordial kind, the wear of a million rains that have flattened and smoothed everything into its oldest stable state. Pockets of sediment lie like shallow bowls, filled with cool, pebbled water that reflects nothing back up save the diffuse light of a sky that's always a muted gray-blue. The materials of the place are earthy, simple: silts and clays that have hardened into rock-hard pans over countless cycles, cracking faintly where they haven't been polished flat. Here and there, veins of iron or quartz peek through, oxidized to rusty hues or gleaming faintly, relics of a earth that long ago decided what it was and what it would remain. No sharp edges—everything is worn, curved, settled into a final form that's both inviting and eternal. The air itself feels ancient, heavy with the scent of wet earth and minerals, redolent of an old library where the pages have turned to dust but the stories linger.

Water pervades without dominating. It's not flooding or relentless, but damp—a persistent whisper from the ground up. Pockets collect in hollows, small basins where the clay holds moisture like cupped hands, turning into ephemeral pools that steam lightly in the cool air. Condensation beads on leaves and stone alike, gathering into droplets that plip softly against the earth. Occasional rains come, not as storms but as fine, intermittent mists that drift through like ghosts, filtering downward in a lazy drizzle that never bothers to hurry. Riverine veins trace their way across the floor, thin channels no wider than a forearm, winding through the silt with a patience that's unchallenged. You might step over one effortlessly, unaware, only to feel the damp seep into your boots later. In the air, humidity hangs like a veil, never oppressive, always cool enough to chill the skin without shivering. It's an enigma of balance: wetness without wetness, movement without rush, as if the water remembers its ancient paths and follows them blindly, faithfully.

And vegetation—that's the heart of it, the all-encompassing mass that defines every breath and bound. This is a pure plant ecosystem, where green isn't decoration but substrate, architecture, the very scaffolding of reality. Plants fill the space utterly, but not in the aggressive sprawl of a thicket or tangle. Their density is sparse, hollow—vast voids yawn between them, creating near-voids where sound echoes strangely and light falls unfiltered. Imagine colossal pillars of growth, spaced so widely that you could stroll for minutes between them without brushing a leaf, yet from afar, they loom like titans guarding the hollow. Great ferns arch upward, fronds as wide as barn doors unfurling into cathedral vaults overhead, their stems woody and ribbed like the columns of some primeval temple. Tree-like structures rise, but not trees in the common sense—more like living factories, their trunks twisted into bulbous chambers that pulse faintly with internal life, leaves plating their crowns in layers that form natural roofs and ceilings. Vines drape down,moss carpets the ground in velvet swaths that give way to bare earth, and fungi erupt like natural furnaces, caps blooming into tangled, rosette formations that exude spores in lazy clouds. Everything is interwoven yet spacious: a root system that forms subterranean networks, visible in places where the soil has eroded away, tangled mazes that gather nutrients from the damp pockets below. Plants climb onto the low rises, forming baroque sculptures—ehemispherical balls of bracken that enclose small worlds within, or spires of bamboo-like stalks that thin into needle points stabbing at the sky. It's architecture born, not built: chambers within trunks that suggest rooms (though none are entered, for the density forbids it), corridors of vine that snake between voids, platforms of matted leaves that provide... nothing, really, for you or anyone, but eternity for the system itself. In this equilibrium, plants grow only to their ancient limits, never advancing or receding—just filling, always filling, in a frozen steady state where death feeds life in a seamless loop, no seed ever sprouting anew without its counterpart decaying.

The temperature hovers in cool constancy: a mild chill, like the edge of autumn that never deepens into winter, never warms into spring. It's bracing but not biting, the kind where breath puffs visibly in the voids, and dew forms on exposed skin without discomfort. Thin fog often coils near the ground, a cool haze that muffles sounds and softens light, born from the interplay of damp earth and shaded canopy. In the open pockets between plants, it feels almost cozy, like a perpetual twilight, but never shifting.

Fauna is abundant, thriving in the generous voids left by the vegetation's discretion. Creatures move through this space not in hordes but in measured waves, their numbers vast when viewed across the hollow. Birds wheel overhead in flocks that eclipse the light momentarily, their calls a raucous symphony; insects buzz in clouds that dance around the fern pillars, laying eggs in moist crevices; small mammals—vole-like beings or scaled crawlers—scurry over the mossy floors, darting from root to root. Larger game prowls the edges: deer with hide patterned like bark, blending into the trunks; serpents as thick as your arm, coiled in watertight pools, eyes gleaming in the mist. Amphibians hop from puddle to puddle, their skin glistening with condensation. They don't overcrowd; they exist in harmony with the voids, populations stabilized by the unchanging abundance of plant-shelter and water. No predator-prey imbalance—everything is in equilibrium, births and deaths mirroring each other exactly, no surpluses or shortages. The animals seem part of the plant matrix themselves, some forms halfway between the two realms, like lizards with fronds sprouting from their backs, or insects that mimic fungal caps. Ecosystem rules are ironclad here: competition is absent, for resources are eternally sufficient; migrations are loops within the hollow, never leaving or arriving; extinction and emergence are concepts from outside worlds. In this never-changing dynamic, time is invariant—events repeat down to the millisecond, lives lived in perfect clockwork without deviation. You might witness a flock landing, preening, then launching anew on an endless cycle, or a snake swallowing a frog only for another to appear from the same pool exactly as the last one did.

At least, that's how it seems. The rules of the world are simple and unyielding: no change, no built intrusions, no tech beyond what nature has integrated—no factories here except in the metaphorical creaking of branches that sound like machinery grinding. And yet, the sounds belie that. It's loud, piercingly so, in a way that fills the voids rather than echoing emptily. Birdsong swells in cacophonous waves, layered harmonies that drown out thought, while insects provide a constant burr, like distant chainsaws humming. Wind through the fronds produces a whoosh like pistons firing, and drips from condensation hit the earth with plops that resonate like heavy footsteps. Rains add their percussive din when they come, fat drops smacking leaves in a rhythm that's almost mechanized, as if hidden engines pump water from the seams of the world. Fauna contributes: calls and shrieks, the rustle of bodies moving en masse, the splash of creatures entering pools. It's industrial in tone, this auditory assault—raw, mechanized, the sort of noise from scaled production lines—yet born entirely of living, untouched elements. Light filters through normally, neither harsh nor dim: a diffuse glow that casts long shadows in the voids, illuminating dew on plants and the glint of water pockets, cycling with a day-night that repeats in unchanging arcs.

Walking through this place is like traversing a living mechanism that's long since achieved its purpose and simply... endures. The low elevation pulls you downward, the ancient smoothing preventing any ascent or descent that isn't imagined. Plants part reluctantly around you, their mass solid yet yielding, as if asserting their right to be. Ecology here is a closed circuit: water feeds the greens, plants shelter the animals, animals fertilize the earth, and time holds it all still. Rules emerge as you linger: don't disturb the pockets, or the damp will rise inexplicably; speak, and the sounds amplify in reflection through the voids; linger too long in one spot, and you might feel the equilibrium tug at you, inviting stasis.

Come here at dawn (or whatever analog this world offers, for it never truly changes), and the mist thins—just enough to reveal newer details. A fern pillar, upon closer inspection, reveals chambers where moss balls have formed intricate interior gardens, miniature ecosystems held within, dense yet self-contained. Touch a trunk, and it hums with an inner vibration, like a motor idling. Step into a void, and a sudden cool gust carries spores that taste of metal and damp. Birds perch in rosettes at the tops, their nests woven from vine and fungi, and below, in the silted pockets, tiny fish dart—how did they get there? Through the rain, perpetually restocked. As the day progresses (or doesn't), shadows lengthen, and the sounds modulate into a deeper roar, as if the whole hollow is gearing up for... nothing. Just existence.

In this place, the landscape defines itself through absence as much as presence: the voids between affirm the plants, the quiet between rains amplifies the wetness, the unchanging stability makes every element eternal. It's a world that feels alive precisely because it's frozen—plants as architecture, fauna as ornaments, sound as the pulse of an forever-idling engine. You leave... or you don't. But the hollow endures, settled into its final form, waiting for the next equilibrium breath.
