The grove—it's about the size of a small hamlet, or perhaps a grand room in some forgotten mansion, hemmed by weathered stone walls that curve like the ribs of a whale, rising no higher than a man's armspan. These walls aren't straight; they've been gnawed by countless rains, their surfaces pocked with wormholes and etched with faint, looping scars that might once have been carvings or engineering diagrams. Inside, the space is entirely saturated, packed so tightly that there's scarcely room to step—every inch is occupied by vegetation that doesn't just grow, but constitutes the world here. The ground itself is a living substrate, a thick mat of mosses and fungal blankets woven into a yielding, breathing floor that squelches underfoot, like stepping on a bed of cooled pitch. It's warm here, perpetually so, the air heavy with moisture that condenses on every surface, beading into droplets that weep from leaves and drip from exposed metal seams. The temperature hovers just above comfortable for bare skin, a damp heat that clings, making everything feel like the aftermath of a recent, unending rainfall.

Water isn't scarce; it's ubiquitous, frequent and near-surface, rising in slow, patient rhythms from unseen depths. You find it condensing on broad, trumpet-shaped leaves that funnel dew into rooty drains, or seeping from cracks in the stone walls, where minerals leach out in milky veins, tinting the moisture a faint, iridescent green. The walls themselves weep water, rivulets tracing fractals down their surfaces, pooling in shallow basins carved into the moss-floor, which absorbs the overflow like a sponge, only to exhale it back as mist during quiet cycles. It's a post-rain landscape perpetually refreshed, where puddles mirror the dense canopy above—a canopy that's not skyward foliage but a interlaced architecture of vines and fronds, packed so densely they form living arches, hallways, and chambers. These plants aren't mere greenery; they're the substrate, the air, the walls, the everything. Masses of living tendrils weave through the air like suspended cables, their stems thickened into ropes that thrum with internal motion, slow contractions that shift the whole grove over centuries. Branches bifurcate into pod-like structures that hang heavy, pulsing with a subdued glow under the normal light filtering through skylights—patched openings in the canopy overhead, where light falls in diffuse beams, illuminating motes of floating spores and drifting seeds that ride the condensing currents.

The vegetation dominates, filling every space without gaps; it's a pure-plant ecosystem, an all-living mass where even the scattered built elements have been assimilated. Here and there, industrial remnants peek from the crowded tangle—half-buried locomotives overgrown with ivy that mimics their engine blocks, vines sprouting from boiler chambers now housing fungal growths. A single building stands partially intact, its frame of rusted girders entwined with roots that twist like steel wool, its roof sloped and furred with moss that siphons rainwater into gutters alive with bioluminescent algae. Faint marks on the walls hint at scaled production—perhaps a factory floor converted by vegetation into a throbbing heart, where pistons of plant-flesh pump in gradual cycles, manufacturing nothing tangible but nourishment for the greater mass. Engines hum just audibly beneath the surface, electrified veins sparking faintly along stems that bifurcate into filament networks, reminiscent of wiring harnesses. It's all mechanized, yet alive: presses of bark compress seed pods into mechanical pellets that feed back into the soil, factories of petal and leaf-feeding off the frequent water to churn out more of themselves in patient, enduring processes.

The air is loud with the sounds of this integration—the drip-drip-drum of condensing water hitting fleshy surfaces, amplified in the packed density where echoes bounce without escape, building to a cacophonous murmur. Machinery groans softly, a low rumble like distant factories idling, interspersed with the wet slaps of leaves unfolding or roots shifting in slow bondage. When you touch a vine, it responds with a faint, metallic hiss, as if venting steam from internal chambers. The weirdness permeates: plants that incorporate tech not as convenience, but as essence—blossoms that open to reveal cogs meshing with pollen-dusted gears, or tendrils that coil like insulated wires, conducting subtle currents that make the entire grove hum in unison. Fauna is rare; you glimpse only shadows sometimes, perhaps a single insect-like thing with wings of translucent membrane fluttering near the weeps, or a sluggish amphibian form half-emerged from a wall-seep, its skin iridescent with leached minerals. But they don't intrude much; the ecosystem is self-sustaining, strange and self-contained.

Changes here are slow, spanned across eons of the grove's existence. Erosion is established, the whole place in its own weathered balance—no rushing torrents, just the patient nibbling of water and wind smoothing edges over millennia. A new vine might push through a rust-flake over decades, a structure's remnant crumbling inch by inch as roots embrace it, turning solidity into substrate. Yet nothing feels urgent; gradients are weak, flux minimal—a puddle might deepen by a hair over a year, the canopy adjusting by imperceptible increments as the packed mass breathes and reforms. It's a place of gradual cycles, a living, electrified marsh encased in stone, where plant and machine merge into a singular, loud symphony of drips and hums, forever renewing itself in the warm, watery embrace of its own rules.
