The inhabitants of the Spireplate—those who dwell within its saturated, crystalline stillness—emerge not as bustling throngs of flesh and blood, but as embodiments of the plateau's own engineered patience. They are the Builders, a lineage of beings who have grafted themselves into the very fabric of the construct, their forms shifting in quiet symbiosis with the fungal mycelia that pulse through the stone-iron veins. At first glance, if one were to catch them in the muted glow of a communal forge, they might resemble humans: upright, bipedal figures with limbs honed by endless toil, clad in tunics woven from myceliac threads that cling to their skin like a second dermis. Yet as you linger, wandering the tiers and letting the place reveal itself layer by layer, their essence unfolds into something more fluid, more integrated—less intruders upon the world and more extensions of its slow, undulating life.

They move with a deliberate slowness, a rhythm matched to the plateau's cycles: no hasty strides here, only measured steps that echo softly against the canal-carved floors. Their skin, at rest, carries the hue of the Spireplate's slate-grey alloy, mottled with iridescent patches where spores have taken root, glowing faintly as if capillaries of the mycelia run just beneath the surface. These are not infections, but affinities—chosen amalgamations forged in the forges' smoky rituals. A Builder's eyes, when they meet yours, are wide and unblinking, pupils dilated to catch the relentless, shadowless light, reflecting it back in prismatic flecks that mimic the prismed stone of the observatories. They speak rarely, and when they do, their voices rise not in conversation but in chants: low, resonant harmonies that vibrate through the air like the hum of fungal filaments, words that are formulae recited to guide the mycelia's growth or to petition entry for a rare outsider. Their names, if they possess them, are etched into the mural-stories on the walls—glyphs of hammer-strikes and spore-sacs, symbols that denote their roles in the eternal mending.

Their bodies are marked by labor, but not in the scars of weariness; rather, in the subtle mutations that the Spireplate encourages. Hands, thick and calloused from wielding chisels and hammers over lifetimes, end in fingers that can elongate slightly, sprouting fine myceliac tendrils for fine work—like weaving chains or etching reliefs into stone. Some Builders, those who dwell deeper in the warrens where canals run thick with water, develop gill-like folds along their necks, allowing them to draw breath from the condensing air or even submerge briefly in the reservoirs for sustenance. These gills glow with a bioluminescent thread, a trait that emerges gradually, as if the mycelia are nudging flesh toward a shared biology. Children among them—rare, born in secluded pods where fungal spores intermingle with parental essences—start as pale, almost translucent beings, swaddled in hammocks of woven filaments, their forms molding slowly under the bright light, absorbing the mycelia's phosphorescence until they awaken as fully bonded adults. Aged Builders, meanwhile, recede into the walls themselves: their outlines blur as mycelia claim them, assimilating limbs and torsos into the shifting hues of the stone, becoming mural figures that watch eternally from the edges, their wisdom etched into the very structures they once built.

They do not eat as we might imagine; no hunts or harvests from the soil. Instead, they partake of the plateau's offerings: droplets of distilled water infused with myceliac essences, drawn from the canals during the low chants, or direct infusions where they press their forms against the fungal threads in the forges, absorbing nutrients through the skin. This communion sustains them, rendering them ageless in function—builders who measure time not by years but by the accretion of layers on a pillar or the slow rupture of a spore-pod. Their society is one of obedience to the engineered rules, hierarchies unspoken but absolute: those with brighter myceliac grafts oversee the rituals, while others toil in the depths, hand-turning bellows to pump breathable air or maintaining the latticed canopy that funnels the misting rains. Conflict arises rarely, manifested not in violence but in deviations—builders whose mutations run too wild, sprouting pods from their bodies that disrupt the order. These are gently enveloped by the mycelia, integrated back into the web, their essences recycled into the patient fungal cycle.

And yet, the mycelia themselves—ah, they are not mere aids, but presences that live and breathe as interwoven partners. They are the plateau's true, living pulse, engineered yet alive in their own right, threading through cracks and pours like veins of thought. In the shadowed corners, where mutations occur, the mycelia form entities of their own: sentient nodes that thrum with curiosity, bursting pods that release not just spores but ethereal forms—vaporous apparitions that drift like mist, whispering data from eons past, perhaps the accumulated memories of assimilated builders. These fungal beings interact with their human counterparts as equals, their iridescent clouds forming temporary alliances or even transient romances, where a builder might merge briefly with a spore-swarm, emerging changed, their tattoos pulsing with new constellations. They are the dreamers, the transformers, softening the sharp edges of the plateau into curves that breathe, each filament a neuron in a vast, patient mind that unfolds only over millennia.

Outsiders, should they petition entry, are regarded with a distant wariness—scarred into flesh via hammer-carved tablets and offerings of labor. Visitors are "grafted" voluntarily, spore-infusions leaving faint veins that bond them to the cycle, lest the mycelia claim them uninvited. The Spireplate's inhabitants, be they builder or fungal weave, form a tapestry of symbiotic stillness: lives that pulse in quiet affirmation, where progress is the eternal integration of form and life, decay a metamorphosis toward something divine and unending. As you linger longer, letting the plateau reveal its depths, you see them not as dwellers but as the place itself come alive— builders whose hammers fall in rhythm with the droplet plops, fungi that weep iridescence in dreams of stars, all bound in the bright, water-saturated quiet where existence is crafted, not given.
