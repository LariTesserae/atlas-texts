In the basin of Weepstone Hollow, life clings like dew on moss—it is not born of sturdy stock nor forged in fire, but knitted from water's quiet insistence and the earth's restless heaving. The people who dwell here, if people they can be called, are not merely residents but extensions of the place: porous beings, transient as fog, their forms shaped by the damp embrace of soil and sky. They call themselves the Hollowfolk, a name whispered on breaths laden with vapor, and they weave through the layered maze of homes with the same fluid grace as rivulets threading the paths. To gaze upon them is to see the hamlet itself incarnate—soft, uncertain outlines that shift with the light's eerie pulse, as if the fungal veining on nearby structures has seeped into their very flesh.

Physically, the Hollowfolk embody the basin's restraint and resilience. They are slight in build, rarely taller than a child's reach above the earth, their frames honed by a diet of root-breads and mineral-infused waters that never fill the belly fully, lest the body's hungers provoke the tremors beneath. Skin is their most striking feature: pale as the underside of toadstools, veined with faint, iridescent capillaries that mimic the luminescent patterns etched into the walls and caps around them. This isn't mere pallor from lack of sun, but a symbiosis with the fungi that dominate this wet world. Tiny caps sprout subtly along collarbones or knuckles, not parasites so much as allies, pulsing gently in rhythm with the lunar cues that wax and wane the mushrooms' glow. The skin itself exudes a perpetual sheen, slick with condensed moisture, as if they've absorbed the weeping rocks' unending sorrow. It beads and clings, carrying with it a subtle chill that sooths bruises from the rough-hewn native timbers or softens the sting of thornbush scratches. Hair grows sparse and wiry, often matted with clinging droplets that obscure the eyes—those orbs, wide and unblinking, adapted to pierce the humid haze, reflecting back the faint glow of fungi in dimmer light, like captive stars in a sunken well.

Clothing, if it can be named such, is a tatter of natural elements: woven rushes twisted with vine cords, draping loosely to allow the air's dampness to caress the body unimpeded. No fabrics here are dyed or spun; instead, they soften with age, a gradual melding into the bearer's form until the boundary blurs between garment and skin. Elders, those grounded in the lower homes carved into earth, wear heavier layers daubed with clay, their forms curving to mimic the basin's humps and hollows. Youths in the cantilevered upper stories favor lighter veils, their bodies lithe and acrobatic, learning to balance on narrowing beams as they navigate the perilous flux of settling foundations. All bear scars—raw scrapes from erosion's bite, faded welts from stabilizing a tilting wall—but these are worn like honors, a testament to survival amid the land's birthing pains. In the dim glow of dusk or fog, when shadows detach and wander as described, the Hollowfolk move with an uncanny fluidity, their outlines dissolving into the misty alleys before reforming, as if they themselves are compositions of vapor, capable of dispersing into the air if needed.

But the true essence of the Hollowfolk lies not in flesh, but in their attunement to the hollow's rhythms. They are custodians of water first, then of earth and fungi. A day unfolds not by sun's arc, but by the drip of the sentinel rocks: waking with the first unbroken trickle, when bodies stir from damp beds of woven mats strewn with moss. The central forum, that sunken pit of springwater, draws them like moths to a quiescent flame. Elders congregate there at dawn, cupping hands to sip the mineral-tinged liquid—elixirs brewed from infusions of crushed thorns and glowing fungal spores, believed to ward off the melancholic dreams that haunt those who hoard the sacred fluid. These potions grant fleeting visions, glimpses of drowned memories from the submerged past, where the basin's upheaval was born of some ancient cataclysm, waters rising to drown untold histories. One elder, Lirra-Moss, her skin laced with more fungal blooms than most, is revered as a keeper of these elixirs; she tends a nook in the forum's pebble-lined edge, grinding spores with bone needles into pastes that shimmer like captured lightning. Youths assist her, their fingers stained with iridescence, learning the art of extraction—how to coax the water's chill into clarity, banishing fevers that the humidity breeds.

Community weaves tighter than the reed roofs above, a density mirrored in social strata that stacks vertically like the homes themselves. Those in the lowest levels—elders with backs bent from constant earthward tending—hold the weight of tradition, their voices low murmurs in the oven-smoked air as they share tales of the great upheavals, when cliffs crumbled and waters surged anew. Above them, families cluster in mid-levels, balancing the day's chores: harvesting rushes for weaves, repairing daubed walls against the wind's eroding gusts, or trading root-loaves in communal ovens while avoiding the temptations of metal-bartered tools that outsiders sometimes bring. Dispute arises rarely, carried on currents of whispers, but when it does—over a stolen sip or a tilted foundation—it dissolves in the pools. Opponents wade in together, the water's chill leeching anger as surely as it clings to skin, visions rising unbidden from the depths: submerged fragments of past wrongs, clarified minds merging into consensus. No blows are struck; instead, bonds form, cups shared as seals, the offender emerging damp and renewed, indebted to the water's mercy.

Youths, wilder and more ethereal, inhabit the upper reaches, their domains the cantilevered fingers that defy gravity. Here, they play amid the dangling herb gardens and prayer niches—tiny idols carved from bone or bulbous rock, offerings of luminescent spores or thorn-pricked tokens. One such youth, named Riven-Drip (for the way her hair curtains like a curtain of falling water), scales the structures with bare feet, her body glowing faintly where fungi have colonized her heels—a gift, they say, from the hollow's weirdness. She and her kin experiment with the air's veined lights, crafting shadow puppets on alley walls that detach at dusk, wandering as if alive. They chant low songs to the waterfowl on rare afternoons, mimicking their calls in hopes of coaxing the birds closer, their feathers plucked for quills that etch fungal patterns into bone slabs, chronicling the elastic time of the hollow where days blur into renewal cycles. Mischief drives them: they tease the shy crabs along rivulets, luring them out with drops of elixir-spiked water, only to release them with whispers of thanks. Predators there are none among them—only a gentle predation upon the threshing, life conserved for the greater cycle.

Deeper still, beneath the visible folk, existence stirs in subtler forms. Not all who live in Weepstone Hollow are corporeal; the basin births spirits of the upheaved clay— formless essences that manifest as ripples in pools or echoes in the dripping rocks. These are the Weepers, as they are mutely called, born from the rocks' mournful excretions. They appear as transient chills, skittering through the air when wind stirs, imparting visions to those who touch the stones with intent: flashes of submerged pasts, drowned clans gasping in liquidated histories. Or they inhabit the shadows that detach at dusk, useful envoys carrying messages between homes when voices must not be raised. Some believe certain Hollowfolk have merged with these spirits—a rare elder, say, whose form fades during deep fogs, leaving behind only a glistening pool, only to reform when smoke clears from the ovens. Others discern fauna intertwined: the crabs, those scuttling nomads, are tended like pets, their shells harvested for tools, but whispers speak of villagers who dream as crabs do, burrowing the earth to escape landslides.

Life here pulses with the fungal network, a living tapestry that connects all. Babies are born mid-immersion in the central well, water's chill prompting the first gasp, skin already stippled with nascent glows. Names are given by the fountains: a droplet's pattern or a mushroom's vein dictating syllables like Ael-Water or Thorne-Glim. Death comes quietly, bodies laid in pools to dissolve back into moisture, nourishment for the roots and caps that sustain the next generation. And yet, amid this quiet thrum, hints of the unknown permeate: outsiders arrive rarely, drawn by tales of elusive elixirs, but they depart changed, skin dampening, eyes wide with the hollow's hum. The Hollowfolk endure in their density, water-bound and earth-hewn, fungi-lit pilgrims in a basin that births itself anew. They are not conquerors of the land, but its intimate pulse—each drip, each glow, each yielding step a thread in the weave, waiting for the cycle to swell once more.
