The Saltpan Flats of Veridian Reach stretch across a high, wind-scoured plateau where the air hums—not with birds or insects, but with the low, grinding resonance of industry that has long since settled into the bones of the land. At 1.5 on the elevation scale, this is no mountain summit nor valley floor, but a tableland worn smooth over millennia—tilted slightly toward the east, where distant, hazy ridgelines blur into the heat. The ground is neither rock nor soil in any conventional sense, but a mosaic of fractured salt crust, rust-stained concrete, and patches of cracked, vitrified earth that glitter faintly when the light catches them just so.

Water here is abundant in presence but elusive in form. At 1.8, it is never absent—yet rarely pooled. Instead, it seeps. Condensation beads on the undersides of corroded gantries and drips in slow metronome from pipe joints onto rusted grates below. After the seasonal rains—brief, warm deluges that arrive twice a year—the flats become a labyrinth of shallow brine lenses, each no deeper than a handspan, reflecting the sky like shattered mirrors. Elsewhere, moisture weeps from the walls of abandoned cooling towers, tracing mineral-laced trails down their flanks, feeding mossless cracks where nothing green dares take root. The air itself feels damp against the skin, though the sun is warm (2.0)—not hot, not gentle, but persistent, like the gaze of a tired overseer.

Vegetation is scant (0.6). A few wiry shrubs of saltbush cling to the lee sides of concrete abutments, their silver leaves curled tight against evaporation. No trees. No grasses beyond brittle, grey tufts that snap at a touch. Life here is not green—it is metallic, mineral, microbial. Lichens of an unnatural blue-green stain the lower surfaces of steel catwalks, fluorescing faintly under UV stormlight. They are the closest thing to flora, and even they seem more like corrosion given form than true vegetation.

The built environment dominates (2.5). This was once a city—not of homes, but of function. A vast, decentralized industrial complex built during the Third Consolidation Era, designed to extract lithium and rare earths from geothermal brines. Now, it endures not as ruin, but as repurposed organism. The original grid of processing sheds, pump stations, and electrolytic refineries has been patched, layered, and stitched together over centuries by successive waves of caretakers, scavengers, and municipal engineers. Buildings lean into one another like tired dancers, connected by elevated walkways, pneumatic tubes, and jury-rigged solar collectors that glint like fish scales in the midday glare. There are no streets—only causeways of perforated metal, raised above the brine-soaked ground, rattling underfoot.

Technology hums at 2.1—not sleek or digital, but industrial in the old, muscular sense. Steam still vents from geothermal taps. Belt-driven compressors clatter in outlying sheds. Arc furnaces in the central district glow through narrow slits in their brick housings, casting long, pulsing shadows at dusk. Electricity is abundant but erratic, routed through exposed conduits that crawl like vines up the sides of silos. The tech is not hidden; it is worn openly, like ritual scarring.

And the sound—2.6, deafening—is not a single noise but a chorus of slow violence. The groan of expanding metal as the sun heats it. The hiss of brine boiling off in open evaporation pans. The deep-throated thrum of subterranean pumps pulling ancient water from aquifers miles below. Distant clangs echo from maintenance crews working on the outer rim, their voices swallowed by the scale of the place (2.1—open, regional, a landscape of systems). There is no silence here, only gradients of noise, none of them soft.

Fauna is nearly absent (0.8). A few dust-colored lizards skitter across hot plates, vanishing into drainage slots. Once, a flock of mechanical kites—autonomous sensor drones shaped like birds—glides silently overhead, their wings clicking minutely as they adjust to thermal currents. They are not alive, but they move with such uncanny grace that newcomers often mistake them for the last avian survivors. Nothing truly wild lingers long; the air is too saline, the ground too unstable, the noise too constant.

Weirdness (1.6) manifests not in overt surrealism, but in subtle wrongness. Pools of brine sometimes ripple without wind. Compasses spin lazily near the old magnetic separation towers. At twilight, the salt flats emit a faint, greenish luminescence—not from bioluminescence, but from piezoelectric stress in the crystalline crust, activated by the day’s thermal contraction. Locals speak of “echo-walkers”—not ghosts, but residual audio imprints from past shifts, played back randomly by the resonance of certain metal corridors during temperature inversions. You might hear a laugh, a shouted warning, a song—all from people long gone—carried on the wind like static.

Time moves slowly here (dynamic: 0.9). Nothing is built anew; everything is maintained, adapted, or slowly reclaimed by salt and steam. The city does not grow—it breathes. In winter, when the rains are heavy, new brine channels cut tentative paths through the flats, only to evaporate and seal shut by summer. The people who live here—fewer than five thousand—move with deliberate pace, conserving energy, speaking little. They understand that haste is useless in a place that changes only in geological whispers.

This is not a dead place. It is a patient one. A landscape that has learned to wait.
