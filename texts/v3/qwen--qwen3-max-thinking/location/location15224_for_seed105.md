The world here is not drowned—it *is* water.  

Not ocean, not sea in any earthly sense, but something older, colder, and stranger: a sky of liquid. You stand—or float, rather—within a high-altitude hydrosphere that has no surface, no floor, only an endless, pressurized column of cold, clear water stretching in every direction. This is not a flood aftermath; it is a state of being. The planet’s atmosphere condensed, collapsed inward eons ago under a slow gravitational sigh, and what remains is a globe-spanning envelope of saturated vapor turned liquid, held aloft by an equilibrium of pressure, cold, and some forgotten geochemical rhythm. Elevation is 1.5—not mountain-high in the terrestrial sense, but *stratospheric* in fluid terms: you are suspended miles above whatever solid core may remain, in a zone where pressure is tolerable, temperature near freezing (0.5), and light barely penetrates (0.5).  

Darkness is absolute save for rare, drifting illuminations: bioluminescent flora that thrive without fauna, feeding on chemosynthetic currents or electrical gradients in the water. Vegetation is lush (2.0), but *vegetation* is a generous term. What grows here are vast, slow forests of kelp-analogues—translucent, gelatinous ribbons that unfurl in spirals, anchored not to earth but to floating mineral nodules or the skeletal remains of long-dead megaflora. Others are spongelike colonies, blooming in fractal clusters the size of houses, their interiors threaded with capillary channels that pulse with pale blue or green radiance. They do not photosynthesize—there is no sun—but instead metabolize dissolved silicates, rare earth ions, and the faint geothermal whispers rising from the abyss below. Their forms are elegant, alien: some coil like seahorse tails, others unfold like origami blossoms made of glass. They grow slowly, over centuries, yet the ecosystem is active (1.7)—not with predation, but with constant drift, dissolution, and regrowth.  

Sound is loud (1.5), yet muffled by the water. Not the roar of waves, but the deep, resonant hum of pressure shifting in the fluid column—a basso continuo of the world breathing. Cracks echo from distant ice lenses forming and shattering in thermal differentials. Groans rise from the slow collapse of hollow mineral spires that have grown too tall and fragile. Sometimes, a vast bloom of vegetation detaches and spirals downward, trailing harmonic vibrations as it tumbles—a sound like a cello string bowed with ice.  

There is no animal life (0.0). Not a fish, not a microbe with motility beyond passive drift. The silence of that absence is profound. Life here is sessile, communal, vegetative in the oldest sense—growth without chase, without hunger that moves. It is a world that breathes but does not hunt.  

Scattered through this liquid expanse are the built remnants (1.3): not ruins in the romantic sense, but lonely, waterlogged constructs of pre-metal technology (1.1). Think not of cities, but of singular, ritualistic forms—obsidian monoliths lashed together with braided kelp-fiber, domes of fired clay now glazed smooth by centuries of current, or spiraling towers of stacked river-stones mortared with lime and ash. These are not shelters—they are markers, observatories, or perhaps anchors for something metaphysical. Their makers are gone, or never were; perhaps they were grown, not built, by an intelligence that thought in currents and tides.  

The scale is open (1.6): you can see for hundreds of meters in the clearer strata, your vision ending not at a horizon but at a soft fade into deeper blue, where light fails and the water chills further. Density is sparse (1.2)—great voids separate the forests of flora, the lone monoliths, the floating geodes of crystalline gas. Emptiness is not absence here; it is a medium, a presence in itself.  

Erosion is established (1.6), but in reverse. Instead of wearing down, things *accrete*. Water deposits thin layers of silica on every surface, encasing blades of kelp and stone altars alike in glassy sheaths. Structures don’t crumble—they petrify into translucent tombs, slowly becoming part of the landscape’s mineral memory. The world is in balance, not decaying but conserving, layering time into its bones.  

And the weirdness (2.0) is this: the water itself sometimes *thinks*. Not with neurons, but with pressure differentials, with standing waves that echo for weeks, with soliton pulses that travel intact across the entire region like ghost messages. Locals—if any remain—might say the sea dreams. And in those dreams, the forests sway in patterns that resemble writing, the monoliths hum in consonance, and the cold, dark water holds its breath for a moment… before exhaling into motion once more.  

This is not a drowned Earth.  
This is a world that chose to become liquid—and succeeded.
