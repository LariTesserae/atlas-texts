Beneath the surface of a world long drained of oceans and stripped of green, the Hollow Expanse stretches—an immense, subterranean vault sealed beneath kilometers of fractured basalt and vitrified crust. This is not a cave in any earthly sense. It is a planetary-scale void, carved not by water but by the slow, tectonic exhalation of a dying mantle, its ceiling lost in perpetual gloom, its floor a mosaic of crystalline salt, powdered obsidian, and windless dunes of rust-fine mineral dust.  

Water is a myth here. At 0.5 on the scale, it exists only as spectral traces—hydroxyl ions trapped in lattice defects of silica shards, or transient vapor exhaled by thermal fractures far below. The air (if it can be called that) is thin, dry, and chemically inert: mostly argon and nitrogen, laced with trace sulfides that glitter faintly when disturbed. Nothing flows. Nothing pools. The ground crackles underfoot like shattered glass, but never yields moisture.

Vegetation is absent at 0.2—not even lichen, not even spores. No roots breach the crust. What might be mistaken for life at a glance is, in truth, mineral mimicry: dendritic crystals of iron-manganese oxides that branch like frozen ferns, or hexagonal plates of anhydrite that stack into fragile towers resembling coral. Yet these are purely geochemical—precipitates from ancient thermal vents now long cold. This is a world after biology, or before it; a stage set for life that never arrived.

And yet—warmth persists. At 2.1, the temperature hovers near 38°C (100°F), not from sunlight (none reaches here) but from geothermal bleed-through along deep faults. The heat is even, unwavering, saturating the space like a breath held for millennia. It gives the air a thick, still quality—dense not with humidity, but with suspended particulates: metallic aerosols, silicate nanoparticles, and crystalline motes that shimmer faintly when agitated.

Elevation is profoundly low—0.2—placing you deep within the planet’s crust. The ceiling arches kilometers above, unseen in the dark, but its presence is felt in the pressure of silence and the way sound dies within meters. There is no sky, only the illusion of infinite depth. And though below surface, this void is not claustrophobic—it is vast (2.6), stretching beyond horizon, continent-sized, with its own subtle weather: thermal plumes that drift like ghosts, carrying dust in slow vortices that never touch the ground.

The land is young in its current form—erosion at 1.0. The terrain is sharp, unsoftened. Fracture ridges rise like broken teeth. Salt flats splinter under stress into polygonal shards. Basalt columns, recently extruded from deep fissures, stand unweathered, their hexagonal faces gleaming with a glassy sheen. Nothing has had time to crumble; everything is freshly exposed, as if the crust split open yesterday and never closed.

Density is high (1.6)—not of matter, but of presence. The air thrums with unseen occupancy. Fauna abounds (1.6), though none resemble earth-life. They are chitin-shelled, multi-limbed, blind things that skitter across salt plains on needle-tips, harvesting electrostatic charges from the dust. Others cling to thermal vents, filter-feeding on mineral aerosols, their bodies woven from silica filaments and metallic enzymes. They move in silent swarms, pulsing with bioluminescent patterns that serve no purpose in this lightless realm—perhaps a vestige, or perhaps a language of heat and vibration. Their abundance is eerie, a riot of life in a place that should be sterile.

Yet the world is mostly quiet (0.9). No wind. No rivers. Only the occasional skitter of limbs on crystal, the pop of thermal contraction in rock, the low hum of piezoelectric fields building in stressed quartz veins. Sound does not carry; it is absorbed instantly by the dense, particulate air and the fractal roughness of the ground.

Scattered across this expanse are the relics of something—built at 1.0. Not cities, not even ruins in the conventional sense. Rather: solitary spires of fused sand, shaped by no human hand but perhaps by ancient, self-replicating machines now inert. Or rings of magnetized hematite, perfectly circular, aligned to celestial poles long since shifted. One finds domes of sintered clay, cracked open, their interiors lined with spiral grooves—as if something had coiled within, then unwound itself into the dark. Technology here is primitive (1.4): these structures show no circuitry, no alloys, only fired earth, shaped stone, and crude lenses of natural glass. Whoever—or whatever—made them worked with hands or mandibles, not nanotech.

Weirdness (1.9) permeates everything. Time behaves oddly near thermal vents—clocks slow, not from relativity, but from some unknown interaction between heat and the local quantum vacuum. Shadows cast by bioluminescent fauna sometimes linger after the source has passed, fading slowly like afterimages burned into the air. The salt plains occasionally emit low-frequency tones when stepped on, resonating at frequencies that make teeth ache and thoughts blur. Some travelers report seeing their own outlines reflected in the dust—not as light, but as absences, hollow silhouettes that follow for hours before dissipating.

And yet, for all its strangeness, the Hollow Expanse is frozen in equilibrium (dynamic: 0.3). Nothing changes. No seasons. No decay. No succession. The fauna neither evolves nor dies out; they persist in perfect stasis, cycling through identical behaviors generation after generation. The heat flux is constant. The dust falls but never accumulates. Even the scattered artifacts show no sign of corrosion—they are as fresh as the day they were made, whenever that was.

This is a world that has achieved stillness not through death, but through completion. It is warm, vast, dark, dense with silent life, and utterly unchanging—a cathedral of mineral and motes, where time has pooled and gone still, and the only movement is the slow, blind pilgrimage of creatures who do not know they are already eternal.
