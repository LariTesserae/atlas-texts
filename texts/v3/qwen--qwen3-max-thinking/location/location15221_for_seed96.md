The air tastes of wet stone and iron. Not rust—not exactly—but the sharp, clean tang of freshly exposed metal meeting moisture. You stand in a place that breathes water but refuses green. Rain has just passed, or perhaps it never truly stopped; it merely pools, beads, and weeps without nourishing anything that could be called alive. The ground glistens, not with mud or leaf litter, but with a mosaic of fitted stone, glazed ceramic tiles, and narrow channels of rivulets tracing deliberate paths through an engineered plain.

This is a high place—not mountainous, not jagged, but elevated like the shoulder of a vast, reclining figure. The horizon stretches in all directions, unbroken by forest or thicket, revealing a regional expanse of interlocking terraces, stair-flights, aqueducts, and dry moats now brimming with shallow, clear water. The scale is human enough to walk across in a day, yet too vast to take in at once: a valley-sized artifact, a constructed basin carved not from nature but imposed upon a substrate that may never have been natural to begin with.

Everything here is made. No soil peeks through. No root cracks a tile. The surface is a tessellation of hand-laid materials: slate-gray basalt bricks, fired clay pipes fused into walls, obsidian shards set into drainage grates, bronze fittings dull with oxidation but still holding their shape. The craftsmanship is precise but archaic—chiseled, not cast; fitted, not welded. Tools left behind lean against low parapets: wooden mallets with mushroomed heads, copper chisels, baskets woven from reed-like fibers that may have been imported or perhaps grown elsewhere and brought here for ritual or function. There is no ornament for beauty’s sake; every curve, every slope, every perforation serves water’s passage.

And water is everywhere. Not flooding, not violent, but insistent. Seeping up through gridded vents in the plaza floor. Condensing on the undersides of cantilevered stone eaves. Dripping from spouts shaped like hollowed vertebrae into circular basins below. The air is saturated—humidity clings to skin like a second membrane—but cool, as if high elevation and constant evaporation keep heat at bay. Sunlight, abundant and sharp, glints off every wet surface, turning the whole plateau into a glittering array of reflections. Bright, yes—but not warm. The light reveals, but does not comfort.

There is no vegetation. Not a lichen, not a spore, not even the ghost of decay. The green world has been purged, or perhaps never invited. In its place: mineral patience. Crystalline salts bloom in the crevices where evaporation leaves its signature—white, feathered fractals that grow millimeter by millimeter over years. Fungal analogs? Perhaps. But nothing that pulses or spreads visibly. This is a pre- or post-biological silence.

Likewise, no fauna stirs. No insect hums, no bird calls, not even the skitter of a beetle across tile. The only sounds are hydrologic and mechanical: the soft plink of a droplet falling into a full basin, the sigh of water finding a new channel through a hairline crack, the occasional creak of a wooden sluice gate swollen with moisture. Mostly quiet—but a quiet that feels observed, held.

The density is overwhelming not through clutter, but through completeness. Every square foot is accounted for. Every surface performs. There is no margin, no wilderness at the edge. Even the sky seems constrained by the geometry below, as if the clouds pause to consider the symmetry before drifting on. You cannot step off the path because there is no “off”—only more design, more intention.

Time here moves like water through a narrow capillary: slow, capillary, inevitable. Cracks propagate at a pace measurable only by decades. Salt deposits thicken like slow breath. The builders are gone—or perhaps they never existed as individuals, only as a collective will that etched this place into being and then withdrew, leaving it to its own quiet metabolism.

And the weirdness? It lies in the paradox: a world of water with no life, of craft with no crafters, of openness packed to the brim with purpose but no one to witness it. It feels less like a ruin and more like a vessel—still holding its charge, still waiting for something to remember how to drink from it.

You stand in a cistern the size of a valley. Clean. Full. Empty of everything but the echo of intention.
