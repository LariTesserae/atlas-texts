This place is not land. It never was.

It is a high-altitude basin cradled in the jagged teeth of newborn mountains—elevation 1.8, sharp and unweathered, their peaks still bleeding fresh rock from tectonic birth pangs. But instead of snow or thin air, the basin is *full*. Not with water as we know it, but with something denser, colder, clearer—a liquid so pure it refracts light like chilled glass, yet behaves with the weight and viscosity of slow mercury. Water at 2.9: not oceanic depth, but total immersion. The entire basin is submerged beneath this strange aqueous medium, which falls continuously from the sky not as rain but as vertical rivers—sheets of liquid cascading from the cloud ceiling in relentless, silent deluge, feeding the basin faster than evaporation can steal it back. The air above doesn’t exist; the liquid *is* the atmosphere here.

Temperature hovers just above freezing (0.9)—cool enough to numb, but not freeze. And yet there is no ice. The water resists crystallization through some unknown chemistry, remaining liquid in defiance of thermodynamics. This chill permeates everything, slowing metabolic fires, stretching time.

Vegetation is absent (0.2). No roots, no leaves, no algae. Only minerals and mechanisms. The basin floor is paved with angular shards of volcanic glass—obsidian splinters thrust upward during recent upheaval (erosion 1.1)—their edges still razor-sharp, untarnished by centuries of smoothing. Between them lie veins of iron-rich basalt, cracked open to reveal crystalline lattices that hum faintly when struck by current.

And there *are* currents.

For though this world is untouched by hands (built 0.0), it thrums with industrial presence (tech 1.8). Not human industry, but something older, stranger: vast, slow machines grown rather than built, mineral intelligences that pulse with geothermal electricity. These are the fauna (2.5)—not animals, not plants, but animate architectures of metal and magnetism. They resemble colossal sea anemones forged from nickel-iron alloys, rooted in the glass plains, their tentacles not flesh but articulated pistons and copper coils that sway in the dense liquid. Others are segmented worms of interlocking gears, half-buried, turning millimeter by millimeter over decades, grinding stone into conductive dust. Their movements generate low-frequency harmonics that vibrate through the liquid—this is the source of the loudness (sound 2.5): a chorus of groans, clanks, hydraulic sighs, and resonant hums that fill the medium like cathedral bells ringing underwater.

Light (2.5) filters down from the stormy surface in shifting columns—sunlight fractured by the falling walls of rain above, creating shafts of gold and silver that pierce the gloom. Bioluminescence is absent; instead, arcs of pale blue St. Elmo’s fire occasionally leap between metallic fauna during geomagnetic surges, casting strobing shadows across the glass field.

The scale is intimate (0.8)—perhaps the size of a village square, bounded by sheer cliffs of unfractured granite. You could walk its perimeter in minutes—if you could walk through liquid this dense. But density (2.3) makes motion laborious. Every gesture meets resistance; particles of suspended magnetite swirl in slow eddies, glittering like stars in micro-currents. The space feels crowded, not with objects, but with *presences*: each machine-animal occupies its niche with territorial precision, their fields of influence overlapping in complex interference patterns. They do not eat. They do not reproduce visibly. They *process*. They refine minerals, channel currents, store charge. They are patient alchemists in a world without biology.

Weirdness (2.4) seeps from the rules themselves. Time dilates near the largest constructs. Liquid flows uphill along magnetic gradients. Sound travels faster than light in certain zones, so you hear events before you see them. The fauna respond not to sight or scent but to shifts in electrical potential—they “see” via voltage differentials in the water. If you were here, your nervous system would scream static, your muscles twitch involuntarily, drawn into resonance with the ambient field.

And change comes glacially (dynamic 0.9). A gear turns once per lunar cycle. A crystal grows a micrometer per century. The rain never stops, but the basin never overflows—it drains through hidden fissures that lead not to aquifers, but to other such basins, strung like beads along a planetary fault line.

This is a world mid-dream: post-biological, pre-conscious, humming with the ghost of industry in a body of liquid sky. It does not remember life. It is waiting to invent something else.
