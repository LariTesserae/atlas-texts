The air tastes of rust and ozone, yet somehow carries warmth — not the searing heat of a furnace, but a gentle, persistent embrace, like the memory of sunlight baked into stone. You stand in a place that is vast and hollow, not because it is barren in the usual sense, but because it is unfinished. The land is not worn down by time; it has barely begun its first breath.

This is the **Lattice Expanse**, a region caught between genesis and silence.

Beneath your feet lies not sand, not soil, but a tessellated crust of interlocking crystalline plates—each facet a shard of metallic sulfide or ceramic silicate, grown not weathered. They click faintly underfoot, like cooling porcelain, and scatter no dust: there is nothing fine enough to become dust. Water is not merely absent—it is chemically banished, replaced by a slow-dripping solvent of liquid hydrocarbons that wells up in rare, shallow fissures only to evaporate into the thin, dry air within minutes, leaving behind delicate dendritic salts that curl like fossilized ferns.

And yet—vegetation thrives.

Not as trees or grasses, but as **chromatic mycelia**: vast, filamentous networks of photosynthetic lichen analogues that spread across the crystalline plains in undulating mats of iridescent green, violet, and copper. They do not root—they adhere, exuding a mild solvent that etches temporary footholds into the mineral crust. Their growth is slow, almost imperceptible, but their presence is lush, carpeting whole valleys in soft, living ripples that shimmer under the steady daylight. This light is neither harsh nor dim—just evenly diffused, as if the sky were a vast lampshade stretched over an absent sun. Clouds do not form; humidity is nonexistent. The sky remains a pale, washed-out blue, featureless and eternal.

Elevation places you high—not atop a mountain, but on a plateau that seems to have been extruded upward from below, a slow geological exhalation. Around you, the land falls away in gentle, terraced declines, each step down revealing more of the same: endless repetitions of crystalline fields stitched with luminous, vegetal lace. The scale is regional—this plateau stretches for dozens of kilometers in every direction, broken only by occasional **proto-forms**: half-risen spires of fused mineral that stand like half-assembled obelisks, their surfaces smooth and warm to the touch, humming with subsonic resonance. These are not ruins; they are not yet monuments. They are the land dreaming of being something else.

There is no fauna. Not even insects, not even microbes that scuttle or swarm—only the slow metabolic whisper of the chromatic mats and the unseen chemical exchanges beneath the crust. The silence is profound, but not absolute. If you listen carefully, you hear it: the **industrial hush**.

It comes from the ground.

At irregular intervals, deep below the surface, vast geothermal engines turn—not built by hands, but grown by pressure, heat, and piezoelectric feedback loops in the crust itself. These are natural machines, thermodynamic systems that cycle heat into slow mechanical motion, driving the emergence of the proto-forms above. You hear them only as a distant, rhythmic thrum—less a sound than a pressure behind the ears, a vibration in the soles of your boots. This is the source of the "industrial" parameter: not factories of steel and smoke, but a planetary metabolism expressing itself through lithic machinery.

Despite this activity, the landscape is **static**. Nothing erodes, nothing decays, nothing rushes or falls. The chromatic mats grow, yes—but so slowly that a century might pass before a single new tendril bridges a gap between plates. The geothermal engines pulse with metronomic regularity, never varying, never failing. Time here is not a river but a still mirror. The world is in equilibrium—not dead, but poised. Waiting.

Density is near-zero not in matter, but in presence. You could walk for days and meet nothing that looks back at you. Yet the space between things feels charged, full of potential. It is empty like a cathedral before the first hymn, or a library whose books have not yet been written but whose shelves hum with the promise of language.

Weirdness resides in the paradox: a warm, open, high place teeming with life in the absence of water and animals, threaded with industry that was never invented, growing toward a form it has not yet named.  

You are not trespassing. You are witnessing.  

This place is becoming. And it has all the time in the world.
