The land is not land. Not anymore.

What remains is a basin of low, sagging terrain—a depression worn into the continental shelf over epochs so vast they have erased their own memory. Elevation barely lifts above sea level; the ground here is a shallow bowl, rimmed by distant, crumbling berms of slag and ferrocrete that once held back something. Now they slump like the ribs of a buried leviathan, half-swallowed by time and the slow creep of water.

Water is everywhere, though rarely visible as open pools. At 1.7, it’s not a flood but a saturation—a constant weep. Condensation beads on every surface, not from humidity alone, but from a thermal inversion trapped beneath a lid of smog and ash-cloud: the sky is never sky, only a bruised gauze of particulate haze, lit from below. Rain falls in fine, cold mists that never quite stop, more breath than storm. Pools gather in the hollows of corroded metal plates, in the cupped palms of broken statuary, in the sockets of gargantuan gears left to rust in place. These mirrors of water reflect nothing clearly—only the blurred suggestion of structures above, warped by ripples from unseen drips.

Vegetation is sparse—1.3—but not absent. What exists is ambiguous. Clumps of grey-green lichen crust iron struts like forgotten mold. In sheltered crevices, thin filaments of something resembling moss pulse faintly with bioluminescent residue, though no sun has touched them in centuries. These are not plants but echoes—bioengineered holdouts from a greening program long abandoned, now metabolizing trace heavy metals and exhaling sulfur vapors. They do not grow; they persist. They do not reproduce; they replicate through slow fragmentation, like memory decaying into habit.

The temperature hovers just above freezing—0.8—not cold enough for ice, but enough to numb flesh and slow breath. The air carries a wet chill that seeps through layers, not from wind, but from stillness. This is a landscape that has settled into its final posture: erosion at 2.7 means it has been worn down not by rivers or glaciers, but by time itself acting as a solvent. Every edge is softened, every angle rounded into slump. Stone? There is no stone. Not natural stone. The bedrock is concrete composite, layered with ceramic plating, fused with carbon slag, all cracked and webbed but holding form—not because it’s strong, but because nothing remains to break it further. It has reached equilibrium with decay.

And yet, it is *built*. At 2.8, there is no natural substrate left. The earth has been entirely overwritten. You stand not on soil, but on the skin of a machine that once spanned continents—a planetary infrastructure now inert in function but not in presence. Great aqueducts of corroded alloy arch overhead, empty of flow but humming with residual vibration. Foundations stretch beneath your feet for kilometers, tier upon tier, each stratum from a different century of engineering. Pipes, conduits, monorail skeletons, coolant channels—all fused together by rust, concrete sinter, and crystalline salt blooms from evaporated fluids. Nature did not reclaim this place; it was never given the chance. The built environment *is* the geology now.

Industrial tech (2.3) hums through it—not as active production, but as a ghost economy of motion. Factories do not run, but their turbines still turn slowly, powered by geothermal siphons or tidal differentials in drowned coastal zones far beyond sight. Conveyor belts inch forward once every few hours, carrying nothing. Compressors wheeze in rhythmic bursts, exhaling air that hasn’t been fresh for two hundred years. This is industrialism fossilized into ritual. The machines perform not to produce, but because stopping would mean death—and even death has been engineered out of the system.

Darkness dominates (0.5). The sun is a rumor. Light comes from below: from fissures in the ground where geothermal vents glow dull orange through grates of warped steel; from flickering sodium strips embedded in overpasses, powered by decaying batteries; from the occasional flare of electrical arcing between severed transmission lines that dangle like vines. Shadows are not absence but substance—thick, viscous, pooling in the hollows of collapsed transit hubs. You do not see the landscape; you infer it from glints, echoes, and the cold press of wet metal against your skin.

There is no fauna (0.4). No birds, no insects, no scuttling things. The silence that should accompany such absence is curiously absent itself—because sound (2.6) is deafening. Not with life, but with resonance. The place thrums. Low-frequency drones from distant turbine halls vibrate in your molars. Steam escapes in rhythmic sighs from pressure valves encrusted with mineral deposits. Metal groans as thermal contraction pulls at welded seams. In the flooded underlevels, water sloshes in vast chambers with the cadence of a slow, mechanical heartbeat. It is loud not because of volume, but because every frequency is amplified by the canyon-like geometry of ruined infrastructure. Sound bounces, multiplies, distorts. A single drip can echo for minutes.

This landscape is strange (1.8)—not fantastical, but uncanny. Time does not move linearly here. Rust patterns bloom in reverse on certain alloys. Gravity seems slightly uneven near the old centrifuge complexes. Compasses spin uselessly; magnetic fields are scrambled by buried superconductors. And sometimes, if you stand very still in the right hollow during a mist-lull, you’ll hear voices—not human, not recorded, but synthesized tones reciting maintenance logs in dead languages, broadcast from no discernible source. These are not ghosts, but system echoes, replaying protocols into the void.

Yet it is dynamic (1.8). Processes continue. Water seeps upward through capillary channels in concrete, defying gravity. Salt crystallizes overnight on surfaces, then dissolves by dawn mist. The fog shifts direction not with wind but with pressure differentials from underground venting. Slow, tectonic settling causes new cracks to appear weekly—silent ruptures that exhale warm, metallic-smelling air. The whole basin breathes, albeit faintly.

This is the Endworks—a name whispered by the few who remember maps. Not a ruin, not a wasteland, but a terminal ecosystem: a fully constructed world that has metabolized its own end and now lives in the afterlife of industry. It does not want anything. It does not need you. But it will accommodate your presence—absorb your footsteps into its wet concrete, echo your breath in its steam vents, and, in time, forget you were ever there.
