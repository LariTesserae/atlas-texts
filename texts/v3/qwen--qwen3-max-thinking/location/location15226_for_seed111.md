High. So high it feels like the world has thinned to a blade’s edge. The air here is spare and cold—just above freezing, never warming—the kind of cool that settles in your marrow, not your skin. Every breath is sharp with altitude, tasting of ozone and stone, and the sky, though vast, seems close, pressing down like a lid on a cooling pot.

This is a place newly born—or perhaps reborn. The land hasn’t had time to soften. Jagged spires of black basalt and fractured granite jut upward like broken teeth, still raw from whatever cataclysm birthed them: a volcanic upthrust? A glacial tear? No one knows. There are no roads, no paths, no marks of human passage—nothing built, nothing shaped by intent. Only nature’s first draft, still wet with creation.

And yet, in this barren-seeming theater, life thrums—not in abundance of green, but in movement, in breath, in presence. Vegetation is sparse: lichens the color of dried blood cling to north-facing rock faces; cushion plants huddle in crevices like frightened children; a few stunted shrubs, wiry and wind-blasted, grip the soil with roots longer than their stems. But fauna—oh, fauna is everywhere. Not large beasts, not herds—but countless small lives: winged, skittering, burrowing, clinging. Clouds of highland moths pulse in the thin air like drifting ash. Stone-hoppers—tiny, six-legged arthropods with iridescent carapaces—leap from rock to rock in synchronized bursts. In the damp hollows, translucent salamanders with too many joints coil and uncoil in slow undulations, their skin slick with condensation.

Water is a whisper, not a presence. After dusk, when temperatures dip, mist rises from fissures in the rock—not steam, but cold exhalations from subterranean aquifers sealed beneath ice. In the morning, droplets bead on stone, pooling in natural basins no wider than a handspan. These are the only reliable sources, and around them, life concentrates: feathers, shed skins, the polished bones of tiny creatures arranged almost ritually—not by hands, but by wind, gravity, and the compulsive circling of scavenger-flies that hum in low, resonant drones.

Darkness dominates. Even at midday, the sun is a pale coin behind veils of high cloud or the shadow of neighboring peaks. Light doesn’t flood here; it filters, fractures, bleeds through narrow canyons in slanted shafts that vanish in minutes. What illumination exists is diffuse, silvered, like the glow inside a geode. Yet the place is loud—surprisingly so. Not with wind (the air is often still), but with sound generated by life itself: the chittering chorus of roosting cliff-bats whose echolocation clicks harmonize into eerie chords; the resonant thrum of wing-membranes vibrating in thermal updrafts; the low, rhythmic pulse—almost below hearing—that seems to come from the rocks themselves, as if the stone remembers the pressure that formed it.

This landscape is small in extent—a single high-altitude caldera rim, perhaps half a kilometer across—but dense beyond measure. Every surface is occupied. Every shadow harbors motion. Even the air feels thick with presences: pheromonal trails, airborne spores, the electric buzz of proximity between creatures that have never learned solitude. You do not walk through this place; you move through its breath.

And it is strange. Not grotesque, not fantastical—but *other*. The moths have eyes on their wings. The salamanders blink with membranes that close sideways. The rocks… sometimes… resonate when a certain insect lands on them, emitting notes that correspond to no known scale. There’s no malice here, no welcome—just a world operating under rules not yet translated into human understanding. Time moves slowly; nothing rushes. Seasons shift over decades. A landslide might take weeks to settle. But life—frenetic, overwhelming life—fills every crevice with urgency, as if aware of how brief even eternity is at this altitude.

To stand here is to feel observed—not by gods, but by the collective attention of ten thousand small eyes, each adapted to low light, each tuned to the slightest tremor in the air. You are not alone. You were never alone. And the mountain, young and sharp and singing in the dark, watches too—quietly—waiting to see if you understand the language of damp stone and clicking wings.
