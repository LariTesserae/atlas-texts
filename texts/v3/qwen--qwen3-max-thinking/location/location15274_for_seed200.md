This is a place where the world is still wet behind the ears.

The air hangs heavy and warm—not the dry, searing heat of a desert, but the thick, breathless warmth of steam rising from living earth. At 3.0 on the temperature scale, it is unrelentingly hot, but softened by the constant presence of moisture. Water is everywhere, though rarely pooled in lakes or rivers. At 1.5, it manifests not as abundance but as proximity: the ground squelches underfoot not because it’s flooded, but because the water table lies mere inches below the surface. After each brief, drenching downpour—more mist than storm, though it arrives with the weight of a monsoon—the land glistens, and every leaf, every stone, weeps droplets for hours after. Hollows fill with shallow mirrors that vanish by midday, leaving behind only the scent of damp loam and the memory of ripples.

Elevation is low—barely above the theoretical sea level, if sea existed nearby. This is a basin, or perhaps the broad, flat lap of a continent still settling. And yet, astonishingly, the land feels new. With erosion rated at a mere 0.1, this terrain gives the impression of having just coalesced from vapor and sediment. There are no canyons, no worn paths, no smoothed stones. Instead, the ground is raw: soft clay still sculpting itself, mounds of silt that shift subtly with each passing root or burrowing creature, outcrops of pale, chalky rock that haven’t yet learned how to crumble. It is geology in its first breath—uncarved, unweathered, almost embryonic, as if the planet exhaled and this place condensed in the exhale.

Despite this newness, life has rushed in with fervor. Vegetation at 2.2 is lush but not chaotic—dense thickets of broad-leafed shrubs, waist-high ferns uncurled like waiting hands, vines that climb not trees (there are no tall trees yet) but each other, forming living trellises of interwoven green. The flora is opportunistic, fast-growing, almost experimental: some leaves shimmer with iridescent veins, others bead with sticky resin that catches the dim light. Mosses, usually pioneers in cooler climes, here thrive in the constant humidity, carpeting every surface in velvety emerald, even vertical faces of newborn rock.

And the fauna—oh, the fauna is overwhelming at 2.8. Not in size, but in presence. The air thrums with wings: iridescent flies the size of thumbnails, hover-moths with feathery antennae, dragonflies that stitch the sky with electric-blue threads. The ground pulses with movement—segmented things in chitin and mucus, skittering beetles with shells like polished obsidian, legless lizards that glide through leaf litter like liquid. In the shallow pools, translucent tadpoles swarm in synchronized clouds, their nervous systems visible as faint pulses of silver. Birds are few, but their calls are constant—a low, resonant cooing that seems to emanate from the mist itself. There are no predators here, or at least none visible; the fauna exists in a state of exuberant proliferation, unchecked, unpruned.

Sound, at 1.7, is loud but not sharp. It is a layered hum: the drone of insects layered over the drip-drip from saturated leaves, the gurgle of hidden seepage, the wet rustle of something moving just out of sight. It does not assault the ears so much as fill them—a sonic fog that matches the visual saturation.

Light, at 0.9, is dim not because it’s night, but because the sky is perpetually veiled. A high, milky overcast filters the sun into a diffuse glow that casts no hard shadows. The world exists in perpetual late afternoon, golden but muted, as if viewed through old amber glass. What light does reach the ground is further diffused by the dense foliage above, creating a cathedral-like hush to the illumination—dappled, but without contrast.

The scale is regional—1.8—not intimate, not vast. You can stand in one place and see the curve of a distant hummock, the shimmer of a far-off wetland patch, the slow drift of mist across a half-mile of undulating ground. It is a valley, perhaps, or a coastal plain that never met the sea. The density (2.3) ensures that nothing feels empty: every cubic meter hums with life, moisture, and organic matter. Air is thick with spores, pollen, the faint metallic tang of evaporating minerals.

And yet, for all this teeming, the place is stable—nearly frozen in time. Dynamic at 0.1 means no wind to bend the reeds, no tides to shift the water, no seasonal turn. The heat is constant. The rains come not in cycles but as punctuation—brief, intense, and then gone, leaving the world unchanged except for the temporary gleam. This is equilibrium in its purest form: not dead, but complete. Everything is exactly as it should be, and has been for longer than memory. It feels ancient, yet also newborn—because nothing has ever needed to change.

Weirdness lingers at 0.9—not grotesque, not alien, but quietly uncanny. The plants grow in spirals that defy phyllotaxis. Some fungi glow faintly at dusk, not with phosphorescence but with a soft bioluminescence that pulses in time with underground water flow. The air sometimes smells of ozone after a rain, though there is no lightning. And if you stand very still, you might notice that the dripping water doesn’t always fall down—sometimes it creeps sideways along a leaf’s edge, as if testing gravity rather than obeying it.

There are no structures. No tools. No sign that anything with hands has ever passed through. This place exists outside of making. It is pure becoming—still wet, still breathing, still deciding what kind of world it wants to be, even as it remains perfectly, eerily still.
