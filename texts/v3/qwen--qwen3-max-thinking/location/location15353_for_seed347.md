This is a drowned city—though not drowned in the way one might imagine. It is not sunken beneath an ocean, nor flooded by a sudden deluge, but rather reclaimed slowly, patiently, by a cold, wet world that refuses to dry. The land lies below surface—not in a chasm or cave, but in a subtle, sunken basin, a shallow bowl carved by glaciers long ago and never quite lifted again. Elevation reads 0.2/3: it is low, yes, but not subterranean—just low enough that water gathers here like breath on a mirror.

The cold (0.4/3) is pervasive but not cruel—a damp, bone-deep chill that lingers in the marrow rather than bites. Frost rarely forms, for the air is too moist, but every surface glistens with condensation. The sky hangs low, grey-white, neither stormy nor clear, casting a flat, even light (1.9/3)—the kind that erases sharp shadows and softens edges into suggestion. It is never truly dark, never truly bright.

Water (2.5/3) is everywhere. Not in roaring rivers or crashing waves, but as seepage, as saturation. Puddles do not drain—they swell into shallow lenses that mirror the sky and the skeletal remains of buildings. Pavements, once proud and straight, now tilt and buckle under the pressure of roots and frost heave. From cracks in asphalt, water wells upward, not from below, but as if the ground itself is sweating. Along the bases of crumbling brick walls, moisture beads and drips in slow, irregular rhythms—weeping rocks made of concrete and mortar. The air smells of wet stone, mildew, and the faint iron tang of rusting rebar.

Vegetation (2.2/3) is lush not in the tropical sense, but in the tenacious, cold-adapted way of mosses, liverworts, sedges, and stunted willows. Every surface is claimed. Brickwork vanishes under velvet carpets of moss; chain-link fences sag under the weight of ivy gone feral. Reed beds sprout in what were once parking lots; saplings push through floorboards in abandoned apartments. There is no bare ground—only layers of organic occupation. This is density (3.0/3) made manifest: not human crowding, but ecological saturation. Life does not merely fill the space—it overflows it, presses into every crevice, knits the ruins together with green thread.

The built environment (2.3/3) is unmistakably urban—a town, perhaps once provincial, now midsize in ruin. Not a village, not a metropolis, but a place with gridded streets, a main square, a defunct train station with tracks now submerged in sedge. Buildings remain standing not through maintenance but inertia—gravity has not yet won. Roofs sag; windows are blind with grime or shattered inward. Some structures are half-collapsed, their innards spilling beams and insulation like entrails. Yet there is order beneath the decay: street signs tilt at angles but still bear names; manhole covers sit askew, exhaling mist. The urban fabric persists, warped but legible.

Technology (0.1/3) is absent. No wires hum, no screens flicker. Any remnants—rusted bicycles, shattered glass, porcelain sinks—are inert, reabsorbed into the mineral cycle. Tools have long since corroded or been buried. This is a post-tool landscape, not because tools were forbidden, but because they were abandoned, and nature, cold and wet and patient, has dissolved their purpose.

Fauna (2.0/3) abounds quietly. Waterfowl—teal, coots, herons—wade through flooded avenues. Frogs croak in basement voids. Shrews and voles tunnel through insulation and leaf litter. Dragonflies hover above stagnant pools. Nothing is loud; nothing is showy. Predation happens in silence: a heron’s strike, a weasel in the reeds. The sound level (0.3/3) is dominated by absence—the near-silence of a place where wind is muffled by wet air and thick growth. What sound exists is dampened: the plink of droplets, the sigh of water shifting underfoot, the rustle of reeds.

Weirdness (0.4/3) is minimal. This is not a dream or a curse—it is simply what happens when a temperate, low-lying town is left alone in a cold, wet climate for decades. The erosion parameter (1.2/3) reflects this: the landscape is young in its current form. The ruins are fresh enough that you can still read street names, recognize room functions, find a child’s shoe half-buried in silt. The processes of decay have only just begun to assert themselves—sharp edges remain on broken concrete, steel beams still hold their shape, though rust blooms like bloodstain.

The scale (2.2/3) stretches beyond the immediate. From a broken clock tower—the only intact vertical— you can see across a valley of roofs swallowed by reeds, to distant ridges where the land rises just enough to stay dry. The drowned town is part of a broader wetland system: marshes to the east, a slow-moving river to the west, forests ringing the basin like sentinels. It is regional, not isolated.

And yet, for all its activity (dynamic: 1.8/3)—the slow creep of roots, the daily rise and fall of groundwater, the seasonal flush of bird migrations—the place feels suspended. Time moves, but softly. Nothing rushes. The cold slows decay; the water preserves even as it dissolves. There is rhythm, but no urgency.

This is a city in the act of becoming soil again—not with violence, but with quiet insistence. It does not mourn its former life. It is too busy breathing.
