High. So high that the air itself feels like a held breath—thin, brittle, and ancient. This is not a mountain as climbers know it; it is the roof of a world grown still. Elevation dominates all else: jagged, wind-carved spines of black basalt and pale quartz rise like the ribs of some fossilized leviathan, arcing across a plateau so vast it curves with the planet’s own skeleton. At 2.9/3, elevation isn’t just height—it’s condition. It is the reason everything else here is starved, sparse, suspended.

Cold drapes the land like a second sky. At 0.4/3, it is not the sharp bite of polar ice but a deep, marrow-cold—persistent, pervasive, older than memory. Frost forms not as snow but as hoarfrost crystals that spiderweb across stone overnight, glittering for an hour at dawn before sublimating into the glassy air. There are no seasons here, only gradations of stillness. Time doesn’t pass so much as accumulate in layers of frost and silence.

And yet—water exists. At 1.3/3, it appears not as rivers or lakes but as phenomena of condensation and accident. In sheltered hollows beneath overhangs of wind-smoothed gneiss, ice lenses form during the brief twilight hours when the air cools just enough for vapor to cling and crystallize. These melt not into streams but into damp, dark stains that seep into porous rock and vanish within minutes, leaving behind only a ghost of moisture and the faint mineral scent of wet stone. Elsewhere, meltwater from high cirques trickles in seasonal pulses—ephemeral rivulets that carve no channels, for the ground is too frozen, too ancient, too resigned. Water is a rumor here, whispered in dew and vanished before it can be named.

Vegetation at 0.8/3 is less life than suggestion. Patches of lichen—gray-green, crustose, clinging like memory to the southern faces of boulders—mark where the faintest breath of humidity lingers. A few stunted cushion plants huddle in micro-craters, their leaves reduced to nubs armored in wax, roots fused to stone. Nothing grows taller than a finger’s width. There are no trees, no grasses, no flowers—only the patient, mineral patience of organisms that metabolize time in centuries.

This place is empty. Density at 0.3/3 means that between one outcrop and the next, there may be nothing for a kilometer but wind-scoured gravel and sky. The emptiness isn’t desolation—it’s spaciousness so profound it feels sacred. You could shout and hear your voice return not as echo but as absence. Structures, when they appear (built: 0.7/3), are almost hallucinatory: a half-buried stone cairn, its purpose forgotten before the first glacier retreated; a single timber post driven into permafrost, blackened by age, leaning at an impossible angle as if the earth shifted beneath it and never settled again. No roofs, no walls—just fragments, as if the idea of shelter once passed through and left only a shadow.

There is no tech. None. Not even the ghost of a wire or the glint of metal. This world runs on geology and atmosphere alone. The only tools are frost wedges and wind abrasion. Life—if it can be called that—uses no implements, builds nothing, leaves no trace beyond biochemical footprints invisible to the eye.

Light at 0.8/3 is dim not from night but from altitude and atmosphere. The sun is fierce but distant, filtered through high, thin clouds that never precipitate—cirrus veils that scatter light into a diffuse, directionless glow. Shadows are soft, edges blurred, as if the world is seen through frosted glass. At noon, the sky is the color of tarnished silver; at “dawn” and “dusk” (though the sun arcs low and constant), it bleeds into bruised purples and cold blues that never warm.

Fauna is rare (1.3/3), appearing only as signs: a single set of hare-like tracks pressed into a patch of fine silt, already half-erased by wind; the desiccated carcass of a high-altitude moth pinned to a rock by ice; once, far off, the distant cry of something avian—high, thin, and broken—cutting through the air like a shard of glass. Nothing lingers. Nothing nests.

But the sound—oh, the sound is loud (2.2/3). Not with life, but with the voice of the place itself. The wind doesn’t howl; it resonates. It pours through stone flutes carved by millennia of erosion, creating bass hums that vibrate in your chest. Ice cracks with the report of rifle shots, echoing for minutes across the basin. Distant rockfalls, triggered by thermal stress, rumble like slow thunder. And beneath it all, a constant, low-frequency drone—the harmonic of a frozen aquifer shifting kilometers below, or perhaps the tectonic sigh of a continent holding its breath. The silence between sounds is so total that when noise comes, it feels violent, necessary, alive.

This is where weirdness blooms (2.6/3). Not fantasy, but fracture. Reality here is thin. At certain angles, at certain hours, distances collapse: a rock formation a kilometer away appears close enough to touch, its texture unnervingly sharp. At other times, the ground beneath your feet seems to flicker—not like heat haze, but like a film reel skipping frames. Gravity feels inconsistent; a dropped pebble may fall straight, or drift sideways for a heartbeat before correcting. And sometimes, looking down a glacial trough at twilight, you see a second version of yourself walking ahead—just out of sync, just out of focus—before it dissolves into the gloom. These are not illusions. The place remembers other laws.

Yet for all its strangeness, the landscape is in equilibrium (dynamic: 0.3/3). Nothing changes—not truly. Erosion (1.6/3) has long since found its rhythm: the same grains of sand abrade the same grooves every century; the same icicles form in the same cracks each cold cycle; the same wind patterns scour the same hollows. This is not stagnation but completion. The land has achieved a state of perfect wear. It is old beyond reckoning, and it has answered every question time could ask. Now it simply *is*.

And it watches. Not with eyes, but with the weight of its stillness. You are an anomaly here—a warm, wet, noisy thing in a world of frozen stone and resonant air. But it does not reject you. It observes, as a glacier observes a fly: with infinite patience, and no expectation that you will remain.
