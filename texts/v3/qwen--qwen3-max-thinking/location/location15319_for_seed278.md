The air here is a held breath—thin, cold, and still. It clings to the skin like damp silk drawn from a riverbed long dry, yet somehow still exhaling mist. This place exists at the edge of what weather remembers: high enough that the sky bruises into violet even at midday, cold enough that frost etches its script nightly onto every surface it can touch. Not snow—not often—but rime, hoarfrost, condensation that freezes before it can fall. Water is not absent, only reticent: it lingers in cracks, pools in shallow basins carved by time rather than flood, seeps through porous stone like a secret. You’ll find it cupped in the hollows of tilted slabs, or suspended as fog that clings to the lee sides of ridges at dawn, vanishing by noon into the pale gold light that barely qualifies as daylight.

Vegetation is a rumor. Lichens crust the rocks in grayscale mosaics—silver, ash, the dull green of old copper. Mosses huddle in micro-refuges where two stones lean together, creating a pocket deep enough to trap moisture and shadow. No trees. No shrubs. Only the occasional wiry stalk of some hardy sedge, brown and brittle, trembling in the rarest breeze. Life here is measured in decades per centimeter.

The land itself is ancient. Erosion hasn’t stripped it—it has *refined* it. Over millennia, wind and ice have sanded down jagged peaks into undulating plateaus, mesas with softened shoulders, valleys that slope with the patience of glaciers. The rock is mostly basalt and shale, layered like pages in a book left open too long. Fissures run deep, some wide enough to swallow a person, others hairline fractures that exhale cold air even in the warmest hour of the year. This is a landscape that has ceased striving. It does not grow; it endures. It has found equilibrium with time.

And yet—scale swells beyond comprehension. You stand on a plain that stretches until the curvature of the earth swallows the horizon. There are no landmarks to break the monotony: no forests, no rivers wide enough to gleam, no mountains sharp against the sky. Only subtle rises, gentle swells of stone, and vast, open basins filled with gravel and dust that shift imperceptibly over centuries. The air is so clear, so still, that distance distorts. A ridge five leagues away seems within walking distance; a bird circling overhead might as well be a ghost.

But the fauna—ah, the fauna is overwhelming. Not in number at any single moment, but in presence. They are everywhere, if you learn to see. Great sky-whales—leathery, buoyant things the size of hills—drift in the upper atmosphere, tethered to nothing, riding thermal strata invisible to human eyes. They pulse slowly, their bodies filtering microscopic organisms from the thin air, exhaling mist that sometimes condenses into the region’s only clouds. Below, on the ground, herds of lithovores move like slow tectonic flows: six-legged, carapaced creatures that graze not on plants but on mineral crusts, their mouths grinding rock into fine powder they excrete as fertile dust. They travel in lines so long they take days to pass a single point, their footfalls silent on the stone.

Smaller things skitter in the interstices: crystalline insects that hum at frequencies just below hearing, their wings shedding prismatic dust that settles into the frost and glints in the dim light. In the water pockets, translucent eels coil, blind and ancient, feeding on chemosynthetic microbes that thrive in the cold, iron-rich seeps. Their movements are so slow they seem carved from glass—yet they are alive, and everywhere.

And then—the hum. Not sound, exactly. More a pressure behind the eyes. A vibration in the marrow. This is the industrial layer, the paradox woven into the wild: buried deep beneath the crust, machines older than memory turn. Not human machines. Something else. Vast geothermal engines, perhaps, or resonant crystalline lattices that transduce planetary heat into low-frequency oscillations. They power nothing visible. They serve no obvious function. But they are *there*—their rhythm syncing with the slow pulse of the lithovores, their heat bleeding upward to keep the subterranean aquifers from freezing solid. This is industry not as construction, but as geology. Not built, but grown. Not imposed, but emergent.

Light is dim not because of clouds, but because the sun is distant here—either by latitude or by some atmospheric filtering you can’t name. Days are long, but never bright. Shadows stretch like taffy, soft-edged and indistinct. At night, auroras sometimes ripple across the sky, not green but deep cobalt and rust-red, casting the land in spectral hues that make the lichens glow faintly phosphorescent.

Sound is nearly absent. No wind to speak of. No rustling leaves. The fauna move with such deliberation they make no noise. Even the sky-whales glide in silence, their passage marked only by the slow distortion of air around them. What you hear, if you listen for hours, is the occasional *tick* of cooling stone, or the almost-inaudible sigh of frost sublimating at dawn. Silence is not emptiness here—it is a texture, a presence.

Change is glacial. Seasons pass in increments too fine to mark without instruments. A lichen patch expands a millimeter per year. A fissure widens by a hair’s breadth each century. The lithovores migrate not annually, but over decadal cycles, guided by shifts in magnetic fields or mineral concentrations too subtle for science to grasp. Time here is not a river but a glacier—massive, patient, carving its will through persistence rather than force.

And the weirdness? It’s not flamboyant. No floating islands, no impossible geometries. The strangeness lies in the quiet harmony of contradictions: industrial yet untouched, teeming yet sparse, vast yet intimate in its details. It feels less like a place and more like a condition—a state of being that the planet settled into long ago and has no intention of leaving. You do not visit this landscape. You are permitted to witness it, briefly, if you are still enough to not disturb its equilibrium.

Stand here long enough, and you begin to feel your own thoughts slowing. Your breath syncs with the pulse of the deep machines. Your eyes adjust to the dimness until you see the world not in light, but in gradients of cold. And when you finally leave—if you do—you carry with you not memories, but a silence that hums.
