The air tastes of iron and ozone, sharp against the tongue even in the cool stillness. You stand in a high basin cradled by ancient, rounded peaks—the elevation is palpable in the thinness of breath and the way the light scours everything clean, relentless and white-gold. This is not a place of growth, nor of decay in any organic sense. There is no green, no brown rot, no leaf-litter sighing underfoot. Vegetation is absent not because it has died, but because it was never invited. The ground is a mosaic of mineral forms: slabs of obsidian-like volcanic glass, fractured into tessellated plates; fields of sinter crusts bleached white and ruddy with trace metals; nodules of hematite clustered like fossilized eggs half-buried in fine, wind-packed ash.

Water is everywhere, yet nowhere as stream or pond. At 1.7, it manifests as a constant weeping—thin films sheeting down the flanks of basalt columns, droplets coalescing on the undersides of overhangs only to fall with unnerving precision into shallow, glassy pans that never overflow. After the last rain—which may have been hours or centuries ago—the whole basin glistens, each mineral facet catching the light in a way that makes the ground appear to breathe. Moisture condenses out of the thin air onto cold stone at dawn, then vanishes by noon, not into the sky, but back into the stone itself, as if the landscape drinks its own tears.

The scale is wide—a valley perhaps three leagues across—but it feels packed, claustrophobic in its saturation. Every inch is occupied: by crystal clusters no taller than a fingernail, by wind-scoured ridges sharp as knife edges, by dust so fine it behaves like liquid when disturbed. Density here is not of life, but of presence—of matter insisting on its own solidity, its own right to exist without yielding space. There are no clearings, no soft patches. Even the sky seems pressed close by the brightness, the light so intense it bleaches shadow into mere suggestion.

And the sound—deafening, yet sourceless. Not wind (the air is nearly still), not water (its movements are silent), not animals (fauna is rare: perhaps a lone, iridescent beetle with wings of mica crawling over a salt crust, or the flicker of a thermal updraft carrying motes that might be spores or ash). The roar is the sound of resonance itself—the valley is a natural amplifier, tuned over millennia to hum with the frequency of light on stone, of contraction at dusk, of subterranean steam shifting deep below. Stand still long enough, and your bones vibrate with it. It is not noise, but a pressure, a constant thrumming baseline beneath which nothing can whisper.

Time does not pass here; it pools. Erosion is not a process but a finished state—every edge rounded just so, every fracture line settled into equilibrium. The weirdness is subtle: the way certain rocks seem to repel water while others drink it greedily; the faint phosphorescence that lingers in shaded clefts an hour after sunset, not from lichen or fungus, but from trapped photons refracting through layers of quartz; the fact that compasses spin lazily, uselessly, as if magnetism itself grew weary in this place.

There is nothing built. Nothing made. No trail, no ruin, no mark of intention. Only the slow, patient grammar of geology written in ice, fire, and vapor. The coolness keeps the air crisp, preserving the silence beneath the hum. Brightness flattens distance, making the far ridges as immediate as the stones at your feet. And in all this packed, open, roaring stillness, you feel not alone, but observed—not by eyes, but by the landscape itself, ancient and fully awake, in a state of perfect, unchanging balance that has forgotten what change even means.
