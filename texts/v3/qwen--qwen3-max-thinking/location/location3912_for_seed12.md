The world here is not drowned—it is *made* of water. Not the ocean, not a flood, but something deeper, stranger: a skyless expanse of liquid held in suspension, not by gravity alone but by an unseen architecture of pressure, memory, and time. The water is not pure—it carries the weight of millennia in its molecules, shimmering with dissolved minerals that pulse faintly, like slow breaths. It is 2.9 out of 3: not total submersion in the sense of ruin, but total submersion as condition of existence. There is no “above.” Only within.

You stand—or float—on what passes for land: a vast, porous lattice of calcified coral-growth and congealed silt, rising in undulating towers and plateaus from the abyss below. This is elevation at 2.4: not mountainous, but *high* in this liquid realm—plateaus cresting near the upper thermocline, where warmth lingers. The air (if it can be called that) is gone; you breathe through gills stitched into your neck by rites older than language, or perhaps you never had lungs at all. The warmth (2.5) is gentle, like blood just cooled—comforting, but not tropical. It carries no steam, no humidity, because there is no evaporation. Only conduction, slow and even, through the endless medium.

Vegetation is 1.8: lush, but not in the way of forests. Here, life grows in filaments and fans—bioluminescent kelp that pulses like sleeping hearts, colonies of glass-thin reeds that chime when brushed by current, and bulbous, translucent sacs filled with symbiotic algae that drift like slow lanterns. They cling to the emergent structures, painting them in shifting hues of jade and violet. Nothing is rooted in soil—there is no soil—but in the raw, embryonic geology (erosion 0.4) of this place: a crust of accreted minerals, still soft, still shaping itself. The land is *becoming*. Cracks widen not by storm or quake, but by the silent push of crystalline growth from within. New ridges swell overnight like bruises healing into bone.

Scale is 1.7—open, but not infinite. You can see for leagues through the clear, dense water, but the horizon curves into a blue so deep it swallows light. The world feels regional: a drowned basin hundreds of miles across, bounded by thermal walls and pressure gradients you sense more than see. Within it, the built environment (1.6) rises in clusters—settlements not of stone or steel, but of woven bone, resin-sealed coral, and compressed mycelial mats. These are towns, yes—organized, layered—but grown, not built. Towers spiral upward like nautilus shells, their interiors honeycombed with chambers where people move with slow, deliberate grace. Bridges of braided tendon-silk span chasms between plateaus. Everything is porous, everything breathes with the water.

Density (1.6) is high, but not crowded. It is the density of a rainforest canopy viewed from within—layers upon layers of presence: drifting spores, nesting filter-feeders, curtains of bacterial film, the slow drift of settlement-smoke (a phosphorescent mist, not combustion). You are never alone, yet never jostled. Life is abundant in form, but sparse in individual presence—fauna is rare (1.2). What animals exist are shy, gelatinous, or semi-sentient: schools of silver eels that move as one mind, blind crustaceans with shells like carved obsidian, and the great float-whales—dormant for decades, tethered to the seabed by root-like tendrils, dreaming in harmonic frequencies.

Technology is primitive (0.6): no engines, no wires. Tools are carved from fossilized coral, sharpened with volcanic glass, bound with sinew and resin. Water is moved through aqueducts of hollowed bone, light is captured in living lenses of jellyfish tissue, and communication is done through resonant drums that send pulses through the water—felt in the chest before they’re heard. Knowledge is etched onto mica sheets and stored in drying caves (pockets of trapped gas, rare and sacred). Metal is unknown; what glints in the dimness is polished shell or pyrite.

Light (1.1) is dim, but never dark. The primary source is biological: the vegetation, the fauna, the very walls of the settlements emit a soft, variable glow—amber, teal, faint rose. There is no sun, no moon. Some say a star once fell into this world and dissolved, seeding the water with luminescence. Others say the light comes from the pressure of thought itself, pooled over eons. Shadows are thick, velvety, and slow to shift.

Weirdness (1.8) is not spectacle—it is texture. Time moves differently here. A day is measured by the slow bloom and retreat of the luminous algae tide. Children are born encased in gelatinous cocoons that harden into personal architecture—homes that grow with them. Some rocks sing when touched in certain sequences. The water remembers voices; if you whisper a name often enough, currents will carry it back to you years later, slightly changed.

Sound (1.3) is mostly quiet, but never silent. There is the low thrum of hydrothermal vents far below, the creak of growing coral, the sigh of water through porous stone. The people speak rarely, and when they do, their words are subsonic—felt as vibration in the jawbone. Silence is not absence, but a form of listening.

And everything is slow (dynamic 1.1). Seasons are measured in centuries. A bridge may take a lifetime to grow. A single thought in the mind of a float-whale might last three human generations. Change is not resisted—it is metabolized. This world does not rush toward form; it *steeps* in possibility.

You are not visiting. You are being absorbed. The water knows your shape now. It is beginning to grow around you.
