Beneath the surface of the world—yet not truly subterranean—lies the Hollow Basin of Lir, a vast, sunken plain cupped within the oldest bones of the earth. It is not a cave, nor a trench, but a shallow depression worn smooth over eons, like the inside of a colossal river stone split open and laid flat across hundreds of square leagues. The elevation here is just low enough that the sky feels higher, stretched taut above a land that has slumped into repose, its edges blurred by time into gentle, undulating rims of weathered stone.

Water is a ghost here—never absent, never present. At 0.8 on the scale, it manifests not as rivers or lakes, but as traces: beads of condensation that weep from cold, glassy veins in the rock at dawn; shallow seeps that glisten for an hour after rare rains before vanishing into porous strata; damp hollows beneath overhangs where air thickens into mist by midday. The people of Lir call these the “breathing places,” and they collect the moisture in shallow basins carved from volcanic tuff, lined with sheets of hammered copper too thin to ring.

There is no vegetation. Not a root, not a lichen. At 0.3, the land is mineral through and through. Yet the ground is not barren—it is *occupied*. Across the basin, strange formations rise like fossilized breath: clusters of sinter towers, milky and porous, built not by organisms but by slow mineral accretion around thermal vents long cooled. Elsewhere, fields of obsidian shards lie embedded in pale clay, their edges softened by time but still catching the light like scattered knives. In sheltered crannies, delicate crusts of crystalline salts bloom in fractal patterns—evaporites left behind by vanished brines. This is a post-life ecology: not dead, but never alive in the green sense. It is a world that remembers water but not photosynthesis.

The temperature hovers in perpetual coolness (1.3)—never cold enough for frost, never warm enough for sweat. The air carries the scent of wet stone and ozone, faint and clean. Brightness (2.6) floods the basin from above: the sky is wide and cloudless most days, and because the land lies slightly below the surrounding rim, sunlight pours in at sharp angles, casting long, crisp shadows that slide across the plain like slow hands. Mirrors of polished mica, set deliberately by the inhabitants, catch and redirect this light into the deeper hollows, turning shaded alcoves into chambers of liquid gold.

And there *are* inhabitants. The built environment pulses at 2.2—urban in scale, though unlike any city above. Lir is a settlement of layers, not towers. Its people build downward and outward, not up. Dwellings are sunken courtyards ringed with low walls of fused ash-brick, their roofs flat and open to the sky or covered with stretched membranes of cured bladder-skin that diffuse light into soft glows. Pathways—worn smooth by bare feet and dragging sleds—crisscross the basin in radial patterns, converging on communal cisterns, forge pits, and the great Listening Stones. There is infrastructure: aqueducts of hollowed basalt channel dewfall; wind-sails of woven reed (imported from distant margins) catch faint breezes to ventilate underground store-rooms; terraces of stacked slate serve as sleeping platforms, angled to catch morning light. Yet the tech is primitive (0.7): no gears, no engines, no smelted iron. Tools are bone, stone, copper, and sinew. Water is lifted by counterweighted buckets. Light is bent, not generated.

Fauna abounds (1.5), though it is not what one expects. There are no birds, no mammals, no insects with chitin or wings. Instead, the basin teems with *creatures of resonance*. Gelatinous, translucent forms—like living lenses—drift just above the ground, drawn to vibrations in the stone. They feed on harmonic tremors: the ring of a struck anvil, the hum of wind through a flute-shaped vent, the footfall of a child running across a resonant slab. Others are segmented, worm-like entities made of interlocking mineral plates that flex and click as they move, harvesting trace ions from damp rock. They are harmless, curious, and abundant—nesting in crevices, pulsing gently in the light. The people of Lir do not hunt them; they *tune* them, placing singing stones near their burrows to encourage growth. These beings are the fauna of a world that skipped flesh and went straight to frequency.

Weirdness is subtle (1.1). Nothing defies physics—yet everything feels *askew*. Time moves differently near the sinter fields; voices echo backward for a split second; shadows sometimes detach and linger after the object has moved. But these are accepted as natural quirks, like the way certain rocks taste of rain even in drought. The true strangeness is in the silence (0.2). Despite the abundance of life and the hum of activity, the basin is profoundly quiet. Sound does not carry far; it is absorbed by the porous ground, the salt crusts, the very air. Conversations are hushed. Tools are padded. Even the fauna move without rustle or cry. What noise exists is intentional: the chime of a copper bell marking midday, the whisper of a prayer sung into a resonant chamber, the soft scrape of a stone blade on bone.

Yet beneath this quiet lies turbulence (2.8). The dynamic energy is not visible—it is *geologic*, *social*, *ritual*. The ground trembles with micro-quakes as ancient faults sigh. The people shift their dwellings seasonally, dismantling one quarter of the settlement while rebuilding another, in a slow, ceaseless dance of renewal. New Listening Stones are erected weekly, tuned to different frequencies, altering the basin’s acoustic map. Children are taught to “read” the land through vibration, pressing their palms to the earth to sense approaching storms or the birth of a new sinter spire. This is a place in constant, quiet upheaval—a bazaar of subtle change, where stability is an illusion layered over deep, churning time.

The Hollow Basin of Lir is ancient (erosion 2.6), worn to its final shape, yet never still. It is sparse (0.7)—vast stretches of empty plain between clusters of life—but never lonely. It is open (1.9), visible for miles in every direction, yet intimate in its hush. Here, in the cool, bright dampness beneath the rim of the world, a society thrives not on growth, but on attunement—listening to a land that has forgotten green, but remembers everything else.
