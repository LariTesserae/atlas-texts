The world here is not drowned—it *is* drowning, perpetually, in an ocean older than memory. Not the ocean of waves and tides, but of pressure, of slow collapse, of liquid so dense and cold it forgets it was ever vapor. This is a place submerged not just below sea level but below time, in the crushing embrace of a post-flood epoch where the surface is a myth whispered by thermal vents. Water isn’t just present—it *is* the medium, the matrix, the only possible state of being. It fills every interstice, presses against every surface with the weight of a dead star, and flows not as rivers but as vast, slow currents that fold through submerged canyons like breath through lungs long gone still.

The temperature hovers just above freezing—0.5 on the scale—but feels colder, leaching warmth not in gusts but in steady, patient siphons. Your skin would numb in minutes, but there is no skin here, no one to feel it. Only the liquid itself, thick with dissolved minerals leached from primordial rock over billions of years, tasting of iron, sulfide, and the ghost of ancient salts. It is not freshwater, nor saltwater as we know it, but something in between: a brine so saturated it verges on gel, sluggish in its movements yet capable of sudden, violent surges.

Elevation is negative in every sense. You are deep—deep enough that the sky is not just absent but conceptually impossible. The land, if it can be called that, is not soil but sediment compressed into a brittle, glassy shale over eons. Erosion has done its final work: mountains have been planed flat, continents ground to powder and redeposited in featureless plains that stretch for leagues under the gloom. What remains are low undulations—hills less than a meter tall, worn smooth as bone, radiating from ancient impact craters or the collapsed calderas of drowned volcanoes. This is the oldest stable state imaginable: not pristine wilderness, but the terminus of all geological storytelling. Nothing rises here. Nothing is born. Everything has already been settled into its final form, then buried, then compacted again.

And yet—paradoxically—the place feels *packed*. Density is near-total. Not with life, nor with matter in the usual sense, but with presence. The water itself is thick with suspended silts, microbial mats, and colonies of extremophiles clinging to hydrothermal chimneys that spew black, mineral-rich plumes. These chimneys, the only vertical structures for hundreds of kilometers, rise like cathedral spires from the abyssal plain, built not by hands but by chemistry: iron sulfides precipitating in fractal towers, brittle as chalk, humming with thermal energy. Around them, the water shimmers with particulate life—ghostly clouds of archaea feeding on chemical gradients, invisible to the eye but saturating every cubic meter. There is no empty space. Even the voids between particles feel occupied by pressure, by cold, by the memory of collapse.

Vegetation is nearly absent. At 0.7, it exists only as faint traces: pale, rootless fronds of chemosynthetic kelp anchored to warm fissures, their fronds translucent and veinless, swaying without rhythm. They are not green but a dull ochre, leaching energy not from light but from the slow oxidation of manganese nodules scattered like fossils across the plain. These nodules—smooth, black, dense—are the only “plants” that endure, inert yet biologically intimate, hosting biofilms that blink in and out of metabolic activity over centuries.

Fauna is rare (1.3), and when it appears, it moves like a rumor. A blind amphipod, translucent and jointed, skitters over a nodule. A gelatinous worm, meters long but thinner than thread, pulses through a current, trailing cilia that harvest microbes. Once, perhaps, a larger shape—a pale, eyeless fish with gills like lace—drifts past, mouth agape, filtering the soup of life. It does not hunt. It does not flee. It simply *passes*, a slow ghost in a world that has forgotten predation.

Light is almost gone (0.4). What little penetrates from the surface—miles above, if “above” even means anything—is diffuse blue attenuation, long since extinguished. Here, illumination comes only from chemiluminescence: faint glows along bacterial colonies, the occasional flash from a startled crustacean, the dull red radiance of hydrothermal vents. Most of the time, it is absolute dark—a darkness that is not empty but *full*, thick with unseen motion and pressure.

Sound is quiet (0.4), but not silent. The dominant note is the low, resonant hum of tectonic stress—a basso continuo vibrating through the crust. Above it, the crackle of mineral deposition, the hiss of superheated water meeting cold brine, the almost imperceptible sigh of gas bubbles collapsing in the deep. No birds. No wind. Only the ocean’s internal monologue, spoken in frequencies too low for ears, felt instead in the bones of the world.

And yet—despite the cold, the darkness, the ancient stillness—this place is *turbulent* (2.8). Not in the way of storms, but in the way of systems pushed to their thermodynamic limits. Currents shift without warning, dragging sediment in sudden avalanches down micro-slopes. Chimneys grow and collapse in days, their fall triggering chain reactions of chemical rebalancing. Microbial blooms erupt and die in hours, altering local pH and oxygen levels with the violence of revolutions. It is a place in constant, chaotic negotiation with equilibrium—forever tipping, forever correcting, forever on the edge of dissolution. Like a mind dreaming in sleep, it churns with invisible energy beneath a surface of apparent peace.

There is no built world (0.2). No ruins, no tools, no traces of intelligence. Even the idea of “construction” feels alien here. The only artifacts are geological: pillow basalts, manganese crusts, the concentric rings of fossilized vent systems. Technology, if it ever existed, would be primitive (0.8)—bone hooks, woven kelp lines, perhaps stone weights—but none remain. Not because they eroded, but because no one ever came. Or perhaps because those who did dissolved into the brine, their bones absorbed into the sediment, their stories rewritten as chemical signatures in the shale.

Weirdness is low (0.3). This is not a place of magic or paradox. It obeys the laws of physics with ruthless fidelity. What feels uncanny is only our own unpreparedness: the mind rebels at a world so saturated, so ancient, so utterly indifferent to the categories of air and land and life as we know them. But the place itself is normal—just *deeply*, *completely* itself.

To be here is to be inside a lung that has forgotten how to exhale. To float in a cradle of liquid time, where every particle has been worn smooth by eternity, and the only motion is the slow, turbulent dance of matter remembering how to be matter. It is beautiful not in spite of its desolation, but because of it: a world that has finished becoming, and now simply *is*, in all its cold, packed, ancient, liquid truth.
