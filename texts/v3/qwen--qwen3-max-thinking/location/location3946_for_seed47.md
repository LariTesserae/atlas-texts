The air tastes of nothing. Not dust, not ozone, not salt—but of the near-absence of matter itself. This is the Great Flattening, a place where time has not merely passed, but congealed into geology. Once towering ranges, now reduced by eons of relentless, glacial erosion into a planetary plain so vast and so subtly contoured that the horizon curves not with the planet’s bulk but with the mind’s inability to hold its scale. Elevation remains extreme—not in peaks, but in the sheer vertical emptiness above: a sky so high and clear it feels like the lid of a cathedral built for ghosts.

Here, water is a theoretical concept. The soil—if it can be called soil—is a sintered crust of silicate and exotic metallic oxides, baked dry not by heat but by aeons of vacuum-like desiccation. There are no rivers, no dew, no ice caps. Only the faintest chemical ghosts linger: hygroscopic salts that pull moisture from passing stellar winds, condensing it into transient, glassy beads that shatter before they can fall. This is a world that has exhaled its last vapor.

Sparse vegetation clings to existence like memory. Not plants as we know them, but lithovores—mineral-crust colonies that metabolize trace radiation and cosmic dust. They grow in fractal filaments across sunward rock faces, black as carbon and brittle as old parchment, appearing only in the cool of the long twilight when the ambient temperature dips to 1.4 on the scale of human comfort: cool, yes, but not cold—more like the chill of a stone chapel at dawn. Their presence is so faint they register only as a whisper of shadow against the pale ground.

Fauna is rarer still. Once in a generation, perhaps, a sky-serpent made of magnetized dust coils through the upper strata, drawn by invisible tides of solar wind, its body coalescing and dispersing like breath on glass. Or a burrower—blind, slow, armored in layered oxides—might shift beneath the crust, stirring dust that hasn’t moved in millennia. But mostly, there is nothing that breathes.

And yet—despite the silence (so profound it hums, a sub-audible pressure in the ears)—this place seethes.

The dynamic parameter is maxed. Turbulence reigns, but not in the way of storms or wind. Here, change is quantum, gravitational, topological. The very ground flickers. At irregular intervals, patches of terrain vanish—not eroded, not buried, but *unwritten*, replaced by smooth, obsidian-like planes bearing faint glyphs too complex for human eyes to parse. These are not ruins. They are edits. Corrections.

Scattered across this continental emptiness are the built remnants: not cities, but singularities of engineering. A spire no wider than a man, stretching ten kilometers into the stratosphere, humming at a frequency that fractures spacetime into temporary lenses. A torus half-buried in the plain, its inner surface lined with crystalline circuitry that pulses with cold light, rewriting local physics in slow, deliberate iterations. These are not human. They predate humanity by epochs, built by intelligences that treated matter as syntax and time as a medium to sculpt. The tech is beyond modern—not just advanced, but ontologically alien, operating on principles that flirt with the edges of known physics: vacuum-state engineering, causal reweaving, entropy inversion.

Light is normal—sunlight, filtered through an atmosphere so thin it might as well be decorative. The sun hangs high, white and indifferent, casting sharp, unwavering shadows that seem too crisp for such an ancient place. But sometimes, during the Flares (unpredictable bursts of high-energy data-radiation from the buried structures), the light bends into geometries that hurt to look at, and the shadows write equations in the dust.

Weirdness is low—but present. Not hallucinatory, not surreal, but *off*. A stone may feel warm on one side and freezing on the other with no thermal source. Footprints vanish minutes after being made, not by wind, but by the ground *remembering* its prior state and reverting. Time feels sticky in places, elastic in others. But these are anomalies, not inversions. The world holds its rules tightly—even as it rewrites them.

This is a landscape at the end of narrative. Not dead, but post-biological. It has settled into its final form, yes—but that form is not stillness. It is the quiet hum of a universe debugging itself. Vast, empty, ancient, and hyperactive beneath its skin. A desert of information, not sand. A salt flat of forgotten causality. And somewhere in the silence between the hums, something is learning how to begin again.
