The air tastes of rust and ozone, thin and dry as ash. It does not breathe—it simply exists, suspended in a high plateau that stretches to every horizon like the skin of a dead god stretched taut over ancient bones. This is not a place that has been abandoned. It has never been alive.

Water here is a rumor. Not even dew dares condense. The ground is a mosaic of fractured silicates—sheets of obsidian-thin basalt, salt-crusted plains that crumble like burnt paper underfoot, and veins of metallic sulfides that glitter dully in the near-absence of light. There are no rivers, no clouds, not even vapor trails. The atmosphere holds just enough pressure to carry sound poorly and heat inefficiently, and what warmth exists (a dry 35°C, constant as a furnace coil) seems radiated not from a sun—there is no visible sun—but from the ground itself, or perhaps from the buried infrastructure below.

Elevation is immense. You stand near the top of a world that has long since stopped growing. The curvature of the land is so gradual it feels flat, yet barometric ghosts whisper that you are two kilometers above some theoretical sea that never was. There is no wind to scour, no rain to carve—erosion has ceased not from lack of force, but from completion. Every edge has been worn to mathematical stillness. Peaks have slumped into mesas; mesas have softened into undulating plateaus. This is geology in its final posture: not decay, but resolution.

And yet—structures rise.

Not organically, not gracefully. These are angular, modular, and layered like sedimentary deposits of purpose. A city—if it can be called that—sprawls across the plateau in grids that obey no cardinal direction, only the logic of load-bearing and thermal dissipation. Buildings are not towers but platforms, stacked terraces of oxidized steel, polymer-ceramic composites, and fused regolith bricks. Windows are rare; when present, they are dark, concave, and sealed. Doors do not open outward but retract into walls with a sigh of pressurized gas, though none have done so in decades, perhaps centuries. The “urban” quality here is not one of habitation but of fossilized function: this was built to last beyond its users.

Industrial echoes linger in the form of colossal pipes that arch like fossilized ribs across dry ravines, their surfaces pitted with corrosion that stopped mid-process. Gear trains the size of houses sit half-buried in mineral dust, frozen mid-turn. There are no active furnaces, no humming transformers—only the occasional low-frequency resonance from deep beneath the crust, a subsonic thrum that vibrates in your molars if you stand still long enough. The tech is not advanced in the sleek, luminous sense—it is brutalist, redundant, built for endurance over elegance. It smells of hot iron and stagnant coolant that hasn’t circulated in a lifetime.

Light is nearly absent. The sky is not black but a deep, starless indigo—perpetually twilight, though there is no twilight star to mark it. Ambient luminescence comes from the ground: faint phosphorescence in certain mineral veins, the dull red glow of cooling thermal vents, and the occasional flicker from cracked photovoltaic tiles still harvesting phantom photons from an unseen source. Shadows here have weight. They pool in corners like liquid and swallow detail whole.

Fauna—if any—moves without sound. Once, you saw a silhouette: low to the ground, multi-limbed, constructed more than grown, skittering between two loading bays before vanishing into a ventilation shaft. It left no tracks. You do not know if it was machine, remnant, or something that learned to mimic both. It did not return.

Sound is a luxury. The silence is not empty but dense—a held breath. When noise comes, it is startling: the ping of contracting metal at thermal shift, the skitter of a loose washer rolling down a corroded ramp, the distant groan of stressed concrete settling into its final shape. These are not signs of life but of entropy performing its last rites.

Weirdness is not flamboyant here. It is quiet, systemic. Gravity feels uneven—not weaker, but inconsistent, as if mass concentrations beneath the crust tug at your inner ear in passing. Time doesn’t flow so much as pool in certain districts; you’ll step from a plaza that feels minutes-old into an alley that radiates the stillness of ten thousand years. The city was not built by humans, or if it was, they edited themselves out of the blueprints long ago. The scale suggests a population in the tens of thousands once lived here. Now, the only presence is the architecture itself—watchful, inert, complete.

Nothing changes. Not truly. The dust accumulates at a rate measurable only by instruments. The rust advances a nanometer per decade. This plateau has entered its terminal equilibrium. It is not dying. It is done.

And in that final stillness, it has become something else—not a city, not a ruin, but a monument to closure. A place that finished its story and folded the last page into its foundation. You stand within it not as a visitor, but as an anomaly—a ripple in a world that has forgotten how to ripple.
