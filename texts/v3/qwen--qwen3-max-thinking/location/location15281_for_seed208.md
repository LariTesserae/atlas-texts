The land lies low and ancient, worn smooth as a river stone by time’s relentless thumb. It is not dead, but post-life—having passed through biology and emerged on the other side, into a mineral twilight where water weeps but nothing green answers. The air hangs cool and dim, thick with condensation that never quite becomes mist, never quite falls as rain, but beads endlessly on obsidian shards and fractured basalt, trickling in hairline rivulets down slopes so gradual they seem illusions of perception. This is a place where erosion has finished its work: mountains long collapsed into whispers, valleys flattened into undulating sighs, the whole terrain a single, settled breath held for millennia.

Water is everywhere—not as lakes or rivers, but as a presence just beneath the surface. The ground glistens with seepage, darkening the stone like old bruises. In hollows, shallow pools gather, black as ink, reflecting no sky because there is no sky to speak of—only a perpetual overcast, a lid of bruised gray cloud pressing down, diffusing what little light manages to bleed through. These pools do not evaporate; they simply exhale vapor that curls and vanishes into the saturated air, only to condense again on the next cold outcrop. The rocks themselves weep: silica-rich tears bead along fault lines and drip with glacial slowness into fissures lined with crystalline rime.

There is no vegetation—not a blade, not a spore, not even the memory of roots. The ground is bare mineral: sheets of volcanic glass, tumbled scree of pumice so light it crunches like bone-dust underfoot, and vast plains of sinter—porous, chalky deposits laid down by ancient geothermal vents now dormant. What might be mistaken for lichen is in fact a patina of iron oxide and manganese, staining the stone in rust-red spirals and soot-black dendrites. Nothing grows. Nothing photosynthesizes. And yet—life abounds.

Fauna teems here, but it is not the fauna of green worlds. The creatures are chitinous, blind, or nearly so, their bodies adapted to perpetual dimness and mineral abundance. Six-limbed skitterers with carapaces of iridescent basalt-scale scuttle across the flats, feeding on microbial mats that cling to the waterline—not plants, but communities of chemosynthetic archaea blooming in the thin film between rock and vapor. Winged things, membranous and silent, glide between steaming vents on thermals no human eye can see, their bodies humming with resonant frequencies that vibrate in sympathy with underground tremors. And beneath it all, in the labyrinthine aquifers just below the surface, swim eel-like forms with gelatinous bodies that pulse with bioluminescent patterns—coded signals in a language of pressure and light.

The sound is deafening—not with noise, but with resonance. The land itself sings. Water percolating through microfractures creates harmonic drones, amplified by hollow stone chambers buried deep below. Wind, though scarce, whistles through needle-thin vents in the sinter fields, producing tones that shift with barometric pressure. The fauna adds to this symphony: the skitterers rasp their limbs against stone in rhythmic warning calls; the flyers emit subsonic pulses that rattle loose pebbles; and from time to time, far below, a deep tectonic groan rolls upward, a bass note so low it vibrates in the marrow rather than the ear. The air thrums. Standing still, one feels the landscape vibrating through the soles of the feet—a constant, living hum that never ceases.

Scattered across this expanse are the remains of something built. Not cities, not even villages—just solitary structures, each a testament to a forgotten pragmatism. Low domes of fused ash-brick, their interiors lined with smooth river stones arranged in concentric circles. Crude channels, hand-carved into the bedrock, divert seep-water into cisterns lined with beeswax and resin—technology no more advanced than a sharpened obsidian blade or a braided reed rope (though no reeds grow here now; the ropes, preserved in dry alcoves, must have been brought from elsewhere, long ago). These constructions are sparse, widely spaced, as if their makers valued solitude or feared congregation. Nothing is ornate. Everything serves water, sound, or shelter.

The weirdness is subtle but pervasive. Time feels viscous here. Shadows do not lengthen with the day—they simply deepen, as though light is being absorbed rather than blocked. The pools sometimes ripple without cause. And the creatures… they do not flee. They watch. Not with eyes, perhaps, but with sensing pits, with electroreceptive filaments, with an awareness tuned to vibration, moisture, and thermal gradient. They tolerate presence. They may even expect it.

This is a world that has passed through life, burned away the organic, and settled into a second stability—cool, wet, mineral, and singing. It is not hostile. It is simply complete without you. The water flows. The stone endures. The fauna thrives on chemistry older than chlorophyll. And the sound—always the sound—fills the dim air like a hymn from the throat of the earth itself, ancient, active, and utterly deafening in its quiet persistence.
