The air hangs thick and warm—hot, but not searing—like the breath inside a steam room left to cool just enough for bare skin to survive. It’s a low place: basin-wide, basin-deep, a shallow depression cupped in ancient, weary stone. The land has settled into itself over millennia, its contours smoothed not by violence but by the patient passage of time—erosion here isn’t destruction, it’s completion. Every slope, every hollow, every cracked terrace feels final, as if the earth sighed and said, *This is enough.*

Water is everywhere, though rarely in rivers or lakes. Instead, it weeps. It beads on obsidian-black outcrops that rise like broken teeth from the silt, condensing from the saturated air in slow rivulets that never quite dry. Pools gather in the hollows—not deep, not clean, but slick with mineral films that shimmer faintly in the dim light: oily rainbows over stagnant mirrors. After each brief, warm rain (which comes often, though never stormily), the ground exhales mist that curls like breath across the flats, pooling in the streets.

For there are streets. And buildings. This is a city—low, sprawling, built not upward but outward, its structures hugging the earth like lichen on bone. The architecture is industrial in its bones: riveted iron, corroded copper, brick blackened by soot and salt, concrete cracked and softened by decades of damp. Towers aren’t tall but broad—foundries, pumping stations, filtration vaults—connected by walkways and gantries that sag slightly under their own weight. Windows are few and small, many boarded or filmed over with moisture-resistant resin. The urban fabric is dense with function, not people: a town built for machinery first, humans second, if at all.

Yet there is no vegetation. Not a weed in the cracks, not a moss on the pipes. The absence is absolute, eerie in its consistency. What should be green is instead rust, salt crust, or bare stone. Life, as we name it, refuses to take root. Perhaps the soil is toxic, perhaps the water too mineral-laden, perhaps something in the air suppresses organic growth. Or perhaps this place simply exists in the interstice between biologies—a world where life hasn’t returned, or hasn’t yet begun, or has been scrubbed clean and replaced.

But the fauna—oh, the fauna *fills* the void.

They are everywhere. Not hidden, not shy, but present with a quiet, overwhelming abundance. Winged things the size of hands drift between gantries on membranes like oiled silk, their bodies segmented, many-eyed, clicking softly as they navigate by sonar chirps too high for human ears. Ground-dwellers—segmented, chitinous, multi-limbed—crawl over pipes and through drainage grates, moving with purpose but no apparent hurry. Some cling to vertical surfaces, motionless for hours, then scuttle in sudden bursts. Others cluster in the weeping pools, not drinking but *filtering*, their bodies straining minerals from the water, exhaling fine mists laced with phosphorescence that glows faintly in the dimness.

They do not flee. They do not attack. They simply *are*—a dominant, established ecology that operates without plants, without green, without what we’d call a food chain. They metabolize chemosynthetically, perhaps, or feed on airborne particulates, or draw energy from the heat differentials in the wet stone. Their biomass is staggering, their presence total, yet they make almost no sound: a soft scrape, a whisper of carapace on metal, the distant hum of a thousand tiny respiratory fans. The quiet isn’t silence—it’s a hush filled with micro-noise, like a library where every patron breathes in unison.

Light is dim, not from night but from a permanent overcast—a haze of warm vapor that scatters the sun into a diffuse amber glow, never bright enough to cast sharp shadows, never dark enough for true night. The city glows faintly in return: amber lamps along walkways, the dull red of cooling vents, the occasional pulse of a signal beacon. Everything feels submerged in a warm, shallow sea of air.

The place feels old. Not ruined, not abandoned—but complete. The industrial apparatus still runs: pumps gurgle in sub-level chambers, steam vents sigh on the hour, conveyor belts inch forward in empty warehouses. Yet no human hands tend them. Perhaps they are automated, maintained by the fauna themselves—some symbiosis between machine and creature that defies categorization. Or perhaps the machines simply persist, like geological features, powered by geothermal taps or ancient batteries, cycling endlessly in a world that no longer needs them but tolerates them.

This is a landscape of wet heat, mineral sterility, and teeming non-green life. A city built by industry, now inhabited by something else entirely—something that found the structures useful, the heat comforting, the constant weep of water sufficient. It is active, yes: vents steam, creatures migrate, rain falls, rust advances. But it is also in balance. Nothing is growing toward climax; nothing is collapsing. It simply *is*, in its own strange equilibrium—a marsh of metal and chitin, warm and wet and humming just below hearing, empty of all but the overwhelming presence of its fauna, quiet as a held breath.
