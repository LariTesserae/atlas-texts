The Hollow City of Dripping Stone is not a ruin, nor a ruin-to-be. It is a city caught mid-gasp—a settlement in the act of becoming, still wet with the breath of its own making.  

It sits high—not atop a jagged peak, but nestled within a vast, shallow bowl carved into the upper flanks of a mountain range so ancient its bones have forgotten their own name. Elevation here is quiet authority: cool air pools like clear water, and mist settles between the ribs of unfinished structures like breath held too long. At this height, clouds skim the ground before dawn, and the light—normal in intensity, yet strangely clarifying—slants through vapour in ways that make stone look soft, almost edible.

Water is not abundant, but it is insistent. At 1.5 on the scale, it is not flooding or flowing in rivers, but seeping, weeping, condensing. Every surface carries a sheen. Dampness beads on raw granite outcrops like sweat on skin. Mosses drink from the air as much as from the earth. After rain, which comes often but never violently, shallow lenses of water gather in the concave hollows of half-formed plazas, turning the city into a mosaic of sky-reflecting mirrors. Gutters carved by hand—not runoff—channel this water into cisterns lined with river clay, but many channels end abruptly, mid-path, as if the builder paused and never returned.

Vegetation thrives at 2.4—not dense jungle, but exuberant opportunism. Ferns uncurl from cracks in limestone blocks. Vines lace through scaffolds of greenwood stilts. Alder and dwarf birch send roots into the thin, peaty soil that has only just begun to form between stones. The green is luminous in the cool air, each leaf edged with dew even at noon. Yet this lushness is precarious, clinging—a guest rather than a host. The land beneath has not yet learned to hold life well; it is still learning the grammar of soil.

And the city itself? Built at 1.9, it is unmistakably urban—a grid of intentions. Streets align with cardinal points, intersecting at squares where stone foundations wait for towers that may never rise. But everything is provisional. Walls stand waist-high, marked with chalk plans for windows that don’t yet exist. Roofs are tarpaulins stretched over frames of sapling timber, weighted with river stones. Doorways lead into open earth. Staircases ascend three steps—then stop, ending in air above a patch of moss. This is architecture as hypothesis, construction as ritual rehearsal.

Technology is primitive (1.3): no iron nails, only pegs and lashings of braided root-fibre. Tools are bone, antler, sharpened slate. Yet the craftsmanship is precise—joints fitted with quiet reverence, surfaces smoothed not for show but for touch. Someone here cares deeply about how a hand meets a wall, how rain slides off a sill. But metal is rare, fire small, and wheels unknown.

Despite its urban density of plan, the actual density of presence is near-zero (0.2). You could walk for an hour through the city and meet no one. The few inhabitants move like shadows—dressed in moss-dyed wool, barefoot on cold stone. They do not speak much. They watch. They tend tiny gardens in chipped ceramic bowls. Their homes are not the half-walls but low, dome-like shelters woven from reeds and propped against boulders—temporary nests beside permanent dreams.

Fauna is rare (1.0). A crow might cross the sky once a day. A salamander slips between wet stones. But the sound (2.3)—ah, the sound is loud. Not with voices, but with resonance. The city hums. Wind moves through unfinished chimneys like flutes. Dripping water echoes in cisterns with the precision of struck bronze. At dusk, the thermal contraction of cooling stone produces low groans that roll through alleyways like ghost carts. The loudest sound is the absence of human noise—the amplification of small things in vast emptiness.

The weirdness (1.3) lies in the city’s temporal dissonance. It feels simultaneously ancient and newborn. The stones are raw geology (erosion 0.1)—sharp-edged, unweathered, as if quarried yesterday from a mountain that hasn’t yet learned to erode. And yet, the layout suggests generations of planning. Lichen has not yet claimed the walls, but the alignment of the central plaza tracks the solstice with uncanny accuracy. It is as if the builders inherited knowledge but not time—knowing how to make a city without having lived long enough to finish one.

Change here is glacial (dynamic 1.1). Moss spreads a millimetre per season. Frost cracks one stone every decade. A child adds a single row of pebbles to a wall each spring, and calls it progress. The city grows like a coral reef—barely, patiently, molecule by molecule—while the wind sings through its ribs and the mist returns each dawn to cradle its unfinished bones.

This is not a place of conquest or commerce. It is a place of waiting—where people build not because they must, but because building is how they listen to the mountain. The Hollow City does not belong to them. They belong to it, and they are still learning its language, one damp stone at a time.
