Beneath a sky so starless it might not exist—only the absolute absence of light, not even the memory of it—lies a place that is both city and forest, ruin and root.  

This is the **Underhollow**. Not underground, not exactly, but embedded: a single, self-contained cavernous chamber the size of a modest town square, yet stretched thin across millennia. Its ceiling is not rock but a membrane of petrified bark, fused into a dome so ancient it has forgotten it was once alive. The air is cool—not cold, but the coolness of deep earth, of stone that has never known sun, holding its breath for eons.  

Water is a myth here. What moisture exists is locked in the cellular lattices of the biomass that *is* the landscape. No rivers, no dew, no rain—only the faint metabolic sigh of plants respiring in slow, geological time. The atmosphere tastes of dust and tannin, dry as crushed lichen, yet thick with the scent of lignin breaking down over centuries.  

And yet—**vegetation saturates everything**. Not as trees or shrubs, but as the very substrate. The ground is not soil but a compressed stratum of rhizomes, fossilized trunks, and interwoven vines turned to a substance like fibrous stone. Walls are living tissue, though their metabolism has slowed to near-stasis: they photosynthesize nothing, for there is no light, and yet they persist, drawing energy from chemosynthetic fungi entangled in their xylem, feeding on trace minerals leached from the airless bedrock below.  

This is an **urban forest**, but not of human design. The "built" quality comes from the plants themselves: arches of fused willow-bark curve into vaulted ceilings; colonnades of bamboo-like stalks, petrified into hollow ceramic tubes, stand in precise grids like the ruins of a temple. Doorways yawn open in the sides of bulbous, hive-like growths—galls the size of houses, their interiors lined with velvety mycelial felt. Staircases spiral up trunks that taper into nothing, leading to platforms that hover unsupported, held aloft by tensile roots gripping nothing but air. This city was grown, not built—layer upon layer over ten thousand years, each generation of flora enveloping the last, sealing rooms inside rooms, corridors inside veins.  

**Erosion has done its work to near-completion**. Edges are gone. Corners softened into curves. Everything has slumped toward equilibrium. No sharp angles remain—only the gentle undulations of forms that have settled into their final, exhausted shapes. The entire chamber feels like the last sigh of a world that has finished transforming.  

Yet for all its **emptiness**—vast distances between solid forms, open chambers the size of cathedrals containing only a single suspended pod or a lone, hollow spire—the space never feels large. It is **pocket-scale**, contained. You could walk its diameter in twenty minutes. But you wouldn’t. Not because it’s dangerous, but because the air resists haste. This is a place of **slow dynamics**: a leaf uncurling over a decade; a root shifting a millimeter per century; spores drifting down from unseen canopies in spirals that take hours to complete.  

And yet, against all expectation, it is **loud**.  

Not with voices—there are no animals here, not even insects. Fauna is absent, replaced by the creak of desiccating fibers, the groan of stressed cellulose, the sigh of air displaced through porous walls. But the true sound is **resonance**. The entire structure acts as a single instrument. Wind doesn’t blow here—but air *moves*, drawn through microscopic channels by thermal gradients too faint to feel. As it flows, it excites the hollow tubes, the bell-shaped galls, the ribbed vaults. The Underhollow hums. Not one note, but hundreds—a chorus of deep, organic drones, like a forest breathing through bone flutes. Sometimes, a shift in pressure sends a pulse through the network, and the whole chamber thrums in harmonic dissonance, a chord so vast it vibrates in your teeth.  

**Technology is primitive**, if present at all. Scattered through the deeper chambers are remnants: clay pots sealed with resin, woven mats fossilized into the floor, tools of obsidian and bone left propped against root-walls as if their users simply dissolved. But these are not human. The hands that shaped them had too many fingers, or none at all. They are artifacts of the First Growers—beings who may have been plant, or symbiont, or something that blurred the line. Their "craft" was cultivation: guiding growth with tension, pruning with intention, coaxing architecture from biology. No metal, no fire—only patience and pattern.  

**Darkness is total**. Not even bioluminescence dares flicker here. The plants have no need for sight, and so they shed no light. You navigate by touch, by sound, by the subtle shifts in air pressure against your skin. In this blackness, the mind begins to see with ears: the chamber becomes a map of echoes, each surface singing its density, its hollowness, its age.  

And the **strangeness**? It lies in the paradox: a place of overwhelming life that is also post-life. A city without citizens, a forest without seasons, a world that has achieved perfect stillness without death. The plants are not dead—they persist, they *endure*—but they have passed beyond growth into a state of eternal maintenance. They are the last witnesses of a biosphere that chose consolidation over expansion, containment over chaos.  

To stand here is to stand inside the final thought of a world that decided, long ago, to stop becoming—and simply *be*.  

And in the darkness, it hums your name—not in words, but in the slow creak of a branch settling into its forever shape.
