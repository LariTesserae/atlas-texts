The air is liquid. Not mist, not fog—real, dense, breathable water suspended in cold equilibrium, a planet-wide ocean without a surface, where sky and sea have forgotten their names. You stand—though “standing” is a courtesy—on a spine of obsidian glass, thrust upward through millennia of slow, cold tectonics. Elevation here is a vertical wound in the world: razor ridges of volcanic glass pierce the aqueous atmosphere like broken teeth, their edges unblunted by time, sharp enough to slice thought. This is a young wound in an ancient, frozen equilibrium—a paradox held taut across continental scales.

There is no soil, no root, no leaf. Vegetation is a ghost memory. What grows—if “grow” applies—is not green but glacial: colonies of silica filaments, crystalline lichen analogues that feed not on sunlight but on piezoelectric stress, blooming in fractal frost where windless currents grind glass against glass. These are not plants, not even fungi. They are mineral metabolisms, slow exhalations of the stone itself, shimmering with internal charge. They do not bend. They *fracture*, with sounds like distant chimes.

Cold is absolute here—not biting, but total. A chill so complete it has ceased to be a sensation and become a condition of being. The water-laden air does not freeze because pressure and salinity hold it in suspended animation, a supercooled sea-atmosphere locked in thermodynamic stasis. Nothing evaporates. Nothing condenses. The system is closed, vast, and utterly still in its composition—yet deafening in its voice.

Sound. Ah, the sound. It is not wind—there is no wind. No storm, no surf, no animal cry in the conventional sense. The roar is structural. It is the groan of planetary-scale hydraulic systems buried deep beneath the glass plains: conduits kilometers wide, lined with corroded iron and humming with the residual thrum of dead industry. Somewhere below, in vaults older than language, pressurized water circuits still circulate through turbine housings the size of cities, driven by geothermal differentials that refuse to equalize. The water itself sings as it shears through fractured bedrock, vibrating at frequencies that resonate through the glass spires, turning the entire highland into a cathedral of infrasound and harmonic shriek. You do not hear it with your ears alone—you feel it in your marrow, a constant, thunderous hum that never began and will never end.

Fauna abounds, but not as you know it. They are not creatures of flesh but of pressure and echo. Gelatinous toroids, translucent and dense as mercury, drift through the aqueous air, propelled not by fins but by rhythmic contractions of internal bladders that modulate buoyancy with surgical precision. They feed on electrical gradients—on the stray currents bleeding from rusted conduits, on the static charge of crystal blooms. Their bodies pulse with bioluminescent glyphs, not for communication but as waste heat made visible. Smaller still are the swarm-swarms: clouds of metallic spores, each no larger than a grain of salt, that coalesce into temporary organs—a lens, a limb, a mouth—only to dissolve seconds later, reassembling elsewhere in response to acoustic cues. They are alive only in aggregate, only in motion, yet here, in this frozen equilibrium, even their motion is cyclical, predictable, eternal.

Scattered across the glass plains are the remains. Not ruins—too intact for that. Single structures: cylindrical towers of riveted steel, half-buried in vitrified sand, crowned with rotors that no longer turn. Observation domes with viewports clouded by mineral deposits. A gantry, suspended between two spires, holding aloft a crane frozen mid-lift, its hook dangling over nothing. These are not human. The scale is wrong—too vast, too indifferent. They belong to an industrial intelligence that treated continents as machine parts, that built not for habitation but for process. Most are sealed. Some vent slow plumes of warm brine, the only thermal anomalies in a world without gradients.

Light is normal—that is, solar. The sky (if such a thing exists) admits a steady, even luminescence, neither dawn nor dusk, filtered through kilometers of suspended droplets. It casts no shadows because there is no discrete surface to shadow; light diffuses through the aqueous medium in soft, directionless glow. Objects do not have edges—they have halos.

And the scale—oh, the scale. From any ridge, you see only more ridges, endless, receding into a liquid haze that shimmers with distant hydraulic thunder. Weather? There is none. And yet the system breathes: not with storms, but with slow, continent-wide tides of pressurized brine flowing through subterranean arteries, driven by forces older than erosion. This place is too vast to be a landscape. It is a single, static organism wearing the skin of a drowned factory.

Weirdness is low—only 0.9—but it bleeds through in the details: the way a crystal bloom *clicks* in sync with a distant turbine pulse; how the gelatinous floaters avoid the steel towers not out of fear, but because their internal currents short-circuit near iron; how, if you press your ear to the glass, you hear not silence beneath the roar, but a single, repeating tone—C-sharp, 27 hertz—that matches no known source.

This is not a world waiting to be reborn. It has already decided its final form. It is complete. Cold, empty, industrial, deafening, and utterly, eternally still in its infinite motion.
