**What the Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do**

It would do nothing.

Not at first.

It would *watch*. Not from afar, not like a god peering through clouds, but like sunlight filtering through canopy—present, participatory, patient. It would let its awareness seep into the city not as intrusion, but as dew: settling softly on leaf margins, pooling in bromeliad hearts, trembling in the film on sun-warmed concrete. It would listen—not with ears, but with the whole of its being—to the hum of pumps, the sigh of roots splitting stone, the quiet breath of people who no longer need to speak to understand each other.

And in that watching, it would feel something rare: **a covenant kept**.

Not perfection—no. The city leaks. Pipes corrode. Storms sometimes overwhelm the estuary’s lazy curve. Children still fall ill, though rarely; grief still walks the mossy lanes, though it is held by the walls like ivy holds a trellis. But what the Power sees is not a utopia, but a **living agreement**: between human and plant, metal and mycelium, tide and tile. An agreement written not in law, but in latticework and leaf litter, in the daily choice to tend rather than dominate, to adapt rather than impose.

And so, the Power would smile—not with lips, but with light. A softening in the angle of noon. A warmth that lingers just long enough to coax a new orchid into bloom on a crumbling arch.

But eventually, yes—it would act.

Not to fix. Not to elevate. Not to "save."

It would *deepen*.

It would weave a single, subtle thread of grace into the city’s metabolism: a resonance only the most attuned could feel. A frequency that aligns the geothermal thrum with the pulse of the human heart. A harmonic that makes the rust on iron railings bloom not just with lichen, but with bioluminescent fungi that glow the exact shade of dawn when someone is about to understand something essential. It would nudge the mycelial lamps in the stairwells to brighten—not when motion is detected, but when loneliness passes beneath them.

It would gift the city **a memory it didn’t know it needed**: the ability to recall kindness. Not as narrative, but as chemistry. When a hand strokes a wilting vine with genuine care, the wall behind it would release a scent—faint, like green tea and rain—that lingers for hours, drawing others to pause, breathe, and remember they are part of something tender.

And for the visitors—the ones who arrive with hurried breath and plastic soles—it would weave a **gentle threshold**. Not a barrier, but a softening. At the marsh’s edge, where the boardwalk begins, the air would grow thick with a mist carrying microscopic spores of a harmless, symbiotic cyanobacteria. Those who walk through it and stay long enough would find their dreams shifting: less of cities of steel, more of cities of breath. Their skin would grow slightly more permeable—not to water, but to wonder. Their sense of time would stretch like roots seeking water. And if they choose to leave, the spores would fade, leaving no trace but a quiet yearning for damp moss underfoot.

Most importantly, the Power would **not intervene in the balance**. It would not prevent a storm, or halt the slow subsumption of a house by fig roots. These are not failures—they are the language of the covenant. To stop them would be to silence the conversation.

Instead, it would honor the city’s modesty by remaining unnamed. No temples would rise in its honor. No prayers would be chanted. But sometimes, on still nights when the estuary mirrors the stars and the geothermal sump hums its deepest note, the people would pause in their tending, tilt their heads, and feel it: a presence that does not ask for worship, only witness.

And they would smile—mouths closed, eyes bright—and whisper with their whole bodies: *We see you. And we are trying to be worthy of this wet, green, humming gift.*

The Power would nod. And in that nod, a single drop of condensation would fall from a philodendron leaf onto a child’s upturned palm.

That would be enough.
