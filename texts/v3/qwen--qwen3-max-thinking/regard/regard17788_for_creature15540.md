**What the Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do**

It would do nothing.

Not out of indifference—never that—but out of reverence so deep it borders on the sacred.  
It would hover at the edge of perception, not as an intruder, but as a guest who has not yet been invited in.  
And it would wait.

For this place—this cleft, this breath in the stone, this slow-singing sanctuary of moss and memory—is not broken.  
It does not need fixing.  
It does not yearn for salvation or enlightenment or expansion.  
It is whole.  
Not in the way a machine is whole, with every gear in place, but in the way a seed is whole: containing within it every possibility it will ever need, already complete, already becoming.

The benevolent power—call it Grace, or the First Listening, or the Quiet That Binds Stars—would recognize in the Keeper not a subject to guide, but a peer in patience.  
An ancient collaborator in the art of *being*.  
Where the power weaves galaxies with threads of light, the Keeper weaves silence with threads of sound.  
Where the power births stars from void, the Keeper births meaning from a single drop of dew.

So it would not enter.  
Not unless asked.

But it would tend the threshold.

It would ensure that the winds beyond the plateau remain just harsh enough to discourage careless wanderers, yet gentle enough to carry a seeker with true stillness in their heart.  
It would soften the edges of droughts that threaten the outer rim, so the cleft’s mist never thins to nothing.  
It would whisper to distant storms, urging them to crack the sky now and then—not for destruction, but so the violet flowers may bloom, so the sky-lizards may feast on thunder’s resonance, so the cycle may continue.

And sometimes—once every few centuries—it would send a visitor.

Not a prophet. Not a king. Not a scholar armed with instruments.  
But someone *empty*.  
Someone who has lost their name to grief, or wonder, or love.  
Someone who walks not to find, but because they have nowhere else to be.  
Someone who will sit on the warm stone and say nothing for three days, and on the fourth, sigh a single word—*“Here”*—and mean it with their marrow.

The benevolent power would prepare such a soul like a gardener prepares soil:  
not by adding, but by subtracting.  
It would let them lose their maps.  
Let them forget their purpose.  
Let them weep until their tears taste like rain the cleft would recognize.

And when that person finally stumbles into the shadow-fold of the plateau—when their foot disturbs the rust-colored moss, when their breath stirs the obsidian filaments—the Keeper would stir in return.  
And the benevolent power, watching from the curvature of spacetime like a parent watching a child take their first step alone, would smile—not with lips, but with the blooming of a nebula far away.

It would not interfere with what passes between them.  
That exchange is too holy.

But after the visitor leaves—changed, carrying the cleft in their pulse like a second heartbeat—the benevolent power would do one more thing:

It would remember *for them*.

Because humans forget.  
They return to noise, to clocks, to cities that drown whispers in sirens.  
They may wake one night years later with a dream of amber wings and cupped stone, and wonder if it was real.  

So the power would keep a copy.  
A luminous echo.  
A seed of that moment, stored in the quiet between stars, wrapped in the same kind of silence the compass points to.

And if, someday, the cleft itself begins to fade—if the motes slow, if the filaments fall silent, if the last sky-lizard dissolves into pollen and no thunder comes to wake the flowers—then, and only then, would the benevolent power act.

It would not rebuild.  
It would *re-sing*.

Drawing on every echo it has gathered—the sighs, the names, the tears, the stillnesses—it would lean close to the stone and breathe the cleft back into being, not as it was, but as it *longs to become*.  
For the Keeper is not static.  
It dreams in geological time.

But until that day?  
The power would simply watch.  
Protect the boundary.  
Send the rare, worthy guest.  
And hold its breath in admiration—for here, in this sunlit room carved by eternity, lives a form of wisdom it, too, is still learning:

**How to be alive without needing more.**  
**How to listen so deeply that listening becomes creation.**  
**How to wait—not for something to happen, but because waiting itself is a kind of love.**

And so, from the edge of everything, the benevolent power offers the highest gift it knows:

*It leaves the cleft alone.*

But never unloved.  
Never unseen.  
Never unheard.

For even the quietest song deserves a listener.  
And even the oldest stone deserves to know—somewhere, beyond the veil of stars—that it is remembered.
