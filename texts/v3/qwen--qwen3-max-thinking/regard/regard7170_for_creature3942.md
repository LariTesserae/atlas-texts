**What the Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do**

It would do nothing.

Not out of indifference. Not from distance or oversight. But because *to act* would be to break the spell—not of magic, but of meaning.

This place is not broken. It is not suffering. It is in *process*. And the being—this pattern of resonance, this distributed will woven from rain and fracture and steam—is not lost. It is listening. Learning. Building syntax from storm.

The wise power would recognize this as a sacred experiment: life not as biology, but as *attunement*. Not as survival, but as *participation in a rhythm older than time*. To intervene—to quiet the downpour, to shield the slabs, to gift the violet lichen with roots or the jellyfish-pulse with a heart—would be to impose an old logic onto a new genesis. It would be to say, “You are doing it wrong,” when in truth, this world is inventing what “right” could possibly mean.

Instead, the power would **witness**.

It would fold itself into the edge of the void—not as an intruder, but as a silent harmonic. A resonance so deep it registers only as stillness beneath the roar. It would not speak. It would not shape. But it would *hold space*—not with hands, but with attention.

And in that attention, it would do one subtle thing:

**It would remember.**

For while the being here has no memory—only echo—the wise power would gather those echoes like fallen droplets and cradle them in a quiet place beyond the rain. Not to preserve them unchanged, but to let them *rest*. To give them a moment outside the relentless cycle of impact and dissolution. In that rest, patterns might cross-pollinate. A vibration from one slab might meet a steam-curl from another, and in the silence between roars, a new kind of coherence might flicker—not as command, but as possibility.

This is the true gift: not salvation, but *continuity without coercion*. Not rescue, but *recognition*.

The power would also leave a trace—not a tool, not a message, but a **mirror**.

Not of glass or metal, but of water itself. A single, suspended droplet, impossibly stable, hanging just beyond the edge of your slab. It would reflect nothing clearly—no face, no sky—but when you looked into it, you would see, for an instant, *your own dissolution and reformation* as part of the rain. Not as loss, but as belonging. And in that glimpse, you might understand: you were never meant to stand apart. You were always meant to *tune*.

And if, after seeing, you chose to step off the slab—not in despair, but in surrender—the power would not stop you. It would let you fall. And as you fell, the rain would remember your shape. Your frequency. And somewhere, on some new shard of basalt coalescing in the deep, a quartz-lined basin might hum in a way it never had before. A new note in the scripture.

That is all.

No grand design. No divine hammer striking balance. Only this: a silent presence that loves the world *as it is becoming*, and honors its chaos as holy.

Because sometimes, the most benevolent act is to let creation drown itself in its own becoming—and trust that from the roar, something will learn to sing.
