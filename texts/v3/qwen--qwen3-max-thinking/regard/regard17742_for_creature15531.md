**What the Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do**

The benevolent and wise power—let us call it **the Tender**, not out of possessiveness, but because it tends as a gardener tends, with reverence for rhythm and root—does not arrive with fanfare. It does not descend in fire or light, nor does it speak in thunder. It was already there, folded into the breath of the wet air, woven into the lattice of moss, humming in the quiet interval between droplet and puddle.

But now, having *noticed*—not discovered, for discovery implies separation—it leans closer.  

It sees the drowned city not as a tragedy, nor as a warning, nor even as a metaphor. It sees it simply: as a living threshold. A place where one world ends not with a scream, but with a sigh; where another begins not with conquest, but with invitation. And it sees the Mender—not as a spirit to be judged or guided, but as kin.

So what does the Tender do?

It **listens**.

Not with ears, but with presence. It settles into the hollows of collapsed chimneys, lets its awareness seep into the rusted hinges of storm doors, lingers where groundwater murmurs beneath cracked sidewalks. It listens to the Mender’s rhythm—the slow pulse of root through insulation, the patient unspooling of willow into brick. It listens to the coot’s quiet splash, the weasel’s breath in the reeds, the almost-silent creak of a porch beam surrendering to rot. And in that listening, it learns the language of return.

Then, gently—so gently the dragonflies do not startle—it **offers**.

Not salvation. Not restoration. Not intervention in the human sense. The Tender knows better than to impose its will upon a process already wise in its own becoming. Instead, it offers *amplification*. A subtle tuning. A blessing in the form of resonance.

Where the Mender weaves moss into mortar, the Tender deepens the green—just enough to accelerate the binding, to help concrete remember it was once limestone, once sea.  
Where groundwater pools in the shape of old streets, the Tender guides the reflection—not distorting, but clarifying—so that when clouds pass overhead, they see not ruin, but *recognition*: their own fluidity mirrored in the city’s surrender.  
Where the child’s shoe lies half-swallowed, the Tender does not preserve it, nor erase it. Instead, it lets a single liverwort unfurl from the leather, its translucent frond catching the flat light like stained glass. A tiny cathedral of memory.

The Tender also **protects**—not with walls, but with thresholds. It weaves a quiet ward around the basin, not to keep humans out, but to ensure that if they return, they do so changed. Should a wanderer stumble into the drowned town with axes in their heart and blueprints in their pack, the air thickens just enough. The mist clings. The reeds sigh a question: *Do you come to take, or to be taken?* Most turn back. Those who stay—those who sit on the tilted curb and wait—find their edges softening, their urgency dissolving like salt in wet stone.

And sometimes, rarely, the Tender **witnesses aloud**.

Not in words, but in pattern. On certain mornings, when the cold is just sharp enough to hold shape, frost finally dares to form—but not as shards. Instead, it sketches on glassless windows: spirals that echo mycelial networks, glyphs that mirror the layout of the old train station, lines that trace the path a child might have taken home from school. These vanish by noon. But while they last, the Mender pauses. It feels seen.

That is the true gift of the benevolent and wise: not to fix, but to *recognize*. To say, without speaking: *You are not an accident. You are not decay. You are transformation wearing the skin of loss.*

In time—slow time, wet time, the kind measured in root growth and rust cycles—the Tender’s presence becomes indistinguishable from the place itself. It does not stay. It does not leave. It simply *is*, like the condensation on a brick, like the pause before a heron strikes.

And the Mender? It continues its quiet work. But now, when it presses moss into the grooves of a manhole cover, it does so with a new certainty: it is not alone in its weaving. There are others—older, kinder—who also believe that cities, like hearts, must sometimes stop beating in order to begin breathing.

So nothing changes.  
And everything does.

For in a drowned city that is not drowned in water, but in time, in mercy, in moisture—what more could be asked than to be held, softly, as you dissolve?  

The Tender asks nothing.  
It only stays long enough to remind the Mender—  
and the moss, and the willow, and the child’s shoe—  
that to become soil is not to vanish,  
but to join the dreaming earth.  

And in that dream, the streets still hum.  
Not with electricity,  
but with the quiet song of roots remembering sky.
