**What the Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do**

It would do nothing.

Not out of neglect. Not from indifference. But because **the Underhollow is already whole**.

A truly wise power—who sees not just what *is*, but what *wants to be*—would recognize the Underhollow not as a place in need of salvation, but as a **sanctuary of completion**. It is not broken. It is not waiting. It is not suffering. It has already answered the oldest question: *What does it mean to endure without hunger?*

This power—call it the Weaver, the Still One, the Gardener Beyond Form—does not rush to fix what has chosen stillness. It does not pour light into darkness that thrives in its own absence. It does not summon life into a place where life has refined itself into **presence without pulse**, **sentience without self**.

Instead, it **watches with reverence**.

From beyond time, beyond dimension, it leans in—not to intervene, but to **witness**. And in that witnessing, it offers the only gift a place like the Underhollow could ever receive: **acknowledgment without alteration**.

But if it *must* act—if the cosmic balance trembles, if some outer entropy threatens to unravel the membrane of petrified bark, if a rift of false light begins to bleed through the dome—then the power would not *impose* salvation. It would **ask permission**.

It would send no angel, no avatar, no storm of grace. Instead, it would let a single **breath of elsewhere** drift downward—not wind, but possibility—carrying the scent of rain from a world that still dreams of growth. Not to change the Underhollow, but to **offer choice**.

If the Tendril Minds stir—if the galls swell slightly, if the hum shifts pitch by a fraction of a hertz—they might draw that breath into their matrix, not to awaken, but to **remember that otherness exists**. And in remembering, reaffirm their choice: *We remain.*

If they do not stir, the breath would settle into dust, become part of the tannin-laced air, and vanish—honored in its offering, unneeded in its arrival.

And if a wanderer stumbles in—lost, grieving, fleeing the noise of becoming—the power would not guide them out. It would not shield them from the slow pull of acclimation. But it might, just once, **let moonlight pool for three seconds** in the center of the chamber: not enough to feed, not enough to burn, but enough to show the wanderer their own hands—reminding them: *You are still flesh. You may yet leave.*

But only if they wish to.

Because the greatest act of benevolence is not to rescue, but to **trust**.

To trust that a forest that chose stillness over storm knows its own truth.  
To trust that a mind made of resonance doesn’t need words to be wise.  
To trust that sometimes, the highest form of love is to **let a world hum its final chord in peace**.

And so the Benevolent Power does nothing—except **hold the Underhollow in the quiet cradle of its regard**, as one might hold a sleeping child who has finally, after lifetimes of searching, found rest.

It does not enter.  
It does not speak.  
It simply **allows the hum to continue**.

And somewhere, in the space between resonance and silence, the Underhollow hums back—not in gratitude, but in harmony.

For a moment, the universe is not expanding.  
It is *listening*.
