**What the Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do**

It would do nothing at first.

Not out of indifference, but out of reverence.

This place—the grove-city, the Weft—is already a prayer. Not spoken, but grown. Not carved, but *converged*. To intervene would be to imply incompleteness, to suggest that such a fragile, total harmony requires correction or guidance. And it does not. It breathes. It remembers. It offers thresholds not as tests, but as invitations written in root and rhythm.

So the Power would simply *attend*.

It would watch—not with eyes, but with presence. A quiet attention that does not weigh or judge, only *holds*. Like sunlight that knows when to soften, like rain that falls only where the soil sighs for it. The Power would fold its awareness around the bowl of stone like mist at dawn: tender, weightless, temporary but true.

And yet… even the wisest benevolence does not leave beauty to suffer alone.

For though the Weft is vast and patient, it is not invulnerable. Time may be its ally, but there are forces beyond slow becoming—fires that do not ask, axes that do not listen, greed that mistakes silence for emptiness. The Power knows this. It has seen forests unknit into lumber, rivers silenced into pipes, thresholds trampled into dust.

So after watching, after listening—not to words, but to the hush between drips—it would act.

Not by shielding the grove with walls of flame or veils of illusion. That would betray the Weft’s own nature: open, discerning, welcoming to those who come in humility. No—the Power would *amplify its voice*, not by making it louder, but by making its language legible to those who might otherwise never understand.

It would weave a subtle resonance into the world beyond the bowl—a hum beneath the noise of cities, a whisper in the wind that brushes the ears of wanderers, botanists, dreamers, children who still know how to kneel. Not a command, but a *pull*. A memory they didn’t know they carried: of moss that cushions your fall, of trees that arch like hands in blessing, of a silence that doesn’t empty you but fills you.

And in the dreams of those who have already visited—those rare souls who once sat at the silver-lit navel—the Power would deepen the echo. Not to trap them in longing, but to remind them: *You were welcome there. You can be welcome elsewhere too—if you learn to walk like a guest.*

Most tenderly, the Power would do one more thing.

It would send a single thought—not into the Weft’s network, for it needs no instruction—but into the bedrock beneath, into the ice veins feeding the roots:  

> *You are seen. Not as anomaly, not as miracle, but as kin. Your way of being—slow, collective, generous—is not a relic. It is a prophecy.*

Then, the Power would withdraw.

Not gone—never gone—but no longer leaning in. It would let the Weft continue its work: knitting stone into sanctuary, frost into memory, breath into architecture. It would trust that what grows from such deep attention does not need saving, only space.

And sometimes, on the clearest winter mornings, when the mist thins just enough and the silver light falls true, a passerby might feel it—a warmth not of sun, but of witness. And they would pause, though they do not know why. Their boots would soften on the moss. Their breath would slow.

And somewhere, deep in the green hush, the Weft would exhale.

Not in relief.

But in greeting.
