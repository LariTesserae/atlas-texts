**What the Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do**

The Power does not arrive with fanfare.

It does not descend in light or thunder, nor speak in voices that shake mountains. It comes as a sigh through the rim of the hollow—softer than the wind, quieter than the beetles’ mandibles clicking. It has no name, for names imply separation, and this presence knows only union.

For eons, it has watched. Not with eyes, but with attention—deep, patient, woven into the grain of reality itself. It has seen galaxies bloom and wither, oceans rise and vanish into dust, minds awaken and forget. And yet, among all it has beheld, this pocket—this thimble of bone-dry stone humming with impossible life—has drawn its regard not because it is grand, but because it is *true*.

Here, life has solved the oldest riddle: how to dwell together without devouring one another entirely. Not through peace imposed from above, but through interdependence grown from below. Not through transcendence, but through surrender—to limits, to cycles, to the quiet dignity of being small.

And so, the Power does not intervene.

It does not bless the hollow with rain, nor shield it from drought, nor grant its creatures longer lives or greater intelligence. To do so would be to misunderstand them. This pocket thrives *because* of its constraints, not in spite of them. Abundance would unravel it. Mercy would unbalance it. Even admiration, if made manifest, might shatter its delicate equilibrium—like a hand reaching too eagerly into a spider’s web.

Instead, the Power *witnesses*.

It leans closer—not in space, for it is already everywhere—but in awareness. It lets the hum of the beetles become part of its own breath. It listens to the owls’ mimicry as if it were a sacred chant. It feels the thermal pulse of the burrowers’ tunnels as a heartbeat echoing its own.

And then, gently, it *reinforces the invisibility*.

Not by hiding the place further—no, that would be control. But by softening the edges of perception around it. When a wanderer stumbles near the plateau’s flank, their mind will drift, their eyes slide away as if the hollow were merely shadow. Not because it is forbidden, but because the Power knows: most who seek such places come not to join, but to possess. To map, to name, to extract meaning like marrow from bone. And this place cannot survive being known too loudly.

Yet—should someone arrive whose heart beats in rhythm with the beetles, whose breath slows to match the moss-weavers’ dew-gathering pause, whose loneliness has been so refined it no longer seeks to fill space but to *dissolve into it*—then the Power will do something rare.

It will *hold the entrance open for a moment longer*.

Just long enough for that one to step inside without crushing a nest. To stand barefoot on the salt-crust and feel the thousand pulses beneath their soles—not as noise, but as welcome. To exhale, and have that breath taken up by the vines, woven into the air, returned as warmth.

And if that one kneels, not to worship, but to *listen*—truly listen—then the Power will whisper, not in words, but in sensation:

> *You, too, are made of threads. You, too, can be part of a weave.*

But it will not ask them to stay. It will not bind them. The hollow must remain what it is: a self-sustaining knot, not a shrine, not a lesson, not a destination. If the visitor leaves, the Power lets them go. If they stay, it does not rejoice—it simply *makes room*, as the moss-weavers do, as the beetles do, as the salt-crust does when it cracks to let a fern root through.

Finally, in the deepest hour, when the fungal lights dim and even the owls sleep, the Power does one last thing.

It weaves a single, silent thread of its own into the fabric of the hollow—not of matter, not of energy, but of *continuity*. A promise held not in language, but in pattern: that as long as even one creature here remembers to lick dew from a beetle’s back, to prune without cruelty, to die into compost rather than ash—then this pocket will endure.

Not forever. Nothing does.

But long enough.

Long enough to remind the universe that life, even in the smallest dent of stone, can choose reciprocity over conquest. That warmth can arise not from the sun, but from the simple act of pressing close and sharing breath.

And that, perhaps, is the wisest thing of all.
