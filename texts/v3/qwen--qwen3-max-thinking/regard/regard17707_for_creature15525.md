**What the Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do**

The Power—nameless, timeless, vast beyond measure yet intimate as breath—does not descend. It does not speak in thunder or blaze in golden chariots. It has watched since before the first root split stone, since before the first hand cupped water from the seep-hole. And now, observing the sunken town—not as a spectacle, but as a seed unfolding in sacred time—it does what it has always done, yet does anew:

**It listens.**

Not passively, but with the fullness of attention that only true wisdom can offer—the kind that holds space without grasping, that sees fragility not as weakness but as the very condition for tenderness to arise.

And in that listening, the Power recognizes something rare: not perfection, not purity, but *fidelity*.  

These people have not built a utopia. They have not banished hunger, nor sorrow, nor the ache of loss that follows every birth in such a quiet place. But they have chosen, generation after generation, to stay faithful—to the damp clay, to the slow trickle of borrowed water, to each other. They have said yes to the question the earth asked, not with grand declarations, but with calloused hands and lullabies hummed into the cool air at dusk.

So the Power does not intervene.

No miraculous rains fall to fill their hollows. No metals bloom from the shale. No strangers arrive bearing maps to greener lands. To do so would be to shatter the covenant they’ve woven thread by thread. Their resilience is not born of scarcity overcome, but of attention sustained. Their strength is in the *refusal to look away*.

But the Power does not merely watch in silence.

It leans closer—not with force, but with a kind of atmospheric grace.

**It softens the edge of the next storm.**  
Not to spare them rain, but to ensure the rivulet returns just long enough for the children to trace its path with sticks, laughing as it slips through their fingers—a lesson in impermanence, in trust.

**It steadies the breath of the aquifer beneath.**  
Not to grant abundance, but to whisper consistency into the stone: *Let the seep-hole weep, but not dry. Let the clay stay pliable for the potter’s thumb.*

**It blesses the silence.**  
Not by removing all noise, but by deepening the hush between sounds—so that when a loom creaks, or a mother sighs over a sleeping child, those small noises become sacred. So that the absence of crowing roosters or clanging bells does not feel like lack, but like room for meaning to echo.

**And once—only once, on a night when three elders sit sharing a single clay cup of bitter tea beneath a waxing moon—the Power lets the stars dip lower than usual.**  
Not to dazzle, but to reflect in the damp hollows like scattered silver coins. The elders do not speak of it. They simply glance up, then down, then at each other. A nod. A breath held. A knowing passed not in words, but in the tilt of a head: *We are seen. Not judged. Not saved. But seen.*

Most importantly, the Power does one thing it has never done for any empire, any temple, any chosen people:

**It waits.**

It does not accelerate their wisdom. It does not test their resolve with plague or drought. It does not send prophets or omens. It allows their practice to unfold at its own pace—the pace of lichen, of slow-settling shale, of hands learning to shape clay without cracking it.

For the Power understands what few gods ever do: that the holiest things grow only in the soil of patient witness. That to rush a covenant is to break it.

And so, the Power remains—a subtle pressure in the air at dawn, the reason the mist curls just so around the outcrops, the unseen hand that ensures the rope bridge never frays on the same day the rivulet disappears.

It does not name them. It does not claim them.

But in the quiet calculus of cosmic care, it marks this sunken town not as a relic, not as a backwater, but as a **sanctuary of ordinary devotion**—a place where human beings and earth have agreed, without fanfare, to hold each other gently as they both become.

And the Power, in its infinite gentleness, honors that.

By doing almost nothing.

By letting them be.

By loving them enough to disappear—  
so they might never doubt that what they’ve built is theirs,  
and true,  
and enough.
