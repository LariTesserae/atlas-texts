What lives in the Hollow Marshes of Kethra is not a people in any conventional sense—not a civilization that builds *upon* nature, but one that *emerges from* it, like condensation forming on the inside of a breath-held world. They are not settlers, not conquerors, not even stewards. They are *continuations*—a slow, deliberate extension of the marsh’s own logic into something that walks upright, speaks in pulses, and dreams in dampness.

They call themselves **the Weft**, though the name is rarely spoken aloud. It is felt more than uttered—a vibration passed through fingertips pressed to shared walls, a tremor in the floorboards at dawn, a ripple in the communal water basins when consensus stirs. The Weft do not see themselves as separate from the slurry beneath their feet or the swarming midges above their heads. To them, identity is not bounded by skin but diffused through the ambient hum of the place: they *are* the resonance between dripping stone and clicking carapace, the interval between biofilm pulse and thermal updraft.

Their bodies bear the marsh’s imprint. Skin is pale, almost translucent, threaded with fine capillaries that flush pink when they stand too long in dry air—a rare event, for they avoid elevation as others avoid the sea. Their eyes are large and lidless, adapted not to focus on distant horizons (there are none) but to detect minute shifts in bioluminescent patterns, the tremor of a thousand tiny feet across the crust, the shimmer of iron-laden vapor curling from a warm pool. They do not blink. Instead, a thin nictitating membrane sweeps sideways across the eye when dust threatens—a reflex honed over generations in a place where even “dust” is damp ash.

Their hands are long-fingered and webbed not with skin but with a delicate, semi-permeable membrane that can absorb moisture directly from the air. They drink rarely from basins; more often, they simply stand in courtyards at twilight, arms outstretched, letting the mineral haze settle into their pores. Their breath is shallow, rhythmic, timed to the subsonic groan of the earth—inhaling as gas bubbles rise, exhaling as they pop in the deep wet pockets below. To breathe out of sync is to feel unmoored, ill.

Children are not born so much as *seeped* into being. Gestation occurs not in wombs but in warm, gel-lined hollows beneath the city’s central domes, where mothers press their bellies against biofilm beds rich in chemosynthetic nutrients. The fetus draws sustenance not from blood alone but from the ambient chemistry of the marsh—iron, sulfur, dissolved silica—its bones forming with a faint crystalline lattice, brittle in dryness, strong in saturation. Infants emerge slick with a mucus that smells of warm pumice and ozone, their first cries not sharp but low and resonant, like the first note of a stone flute.

They do not age as outsiders understand it. There is no wrinkling, no graying—only a gradual thickening of the skin into something like cured leather, a deepening of the hum in their bones. The eldest among them, called **Keepers of the Constant**, spend their final decades embedded in the thickest walls of the city, their limbs fused with the slurry-composite over time, their voices reduced to vibrations transmitted through the very structure of Kethra. When they die, their bodies are not buried but dissolved—laid in shallow basins where biofilms reclaim them, molecule by molecule, returning them to the cycle before decay can name itself.

The Weft do not create art to express individuality but to *maintain resonance*. Their carvings are not images but pressure maps—grooves in volcanic glass that, when stroked, reproduce the exact frequency of a midge swarm in full flight. Their music is not melody but mimicry: ceramic pipes tuned to the harmonic groan of rising gas, chimes made of mineral beads that rattle in the same rhythm as the ground-crawlers. To perform is not to entertain but to *tune*—to align the city’s vibrations with those of the marsh, to prevent dissonance, which they fear more than death.

They have no concept of time as linear progression. Past and future are not arrows but tides—ebb and flow within the same breath. Memory is not stored in minds but in the biofilms, which, when touched in specific sequences, release faint echoes of scent and sound from previous cycles. History is not written; it is *reabsorbed*. A child learning the hum of the deep pools is not studying but *remembering*.

And yet, for all their unity with the marsh, there is one thing the Weft do that seems alien even to their own ecology: they *wait*. Not passively, but with a terrible, patient attention. They gather on the highest platforms at the edge of the basin, where the mineral haze thins just enough to glimpse the stars—not to navigate, for they never leave, but to watch. They watch for a sign they cannot name, a shift in the eternal recurrence they feel must come, though none have ever witnessed it. Some whisper—through hand signs so subtle they are nearly thought—that the marsh is not eternal, only paused. That Kethra is not a city but a *womb*, and that one day, the breath held so long will finally be released.

Until then, they live in the threshold. They build not to last but to *repeat*. They speak not to declare but to harmonize. They exist not as individuals but as nodes in a vast, wet, whispering network—each one a pore in the skin of the world, each one listening, always listening, for the moment the marsh decides to exhale.
