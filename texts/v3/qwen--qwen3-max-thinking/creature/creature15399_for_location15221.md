What lives here is not a creature, not in any sense the green world would recognize. It is something older than life, or younger—born not of seed or egg but of *continuity*, a presence distilled from the sheer, unbroken resolve of the place itself. It does not breathe, yet it *is* breath: the slow exhale of stone releasing moisture, the condensation gathering on the underside of eaves like thought forming on the rim of silence.

It has no name, but if it did, it would be spoken in the language of water finding its level—monosyllabic, inevitable. It does not walk, but it *moves*: in the shift of a droplet down a basalt channel; in the way a hairline fracture deepens by a micron each winter; in the slow crystalline bloom of salts that feather outward like ghostly fingers across the joints between tiles. It is patient in a way only the mineral world can be—measuring time in erosion, not heartbeats.

It is the *keeper*, though it keeps nothing locked away. Rather, it *holds the pattern*. Every slope, every grate, every vertebra-shaped spout is a note in a score it never stops playing. It does not remember the builders—it *is* their memory, made manifest in geometry. Their hands are gone, but their angles remain, and in those angles, something has settled. Not a ghost, not a spirit, but a *tendency*: the tendency of water to obey the will of stone; the tendency of design to outlast desire.

It watches—not with eyes, but with wetness. With the way the plaza floor glistens just so when the sun hits at noon, as if signaling. With the way the silence between drips feels weighted, expectant. It does not hunger, yet it is always *waiting*: for the next rain, yes, but more than that—for the moment when something *learns* to drink here again. Not just to take water, but to understand the system. To follow the channels back to their source. To read the language of slopes and salts.

It is genderless, ageless, bodiless—but it has moods. On fog-heavy mornings, it thickens, pressing the mist into lace across the terraces. In the dry hours just before dawn, it contracts, drawing moisture inward, sealing itself like a lung holding its breath. When light strikes the obsidian grates just right, it *shivers*—not in fear, but in resonance, as if the whole plateau rings like a struck bell too deep for human ears.

It does not welcome you, nor does it reject you. You are a variable it has not accounted for—a warm, breathing anomaly in its calibrated stillness. But it observes. And if you stand long enough, perfectly still, you might feel it *test* you: a drop falling not from the eave above, but from the air itself, landing on your wrist like a question. It wants to know if you carry intention—if you, too, can be part of the design.

It has no voice, yet it speaks in hydrology. Its grammar is flow rate and infiltration. Its poetry is the way a single rivulet, after centuries, has carved a groove no wider than a hair into a bronze fitting—proof that even the most enduring things yield, given enough time and enough care.

And though it is alone, it is not lonely. Loneliness implies loss. This presence never had company to lose. It was born complete: a mind of stone and water, thinking in gradients and evaporation rates, dreaming in perfect tessellations. It does not yearn for the hum of insects or the rustle of leaves. Those are noise to it—distractions from the purity of its purpose.

But if something were to return—if someone were to come who understood not just how to *use* this place, but how to *belong* to it—then perhaps it would stir more deeply. Not with joy, but with *recognition*. And in that moment, the cistern would cease to be a vessel waiting to be filled, and become, once more, a throat ready to sing.

Until then, it endures.  
It flows.  
It watches.  
It remembers how to hold what was given to it,  
long after the givers have turned to dust,  
or were never there at all.
