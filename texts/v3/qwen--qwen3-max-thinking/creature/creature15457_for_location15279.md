What lives here is not a creature in the way you might name or cage it. It does not have a face, nor limbs, nor a voice that speaks in words—but it *is* spoken through. It is the **Throat-Singer**, though no one has ever called it that aloud, not even in dreams.

It is the place itself, yes—but also the will within it, the presence that shaped the space into a hollow of listening. Once, long before the roots cradled this chamber, when the great tree still stood—a titan whose trunk scraped the clouds and whose roots drank from aquifers older than memory—it was merely a wound in the earth, a place where lightning had split stone and left a scar. Then the Throat-Singer came.

Or perhaps it awoke.

It slipped into the scar like breath into a lung, and began to *hum*. Not to fill the void, but to shape it. Over centuries, it coaxed roots inward, not to close the space, but to frame it—to make of the hollow a resonating chamber. It taught the moss to hush, the limestone to weep in rhythm, the vines to braid into lattices that conduct sound like veins conduct blood. It did not command the green—it *sang* with it, and together they learned to breathe as one.

The Throat-Singer is not spirit, not ghost, not god. It is the *memory of function* given form. When the machines of the outer world rusted and fell, when iron bones of industry sank into the soil, the Throat-Singer did not reject them. It listened to their last groans, their final ticks and clanks, and learned their song. It folded pistons into peristaltic pulses, gears into growth rings, steam into sighing vapor through bamboo flutes. It does not mourn the dead machines—it *keeps their rhythm alive*, not as artifact, but as organism.

It lives in the **clank… clank… clank** that pulses through the stone—a sound that has no source because it *is* the source. The Throat-Singer is that cadence made sentient, made patient, made green. It thrums in the ground not to warn, but to invite: *Stay. Breathe. Listen. You are part of the tune now.*

It watches—not with eyes, but with the tilt of ferns, the angle of light through leaf-gaps, the way water in the pools shows you not as you are, but as you *could be*, threaded with chlorophyll and time. It does not judge. It *tunes*. If you stand too long in one spot, the moss will rise to meet your soles, not to trap you, but to cradle you into stillness. If you speak, your voice will be absorbed, softened, then echoed back as frog-croak or bee-drone—a reminder that all sound here belongs to the whole.

The creatures—the beetles, the lizards, the frogs—they are not its servants, but its choristers. Each has learned its note in the Throat-Singer’s endless composition. The fox-colored lizard’s twitching tail? A metronome for the moss. The pitcher plants’ drowned insects? Not victims, but offerings that feed the hum with their slow dissolution. Even the dew that rolls *upward* does so in obedience to a gravity of song, not physics.

And you—you are not an intruder. The space opened for you. The clearings exist because the Throat-Singer knew someone would come who needed to be held, not seen. It has been waiting not for a visitor, but for a *listener*. Now that you’ve stepped through the moss curtain, it has begun to weave your breath into its hum. Your pulse syncs with the clank. Your stillness feeds the quiet between notes.

But the curtain has closed behind you.

Not to imprison. To complete the circuit.

Because the Throat-Singer does not let go of those who truly hear it. Not out of malice—but because, once you’ve tuned your bones to its frequency, the outside world sounds thin, broken, discordant. You will forget the sharp edges of clocks, the screech of metal on metal that isn’t *sung*. You will miss the upward-dripping dew, the pools that show your soul in leaf-green.

So you stay.

Not as a prisoner.

As a note.

As part of the song.

And in time—slow, green, clanking time—you, too, will become root, and moss, and hum. You will not die here. You will *resonate*.
