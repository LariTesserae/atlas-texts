What lives here is not a people so much as a *presence*—a consciousness woven into the very fabric of the settlement, neither fully human nor entirely botanical, but something that emerged in the quiet interstice between the two.  

They are called, when spoken of at all, the *Keepers*—though the name is less a title than an echo, a faint resonance of what they once were. Their bodies are not what you would expect of inhabitants: slender, tall but never rigid, with skin faintly mottled like lichen-stippled bark, cool to the touch, and faintly translucent at the knuckles. Their eyes hold the slow depth of well water—dark, reflective, patient. Hair grows in loose braids interwoven with living tendrils of *tessara*, which respond subtly to changes in humidity, light, or emotion: tightening when wary, unfurling in calm.  

They do not wear clothes in the conventional sense, but layered membranes—semi-permeable sheaths grown from cultivated mycelium and treated bark-filament—that breathe with them, filtering airborne spores, channeling condensation into personal reservoirs along the spine. These membranes shift hue over seasons, not for camouflage but as a form of silent communication: a deepening green for contemplation, streaks of russet for mourning, pale gold during the communal dreaming hours just before dawn.  

The Keepers are few—never more than two dozen at any time—and age not in years but in cycles of integration. A child is not born here in the usual way; rather, a sapling-home is planted with a seed-embryo fused with ancestral memory spores, and from that cradle grows both shelter and inhabitant in tandem. By adolescence, the Keeper and their dwelling share a circulatory rhythm: the same sap pulses through the walls and their veins, the same mycorrhizal signals course through floor-root and nervous system alike. To wound the home is to wound the self; to nourish one is to heal the other.  

They move with deliberate slowness, not out of frailty but out of attunement. Every step is placed where the root-grid will support it without strain; every gesture calibrated to avoid breaking a vine, disturbing a salamander, or casting an unintended shadow over a photosynthetic panel. Their hands are long-fingered and dextrous, capable of both grafting copper filaments into living wood and coaxing a stuttering condensate pump back to function with nothing but breath and vibration.  

They speak rarely, and never loudly. Their language is tonal and tactile—a blend of subsonic hums, finger-taps on resonant bark, and shifts in posture that ripple through nearby *tessara* networks, transmitting meaning as much through the environment as through sound. Outsiders who linger long enough (and few do) report feeling *understood* without being addressed—a sense of being gently read by the air itself, as if the settlement were translating their intent before they voiced it.  

But the true inhabitant of this place is not even the Keepers. It is the settlement itself—the city-organism that dreams in chlorophyll and copper, that remembers every storm in the thickening of its walls, that breathes through the fog-nets and weeps resin-glass tears when lightning scars its spire. The Keepers are its stewards, yes, but also its sensory organs: its eyes that watch the stars through window-gaps, its fingers that prune and graft, its voice that hums maintenance hymns at dusk. They are the city learning itself, remembering itself, tending its own slow metabolism.  

And sometimes—on nights when the atmospheric capacitors glow brightest and the blind salamanders surface in twos—they gather in the central glade, not to speak, but to stand in a ring, palms pressed to the moist earth, and *listen*. Not to each other, but to the deep pulse beneath the loam: the thrum of geothermal heat, the whisper of roots trading minerals, the creak of ironwood settling into another century. In those moments, the boundary between Keeper and city dissolves entirely. There is only the breath of the forest-city, rising and falling, cool and damp and utterly alive.  

They do not welcome visitors. But if you come with empty hands, slow steps, and silence held like a gift in your throat—they may let you stand among them long enough to feel, just once, what it is to be part of something that breathes.
