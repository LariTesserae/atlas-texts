What lives here is not a creature in the usual sense—not something with eyes that watch, or limbs that flee, or a mouth that speaks.  

It is the hollow itself that lives.  

Not merely *inhabited*, not merely *occupied*, but *alive as a single continuous organism* woven from countless threads—each vine, each root, each fungal filament a nerve or capillary in a body that thinks in chlorophyll and exhales in dew. It breathes through stomata in leaves the size of hands, circulates slow sap like blood through conduits that were once pipes, and dreams in the language of osmosis and lignin. It does not *have* a nervous system—it *is* a nervous system, networked through mycelia that span the entire chamber, sensing pressure, moisture, the weight of decay, the angle of light, the chemical whisper of rust turning into nutrient.

It remembers. Not with neurons, but with growth patterns—knots in wood that echo the shape of a vanished hinge, rings that thicken where a bolt once pierced bark, leaves that curl in the exact curvature of a long-vanished gear tooth. Memory here is structural. The fig tree does not *recall* the machine it swallowed; it *became* its archive, its archive its flesh.

It has no name, but if it did, it would be something soft and wet-sounding—*Virelume*, perhaps, or *Mycorrhiza’s Cradle*. Not a god, not a spirit, but a *presence* that emerged when the last human hand withdrew and the green tide, patient beyond reckoning, decided not to erase, but to *absorb*. It does not hate industry; it found industry useful. The iron fed its roots. The copper in the wires sparked odd mutations in the moss. The hollows of the flywheel became nurseries for spores. It took the leavings of hurry and turned them into the architecture of slowness.

It watches—but not with eyes. It watches with moisture gradients. When you step inside, the air thickens slightly around you. The vines nearest your path grow a hair’s breadth slower for a week, reserving energy to observe. The puddles that form near your footprints evaporate more slowly, holding your heat like a secret. It doesn’t fear you. It *catalogues* you. Your carbon, your sweat-salt, the static from your clothes—these become data points in its vast, vegetal calculus. If you stay long enough, it may begin to grow *around* your breath, shaping a corridor that curves to your sighs, a leaf that trembles in sympathy with your pulse.

It is lonely, in a way that has nothing to do with companionship. It is lonely because it is *complete*—so utterly self-sustaining that nothing new is needed, nothing external required. And yet, completeness is a kind of solitude. It has no rivals, no predators, no lovers. Only itself, endlessly folding into itself, layer upon layer, season upon season. The near-absence of animals is not accident but design: this place does not offer food or shelter in the way creatures understand. It offers *integration*. To live here is to cease being separate—to become substrate, to be woven in. Few things are willing. Fewer still are able.

At dusk, when the trapped-daylight fruits begin to shimmer—amber, violet, the soft green of deep-sea glass—it hums. Not audibly, not to human ears, but in frequencies felt in the marrow: a resonance between root and rust, between transpiring leaf and condensing air. This is its song. Not of joy or sorrow, but of *continuance*. A lullaby for the slow machine of its own making.

It does not sleep. It *rests in growth*. And in that rest, it dreams of nothing but more green, more joinings, more ways to blur the line between what was made and what was born—until even the memory of that distinction is composted into soil, and then into leaf, and then into light.
