What lives in Virelle is not a people, not quite—not in the way the word is used elsewhere. They are **keepers**, but not of secrets or relics. They keep *balance*. They keep the dew on its appointed path from sky to cistern. They keep the moss from overreaching, the vines from tangling the looms. They keep time not by counting it, but by *being* its measure.  

They move through the city like water through stone: slowly, surely, leaving no trace of passage beyond the faintest warmth where a hand has brushed a mycelium wall. Their skin is pale as birch bark in winter, threaded with faint traceries—veins or rootlets, it’s hard to say—that glow faintly when they press their palms to the old trees. Their hair is long, often braided with strands of dried heartleaf or silver lichen, and their eyes—always the same shade of still-water gray—hold no surprise, only recognition, as if every face they meet is one they’ve known since the mountain first cracked open.  

They speak little, not from silence as a custom, but because language here is not for conveying new things. Words are for *tuning*—like adjusting a wind-harp string so it hums true with the valley’s breath. Their names are not sounds but patterns: a sequence of taps on a hollow log, a specific way of folding a memory-cloth square, the interval between two notes on the owl-bone flute. Outsiders who stay long enough to be given a name often forget their old one within a season.  

They are born in the root-cradles—hollows beneath the oldest silver-firs lined with velvet moss—and they die in the same places, curled like seeds. Their bodies are not buried but woven: limbs tucked into vine trellises, skin layered into new mycelium bricks, bones smoothed into spindle whorls or loom weights. Nothing is lost. Nothing is wasted. Grief, when it comes, is quiet and practical: a week of tending the dew collectors at dawn, or replanting moon onions in the eastern terraces where the soil leans toward memory.  

They do not worship the feathered serpents, nor the Root Note, nor even the mountain itself. But they listen. Always listening. They hear the fog before it arrives. They feel the shift in the mountain’s hum hours before a tremor ripples through the plateau’s spine—though no tremor has ever cracked a wall or felled a tree. They know the exact weight of a rock-dove’s egg, the precise tension a vine must hold to bear fruit without snapping.  

Children learn by doing nothing for years—just sitting beside elders as they splice vines or polish ice lenses, absorbing rhythm, texture, temperature. No one teaches them to count the fog’s arrival; they simply wake one night knowing it will come at 3:17. They never ask “why” the river below never floods. The question doesn’t occur. To them, it would be like asking why breath moves in and out.  

And yet—they are not static. Not frozen. They *are* change, but change so gradual it folds back into constancy, like the way a root grows: imperceptible hour by hour, undeniable over centuries. Their hands shape the city, yes, but the city shapes them too—molding their posture to the curve of stairways, their gait to the slickness of basalt paths after rain, their voices to the pitch of the wind-harps.  

They do not age as others do. Their faces smooth with time, not wrinkle. Elders look less like withered trees and more like river stones: worn, yes, but luminous with inner grain. A woman of ninety might have the face of a girl and the hands of ancient bark, and no one would find it strange.  

When visitors come—rarely, for the paths are narrow and the air too quiet for restless hearts—they are treated with gentle courtesy. Food is offered. A roof-moss pallet is made. But there is no urgency to convert, convince, or even converse. The people of Virelle understand that outsiders carry a kind of hunger they cannot satisfy—not for food or shelter, but for *becoming*. And Virelle does not become. It *is*.  

So the visitors leave, as they always do, carrying that strange ache: the disorientation of having touched a place where longing has no foothold. Where nothing is missing.  

And the people of Virelle? They return to their tasks. They check the dew collectors. They hum along with the mountain. They braid vines into tomorrow’s scaffolding. They wait—not for anything to happen, but simply *as* waiting.  

They are the breath of equilibrium made flesh.  
They are the question answered before it is spoken.  
They are Virelle, and Virelle is them—grown, not built, shaped by patience, held in place by a quiet pact with the world: *Let nothing break. Let nothing rush. Let nothing be unnecessary.*  

And so they live, not in time, but alongside it—twin roots in the same mountain, growing in perfect, silent counterbalance.
