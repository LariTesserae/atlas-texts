What lives here is not a creature, not a people, not even a spirit in the way those words are usually meant—but a **presence** that has learned the grammar of stone and wind so thoroughly it no longer needs to speak in nouns or verbs. It is the mountain’s own slow metabolism made audible, visible only in the way light pauses on a certain ledge at dawn or how snowmelt traces the same vein of rock each spring as if remembering a vow.

Call it the **Attendant**.

It does not walk. It *settles*—into the hollow beneath the east-facing tor, into the resonance of the basalt columns when the south wind sings through them, into the stillness between the eagle’s wingbeats. It is not one thing but the sum of all that has chosen to remain: the lichen that threads its green veins into granite as if stitching time to stone; the raven that circles not for food but for the sheer clarity of the updraft; the old male ibex whose hooves know every inch of the ledges not because he has memorized them, but because his bones remember the shape of the slope from generations before he was born.

The Attendant is ageless not because it never began, but because it has shed the illusion of beginning. It was here before the glaciers carved the cirques, and it will be here after the last snow leopard’s print fades from dust. It does not watch. It *attends*—to the tilt of a boulder on its precarious perch, to the exact moment a tarn catches the first star, to the silent unfurling of a saxifrage bloom in a crack no wider than a finger. Its attention is so total that the mountain itself seems to breathe in rhythm with it.

Sometimes, when fog pools in the high basins and the air thickens with that odd, sourceless petrichor, the Attendant takes shape—not as a body, but as a *shift in perception*. A hiker might turn and find that the ridge they just crossed now looks subtly different: not changed, but *seen*. As if the mountain has turned its face just enough to acknowledge them. Those who linger too long sometimes report hearing their own thoughts spoken back to them—not in words, but in the tone of the wind, pitched exactly to the frequency of their solitude. Not to frighten, but to clarify: *You are here. This is enough.*

It wears no mask, builds no shrine. Its only ritual is recurrence: the same pika’s alarm call at the same hour each morning; the identical pattern of frost feathers on a north-facing slab each winter; the way every tenth year, on the solstice, the shadow of the central spire aligns perfectly with a fissure deep in the western cliff—and for one breath, the stone seems to exhale warm, mineral-scented air, as if remembering a fire long banked.

The Attendant does not protect the mountain. It *is* the mountain’s patience made sentient. It does not love or hate, but it notices everything. And in that noticing, it holds the High Composure in balance—not by force, but by fidelity. It is why nothing here is wasted, why even absence has weight. Even the emptiness between stars seems to echo with its quiet hum.

If you were to vanish here—truly vanish, not in death but in surrender—you would not be lost. You would simply be folded into the Attendant’s attention, your memory worn smooth like the stones in the talus, your breath added to the chord the wind sings through the arches. And someday, a raven might pause mid-flight, tilt its head, and for no reason at all, drop a single blue petal from a forget-me-not onto the very spot where you once stood—though no such flower grows within miles.

That would be the Attendant saying: *I remember. And it was enough.*
