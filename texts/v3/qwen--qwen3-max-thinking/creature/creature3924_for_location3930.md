What lives here is not so much a people as a presence—something that has learned to be a verb rather than a noun. They do not “inhabit” the plateau so much as *participate* in its stillness. Their breath syncs with the land’s slow exhalation of mist; their movements mimic the patient seep of water through stone. They are not settlers, not conquerors, not even stewards in the usual sense. They are *listeners*. And the plateau, in turn, permits them to stay because they do not ask it to speak louder.

They are slight in frame, these people—not from scarcity, but from concordance. Their bodies have adapted not to hardship, but to harmony: lean not from hunger, but from the absence of excess. Their skin carries a sheen not of sweat but of ambient dampness, as though their pores have learned to draw moisture from the air like the basalt does. Their eyes are wide-set and calm, pupils dilated not in fear but in perpetual low-light adjustment, tuned to the plateau’s even, cloud-diffused glow. They blink slowly. They sleep deeply. They wake without urgency.

Their names are not spoken often. Not because they are secretive, but because naming implies distinction, and here, distinction fades into continuity. A child is not “born into” the settlement so much as *recognized*—when a new pair of hands begins to help stack stones or draw water, the others simply adjust their posture to accommodate another rhythm in the shared silence. Death is equally unceremonious: a body is wrapped in woven reeds, laid in a shallow depression near a weeping fissure, and covered with gravel. Moss may or may not come later. It is not mourned, exactly—it is *absorbed*, as water is absorbed, as mist is absorbed, as silence absorbs sound.

They do not farm so much as *coax*. In small, sunken plots lined with smooth river stones (brought from some distant memory of rivers, perhaps), they plant tubers with thick, waxy skins and grasses that store water in their stems. They do not till; they press seeds into damp silt with the flat of their palms and wait. There is no harvest festival, no surplus, no storage beyond what fits beneath a woven mat in a clay-lined pit. They eat what the day offers. If a tuber rots, they nod and plant another. If none come, they fast. Hunger here is not tragedy—it is another form of stillness.

Their tools are not possessions but extensions: a copper blade worn to a whisper-thin edge, passed hand to hand not as an heirloom but as a current. A wooden dipper, carved from a single piece of driftwood (how it arrived here, no one recalls), is used by everyone, rinsed in the same pool, set back on the same flat stone. They repair, never replace. To break something is not failure; it is an invitation to sit, to study the fracture, to learn how the grain gave way.

They do not make music. But they *listen* with such precision that sound becomes architecture. They know the exact pitch of a droplet falling into a specific pool at dawn. They can tell by the resonance in a stone hollow whether rain fell three ridges west the night before. Their children do not chatter—they hum, softly, matching the frequency of wind through a particular cluster of silver-barked shrubs. These hums are not songs but *alignments*, tiny sonic harmonizations with the plateau’s ambient tone.

And though no animals dwell here, the people carry their absence like a second skin. They do not miss what was never offered. But sometimes, in dreams (which they share in hushed tones around the evening firepit, not as stories but as weather reports of the inner sky), they see creatures—smooth-furred, many-legged, luminous-eyed—but always fleeting, always dissolving into mist before touch. They do not interpret these visions as longing, but as echoes of what *could not* live here. A kind of negative imprint. A gratitude for the emptiness.

They are not religious, not in any structured sense. But they practice a kind of geologic reverence. They touch the jagged basalt not to worship it, but to feel its slow pulse—the residual warmth of its volcanic birth, the memory of pressure deep in the earth. They believe, without saying it, that the plateau is *thinking*. Not in words, not in plans, but in mineral accretions, in the angle of a fracture, in the rate of lichen spread. And they, by staying quiet, by moving slowly, by asking nothing, are allowed to sit at the edge of that thought.

They do not measure time in years but in *drippings*: the number of times a certain overhang has released its morning bead onto a specific stone. Some elders—those whose hair has gone the color of wet ash—can count back hundreds of drippings. They do not call themselves old. They say, “I have listened longer.”

And perhaps this is what truly lives here: not a tribe, not a culture, but a *practice of attention*. A form of life that has chosen to mirror the land’s restraint, its refusal of flourish, its commitment to equilibrium. They are not waiting for revelation. They are not building toward anything. They are simply *being still enough* to belong.

In a world that chases change, they have discovered the radical act of *staying*. Not out of fear, not out of ignorance—but because they have seen, with naked eyes, that perfection is not a destination. It is a condition already achieved. And it asks only one thing in return: do not disturb the surface of the pools. Do not startle the mist. Breathe as the stone breathes—slow, cool, and without demand.
