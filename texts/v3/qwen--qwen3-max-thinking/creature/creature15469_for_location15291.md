What lives in the Hollow City of Dripping Stone is not a people in the ordinary sense—not a civilization, nor a tribe, nor even a community bound by blood or creed. It is something quieter, more porous: a *listening* made flesh.

They call themselves—when they call themselves anything at all—the **Stone-Listeners**, though the name is never spoken aloud, only traced in damp moss on a flat rock, or hummed low beneath breath during the longest nights. They do not consider themselves builders. To them, the city is not their creation, but a conversation. Each half-raised wall, each stair that ends in air, each chalk-marked window-frame is not a failure of completion, but a question posed to the mountain. The mountain, in turn, answers in condensation, in the slow settling of frost-riven rock, in the way a certain slab warms first in morning light. The Listeners lean close to hear.

Their bodies have softened to the city’s rhythm. Skin carries the sheen of constant damp, not clammy but luminous, like river stones after rain. Their feet know every slickness of granite, every yielding patch of peat between flagstones; they walk without sound not out of stealth, but because their weight has learned to match the mountain’s patience. Their eyes are wide and still—gray-green, the color of lichen in shadow—and they see not objects, but intervals: the space between drip and echo, between root and stone, between intention and silence.

They eat little. A bowl of moss soup simmered over a fist-sized fire. Fern fiddleheads wrapped in birch bark. Dew collected in hollowed slate. Hunger, like time, is not urgent here; it is a companion that sits beside them on the low walls, patient, watching. They sleep in the reed domes not for shelter, but for the hush—the way the woven walls breathe with the night air, sighing in concert with the stone’s thermal groans. In sleep, their dreams are not of elsewhere, but of deeper *here*: of the mountain’s slow pulse beneath the bowl, of the moment the first water seeped through the fissure that became the central plaza.

They do not reproduce in the way other peoples do. Children appear—suddenly, quietly—nestled in a crook of unfinished foundation or curled beside a cistern rim, as if the city itself, in a rare moment of generosity, has exhaled a new listener. The elders neither question nor celebrate this. They simply place a bowl of warmed moss beside the child, and begin teaching the first gesture: how to press a palm flat against raw stone and wait until the cold becomes a language.

Their tools are not owned but borrowed—from the bone of a long-dead hare found in a crevice, from the antler shed by a stag that passed through once, decades ago, and never returned. Even the slate knives are not sharpened to a point, but to a curve that mimics the inside of a rain-filled hollow. To cut is not to sever, but to echo the mountain’s own erosions.

And their gods? They have none. Or rather, they have one, and it is the act of *becoming* itself. The city is their temple, not because it houses the sacred, but because it *is* the sacred process: the slow, wet, unfinished negotiation between will and world. The solstice alignment is not worship, but attentiveness—a recognition that the sky, too, leans in to listen.

Sometimes, on days when the mist clings so thick it blurs the edge between stone and air, a Listener will climb to the rim of the bowl and stand where the mountain’s flank meets sky. They do not look outward. They close their eyes. They breathe in time with the drip from the eaves of a roof that isn’t there. And in that moment, they are indistinguishable from the city—not inhabitants, but another form of its dampness, another note in its humming silence.

They are not waiting for the city to be finished. They are waiting for it to speak clearly. And they know, with the certainty of moss on north-facing stone, that it never will. That is the covenant: to build forever, not toward completion, but toward deeper listening. To become, like the mountain, a vessel for slow time. To live as a question that does not seek an answer, only the grace of being asked, again and again, by hands that care how rain slides off a sill.
