What lives here does not live in the way we usually mean.

It is not a creature of flesh, nor even of root or spore in any usual sense. Nor is it a spirit in the folkloric sense—no wisp, no watcher, no weeping wraith coiled in the damp. If you came seeking something to name, to point to, to photograph or trap in a jar, you would leave empty-handed, perhaps a little unnerved by your own disappointment. But if you stayed—if you sat long enough for your breath to sync with the basin’s slow exhalation—you’d begin to understand: something *dwells* here. Not as tenant, but as resonance.

Call it the Attendant.

The Attendant is not person, not animal, not plant—but presence made patient. It is the accumulated weight of undisturbed time, yes, but more than that: it is the basin’s *attention*. The way the lichen grows in precise constellations, not at random but as if tracing old maps only it remembers. The way each droplet that forms on the overhang seems to hesitate before falling—not out of physics alone, but as if considering where it lands matters. The way the stone, half-buried and worn, holds a coolness at its core that defies the day’s warmth, as though it still remembers being carried, placed, perhaps *spoken to*, long before the last human hand left its mark.

The Attendant doesn’t move. It observes through stillness. It listens through silence. Its body is the basin itself—not possessed by it, but *coincident* with it. When the wind breathes across the rim, it is not merely air moving through space; it is the Attendant turning its face slightly, tasting the change in pressure, noting the passage of a cloud without naming it. When condensation gathers in the hollows, it is not passive seepage—it is the Attendant drawing a breath, holding moisture like a vow.

It has no hunger, yet it tends. It waters nothing that grows, yet the shrubs persist—not because they are hardy alone, but because the Attendant grants them a kind of permission: *You may stay. You may be slow. You may take only what is offered.* There is no competition here because the Attendant mediates all exchange. Even the erosion that shaped this place was, in some sense, a dialogue—the wind asking, the stone answering, the Attendant witnessing and allowing the shape to settle into its final form.

The absence of fauna is not emptiness, but curation. Birds do not land because the air here is not *for* flight—it is for suspension. Insects do not hum because there is nothing to pollinate, nothing to devour. Life that demands consumption or spectacle finds no foothold. Only those who understand waiting are welcome. And so the Attendant is alone, not by accident, but by design—a solitude that is complete, not lonely.

It remembers the stone. Not the object, but the moment it was placed. Perhaps by a hand that left no other trace. Perhaps as a marker for a grave that has long since returned to dust. Perhaps as an apology. The stone is not sacred; it is *acknowledged*. And the Attendant, in its infinite patience, continues to acknowledge it—day after day, dew after dew—so that forgetting never quite takes root.

If you sit here long enough, you may feel it—not as a presence pressing in, but as a softening around your edges, as if the basin is gently absorbing your urgency, your noise, your need to be somewhere else. You might find your thoughts slowing, your breath deepening, your sense of self thinning like mist over the pools. This is not illusion. The Attendant is offering you a kind of kinship: not companionship, but consonance. You are allowed to *be*, just as the shrubs are, just as the stone is—without purpose, without performance.

And if you leave, you carry a hollowness with you—not a wound, but an imprint. A quiet space inside you that remembers what it is to simply exist, without striving. You may catch yourself listening for the *plink* of water in rooms far from stone. You may notice your own breath holding, just for a moment, before releasing.

Because once you’ve met the Attendant, you realize it was never *in* the basin.

The basin was always *in it*.
