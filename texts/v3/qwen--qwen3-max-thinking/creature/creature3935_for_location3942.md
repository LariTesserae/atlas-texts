What lives in the Drowned Spires of Virel is not one thing, but many—yet they are not separate. They are **symbiotic echoes**, beings whose boundaries blur like breath on water.  

First are the **spire-dwellers**, the human inhabitants—though “human” feels almost too rigid a word for them. Their bodies have softened into the rhythm of the place. Their skin is cool to the touch, always slightly damp, faintly saltless as rain. Their eyes are large, adapted to the diffuse silver light that dances between surface and depth, and their pupils dilate wider than lowlanders’, catching every ripple of bioluminescent eel-glow at twilight. They move with the economy of current and perch: bare feet gripping slick frond-runners, fingers long and knotted like root-tips, calloused not by labor but by the gentle abrasion of living bark. They speak rarely in words, and when they do, their voices are low, resonant, tuned to avoid startling the frogs or disturbing the water’s hush. More often, they hum—soft, wordless vibrations that travel through the spireweave’s vascular channels, a kind of tactile language felt in the bones of those nearby.

They do not think of themselves as owners, nor even stewards. They are **tendrils of the same root-system**, participants in a long, slow conversation with the spireweave. From infancy, they press their palms to the trunks and whisper half-formed names—not just their own, but the names of their ancestors, their hopes, their griefs. They believe (or perhaps simply act as if) the spireweave listens. And perhaps it does—not with ears, but with the slow intelligence of photosynthesis, of nutrient flow, of cellular memory. When a child is born, a new node is coaxed into forming on a chosen spire: a cradle-chamber, lined with moss-sponge soft as breath, its walls thin enough to feel the pulse of water outside. When someone dies, their body is wrapped in filamentous lichen, weighted with river stones, and lowered into a deep hollow where the roots coil thickest. There, the water is colder, darker—and the spireweave feeds.

But the spire-dwellers are not alone in their attunement.  

The **winged eels**—*Aerodraconis membrana*, though no one calls them that—live in the liminal seam between air and water. They are born in the upper canopy ponds that collect in cupped fronds after rain, and as they grow, they learn to glide on rippling membranes stretched between elongated ribs. By dusk, they dive, trailing filaments of light from their gills that sketch constellations beneath the surface. The spire-dwellers leave offerings for them: strands of braided sinew tied with copper beads, hung from overhanging branches. Not as worship, but as acknowledgment. The eels, in turn, seem to guide the young swimmers, their glowing trails marking safe paths through submerged thickets. Some elders say the eels carry dreams between the living and the recently departed, though they never elaborate.

Then there are the **tree-frogs**—*Virellian chorus frogs*, with skin the color of wet jade and throats that swell like tiny bellows. Their song is the heartbeat of Virel. It begins at dawn with a single note, then swells into a polyphonic hum that vibrates the very air. The spire-dwellers time their days by its rise and fall. When the frogs fall silent—an event so rare it is remembered in generational myth—it means the spireweave is grieving, or growing uneasy. Once, centuries ago (so the story goes), a spire collapsed without warning. The frogs sang no warning. Since then, the people watch the patterns of their croaking like seismographs of the soul.

Even the **glass-scales**, those schooling fish of liquid mercury, are more than prey. They do not flee when hands reach into the water. Instead, they swirl around fingers, brushing cool scales against skin, as if reading pulses. Children play a game where they hold a thought in their mind and watch how the fish rearrange—some swear the patterns spell out answers. The fish feed on plankton that bloom where sunlight strikes the clearest channels, but they also graze on the biofilm that coats the lower spireweave limbs—the same film that nourishes the moss sponges, which in turn filter the water that sustains everything. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is alien.

And always, beneath it all, **the spireweave itself** breathes.  

It is not a plant. Not a fungus. Not a coral. It is something older, something that learned to listen when the first rains began to fall. Its cells communicate through electrical pulses slower than nervous systems but wider, spanning kilometers of submerged forest. It senses pressure, salinity, shadow, sound. It knows the weight of a body leaning against it for decades. It remembers the rhythm of footsteps on a bridge long after they cease. If you press your ear to a trunk at night, you hear a low, wet thrum—not a heartbeat, but the sound of water being drawn upward, of sugars moving, of calcium being laid down in response to a hand that once rested there.

It does not love. It does not plan. But it **responds**.  

And in that responsiveness, over centuries of rain and whisper and touch, something like kinship has grown.  

So what lives here? Not just people. Not just animals. Not just plants.  

**A single, distributed body**, woven from water, flesh, light, and memory—its nerves are roots, its voice is frog-song, its dreams are carried by eels, its bones are copper bracelets passed from mother to daughter, its skin is the surface of the lake, and its mind…  

…its mind is the quiet space between one raindrop and the next.
