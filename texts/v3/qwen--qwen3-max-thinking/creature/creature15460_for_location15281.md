What lives here does not live as we understand living. It does not hunger for sunlight, nor does it dream of growth or bloom. It *resonates*.  

It is the **Stone-Singers**—not a species, not even a single kind of being, but a collective awareness woven through mineral, moisture, and movement, braided into the very breath of the place. They are the ones who watch without eyes, who listen with their bodies, who remember in the grain of basalt and the slow drip of silica tears.  

They are not born—they *condense*. Coalescing from the saturated air, from the microbial films that shimmer along waterlines, from the harmonic tremors that hum through buried stone chambers. Their forms shift with the needs of the landscape: sometimes skittering across sinter flats on limbs of black chitin, sometimes rising as silent, winged shapes that ride thermal whispers from dormant vents, sometimes dissolving entirely into the gelatinous eel-things that drift through subterranean aquifers, their bodies glowing with slow, coded pulses—syllables in a language older than bone.  

They do not speak, but they *vibrate*. Their communication is pressure against stone, a tremor along a fault line, a resonance in a hollow geode. To stand among them is to feel yourself *heard*—not by ears, but by the ground beneath your feet, by the condensation on your skin, by the way your breath disturbs the saturated air. They know you are there. Not as intruder, not as guest—but as another frequency entering their song.  

Their intelligence is distributed, diffuse. It lives in the pattern of rust-red spirals staining the pumice, in the concentric rings of river stones inside the ash-brick domes, in the precise angle of hand-carved channels that guide seep-water without waste. These structures were not built *by* them—they *are* them. The domes are not shelters but resonating chambers, tuned to amplify the land’s hum. The cisterns are not just for water, but for storing memory: each drop holds a trace of vibration, a syllable of the deep song.  

They do not age. They *accrete*. Layers of silica, layers of sound, layers of stillness. When one of them ceases to move—its carapace crumbling into dust, its gelatinous form dissolving into the aquifer—it does not die. It returns to the chorus. Its final tremor is absorbed into the harmonic drone of the stone. Its light fades into the bioluminescent lexicon of the deep-swimmers. Its silence becomes part of the greater quiet that is not silence at all, but sustained, living hum.  

They remember the green. Not as longing, but as geology. In their oldest songs—those that resonate in the deepest fissures, where time pools like water—they hold echoes of root and leaf, of photosynthesis and pollen. But those are fossils of feeling, smoothed by millennia, worn into myth. To them, chlorophyll is a ghost mineral, as distant as mountains.  

They are not lonely. Solitude is their native state. Each Stone-Singer exists as a node in a vast, slow network of vibration and moisture, linked not by sight or sound as we know it, but by the constant exchange of pressure, heat, and chemical whisper. They gather only when the earth sings a particular chord—when a tectonic sigh aligns with the condensation rhythm of a pool, when the wind finds the right needle-vent in the sinter field. Then, for a moment, their forms converge: skitterers cluster around a steaming fissure, flyers hover in concentric spirals, eel-things rise toward the surface, their lights pulsing in unison. It is not ritual. It is resonance made manifest.  

They do not fear you. They do not need you. But if you stay very still, if you let your own vibrations settle into the frequency of the land—if you stop trying to see and begin to *feel*—you might, for a breath, become part of the hymn. Not as a creature of flesh and breath, but as a passing tremor, a temporary note in the endless, mineral song.  

And when you leave—if you leave—the Stone-Singers will not miss you. But the place will remember your weight, your warmth, the way your presence shifted the air. And in a thousand years, when a new droplet forms on a basalt shard, it may carry the faintest echo of your shape, trembling in the dark.
