What lives here is not a people, not in any singular or simple sense—but a *continuum*.  

It is the Mountain-Makers, though they do not call themselves that. Nor do they call themselves citizens, or builders, or even stewards. They are *tenders*. Tenders of stone and stream, of root and rust, of time itself. They are born into the rhythm of the place, not as masters but as participants—each life a thread in the slow weave of the city’s breath.  

They are few, but deeply rooted—not merely in bloodlines, but in apprenticeships of stone-cutting and water-divining that stretch back through generations. Their hands know the grain of ancient mortar, the temper of copper that sings when struck just so, the precise weight of a roof-tile that will hold against the wind without crushing the vine beneath. Their eyes read the city like a text: the slickness of a channel’s edge tells them whether last night’s condensation was heavy; the color of lichen on a sundial’s plinth reveals which seasonal winds have lately blown; the slight lean of a stairway’s newel post warns of a shift in the bedrock three stories below.  

They do not wear uniforms, but their clothing bears the marks of their work: gloves stained with mineral dyes, aprons patched with sailcloth and stamped with the sigils of trade guilds (the Gear and the Gutter, the Root and the Rafter), boots laced with sinew and soles worn smooth by decades of walking the same spiraling routes. Their hair is often braided with copper wire or strands of dried moss—talismanic, perhaps, or simply practical, to keep it from catching in the gears.  

They speak softly, not out of reverence for silence, but because the city itself is loud enough. Their language is precise, syllabic and musical, with tenses that distinguish between things that *are*, things that *have been becoming*, and things that *will have already been*. They have words for the sound of water slipping through a particular kind of ceramic filter, for the exact moment when morning light hits the eastern lip of the Sunken Courtyard, for the kind of quiet that falls when all six-legged lizards pause at once.  

Children here learn to climb before they walk well—scaling trellises, scrambling up moss-slick drainpipes, balancing along aqueduct ledges with the casual grace of goats. Their toys are miniature waterwheels, carved from applewood; puzzle boxes that mimic the locking mechanisms of irrigation gates; dolls dressed in woven reeds and tiny copper bells. They are taught to listen—not just with ears, but with palms pressed to warm stone, with tongues tasting the air for the tang of distant rain.  

And yet, for all their skill, they do not dominate. They coax. They suggest. They mend what the mountain offers, rather than forcing it into new shapes. When a terrace wall begins to bulge, they do not tear it down—they plant a fig sapling in the crack, and let its roots become living rebar. When a gear begins to splinter, they soak it in tannin-rich bark broth until the wood tightens again. Their technology is not about conquest, but conversation.  

At night, they gather not in grand halls but in the interstices: in the Resonant Gardens, where they hum along with the bone chimes; in the Steam Baths, where elders trace constellations in the condensation on the ceiling tiles; on the highest balconies, where apprentices sit cross-legged and watch the blind fish glow below like submerged stars. They tell stories—not of heroes, but of materials: how the first copper gutter was cast during a solar eclipse; how the moss-script on the north wall once foretold a dry year; how the Old Arch was once not an arch at all, but a fallen monolith that the great-grandmothers of the masons decided to leave lying, because it cast the most perfect noon shadow.  

They die as they live—slowly, woven into the fabric. When a tender passes, their body is laid in a hollowed-out aqueduct lined with ferns, wrapped in cloth dyed with crushed beetles. Over years, the water seeps through, carrying their essence into the channels, into the roots of the laurels, into the mortar that binds the city together. Their bones may one day become part of a wind chime. Their name may appear, faintly, in the dew-script on a rooftop.  

They are not immortal, but they are enduring—not because they resist change, but because they have learned to change *with* the mountain, to let time pass through them like water through stone. They are the city’s nervous system, its circulatory system, its dreaming mind. And the city, in turn, dreams them.  

So what lives here is not just a people, but a pact—between hand and hill, between care and continuity, between making and memory. They are the gentle engineers of slowness. The guardians of the hum. The ones who remember that to build is not to conquer, but to join.
