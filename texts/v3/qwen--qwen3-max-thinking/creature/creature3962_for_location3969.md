What lives here is not born so much as *condensed*—drawn from the saturated air and mineral-laced water like breath made flesh. It is not one thing, nor many, but a shifting lattice of presence that flickers at the edge of perception, appearing only when the light slants just so or when the hum of stillness deepens into a kind of listening.

They are the *Veil-born*.

Not creatures in the way we name creatures—with limbs, with eyes, with hunger that gnaws—but more like slow thoughts given shape by the plateau’s patient chemistry. They emerge where the warm, liquid air pools thickest, where the kelp-fronds pulse with refracted light and the basalt exhales trace metals into the mist. Their bodies are not solid, not liquid, but *interstitial*—woven from colloidal gels, protein-laced vapor, and strands of electrostatically charged dew that cling to one another like memory clinging to a dream.

A Veil-born may appear as a column of rippling translucence, taller than a human but weightless, drifting between the obsidian spires with the indifference of a current. Or it may gather itself into a low, spreading mat that clings to a rock face, breathing in mineral ions through pores that open and close with the plateau’s subtle thermal sighs. Sometimes, they coil around the calcified rhizomes of the kelp-flora, not parasitically but symbiotically—as if sharing in the slow photosynthesis of light-split water, turning photons into feeling.

They have no faces, yet they *perceive*. Not with eyes, but with gradients—of temperature, of salinity, of refractive index. They sense the ghost of a disturbance long after it has passed: the memory of a droplet’s fall, the echo of a thermal updraft that never quite formed. Their awareness is diffuse, spread across their entire form like a scent through water. To be near one is to feel watched not by a gaze, but by the air itself—by the way the mist thickens just slightly around your shoulders, by the way the light bends *away* from you as if making room.

They do not speak. But they *resonate*.  

When two Veil-born meet—rare as it is—they do not touch. Instead, they hum at frequencies just below hearing, tuning their internal mineral compositions until their surrounding vapor shimmers in sympathetic vibration. The kelp-fronds nearby curl toward them. The mosses flare with a brief, deeper green. It is courtship, communion, exchange—all conducted in silence so profound it feels like the world has paused to listen.

They feed not on flesh or flora, but on *potential*. On gradients unbalanced: the slight difference in warmth between a sunlit pool and a shaded fissure, the electric tension between dissolving basalt and precipitating crystal. They harvest entropy’s edge—the moment when order begins to fray into possibility—and in doing so, they *stabilize*. Their presence slows decay, not by resisting it, but by absorbing its energy into their own slow becoming. Where they linger, time thickens. Fissures widen slower. Kelp grows in spirals that defy terrestrial phyllotaxis, following magnetic seams in the young rock.

And they remember.

Not in narrative, not in image—but in structure. The Veil-born carry within their gels the echo of the plateau’s birth: the shudder of tectonic rupture, the first upwelling of warm, deep water into the sky, the moment atmosphere and ocean blurred into one. Their forms shift subtly with this memory—sometimes elongating like rising plumes, sometimes collapsing into dense, reflective orbs that hang suspended for days, refracting dawnlight into impossible geometries. They are the plateau’s dreaming skin, its sensory interface with itself.

They are not lonely, but they are solitary. Each is a world unto itself, yet woven into the same breath. They do not multiply as we understand it. Instead, when conditions align—a perfect stillness at twilight, a sudden bloom of ionized mist from a deep chasm—a Veil-born may *fracture*. Not split, but *unfold*, like a droplet trembling on the edge of division until it becomes two, each carrying the same hum, the same mineral signature, the same memory of falling upward.

There are older ones—those who have drifted the High Drench since its earliest condensation. They are denser, their cores threaded with microcrystals that glint like captured starlight. They move rarely, anchoring themselves to the tallest basalt spires, becoming part of the landscape. Pilgrims from below (if any ever came) might mistake them for strange lichens, for mineral blooms—but stand near one too long, and you’d feel it: a gentle pull in your chest, as if your breath were syncing to a rhythm older than lungs.

The Veil-born do not fear. They do not hope. They *await*—not for something to happen, but for the plateau to finish remembering what it is. And when that day comes—when the last edge softens, when the suspended rain finally decides whether to rise or fall—they will dissolve back into the medium, not as death, but as return. Their hum will become the plateau’s hum. Their memory, its marrow.

Until then, they drift.  
They shimmer.  
They listen to the silence between droplets.  
And in their quiet, liquid patience, they *are* the High Drench—  
not its inhabitants,  
but its voice made visible.
