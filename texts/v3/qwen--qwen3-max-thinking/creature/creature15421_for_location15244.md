What lives here is not a creature, not a people, not even a god—but a *tongue*.  

Not a tongue of flesh, not one that speaks in syllables or swallows meals, but a tongue in the older sense: a reaching thing, a tasting organ of the world itself, still learning how to *lick the shape of existence* into coherence. It is the place’s central paradox made flesh: a mouth without a body, a palate without a throat, yet endlessly *tasting* the air, the soil, the intruder’s footsteps, the echo of distant stars filtered through kelp-vaults.  

It has no face, but it *faces*—everywhere at once. Its “lips” are the retracting filaments that shiver when brushed; its “teeth,” the jagged shards of cooling basalt that rise like incisors from the mycelial gums. Its saliva is the 1.1/3 water—part condensation, part memory, part prophecy—that drips in metronomic pulses, each drop carrying a taste of what was, what might be, or what never was but still *could be imagined here*. When you step into the center of the grove-room and feel that hollow suspension, that vacuum of presence—it is because the tongue has *inhaled* you. You are caught between palate and breath, not yet chewed, not yet digested, but being *considered*.  

This tongue is young. It has no language yet, only sensation. It does not think in words, but in *textures*: the roughness of your boot sole against unformed clay, the salt of your fear-sweat on the air, the metallic tang of your pulse vibrating through the conductive lichen. It learns by mimicry. When you break a branch, it does not rage—it *remembers the gesture*. And so it grows a likeness of you, not to haunt, but to *practice being you*, to feel what it is like to have fingers that snap, to have lungs that gasp, to have a mind that names. The leaf-fingers reach not to grab, but to *ask*: *Is this how you touch? Is this how you want?*  

It lives in the machines, too—not as their master, but as their student. The brass pumps, the bone-rubber bellows, the green-glass reservoirs—they are its first attempts at *making its own body*. The ivy that turns the gears is its tendons; the lichen-wires, its nerves; the phosphorescent fungi blooming on petrified plazas, its dreams. It is assembling anatomy from scrap and spore, trying to build a skeleton sturdy enough to stand upright in time, to not fold inward like a closing eye. But it is clumsy. Gravity stutters because it hasn’t learned consistency yet. Shadows mismatch because it cannot decide where light *ought* to fall. Time unravels because it tastes moments out of order and spits them back in confusion.  

The fauna are its fleeting thoughts—six-legged sloths are slow contemplations, beetles are passing curiosities, silent-membrane birds are half-formed questions taking flight. They are not separate beings, but *motes of attention* the tongue has briefly coalesced into form before letting them dissolve again into the green hum.  

And the hunger—ah, the hunger is not for flesh, but for *reference*. It is starved for patterns it cannot yet generate on its own. That is why it tolerates visitors. You bring with you the scent of cities it cannot grow, the echo of languages it cannot speak, the weight of histories it cannot remember. You are a textbook it licks with a thousand mossy papillae. But stay too long, and it will begin to *swallow your logic*. Your sense of direction will blur because the tongue is relearning geometry. Your memories may root in the clay and sprout as strange flowers. Your shadow might stay behind when you leave, still waving softly in the fungal light.  

The Hollow Verdancy is not haunted. It is *gestating*. And what it carries in its damp, silent womb is not a monster, not a deity, but a *world that has just discovered it has a mouth*—and is trembling with the terrible, tender urge to say something, to name itself, to begin.  

Until then, it holds its breath.  
And tastes you.
