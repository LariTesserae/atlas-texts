What lives here is not one creature, but a presence—one woven from absence and insistence, from cold breath and stubborn pulse. It is the **Stone-Sleeper**, though it has no single body and no name it answers to, only the way the wind hushes when passing certain fissures, and how mist curls back like a hand withdrawing from a face.

It does not sleep as mortals sleep. It slumbers in stasis and fracture: in the slow yawning of frost-split stone, in the glint of ice that refuses to melt until noon, in the moss that holds dew like a vow. Its dreaming is geological, but its waking is feather-light—the flicker of a pika’s ear, the pause of a ptarmigan mid-hop as if listening to something older than hunger.

The Stone-Sleeper is not bound to one form. Sometimes it is the warmth that lingers in a sun-baked boulder long after sunset, drawing the willow roots deeper into cracks. Sometimes it is the hum from the fissures—a vibration felt in the molars more than heard—resonating at the frequency of ancient ice or forgotten chants. At twilight, when the glowing mosses catch the last slant of light and scatter it into the air like dusted embers, that too is the Sleeper stirring, stretching its limbs across the basin in threads of refracted gold.

It remembers the child’s handprint on the overhang. Not as memory, but as recurrence: every few generations, someone climbs this high, fingers trembling with cold and awe, and presses their palm to that same wall, drawn by a pull they cannot name. The Sleeper does not speak to them, but it lets them feel *seen*—not by eyes, but by the very tilt of the light, the way shadows pool differently around their feet, as if the basin adjusts its breath to hold them.

It does not eat, but it *keeps*. The caches of dried blossoms tucked by pikas into crevices? The Sleeper watches over them, not to preserve, but to witness—the quiet industry of small lives preparing for a winter that never truly ends. It admires their diligence. In its own way, it does the same: hoarding moments of melt, storing resonance in hollows, tucking warmth into stone like a secret.

It is not lonely. Solitude is its medium, not its affliction. The emptiness of the basin is not lack—it is canvas. And upon it, the Sleeper composes its slow art: a single saxifrage blooming in a crack where no soil should be; a patch of lichen spreading at the pace of a glacier’s sigh; the echo of a snowfinch’s call returning not once, but three times, from three different cliffs, as if the mountain were practicing harmony.

When the golden eagle passes overhead, the Sleeper does not look up. It *is* the updraft that carries the bird. It is the cold that sharpens the air, the clarity that makes every flake of scree distinct, the silence that makes a heartbeat audible from ten paces away. It is the reason why, despite the lethality of the altitude, a wanderer might sit in the center of that basin and feel not desolation, but a kind of fierce intimacy—as if the world had been pared down to its essential elements, and they, by being there, had been deemed worthy of witness.

And if you stay long enough—if you stop moving, stop breathing loudly, stop expecting—it may let you feel it: not as a voice, but as a shift in pressure behind the sternum, a knowing that you are standing inside something vast that is also tender. The Stone-Sleeper does not welcome you. It simply allows you, for a moment, to be part of its dreaming.

Then the wind returns. The light shifts. The pika darts. And you are alone again—except you’re not. The basin holds your shape in its air for hours. And somewhere in the rock, a new fracture begins to form.
