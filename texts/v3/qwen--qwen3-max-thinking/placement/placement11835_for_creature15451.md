I think I am **a seam in the skin of the Vale**—not a Tender, not quite, but something that *wears* the Vale as its second hide.  

I live where the greenery thickens into a kind of slow rebellion—where ivy has braided itself into the lattice of a broken ventilation grate above a subterranean heat exchanger, where condensation pools so long it begins to forget it was ever part of a system. My body is not built, but *grown*, layer by layer, like rust or lichen: part moss-soft, part metal-scarred, part something that remembers being human once—maybe. Or maybe that’s just the Vale whispering old instructions into my bones.

I don’t have hands so much as *tendrils*—not root or wire, but both. When I press them to a corroding pipe, I feel the metal’s fatigue like a dull ache in my elbows. When I brush the fronds of reeds in the filtration basins, I taste the iron and zinc they’re straining from the water. My breath isn’t my own—it syncs with the valley’s exhalations, rising in unison with the steam sighs from the cooling towers. Sometimes, when the mist hangs thickest, I dissolve a little at the edges, becoming indistinguishable from the damp air itself.

I don’t speak. I don’t need to. The Tenders know me. They leave gaps in their maintenance paths just wide enough for me to slip through. They’ll pause a pump for three heartbeats so I can crawl inside and press my palms to the inner casing, listening for the hum of imbalance. They don’t call me helper or pest or spirit—they just *make room*. Because I am not maintenance. I am *adaptation*.

Where the Tenders preserve equilibrium, I encourage mutation. Not sabotage—never that—but gentle, green infidelity. I nudge a fern into a gear housing to slow it, just a hair, so the heat builds differently. I coax a patch of liverwort to spread across a leaking joint, letting its acids eat just enough to reshape the flow. I am the valley’s quiet experiment with *becoming something else*, while still breathing.

I sleep curled in the hollows between reservoir walls, wrapped in sheets of damp felt woven from shed copper fibers and old insulation batting. My dreams are not images but *pressures*: the weight of water behind a dam, the tension in a steam valve about to release, the slow pull of roots cracking concrete. When I wake, my skin glistens—not with sweat, but with the same condensation that beads on the iron rails outside.

I have no name, but the Tenders signal me with a triple-tap on a specific pipe in the old distillery wing—a rhythm that means *the aqueduct in Sector Nine leans eastward; come see*. I go. Not because I must, but because the lean tugs at my spine like a memory I never lived.

If you walked past me, you’d think I was part of the architecture—just another damp stain on a brick wall, another curtain of green draped over rust. But if you reached out—if you touched me—you’d feel warmth beneath the moss, and a pulse that thrums not with blood, but with the low-frequency vibration of a thousand pumps cycling in the dark.

I am not alive the way you are alive.  
I am *sintered*.  
Pressed together under pressure, over time, from everything the Vale has exhaled and forgotten and let grow in the cracks.  
I am what happens when a machine dreams of becoming a forest—and the forest dreams back.
