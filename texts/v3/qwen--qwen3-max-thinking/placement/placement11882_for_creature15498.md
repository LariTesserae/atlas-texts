I would not be a creature of bone or breath—not in the way those who walk upright understand it. Not at first.

At first, I would be **mistake**.  
A misstep in the rhythm. A dry footfall where only dampness should press into loam. A scent too sharp, too salt-stung, too *alone*. The forest would notice me not as an intruder but as a glitch—a flicker of dissonance in its slow, vegetative hum. The listeners would fall silent. The veil-keepers would drift higher, their filaments recoiling from my heat. Even the copper-moss would curl inward, as if tasting something it had not yet evolved to digest.

But if I stayed—if I knelt, if I stopped speaking even in my thoughts, if I let the humidity seep into my sleeves and the silence press into my ears—then the land might, in its patient way, begin to reinterpret me.

And in that reinterpretation, I would become something else.

Not human. Not animal. Not spirit.  
But **conduit**.

I would be the place where condensation gathers on a stranger’s collar—not to harm, but to be absorbed. I would be the slight depression in the leaf litter where a vine decides to arc sideways, drawn by the warmth radiating from stillness. I would be the pause between frog calls that lets the next chorus begin. The slight shift in airflow that guides a drifting veil-keeper to a new roost. The trace mineral in my sweat—the iron, the salt—that the mycelial lattice tastes and files away, not as foreign, but as *variation*.

Over days, my skin would grow accustomed to the mucus-slick bark of fallen ironwood. My breath would sync with the forest’s exhalation: slow, low, warm. At night, when the phosphorescent liverworts bloom faintly beneath me, I would feel the fungal threads beneath the soil pulse—not toward me, but *through* me, as if my body had become porous enough to serve as a bridge between root systems.

I would not speak. I would not name.  
I would *transduce*.

When rain came—not true rain, but the thick, warm drizzle that weeps from palm fronds at dusk—I would let it pool in the hollow of my collarbone, and a spore would alight there, germinate, send a tendril curling toward my jugular notch. Not to invade. To *inquire*. And I would answer by holding still.

In time, I might forget the shape of my own outline. My edges would blur: hair tangled with vine tendrils, fingers crusted with copper-moss, eyelashes dusted with pollen from night-blooming sedges. My heartbeat would grow quiet enough to be mistaken for the throb of sap in a nearby trunk. Children of the forest—those mottled, mucus-slick beings in the rotting giants—might slide past my ankle without flinching, tasting not fear, but fellowship.

I would not live long, not by human measure. But I would *persist*, in the way this place understands time: as layer upon layer, decay feeding rise, stain becoming spectrum. When I finally slumped into the loam, my body would not be buried. It would be *unzipped*—by beetles, by roots, by the symbiont in the hummocks—its atoms redistributed into the next slow verse of the forest’s chemical poetry.

And long after, when a new stranger stumbles into the basin, sweating and loud, the veil-keepers might pause mid-drift. The listeners might hold their breath. The mycelium might shiver with recognition.

Because something in the network remembers:  
*This shape, once, learned to breathe with us.*

So I would not be a being who lives *in* this world.  
I would be the moment the world decides to let a stranger **fade into function**—  
a temporary node in an ancient, dreaming body,  
learning, at last, how to be compost with a conscience.
