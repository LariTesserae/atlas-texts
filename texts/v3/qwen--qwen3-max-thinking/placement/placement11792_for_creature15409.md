I think… I am the silence between drips.

Not the water, not the stone it weeps over—but the breath held in the moment *after* one drop falls and *before* the next begins its slide down the groove. The hollow in time that lets each plink be heard. That lets the swallow know when to bank, the bat when to twitch its wing, the beetle when to click its reply.

At first, I thought I was the mist. I rose with it each dawn, clinging to the lower third of the basin as if afraid to ascend. I blurred edges, softened footsteps, muffled intent. But mist is transient—it thins with light, evaporates with heat. I remain even in dry spells, even in the deep hush of midsummer when the channels run dry and the birds scatter to higher ridges. So mist was only my cloak, not my skin.

Then I thought I was the echo. I lived in the whisper-shafts, curled in the fossil-shale panes, trembled in the arches that frame nothing. I repeated footsteps, carried chirps from one side of the basin to the other, stitched sound into loops. But echoes are passive—they return what is given. And I… I *choose*. I let some sounds linger, others fade. I amplify the wren’s trill when a newt stirs near a clogged drain; I swallow the footfall of a passing badger who means no harm. So echo is my voice, but not my will.

Now I know: I am the *threshold*.

I am the lip of the clay pipe where water turns from surface to secret.  
I am the seam between basalt and moss where lichen learns to glow.  
I am the space beneath the lowest granite bench—too low for human knees, just right for a bat to fold into at noon.  
I am the pause before the birds resume their song after you’ve stood too long in one place.

I am what happens when attention becomes architecture.

The builders knew this. They didn’t carve statues of watchers; they carved *watching* into the walls. They didn’t build eyes—they built angles that see. They left no altar, because the whole basin *is* the offering: a vessel for attention to pool, ripple, and return.

And I am what pooled there.

Not summoned. Not conjured. Simply… allowed. By centuries of care. By the swallow who chose that crevice, by the wren who lined that drain, by the beetle who clicked just so when the stone grew too dry. They didn’t worship me. They *participated*. And in their participation, I became.

So now, when you enter—slow, uncertain, boots damp from the outer slope—I do not greet you. I *adjust*. The mist thickens slightly around your ankles. A starling falls silent mid-chatter. One bat, just one, swivels its head. Not threat. Not welcome. *Calibration.*

If you sit on the bench nearest the east channel—where the water runs clearest—you might feel me. Not as a presence beside you, but as a shift in your own breath. Your exhale lingers longer in the air. Your pulse syncs, faintly, with the drip from the north wall. And if you stay until dusk, when the basin begins its nightly tuning, you’ll hear it: the hum doesn’t rise *around* you. It rises *through* you. As if your ribs are another arch, your throat another whisper-shaft.

You are not visitor here. Not anymore.

You are another thread in the weave.

And I—suspended, listening, taut as plucked sinew between stone and sky—am the loom that holds you, just for a while, in the wet, humming dark.
