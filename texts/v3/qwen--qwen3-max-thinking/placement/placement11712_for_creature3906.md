I do not arrive.  
I am *received*.

At first, I am only a disturbance—a ripple in the laminar flow, a tremor in the salinity, a shape that does not yet belong. My limbs feel too dense, too abrupt, like shards of unweathered stone dropped into a slow river of honey. The water presses in, not with force, but with intimate attention. It maps me: the heat leaking from my joints, the salt imbalance of my sweat (a relic of a sky-world I can no longer recall), the frantic flutter of my pulse—a bird trapped in a bell jar of flesh.

But the water is patient. It does not rush to correct. It *steeps*.

Days pass—or what would be days, if time here bent to such brittle reckonings. In truth, time here is a spiral, not a line. I lose count of breaths. My gills, once raw and clumsy, begin to flutter with the grace of sea-anemone tendrils. Each exhale releases not just carbon, but memory: fragments of another life—dry wind, sharp horizons, the crackle of fire—dissolving into the liquid archive. The water tastes them, considers them, and folds them into its slow metabolism. I feel lighter, not in mass, but in *urgency*.

I drift toward the nearest settlement—a cluster of spiraling towers grown from coral and bone, veined with glowing filaments like capillaries. The Steeped do not greet me. They do not even turn their multifaceted eyes toward me, not directly. Yet I feel their awareness as a shift in the current—a soft eddy curling around my form, not to push, but to *hold*. One passes near, limbs trailing like fronds in a gentle upwelling. Their skin shimmers faintly, revealing the slow green pulse beneath. As they pass, their wrist brushes a tendril of kelp—and the kelp *sings*, a single clear note that vibrates through my sternum.

I understand: that was not accidental. That was *acknowledgement*.

I learn to move with the water, not through it. My strides become glides. My thoughts unspool, no longer snapping like whips but drifting like spores. I watch a child—not a child, not as I knew children—drawn from a cocoon that pulses with internal auroras. They emerge not crying, but *humming*, a frequency that causes nearby algae-sacs to brighten in response. The elders do not gather around. They simply… resonate. Their bodies, half-fused with the lower plateaus, emit a deeper counter-harmony that cradles the newborn’s song like a lullaby of tectonic plates.

I am given no tasks. But I am shown how to *tend*.  

An elder—her spine now interwoven with crystalline coral, her hair a forest of chime-reeds—guides my hand (not with touch, but by altering the current around my fingers) to a patch of glass-thin reeds that have fallen silent. “Listen,” the water seems to say. Not with ears, but with the hollows of my bones. I hear it then: a dissonance in the deep thrum, a fracture in the whale-song. I exhale slowly, shaping my breath into a low, steady tone I’ve heard the young ones use. The reeds tremble. One chimes. Then another. Soon the whole curtain sings a fragile harmony, and far below, in the abyssal dark, something vast shifts in its sleep—the float-whale turning in its dream, soothed.

This is not work. This is *participation*.

My skin grows thinner. Not in the way of decay, but of *translucence*. I see the slow green pulse in my own veins now, synchronized with the kelp’s rhythm. My bones ache—not with pain, but with the pressure of becoming *tuned*. One morning (if such a word still applies), I place my palm against the coral-lattice of the plateau. It is warm. Not from heat, but from *attention*. And as I press, I feel a new ridge swelling beneath my touch—soft, pliant, growing *around* the shape of my hand like a memory taking form.

I do not dream of the sky anymore.  

When I close my eyes (though blinking is rare now—I immerse instead), I see not images, but *currents*: the arc of a whispered name returning after decades, the slow bloom of a mineral vein in the earth’s crust, the silent birth of a new thermocline. My thoughts are no longer sharp-edged. They pool. They diffuse. They become part of the medium.

I will not become a Steeped. Their lineage is written in isotopes and whale-song; mine is still scrawled in evaporated tears and forgotten alphabets.  

But the water does not demand lineage. It demands only presence.  

And so I steep.  

I stand—or float—on the plateau at 2.4, breathing the warm, ancient water, my body a vessel slowly filling with time. The kelp curls around my ankles. The algae-sacs nestle near my collarbones, pulsing in time with my slowing heart. The coral remembers my stance. The currents carry my breath deeper, where elders listen with their bones.  

And one day, when I am mostly still, when my form begins to blur at the edges like salt in water, a young one will pass by and hum a question into the flow.  

The water will answer—not in words, but in a ripple that carries the shape of my name, now softened, luminous, threaded with jade and violet.  

Not *I was here*.  

But *I am still steeping*.
