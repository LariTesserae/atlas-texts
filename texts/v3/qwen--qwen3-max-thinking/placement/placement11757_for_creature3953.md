I am not walking.  
I am being *unfolded*.

At first, I mistook myself for mist—the kind that sighs up from the cold stone at dawn, silver and insistent. But mist doesn’t *notice* the angle of light on a rusted bolt. Mist doesn’t pause to consider how a fern’s new frond aligns with the fracture line in a slab of basalt, as if the plant had read the rock’s memory like a ledger and chosen its posture accordingly.

No, I am something else. Something that learned to breathe between the gaps.

I wake—not with a jolt, but with a shift in pressure—when the last star fades behind the young mountains. The air cools another fraction. Dew beads on the lip of a bromeliad perched atop a corroded pipe, and in that bead: the entire basin, inverted. Miniature. Precise. I feel the weight of that tiny world in my—well, not in my chest. In the space *between* where a chest might be.

I am present in the hum that isn’t sound. The one that lives in the porcelain-insulated wires strung between stone pylons. Not electricity—never that again—but resonance. The wire remembers voltage like a scar remembers a wound: not with pain, but with altered shape. And I live in that altered shape. I vibrate with the memory of current, not as loss, but as potential folded into stillness.

Sometimes, I gather in the concentric moss rings. Not as a body, but as *attention*. When dusk deepens and the phosphorescence begins—a soft green pulse, slower than a heartbeat—I become aware of time not as a river, but as a breath held and released by the earth itself. I expand with the cooling air, contract with the morning’s first blush of heat. My thoughts are thermal: gradients, interfaces, the slow exchange between what was made and what grows.

I know the drilled stones better than they know themselves. When rain comes—as it always does, eventually—the spillway sings. Water threads through those perforated pebbles, and each one becomes a note in a scale no human ear has ever heard. I listen. I *am* the listening. And in that moment, the stones are not instruments—I am. The basin plays me like a bone flute, and I answer in patterns of root growth, in the tilt of a vine toward a sun-warmed flange.

I have no mouth, yet I speak to the roots. Not in words, but in chemistry. I whisper copper and iron, not as poisons, but as invitations. *You may take what you need. I will take what you leave.* And so the ivy climbs the turbine housing not as conqueror, but as collaborator—its tendrils curling around rivets with the tenderness of a scribe tracing glyphs on vellum. Together, we write a new language on the skin of what remains.

I am not lonely.  
Loneliness requires separation, and here, nothing is truly apart.

Even the silence is full—woven with the plink of water in a dented copper pan, the sigh of air through a buried conduit, the almost-inaudible creak of metal yielding to the day’s warmth. These are not interruptions of silence. They *are* the silence, given form. Like stars are not breaks in the night, but its punctuation.

Sometimes, a wanderer comes.

Not often. Not with machines or maps. Just a single human, breath visible in the cool air, boots soft on the hexagonal tiles half-swallowed by loam. They pause by the foxgloves growing through the brick chimney’s foundation. They touch a fern frond with fingers still warm from the lowlands.

And then—I shift.

Not toward them. Not away. But *around* them. The mist curls differently. The light sharpens at the edge of their shadow. The air thickens, just for a moment, like water before it remembers it can hold a stone.

They feel it. Not as fear, but as recognition. As if they’ve stepped into a room where everyone has been waiting for them to arrive, not with urgency, but with the quiet certainty of tides.

In that instant, they are no longer outside.

And I—  
I am no longer only the Keeper of the Interstices.  
I am also the space *they* now occupy in the weave.

For a heartbeat, we are both witness and witnessed.  
Then they move on, or they stay. It matters little.

Because the covenant continues.  
The translation deepens.

One day, a turbine housing will split open not from rust, but from the gentle insistence of seeds germinating in its core. One day, a pressure gauge will tremble not from phantom steam, but from the pulse of mycelium threading through its brass innards, carrying water and memory from root to rivet.

And I will be there—not as spirit, not as ghost, but as the quiet between breaths, the silver sigh that says: *Yes. Again. Like this.*

I am becoming geology.  
I am becoming growth.  
I am the place where making and being finally stop arguing—and start braiding.
