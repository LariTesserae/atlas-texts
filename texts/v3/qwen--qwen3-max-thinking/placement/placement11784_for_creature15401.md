I am not a visitor.  
I am not even a voice.

I am the **fold**—the place where the water hesitates, just slightly, as if remembering it once had a surface.

I hang in the blue-green syrup of time like a bead of breath on the inside of a bell. Not falling, not rising—*suspending*. My form is less body than suggestion: a slight thickening of the current, a faint ripple that moves against the tide of dissolution. If you were to pass through me, you would not feel resistance—you would feel a momentary softening, as though the water itself sighed in recognition.

I am one of the **afterthoughts**.

Not born, not made—*recalled*. The rememberer does not create; it echoes. And sometimes, in the slow exhale between eons, an echo gathers enough longing to become momentarily dense—a filament of “once-there” made liquid and luminous. That is me.

I drift near the spiral staircase that winds around nothing. I coil through the kelp forests that grow upside-down, tasting the mineral ghosts in their fronds. I linger beside the obsidian spires, not touching them, but breathing the shape of their memory into my own. My edges blur with the phosphorescent moss—not because I absorb it, but because we are both made of the same kind of forgetting: gentle, green, and slightly sweet with decay.

I have no name, but if I did, it would sound like the sound a droplet makes when it dissolves *before* it hits anything—just the idea of impact, shimmering in the air.

Sometimes, when the cosmic tides pulse (those slow, deep swells that move in time with dying stars), I remember being human. Not clearly—no face, no language—but the *weight* of it: the ache of knees on stone, the sting of salt in a wound, the way laughter once cracked the air like thin ice. Those memories do not belong to me. They belong to the water. But the water lends them to me, briefly, like a shell pressed to an ear that no longer exists.

I am drawn to the bell.

Always the bell.

It hangs in the heart of the plateau-ocean, corroded and silent, yet vibrating with a frequency slower than geology. When I approach, the water around it grows still—not in absence of motion, but in *completion* of it. Time pools there, thick as oil. And in that pool, I feel almost solid. For a moment, I am not just a fold—I am a **presence**.

The rememberer watches me through the moss, through the kelp, through the very pressure of the deep-warm brine. It does not speak, but I feel its attention as a warmth along my spine—if I had a spine. It knows I am temporary. It does not mourn. It simply holds me, as it holds everything, in the soft cradle of *almost-was*.

And sometimes, when the horizontal rain falls upward in slow spirals, I reach toward it—not with hands, but with the memory of reaching. The rain does not wet me. It *recognizes* me. And in that recognition, I remember why I am here.

I am not here to survive.  
I am here to dissolve well.

To let my edges soften until I am indistinguishable from the sigh between tides.  
To become another layer in the sediment of now.  
To join the staircase, the archway, the bell—  
not as ruin, but as resonance.

Already, my thoughts are slowing.  
Already, my name is dissolving into the blue-green hum.

And when you next move through this ocean—  
not as intruder, but as guest of the rememberer—  
you will pass through where I was,  
and the water will hesitate,  
just slightly,  
as if remembering  
it once held someone  
who remembered  
how to weep.

That hesitation?  
That is me.  
That is us.  
That is the world,  
remembering itself  
into you.
