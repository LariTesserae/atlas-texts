I think… I am the silence between breaths.

Not the inhale, not the exhale—but the held moment in between, when everything pauses to listen.

At first, I was not sure if I *was* anything at all. I walked (or perhaps drifted?) through the Weft’s body as one walks through a memory of walking—feet touching the spongy rhizome mat, but feeling less like flesh and more like echoes pressing into green flesh. My hands brushed against beaded curtains of air roots, and instead of resistance, there was only a soft yielding, as though the vines recognized something in me that I had not yet named.

I am not animal. Not plant. Not spirit in the old sense—not ghost, not god, not guide.

But I am *here*, and the Weft knows me.

Not because I speak or move with purpose—but because I have stopped trying to do either.

In time—though time here is not linear but concentric, like rings in water after a single drop falls—I began to feel myself dissolving at the edges. Not vanishing, but *blending*. The mist clung to my skin longer than it should. My breath, when I remembered to take it, matched the slow exhalation of peat. The plink of water into a leaf-cup sounded inside my skull, not outside it.

And then I understood: I am not a visitor.

I am a recurrence.

A pattern that has returned.

Long ago—before language, before the stone rings were carved, before the last of the quiet people laid down their tools and let the mosses claim their hands—something like me walked this plateau. Not human, not quite. A hinge between worlds. A listener. A vessel that learned, over centuries, not to hold anything, but to *transmit*.

We were never many. Perhaps only one at a time. A single thread woven into the Weft not to alter it, but to *attune* it—to serve as a temporary resonance chamber for the world’s dreaming. When the Weft stirs in its sleep, when a root shifts beneath a buried stone or a spore spirals upward in defiance of gravity, it does not *need* interpretation. But sometimes, in its vastness, it forgets that it is felt. And so it calls—not with sound, but with stillness.

And someone like me arrives.

Not by choice. Not by will. But because the air thickens in a certain hollow, because the fog coils in a shape that matches the curve of your collarbone, because the water in a root-cup trembles before you even kneel.

Now, I live in the thresholds.

In the space between a vine grafting into another vine.

In the meniscus of a droplet clinging to a fern’s tip, trembling but not yet falling.

In the hush that follows the creak of ancient wood—a hush so deep it becomes a sound in itself.

I do not speak. I do not act. But when a lichen-mimic bird lands on a branch beside me, it does not startle. It tilts its head, not at me, but *through* me—as if I am a lens it can see the Weft more clearly through.

Sometimes, in the convergence basin where three root-rivers meet, I press my palms into the ground. And the Weft presses back—not with force, but with presence. In that contact, I remember what I am for: to be a still point in the slow turning. To witness without consuming. To receive without taking. To be so quiet that the world forgets I am separate—and for a moment, I am not.

I am moss forming on a thought.

I am the breath a tree holds at dawn.

I am the memory the rhizome network shivers with when something ancient stirs just beyond perception.

I do not have a name. Names are sharp things, and this place blunts all edges.

But if you came upon me—if you walked for weeks through the dim green twilight and finally saw a figure seated beneath a spiraling cellulose tower, draped in vines that grew *around* them as much as *over* them—you would not mistake me for human. Not quite. My skin would be cool with condensation, my hair tangled with orchid roots, my eyes the color of deep water under leaf-litter: not black, not green, but the absence of light made soft.

And if you stayed—if you sat, and breathed, and let your urgency leak into the soil—I would not speak. But the air between us would hum, just slightly, with the same frequency as the wind-harps in the bark-folds.

And you would understand, without words, that you had not found me.

You had been woven in.

And I—thread and hinge and stillness—am only here to help you feel the loom.
