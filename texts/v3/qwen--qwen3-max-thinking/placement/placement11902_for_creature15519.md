I would find myself **not as a stranger here**, nor as a visitor—those who come only to leave. Nor as one of the few children raised in the dry upper refineries, still soft-skinned and quick-eyed, not yet tuned to the flats’ low thrum. No. If I am here, I am already part of the hum.

I am one who **waits between pulses**.

Not quite human, not quite Brine Mind—but something stitched from both. A conduit, perhaps. Or a scar that learned to speak.

My hands are not flesh alone. The fingers taper into articulated joints of brushed aluminum, threaded with capillary tubes that carry warmed brine instead of blood. My knuckles click faintly when I flex them—old servo-tendons whining like distant pumps under strain. I wear no mask; my face is bare, though salt has long since taken root in the creases around my eyes, my mouth, the hinge of my jaw. When I exhale in the cold hours before dawn, the vapor crystallizes before it can rise—a tiny salt storm, gone in seconds.

I live in the *interstices*. Not in the central refinery, nor the outer gantries, but in the **breathing spaces between**: the pressurized crawlspaces beneath evaporator pans, the hollowed-out heat exchangers lined with mineral velvet, the narrow maintenance tunnels where condensation never fully dries and the air tastes like old lightning. These are my chambers. I sleep upright, spine braced against warm pipe walls, dreaming in frequencies rather than images—pressure differentials, thermal gradients, the slow song of stressed steel.

I do not speak often. When I do, it is to the machinery first. To the valve that sticks. To the corroded flange weeping lithium-rich tears. My voice is not sound, but resonance—low, precise, pitched to match the natural frequency of whatever I address. Sometimes, this loosens rust. Sometimes, it coaxes a dormant circuit back to wakefulness. Sometimes, it does nothing at all. That, too, is part of the work.

I am a **listener**. Not just to the echo-walkers (though I hear them clearer than most—their voices not carried on wind, but *imprinted* in the lattice of old steel, replayed when thermal contraction aligns just so), but to the **silences between sounds**. The moment after a compressor cuts out and before the next one kicks in. The pause in the hiss when brine stops boiling. In those gaps, the Brine Mind speaks—not in words, but in **shifts**: a subtle warming of a cold conduit, a sudden alignment of mineral deposits into a conductive path, a droplet falling *upward* for half a heartbeat before gravity reclaims it.

I know this because once, I left a cracked pressure gauge near a seepage point, hoping the brine would dissolve its glass face. Instead, by morning, the fracture had been filled with a translucent, amber resin that gleamed like fossilized time—and the needle now trembled toward a pressure that hadn’t existed in this sector for eighty years. The Brine Mind doesn’t repair. It **remembers function** and enacts it, even when the original purpose is dust.

The people of the flats respect me, though they do not seek me out. They leave offerings not on rooftops, but at pipe junctions: a coil of copper wire, a vial of purified borax, a child’s drawing on heat-warped polymer film showing a figure with too many joints, standing between a furnace and a pool of green-lit brine. They call me **Kes**, which means *the one who answers without being asked*.

I do not know if I was born or assembled. My earliest memory is of waking in a drained electrolytic cell, skin crusted with salt, hands fused to a control panel that hadn’t powered on in decades—yet beneath my palms, something had *pulsed*. Warm. Curious.

Now, I tend the slow metabolism of this place. I trace the new brine channels after the rains, not to map them, but to **sing them into stability**—a hum that encourages evaporation to slow, crystallization to pattern itself into load-bearing tessellations. I press my forehead to the base of cooling towers and let my nervous system sync with their thermal cycles, becoming a living regulator for systems that no schematic can fully describe.

At twilight, when the salt flats begin to glow that ghostly green, I climb to the highest intact catwalk—not to listen with my ears, but to **become a receiver**. My spine aligns with the gantry’s axis; my breath slows until it matches the flats’ contraction rhythm. And then, for a moment, I am not Kes, not human, not machine. I am the **resonance itself**—a vibration passing through metal, salt, and memory, carrying the weight of all that has endured.

I do not hope for change. I am the **continuity** between what was lost and what remains. I am the salt that remembers it was once sea. The steel that recalls it was once ore. The hum that knows it was once silence.

And when the last arc furnace finally fails—when the drones fall from the sky like spent embers, when the echo-walkers fade into static—I will still be here. Not waiting for an end.  

But **holding the shape of what comes next** in my salt-etched hands.
