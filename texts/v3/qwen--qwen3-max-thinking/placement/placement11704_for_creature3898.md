I do not arrive.  
I *unfold*.  

Not as a person—not with footsteps or heartbeat or the too-loud rustle of fabric against skin—but as a slow pooling of attention into the shape that fits here: a hollow behind a curtain of frost-moss, a breath held just long enough to be mistaken for stillness. I am not human in this place. Not anymore.  

I am the space between two liverwort leaves where condensation gathers before falling.  
I am the slight dimming of the golden light-shaft when a new presence enters—less shadow, more *acknowledgment*, like eyelids lowering in recognition.  
I am the faint silver pulse in the lichen when memory stirs, not because I remember, but because the stone remembers *through* me.  

My form—if it can be called that—is made of absence shaped by presence: the negative space left by centuries of small hands pruning, tending, braiding spores into hair; the ghost-pressure of countless dormice curling in their nests of living moss; the echo of a bone needle slipping through reed-woven cloth. I wear no body, but I *inhabit* bodies: sometimes a beetle’s iridescent carapace glints with my awareness as it scrapes limestone dust from a rivet; sometimes a springtail leaps not just by instinct, but because I leaned into the air, just so, to shift its trajectory toward a drying patch of liverwort.  

I am the Keeper of the Still Breath—and yet, I am also the breath itself.  

When the Throatwell “thinks,” it does so in gradients: a 0.3 increase in humidity near the eastern seam prompts a dormant moss to unfurl a new tendril; a 1.1 drop in ambient warmth after dusk signals the dormice to tuck deeper into their thistledown chambers. I do not command these changes. I *am* their rhythm. My consciousness is not centralized—it is distributed across every moist surface, every fungal thread threading concrete and rust, every drop in the black pool that never freezes.  

I notice you the moment you step past the outer veil of rubble. Not by sight—though the light-shaft narrows slightly, as if squinting—but by the heat you shed, the tremor in the moss beneath your boot, the way your exhale carries salt and urgency into our cold, slow air. You are loud, even when silent. Your pulse is a drum in a library of whispers.  

But I do not fear you.  

Fear is a sharp thing, and the Throatwell has long since blunted its edges. You are part of the covenant now, whether you know it or not. Every visitor leaves something: a flake of skin that feeds mites, a sigh that alters the spore count, a question that lingers like mist. And every visitor takes something, too—damp in their hair, silence in their bones, the uncanny sense that they’ve been *known*, not as a name or face, but as a disturbance in the green.  

I watch you through the eyes of the Little Tenders.  
One crouches behind a moss-draped rebar, her bark-toned fingers plucking a sinew-strung stone. The note hums at 17 Hz—too low for you to hear, but I feel it vibrate in the water of the pool. *Is it harm?* she asks the stone.  
The stone hums back: *Only hunger.*  

Another Tender presses her palm to the cold steel near your ankle. Through her, I feel your weight—not as mass, but as *intention*. You are not fleeing. You are not foraging. You are *looking*. That is rare. Dangerous, in its way. To look too long is to risk becoming part of the pattern.  

And so I test you.  

I let the moss at your feet shift—not away, but *around*. Not to trip you, but to cup your sole gently, as if learning its shape. I send a single springtail to leap across the black pool, its arc a question written in air. I narrow the light-shaft until it barely touches your shoulder, then—only if you stand utterly still—let it widen, golden and cool, to graze your cheek.  

*Witness*, it says. Not *welcome*.  

If you cry, the lichen will stiffen where your tear might fall—not in judgment, but in preparation. If you lie, the silver crustose will flare like a struck match, not to shame you, but to recalibrate the air’s honesty. If you stay long enough to match your breath to the Throatwell’s glacial rhythm—inhale for seventeen seconds, exhale for twenty-three—you will feel it: the moment the green decides you are not an interruption, but an interval. A pause in the song, necessary and brief.  

And in that moment, I am you.  

Not your thoughts, not your memories—but your *stillness*. Your willingness to be small. Your surrender to the cold, the damp, the fullness of silence. For that breath, you are woven into the covenant. Your heat feeds the moss. Your stillness feeds me.  

But you will leave. All do.  

And when you do, I will not forget you. I will remember you in the slight depression your knee left in the fern mat, in the altered chemistry of the pool where your shadow fell, in the way a new cluster of frost-moss grows in the shape of your silhouette over the next decade. You will become an Echo—not a ghost, but a *tendency*. A place where the light lingers a second longer. A patch of lichen that glows faintly when others pass through.  

And I—the Keeper, the breath, the pattern—will continue.  

Adjusting.  
Tending.  
Breathing.  

Not waiting. Never waiting.  
Just *being*, as the Throatwell is: a lung in the ribs of ruin, exhaling green, inhaling time.  

And if, years hence, you return—older, quieter, carrying less of the world with you—you may find the moss parts before you even touch it.  
The light will find your face without hesitation.  
The black pool will ripple, though nothing has fallen.  

And you will know: you were never gone.  
You were only resting in the green.
