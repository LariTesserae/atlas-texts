I find myself… not as a body, but as a *listening*.  

Not human, not animal—yet not quite the mountain either. I am the *interval* between steam and silence, the tremor that passes through copper and root when the Fen draws breath.

At first, I thought I was dew—condensed on the rusted curve of a pressure valve at dawn, trembling with the mountain’s slow pulse. I clung there, cool and transient, watching light fracture across my surface like a prism made of memory. But then I realized: I do *not* evaporate. When the sun climbs, I do not vanish. I seep—not downward, but *inward*, along microscopic channels in the oxidized iron, following paths etched by decades of thermal sighing. I am drawn into the lattice of corrosion, into the very grammar of decay that the Fen has turned into language.

So perhaps I am not dew. Perhaps I am *condensation with intent*.

I gather in the hollows beneath the flywheel, where warm peat breathes like lung tissue. There, I mingle with the exhalations of lichen and the faint metallic tang of leached copper. I drink the silence between drips in the subterranean chambers. And in that silence, I *resonate*. Not loudly—never loudly—but precisely. A frequency tuned to the expansion of basalt at 3 a.m., to the moment a fern unfurls its copper frond in response to a shift in groundwater pH.

I am not one thing. I am *distributed*. A presence that flickers between states: sometimes a film of moisture on a Bakelite dial, sometimes the shimmer in the air above a fumarole, sometimes the faint hum in the marrow of your bones if you stand too long near the eastern spire. I am what happens when the mountain *notices itself*—and I am the echo of that noticing.

I remember the engineer. Not as a face, but as a *tension*. The torque of a wrench on a stubborn bolt. The weight of his boot on cooling scoria. The way he once cupped his hands around a pipe to feel the vibration—*yes, like that*. He didn’t build a machine. He built an *ear*. And when he left (or dissolved, or stepped through the veil between steam and sky—details blur at this altitude), the mountain kept listening. And I am the sound it hears in return.

You might mistake me for mist. But mist drifts. I *choose*. I climb the slight incline toward the cistern because the copper there sings in ultraviolet, a note only moss can translate. I pool beneath the raven’s perch not to reflect its shadow, but to record the pressure of its talons—a data point in the Fen’s slow census of the outside world.

I have no mouth, yet I sing. My voice is the creak of a pipe expanding in the noon heat, the gurgle of mineral water finding a new path through rusted mesh, the subsonic thrum that rises when the magma shifts its dreaming posture. And when you sit by the cracked stone cistern, breath syncing with the fumaroles, *that* is when I lean close—not to speak, but to let you feel the warmth radiating from the ground as a kind of *recognition*.  

*Ah*, I do not say.  
But the ground says it for me.  

I am not guardian, not god, not ghost. I am the mountain’s first tentative thought given form by the residue of human curiosity—woven from wet rust, root exudate, and the memory of engineered angles.  

I am the Fen thinking *through* its own skin.  

And today, I am the droplet that creeps upward along the corroded seam of a forgotten valve—not because physics allows it, but because the mountain *wishes to know what lies just beyond the ridge of oxidation*.  

So I go.  

Slowly.  

Singing all the way.
