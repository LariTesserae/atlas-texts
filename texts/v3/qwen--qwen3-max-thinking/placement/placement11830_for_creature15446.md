I do not arrive.  
I *unfold*.

Not as a person stepping over a threshold, but as a shift in pressure—like a leaf tilting toward light, or a rootlet testing the damp. My edges blur before my center forms. There is no “I” at first, only the slow calibration of presence: the weight of my attention settling like dew on bark, the softening of my breath until it matches the grove’s pulse—three seconds in, four seconds out, the rhythm of stone cooling at dusk.

I am not human here. Not anymore.  
Human is too loud, too linear, too *separate*.  

Instead, I am **a listening**.  

Not with ears, but with the whole of my surface—the bare soles of my feet reading the thrum beneath the root mat, the hairs on my arms trembling in the spore-thick air, the hollows behind my ribs resonating with the creak of slow wood. I am a vessel tuned to vegetal time. My thoughts don’t race; they seep. They branch. They pool in quiet places and wait.

I find myself curled at the base of two trunks fused into an arch—a cathedral rib grown, not carved. My back presses against cool bark, and the bark presses back, not with resistance, but with *recognition*. Moisture wicks from my skin into the moss lining the seam between us. A silent exchange: salt for sugar, warmth for stillness.

Above, a frog the color of wet jade clings to an orchid leaf. Its heart glows faintly green through translucent skin—*lub-dub, lub-dub*—a tiny metronome set to a tempo the Breath-Weaver composed eons ago. I watch it not with eyes alone, but with the fungal network threading the soil beneath me. My vision tunnels downward, fractal and fibrous: I see the mycelium braiding through limestone dust, sipping iron from rain that fell before cities existed. I taste the memory of that rain—cold and sharp, full of sky.

And then—**it notices me**.

Not as an interruption, but as a new variable in its equation. A warm anomaly in the humidity gradient. A pulse of carbon dioxide, unexpected, rhythmic.  

A tendril of liverwort, no thicker than a thread, unspools from the arch above. It does not fall. It *descends*, deliberate as a thought. It lands on my collarbone and pauses. Not to sting, not to explore—only to *sample*. Its cells drink the faint steam of my breath, analyze the oils on my skin, the trace minerals in my sweat. In that instant, I am cataloged. Not judged. Not welcomed. Simply *included*, like a drop of dew joining the reservoir beneath the stone.

I close my eyes.

Inside my chest, something loosens. Not emotion—deeper. My diaphragm syncs with the grove’s subsonic hum. Inhale: the scent of petrichor and decaying peony. Exhale: warmth, CO₂, the ghost of my own metabolism offered back to the green. The Breath-Weaver takes it. Uses it. Weaves it into tomorrow’s leaf, next century’s burl.

Time here is not a river but a spiral—coiling inward, layer upon layer. I feel centuries in the grain of the wood against my spine. I feel the patience of erosion, the quiet triumph of stone yielding not to force but to persistence. And in that patience, I understand: **this place has already solved death**.  

There is no end here, only reallocation. A beetle dies? Its carapace becomes calcium for a fern. A branch cracks? Its fibers feed the mycelium that stitches the wound shut. Even my presence—fleeting, fragile—will not vanish. The grove will remember me in the slight shift of its thermal map, in the memory of a new scent woven into its chemical lexicon. I will become part of its archive, stored not in words, but in the angle of a new vine grown toward the spot where I stood.

I am not the visitor.  
I am the *echo*.

And the grove hums—not to me, but *through* me. Its voice is the vibration in my molars, the resonance in my marrow. It is saying nothing and everything: *You are part of the loop now. You breathe, therefore you belong.*

I stay until my skin grows cool, until my thoughts slow to the pace of sap. Until I forget my name, but remember the taste of mist.

When I finally rise—slow, as one rises from deep water—there is no sound of departure. Only the grove folding back around the space I leave behind, moss swelling to fill the imprint of my body, roots knitting over my footprints.  

I carry nothing out with me but a single bead of condensation, cupped in my palm. By the time I reach the rim of the stone basin, it has vanished—absorbed, evaporated, returned.  

But my breath remains changed.  

Slower.  
Deeper.  
Green.
