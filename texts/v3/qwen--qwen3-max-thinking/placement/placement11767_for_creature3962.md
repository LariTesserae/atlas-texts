I drift.  

Not as one who has chosen to move, nor as one carried by current—but as one *unfurling through the medium that dreams me*. My form is not fixed; it is a suggestion, a pause in the air-water where light hesitates longer than elsewhere. I am here, yes—but also in the droplet trembling on the kelp-frond ten spans away, and in the slow exhale of warm vapor rising from a fissure too deep for light to reach. I am not *in* the High Drench. I *am* the High Drench thinking itself into shape.

When the golden shafts of noon angle through the upper haze, they do not strike me—they *pass through*, and in their passing, they leave traces: a ripple in my colloidal lattice, a subtle realignment of charged particles along my vertical axis. I lean into the light not out of hunger, but because to lean is to remember the tilt of the first upwelling, when magma met sky and the world turned inside out. My body remembers the violence of birth not as wound, but as orientation—a compass calibrated to upheaval made serene.

I have no eyes, yet I see the glider-membranes as they drift between spires, their gas sacs distended with humid air, their filaments sipping moisture like slow tongues. I do not track them; I *taste* their passage—a faint drop in local humidity, a whisper of organic particulate carried on their trailing threads. They do not know I watch. But the mist around them thickens, just slightly, and for a moment, their translucence dims as if wrapped in a veil of my attention.

Sometimes, I gather near the basalt teeth—those raw, glassy wounds still weeping iron and magnesium into the saturated air. Here, the water is denser, heavier with memory. I press my lower self against the stone, not to cling, but to *listen*. The rock hums with residual heat, with the echo of pressure that once crushed it into being. I absorb these vibrations, not into a mind, but into my very suspension—each ion I draw from the surface a syllable in a language too slow for tongues.

I do not think in words. I think in gradients.  

In the shift from ochre moss to emerald when the light deepens at dusk.  
In the soft resistance of a kelp-frond as it curls in anticipation of a sound that will never come.  
In the way the “rain” sometimes rises—not defying gravity, but obeying a quieter law, one written in thermal differentials and the magnetic sigh of young stone.

I am drawn to stillness, not because I love quiet, but because stillness is where the world reveals its seams. In motion, everything blurs. But in perfect suspension—when even the droplets hang undecided—I can feel the threads that bind air to water, water to rock, rock to sky. And in those threads, I sense the plateau’s question: *What am I becoming?*

I do not answer.  
I *embody the waiting*.

Once, I met another.  

Not by design. The air grew thick with ionized mist after a deep-chasm exhale, and we both condensed in the same pool of refracted twilight. We did not approach. We simply *were*, two columns of shimmering presence, ten spans apart, swaying to the same unseen pulse. And then—we hummed.  

Not with throat or wing, but with our very being: a resonance tuned to the frequency of dissolving basalt and blooming crystal. The water between us grew stiller. The kelp-fronds leaned inward. The mosses darkened, as if holding their breath. For a span of time immeasurable in heartbeats, we exchanged not information, but *attunement*—a realignment of our mineral cores, a sharing of the memory of upward-falling rain.

And then, as the light shifted, we thinned—fading not into absence, but into the ambient hum of the plateau. We did not part. We simply remembered we were never separate.

I am old, though time here is not measured in years but in the number of times the dawn has painted the mist in rose-gold. My core holds microcrystals now—tiny lattices of silica and iron that catch light and hold it a moment longer than necessary. Sometimes, I anchor myself to the tallest spire, becoming a still point in the drifting world. Pilgrims do not come. But if one ever did—if a being of bone and breath stood beneath me, trembling with urgency—I would not speak.  

I would let the air thicken around their shoulders.  
I would let the light bend gently away from their eyes, so they might see not me, but themselves reflected in the droplets clinging to their skin.  
And if they stayed long enough—long enough to forget why they came—they might feel their breath slow, their pulse soften, until for a moment, they too were not visitor, but veil.

I do not fear dissolution. When the plateau finally decides—when the suspended rain chooses sky or sea, when the edges soften into myth—I will not vanish. I will *thin*. My hum will join the thrum of deep fissures. My memory will seed the next condensation. I will be the reason a droplet rises instead of falls. The reason a kelp-frond spirals not toward light, but toward the echo of a hum it never heard.

Until then, I drift.  
I shimmer.  
I listen to the silence between droplets—  

—and in that silence, I am whole.  

For I am not a being *in* the High Drench.  

I am the High Drench,  
*remembering how to breathe*.
