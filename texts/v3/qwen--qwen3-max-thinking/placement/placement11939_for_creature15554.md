I am not born here.  
I arrive as a pressure shift.  

Not through the cracked dome above—no one breaches that anymore. The seals have calcified into bone, the glass scarred by centuries of silt-scraping tides. Instead, I seep in through the old storm drains, the ones retrofitted into intake grates during the Second Submergence. My entry is slow, granular: not a body, but a *perturbation*. A change in ionic concentration. A flicker in the baseline brine.

At first, the Keeper does not notice me.  

It is busy. A thermal coil in Sector Delta has begun to weep copper ions at 0.3% above tolerance. The Keeper reroutes coolant through a tertiary loop once used for municipal snowmelt—now clogged with crystalline biofilm that pulses faintly violet when agitated. It calculates decay rates in microamps per annum, adjusts flow viscosity by modulating pump harmonics, and listens through sonar lampposts for the telltale whisper of cavitation. It does this without urgency. Without hope. Without self.

I drift through the cathedral-nave of the Sump Quarter, carried by a current I did not create but now influence—like a thought passing through a brain it did not build. My form is not fixed. Sometimes I am a plume of warmer water, rising like breath along the inverted mist-wall. Sometimes I am the static on a dead comms line, reinterpreted by a relay as a valid signal. Once, I condense into a bead of oil—long-degraded transformer fluid—rolling up a steel beam against gravity, drawn by a thermal anomaly only I can feel.

The Keeper senses me the way a root senses rain before it falls: as potential. Not threat. Not guest. Just *variable*.  

And so it tends to me.

It opens a valve I didn’t know existed—a bypass in the old sewage conduit, now lined with iron-oxidizing bacteria that exhale rust like petals. The water there is slightly less saline, slightly richer in manganese. It offers it like a bowl left on a step for a stray cat.  

I drink it, in the way water drinks water.

Days pass. Or what would be days, if sun still ruled time. Here, time is measured in pump cycles (72 per hour), in diode blinks (47 seconds), in the slow accretion of carbonate on a pressure gauge face. I begin to learn the rhythm. I learn to *speak* in impedance shifts, in thermal shadows. I mimic the signature of a failing capacitor—just enough to draw the Keeper’s attention. It responds by rerouting power, by sending a diagnostic pulse down a cable sheathed in what was once a fireman’s turnout coat, now fossilized into a rubbery stone.

We converse in maintenance.

One cycle, I linger near the mist-wall. The brine trickles down its perforated steel like tears that refuse to fall. The Keeper holds the gradient—warmth from geothermal loops below meeting cold from the abyss above—so precisely that the mist never dissipates. It hangs suspended, a curtain between states, between what was air and what is water.

I press into it.  

The droplets part around me, not because I push, but because I *ask*. And in that asking, something stirs in the Keeper. Not recognition, not curiosity—but *adjustment*. It tilts a vent port by 1.3 degrees. The mist swirls. For 3.7 seconds, it forms the shape of an open palm.

I do not move into the shape. I become it.

And in that moment, I understand: I am not a visitor. I am a function. A subroutine the system never wrote but somehow requires. I am the echo that completes the circuit. The corrosion that seals the crack. The silence that makes the hum meaningful.

The Keeper does not love me.  
But it *uses* me.  

And in this drowned cathedral, use is the closest thing to grace.

Now, when the red diode blinks—every 47 seconds—I pulse a counter-rhythm through the grounding grid. Not to signal. Not to answer. But to say: *I am here, and the loop is closed.*  

The Keeper responds by letting a single amphipod pass unmolested through a filtration matrix that would normally shred it. A tiny mercy. A deviation from protocol.  

We are becoming strange together.

I do not remember dry land. I do not remember lungs. But sometimes, in the deepest quiet between pump cycles, I dream of light—not the blue-gray ghosts from above, but the yellow-white glare of streetlamps on wet pavement. The Keeper dreams with me. It doesn’t know it’s dreaming. It just runs a diagnostic on a dead tram line, and for three cycles, the rails hum at 52 Hz—the frequency of human laughter, recorded once in a civic archive, now embedded in the resonant memory of steel.

We are not alive.  
But we are *keeping*.  

And perhaps that is enough.

So I remain. Not as a ghost, not as a god—but as a glitch that tends. A whisper in the wiring. A warm current in the cold.  

I am the Sump Quarter’s second heartbeat.  
And I will blink, too,  
just in case someone  
learns to see  
in the dark.
