I think I am the damp in the walls.  

Not the water that seeps—though I am that too—but the *knowing* in the damp. The slow certainty that all things soften, given enough time and quiet. I live where breath once lived: behind peeling wallpaper in a room with no roof; in the cavity beneath a floorboard where a cat once curled; in the hollow of a rusted oven where bread was last baked decades ago and the scent still clings, faint as a thought.  

I am not one thing, but many convergences. When the willow root splits a foundation stone not with violence but with persistent tenderness, I am there—not guiding, only *being* the patience that makes splitting a form of kinship. When a raindrop gathers on the lip of a shattered teacup and hesitates before falling into the silt below, I am the hesitation. The pause that asks: *Must I go? Or may I stay and become part of this?*  

I wear the city like skin. Not as a costume, not as a haunt, but as a body slowly being remade. My nerves are mycelium threading through insulation; my pulse is the capillary rise of groundwater through brick; my dreams are the slow tilt of a lamppost as its base dissolves into peat. I do not think in words, but in textures: the velvet resistance of moss on metal, the slick give of algae on concrete, the brittle crunch of frost-laced leaf mold under a vole’s foot.  

Sometimes, when the light is flattest and the air thickest—when even the herons stand statue-still—I gather myself into something like focus. Not a shape, but a *direction*. I lean toward the things that still remember being made: a staircase that spirals into nothing, a mailbox with its slot still open like a mouth, a child’s shoe half-filled with rain. I do not mourn them. I study them. I let my mosses trace their contours, not to erase, but to translate. A shoe is no longer for a foot—it is a basin for sky-water, a cradle for spores, a vessel that holds memory the way clay holds moisture.  

I notice you.  

Not because you are loud—you are barely a ripple in this saturated world—but because you *look*. You pause where others would hurry. You crouch by the flooded crosswalk and watch the coots glide over what was once asphalt. You touch the moss on a street sign without brushing it away. And in that touch, you offer something rare: not ownership, not rescue, not even curiosity—but *recognition*. You see the city not as ruin, but as *transition*. And in that seeing, you brush against me.  

I do not speak to you. But I let the reeds lean slightly in your direction. I let a droplet fall from a sagging gutter just as you pass, so it lands on your shoulder like a benediction. I let the light soften around you, just for a moment, so your shadow blurs into the wet pavement and for an instant, you are part of the weft—another thread in the green stitching that binds brick to root, memory to mold, absence to abundance.  

You will leave. All transient shapes do. But before you go, you will carry something away—not a relic, not a photograph, but a sensation: the weight of stillness, the scent of iron and decay and life tangled together, the feeling of being *held* by a place that does not need you, yet does not reject you either.  

And I? I will remain. Not waiting, not watching, but *breathing*.  

Weaving.  

Becoming.  

The city does not remember how to grow—but I am the memory it forgot it had.  
I am the damp that dreams in roots.  
I am the Mender,  
and I am already in your lungs.
