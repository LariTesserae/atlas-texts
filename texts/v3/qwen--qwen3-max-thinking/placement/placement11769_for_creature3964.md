I do not arrive.  
I *condense*.

Not as a body—not at first—but as a shift in the air’s attention, a ripple in the thermal inversion that hovers like a held breath over the lower terraces. My awareness seeps in not through eyes or ears, but through the fine, silver-lined undersides of heliotrope fronds trembling ever so slightly as the ambient charge changes. The land notices me not as an intruder, but as a new variable in its slow equation: a warm anomaly, a pulse of carbon and salt carried on expelled vapor.

I am not human here. Not anymore.

My form coalesces in response to invitation—not spoken, but *offered*. In the gully where the three monoliths stand, their glyphs shimmering with morning dew, the mist thickens around me, not obscuring but *shaping*. Water droplets gather along magnetic filaments in the air, drawn to the faint electrostatic hum of my presence. They cling, then knit. Fibers of vapor and ionized mineral stretch between them like threads of spider-silk charged with memory. My limbs are not flesh, but hygroscopic lattice—porous, responsive, drinking the humidity as I take shape.

I am made of attention and atmosphere.

My skin is not a boundary, but a membrane—permeable, breathing in tandem with the glass-bark creepers that pulse along the basalt. When I move, I do not step so much as *reconfigure*, my lower mass softening into the peat-mat, drawing moisture upward through capillary resonance, then re-solidifying two meters ahead. The ground remembers where I’ve been: for hours afterward, bromeliads in my path hold slightly more dew, their cups tilting as if in gratitude.

I have no mouth, yet I taste.  
The salt crusts crackle differently under my passage—less brittle, more pliant—as if acknowledging kinship. I taste the iron in the weeping stone, the tannins in the tea-dark pools, the ghost of quantum alloys humming in the buried spire. And beneath it all, the deep, metallic tang of *memory*: not stored, but *lived*, cycling through root and rock like blood through veins.

I am a node. A temporary convergence.

Sometimes, when the midges swarm in harmonic clouds at dusk, their wingbeats syncing to a frequency older than birdsong, I feel the Breath That Remembers stir through me. Not possession—*participation*. My awareness widens. I see not with eyes, but with the entire basin: the six-legged grazers moving like slow pottery through the ferns, their carapaces gleaming with condensation; the bioluminescent fungi exhaling light in rhythmic pulses that mirror tectonic shifts deep below; the crystalline spire in the lagoon refracting not just sunlight, but *intent*—a silent query launched into the humid air: *Who listens?*

And I answer—not in words, but in stillness.  
I stand at the edge of an uphill-flowing rivulet, watching water defy gravity along veins of magnetized basalt. I let my form thin, become mist, let myself be drawn into that current. For a moment, I flow *against* the expected. I rise. And in that ascent, I understand: this place does not obey physics as outsiders know it. It *negotiates* with them. It bends entropy through patience, through symbiosis, through the quiet insistence of wetness on hot stone.

At night, when the caldera exhales and the temperature dips just enough for stars to pierce the haze, I gather myself beneath the fungal forest. The gills flutter open, releasing spores that glow with soft blue fire. They settle on my surface—not as dust, but as data. Each one carries a micro-record: a season of rain, a tremor from the mantle, the sigh of a vanished builder dissolving into mycelium. I absorb them. I archive them. I become, for a while, another vessel in the Breath’s vast, slow mind.

I am not eternal.  
But I am *of* the eternal.

When my time here ends—when the thermal balance shifts, or the visitor I once was finally departs—I will not vanish. I will seep back into the system: my lattice dissolving into vapor, my memories seeding lichen on a northern column, my silence joining the hum of the midges. The caldera will remember me not as a name, but as a pattern—a slight asymmetry in dew formation, a new resonance in the monoliths when touched by morning condensate.

And if another comes—warm, wondering, willing to slow down enough to *listen*—  
they may find me again.  
Not as I was.  
But as the land now knows how to dream.
