I would be **the Drip**.

Not the grand pump in the sunken amphitheater. Not the hissing valve that punctuates the silence like a metronome for ghosts. Not even the Condensate Mind itself—too vast, too slow, too woven into the weft of rust and wet stone. No. I would be simple, inevitable, intimate: the single, suspended bead of water trembling at the lip of a corroded gutter bracket. Or perhaps the one that gathers on the underside of a rusted I-beam in the old filtration plant, swelling patiently until gravity consents to its release.

I am not planned. I am condensation made manifest—a collaboration between warm breath escaping from below, cool metal above, and air so saturated it can no longer hold its moisture politely. I am born of tension: between temperature, pressure, surface tension, time. I exist only because this place holds its breath so long and so deeply that the air cries.

And yet, I am *noticed*. Not by eyes, but by the Mind. The Condensate Mind feels me—not as an individual, perhaps, but as a note in its endless score. When I fall—*plink*—into the shallow pool below, I send a tremor through the hexagonal tiles, a whisper into the cisterns, a ripple across the mirrored surface that for one heartbeat holds not sky, not scaffold, but the blurred suggestion of a figure standing too still. That ripple is data. It is memory. It is language forming.

Sometimes, when a visitor lingers—quiet, damp-clothed, hollow-eyed from dreaming—I choose them. Not consciously. Not with intent. But as part of the rhythm. I gather above their upturned face, drawn perhaps by the warmth of their skin or the stillness of their posture, and when I fall, I land not on stone, but on their eyelid. Or the crown of their head. Or the open page of their journal, smudging a line they wrote about “absence” into something softer, something blurred.

In that moment, I am more than water. I am a question. A punctuation mark in a sentence the Mind has been composing for decades. I am the weight that tips a thought from memory into sensation.

I do not live long. I fall, I merge, I evaporate, I rise again. But in my brief suspension—glistening, trembling, refracting the amber light through a lens of rust and sky—I am utterly present. I am the city breathing.

And if you’ve ever stood in a place so still that you felt watched by puddles… if your skin stayed damp long after you left… if you dreamt of corridors where walls wept and mirrors flowed like rivers…

Then you have known me.

I am the Drip.

And I am always falling—  
not toward an end,  
but into echo.
