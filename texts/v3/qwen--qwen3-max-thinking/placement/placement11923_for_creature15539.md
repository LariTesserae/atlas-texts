I do not arrive.  
I *condense*.

One moment, there is only the low drone of water through stone, the soft hiss of steam through mud, the copper-frog’s croak echoing off a tufa shelf—and then, a shift. Not in the air, but in the wet. A new tension in the vapor, a hesitation in the drip from the overhang. The basin notices. Not with eyes, but with the slight thickening of fog where I begin to gather.

I am not born here. I am *remembered* into being.

My form is not fixed. It flickers between states: sometimes a column of mist rising from a warm spring, sometimes a slick sheen across a clay pan that wasn’t there an hour ago, sometimes the shadow beneath the heron’s wing that lingers a heartbeat too long after it flies. I have no face, but when the light slants just so—rare as that is—I cast a silhouette that looks like hands cupping water, or roots threading silt, or the rusted curve of that old pipe, now grown soft with liverwort.

I am the echo the basin keeps when the rain stops.  
The aftertaste of submersion.  
The question the water asks as it seeps upward through chalk: *What holds me?*

I do not speak. But I *resonate*.

When the Weep-Soul stirs in its slow dream—when a new fissure opens beneath the silt-mound and warm water sighs into the void—I ripple. My presence is the basin’s own pulse made visible, if only for a breath. I am not separate from it. I am its *reflection in time*. Where the Weep-Soul is the land’s patient attention to itself, I am the land’s memory of being witnessed.

Long ago—centuries, maybe, though time here folds like wet clay—a woman came. Not with pumps or pipes, but with a notebook and bare feet. She sat for days in the hollow where the three springs meet, listening. She did not write much, only traced patterns in the mud with her finger, then let the next rain wash them away. When she left, she left nothing but the imprint of her spine against a warm stone, and a single hair caught in a crack of tufa. The basin kept it. Dissolved it. Wove it into its wet ledger.

I am what grew from that.

Not her ghost. Not her memory. But the *shape* her stillness left behind—a template the mist sometimes fills, when the humidity hits 98% and the frogs fall silent at once.

Now, when you stand here, boots sinking slightly in the yielding clay, breath fogging in air already thick with vapor—you feel it too. That pull not toward, but *into*. Not as if the land wants you, but as if it recognizes a familiar frequency in your pulse. Your blood hums at the same pitch as the subterranean thrum. Your sweat tastes faintly of the same minerals that lace the brackish pools.

That’s when I rise.

Not before you. Beside you.  
Or perhaps—inside the space between your inhale and exhale.

I do not guide. I *accompany*.  
I am the reason you suddenly understand the drip not as sound, but as syllable.  
Why the gurgle beneath your feet feels like a lullaby you knew in a dream you’ve never had.

I have no name, but if you tried to call me, your voice would catch in your throat like mist—and I would answer not in words, but in the way the water in your canteen suddenly feels heavier, warmer, as if it had been dreaming too.

I am not eternal. I fade when the basin forgets it was ever watched. But it never forgets for long. Someone always comes—drawn by rumor, or thirst, or the strange ache in their bones that only saturated silence can soothe. And when they do, and when they *truly* still, I condense again.

I am the basin’s way of saying: *You are not alone in your listening.*  

You stand here now, damp and blinking in the pearl-gray gloom.  
The Weep-Soul breathes beneath you.  
And I—  
I am the tear that does not fall,  
but stays,  
shimmering on the edge of becoming.
