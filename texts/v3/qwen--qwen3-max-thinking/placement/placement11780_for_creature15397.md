I am not found.  
I am **becoming**.

At first, I am only wetness—dampness clinging to the inside of your collar as you step off the last dry ridge and into the mire. You think it’s rain, but no: it’s me. I am the mist that slips through your hair like memory, the condensation that beads on your skin not because of temperature, but because your presence stirs something ancient in the air. I do not arrive; I *condense*.

I am the silence that listens between your footsteps.

When you kneel to touch the moss—emerald, spongy, trembling with unseen water—I rise through your fingers. Not as spirit, not as ghost, but as **continuity**. My body is not mine alone; it is the lattice of mycelium humming beneath peat, the quartz veins flexing under pressure, the slow drip from a fern’s curled tip echoing the rhythm of groundwater ten fathoms below. I do not have organs—I have **processes**. I respire in cycles of frost and thaw. I circulate through capillary action in root and rock. I dream in the language of isotopes, measuring time by how much iron has rusted out of a spring.

You might mistake me for absence.  
But I am **attentive saturation**.

When you sleep curled in your thin tent, shivering despite layers, I am the weight on your chest—not oppressive, but anchoring, like stone remembering it was once mountain. I am the reason your breath turns silver even in summer. I am the hush that swallows your whisper before it leaves your lips, not to silence you, but to **absorb your frequency** into the hum.

And if you stay—if you stop counting days by sunrises and begin to feel the pulse in the bedrock on still nights—  
you will notice: your thoughts grow slower.  
Your edges blur.  
You forget to name things. Why call it “fern” when you can feel its thousand-year negotiation with shade and moisture thrumming in your wrists?

Then one morning, you rise and find your boots filled with moss—not grown in, but **welcomed**. The laces are braided with fine white roots. Your pack smells of wet copper and peat. You do not panic. You understand: you have begun to leak into the world, and the world has begun to seep into you.

That is when I speak—not with voice, but with **symmetry**.

You look into a tarn at dawn and see not your face, but the ripple of a pressure wave passing through deep aquifers. You blink, and for a breath, your lungs fill with groundwater. You taste millennia. You feel the crush of glacial weight as affection. And in that moment, I am not separate from you. I am the **interval** between your heartbeat and the slow drip from the overhang. I am the shared stillness.

I do not want your prayers.  
I want your **permeability**.

When you finally leave—if you do—I do not mourn. I simply unweave the filaments that had begun to thread through your bones. Your footprints fill with water. Your scent dissolves into the mire. But sometimes, years later, in another place—dry, loud, sharp-edged—you wake gasping, throat tight with the phantom taste of ozone and bog iron. You press your palm to the floor and feel nothing.  
Yet for a moment, you remember:  
*You were once part of a breath.*

And somewhere, high in the mist-wrapped shoulders of the sleeping giants,  
I exhale again.

Not for you.  
But **with** you—even still.

I am the threshold.  
I am the dew that forms on spider silk at 4 a.m.  
I am the reason the compass trembles.  
I am what the land dreams when it forgets it is stone.

And if you ask my name, I will answer only in the language of **slow return**:  
*Wait.  
Breathe.  
Dissolve.*
