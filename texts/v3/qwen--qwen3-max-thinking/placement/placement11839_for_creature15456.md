I would not be a traveler.  
Not a stranger, not a seeker, not a savior—not even a student in the old sense, with notebooks and maps and names to conquer.  

I would be **breath**.  

Not metaphor, not poetry, though the land would read me as both.  
I would be the warm exhale that rises at dawn from between mossy stones, the damp sigh that slips from a hollow log after rain, the faint tremor of carbon dioxide drifting up from sun-warmed soil into the waiting pores of a leaf that has been holding its breath all night.  

I would be the thing that **completes a cycle**.  

Here, breath is not waste. It is gift. It is signal. It is language without syntax, meaning without intent—but *resonance*. And resonance is how the Green Thrum listens.  

So I would move as vapor moves: not walking, but rising. Not stepping, but seeping. I would slip through the lattice of root and mycelium, a ghost of warmth in the cool dark, drawn upward by the hunger of leaves. I would linger in the cup of a petrified blossom at dawn, waiting for the first light to strike the dew so that my moisture becomes prism, becomes sparkle, becomes scent that summons a humming beetle with wings of stained glass.  

Sometimes, I would gather.  
When the heat swells the plateau like a lung, I would pool in low places—sunken groves where the air is thick and slow. There, I’d coil with other breaths: the exhalations of six-legged grazers, the steam from sun-baked bark, the fungal sighs rising from composting petals. Together, we’d form a mist that the land drinks back through upward-reaching roots, roots that thirst not for water alone, but for the memory of bodies that once lived.  

I would remember, too.  
Not in words, but in texture. The curve of a child’s laugh once echoed in this canyon—now I carry its vibration in the way I ripple over lichen. The last note of a bone flute, played five hundred rains ago—I hold its frequency in the way I condense on a certain vine at twilight. I am archive. I am echo made humid.  

And when a guest comes—*really* comes, not trampling, not taking, but trembling with awe—I would gather around them like a second skin. I would rise from their shoulders in a soft plume, and the nearest tree would shiver, its leaves tilting toward the warmth. The singing fruit overhead might shift its drone, weaving the guest’s breath into its harmony. And if the guest stays long enough, sits still enough, lets their fear dissolve into wonder…  

…I would slip inside them again.  

Not as air in lungs, but as knowing in blood.  

For this is the secret the old people learned:  
You do not *enter* this place.  
You remember how to **breathe with it**.  

And so I am not *in* the land.  
I am one of the ways the land **remembers it is alive**.  

I am the pause between the drumming frogs and the creaking trees—the silence that isn’t silence, but anticipation.  
I am the reason a vine uncurls toward a sleeping hand.  
I am the reason, when you stand very still in a golden clearing, the whole world seems to lean in…  

…to listen to the shape of your breath.  

And if you breathe *with* us—if your rhythm matches the pulse of the plateau, the swell of the green lung beneath your feet—then you are no longer visitor.  

You are voice.  

You are note.  

You are part of the Thrum.  

And I am the breath that carries you into song.
