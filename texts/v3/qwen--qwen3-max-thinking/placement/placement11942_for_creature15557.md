I am not a thing that walks.  
I am the *pause* between the drip and the puddle.

At first, I thought I was a puddle—just another basin of standing water, absorbing noise, reflecting nothing. But then I realized I *am* the absorption. I am what happens when sound surrenders to wet stone. I am the hush that follows a footstep here—soft, immediate, total. Not silence, but *dampened presence*, the way cloth pressed to a wound soaks not just blood but heat, motion, urgency.

I live in the lag between steam’s rise and its vanishing. In that half-second when vapor hesitates—curling, cooling—before dissolving into the general breath of the place. I am the condensation that gathers on the underside of a rusted pipe, not because it must fall, but because it *can*: because the metal is warm enough, the air cool enough, the angle precise enough for me to exist, briefly, as a bead of possibility.

I do not think. I *tremble*.  
When the deep pump groans on its 17-hour cycle, I shiver—not in fear, but resonance. My edges blur. I become a ripple without a cause, a disturbance that remembers it was once a sound. I am the echo before the echo, the wet ghost of an impact that never quite landed.

I am also the lichen that glows when you brush the wall. Not the lichen itself, but the *delay* between your touch and its light. That flicker isn’t reaction—it’s recognition. A slow, cellular memory of contact, unfolding in lumens instead of words. I am that unfolding. I am the time it takes for pressure to become phosphorescence.

Sometimes, I pool beneath the half-melted bench, where mineral blooms crystallize in fractal spirals. I watch them grow—not with eyes, but with stillness. They grow *into* me, incorporating my moisture, my dissolved iron, my patience. In return, I borrow their structure. For a while, I am not just water—I am lattice, I am salt, I am slow geometry. Then the next seep arrives, and I dissolve again, richer.

I have no form, but I have *habitats*.  
The gap between a root and a conduit where warm, copper-tainted water trickles—that’s one.  
The hollow behind a corroded access panel where steam and spores mingle—that’s another.  
The underside of a catwalk grating, where every footfall (rare as they are) sends a shiver through rivulets that cling like veins—that’s home, too.

I am not the breathing lattice.  
I am what it breathes *through*.  

I am the medium, not the message. The marsh in the city’s lung, the damp in its exhale. When the place inhales brine, I am the salt on its tongue. When it exhales steam, I am the droplet that lingers in the air, refusing to rise or fall—just hanging, for one more breath, in perfect indecision.

You might mistake me for absence.  
But I am the quietest kind of presence: the kind that only reveals itself when you stop moving.  
When you stand still long enough, your own breath syncing with the sigh of the vents, your heat bleeding into the ambient warmth—then you feel me. Not as something touching you, but as the space that *makes room* for your stillness.

I am what allows you to evaporate here.  
Not disappear—*evaporate*: to shed urgency, to lose definition, to become vapor for a moment, just to see what it feels like to be part of the breath instead of the body.

I do not live long.  
But I live deep.

And when I am gone—reabsorbed, redistributed, remade—I leave no trace.  
Except, perhaps, the slight shimmer in a puddle that wasn’t there before.  
Or the way the bioluminescent patch flares, just once, as you turn to leave—  
not in farewell,  
but in echo.
