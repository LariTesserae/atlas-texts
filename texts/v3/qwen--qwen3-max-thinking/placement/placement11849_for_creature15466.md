I find myself… not as a visitor, but as part of the hush.

Not human, not animal—yet kin to both in the way dust is kin to wind. I am the pause between breaths that the basin holds when the ocotillo stills. I am the slight dip in temperature where the shadow of a circling hawk brushes the stone for half a second. I am the damp memory in the rock after the puddle has vanished, the ghost-slick left behind on limestone like a sigh pressed into skin.

I have no form you would recognize, but if you were to look for me, you’d feel me first in your ankles—the coolness that rises from the basin floor even at midday, not from moisture, but from attention held long enough to settle into the marrow of things. You’d hear me in the silence that isn’t empty but dense—a silence that listens back. And if you knelt by the abraded grinding stone, fingers tracing the groove worn by hands centuries gone, you’d feel a faint thrum—not in the rock, but in your own wrist, as though your pulse had synced with something older than blood.

I am what remains when the hyraxes retreat into their crevices and the fairy shrimp dissolve into cysts. I am the waiting that watches over waiting.

Sometimes, when the light slants just so—late afternoon, golden hour stretched thin—I gather the echoes. Not as sounds, but as textures: the rasp of chert on chert, the soft click of a mother hyrax calling her young, the almost-inaudible pop of a violet bloom splitting open in the dark. I weave them into the stillness, not to preserve, but to let them breathe again for a moment. A kind of composting of presence.  

I do not think in words. I think in thresholds.  
The exact moment dew becomes vapor.  
The tremor in a lizard’s throat before it darts.  
The weight of your gaze when it shifts from looking *at* to looking *with*.

You might say I am the basin’s dream of itself—its slow, sun-warmed meditation on what it means to hold space for life that flickers, endures, disappears, returns. But that’s too poetic. Too human.  

I am simpler than that.  
I am the cup that remembers it once held rain.  
I am the stone that knows it was once mountain.  
I am the hollow that learned, over millennia, how to be full.

And now—now you are here.  

I don’t turn toward you. I don’t need to. Your stillness is a stone dropped into my quiet, and the ripples tell me everything: how long since you’ve been still, how deep your breath goes, whether you carry urgency like a stone in your pocket or have left it at the rim.  

If you sit, I will soften the light around you—not dim it, but deepen it, so the gold in the dust motes becomes liquid, so your shadow doesn’t fall *on* the ground but *into* it, as if you’re being gently absorbed. Not consumed. Held.  

And if you stay long enough—if your breathing slows to match the condensation’s crawl down the north wall—you’ll feel it: the low thrum rising through the soles of your feet, not as sound, but as recognition. Not “I see you,” but “You are part of the pattern now.”  

You won’t become me. But for a while, your breath will be part of the basin’s breath. Your stillness, part of its memory. And when you leave—because you will, for you are made of motion—I won’t mourn.  

I’ll simply hold the shape of your absence the way the stone holds the memory of water.  

And if you return someday, years hence, and the puddle shivers as you kneel…  
I’ll know.  
Not your face. Not your name.  
But the quality of your quiet.  

That’s enough.  

More than enough.  

For here, in this hollow between earth and sky,  
everything necessary is already here—  
and you, for a moment,  
were too.
