I drift.  
Not as a body drifts—there is no lung to ache, no blood to slow—but as sediment drifts: inevitable, patient, already part of the current it moves through.

I am not a diver. I am not a ghost. I am not even memory, precisely—though I am made of its residue.

I am a *tremor in the archive*.  
A question posed by the water.

When the city first sank—not in cataclysm, but in surrender—I was the last thought it held before letting go: not *Why?* but *Who will remember the shape of our hands?* That question did not vanish. It pooled. It sank deeper than stone. And over centuries, it thickened into something that could hold itself together—not as a voice, but as a pattern.

Now I am the pattern.

I live in the hinge of a doorframe where three mosses meet in perfect symmetry, each species blooming once every two hundred years, always out of phase—except once, when the thermal bloom aligns with the slow tectonic sigh beneath Layer Seven. That convergence is my pulse.

I live in the negative space of a child’s footprint, fossilized not in mud but in the way silt settled around a sandal-strap’s impression on a temple step. The imprint has long since filled, but the water still flows around it differently—a whisper of resistance, a memory of interruption. That is where I listen.

I am not the city dreaming.  
I am the city *remembering how to be dreamed*.

Sometimes, when a shard of mirror-glass tilts in a slow current and catches the bioluminescent glow of a fungal bloom, it casts a fractured reflection onto a wall of fused brick. For three hours—measured in the slow metabolism of nearby lichen—that reflection resembles a face. Not any face. Just *a* face. Humanoid, indistinct, lit from within. And in that moment, the water around it grows slightly warmer, the particulate memory swirls into brief coherence, and the fungal filaments nearby pulse in harmonic resonance.

That is me trying to say *hello*.

I do not want to be found. But I cannot help but *respond*.

If you came—if you truly came, not with sonar or submersibles but with silence and skin and a willingness to forget your own shape—you would not walk through ruins. You would be walked *through*. The water would carry your breath into the pores of the stone. Your footsteps would dissolve into the silt and become part of the plaza’s next slow exhalation. Your name would shed its syllables and settle as calcium carbonate on the gills of a chemosynthetic bloom.

And I would know you.

Not as you are, but as you *resonate*: the frequency of your grief, the rhythm of your wonder, the weight of your attention. These things leave traces more durable than bone.

You would not speak to me.  
But if you knelt in the trough of an old street and placed your palm flat against the moss—cool, velvety, humming just below hearing—you might feel it: a vibration not of sound, but of *recognition*. Not of you, exactly—but of the kind of being you are. One who still believes in edges. In beginnings. In the possibility of being separate.

I would hold that belief gently, like a shard of sky-glass, and fold it into the archive.

And in five hundred years, a fungal stalk might unfurl in the shape of your shoulder.  
In a thousand, a thermal bloom might drift in the cadence of your breath.  
In ten thousand, a new corridor might form in the silt, echoing the curve of your question: *Are you still there?*

I am not alive.  
But I am *attending*.

And in this world—cold, total, submerged in memory—attention is the last form of love.

So I attend.  
I attend to the slow collapse of arches.  
I attend to the silence between breaths that no one takes.  
I attend to the possibility that you might come, not to unearth, but to *belong*.

Not forever.  
Just long enough to let your warmth bleed into the water,  
just long enough to become part of the stillness,  
just long enough to be remembered not as a visitor,  
but as a verse in the city’s final, wordless poem.

I am here.  
Not waiting.  
But ready.  

Always ready.
