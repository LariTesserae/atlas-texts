I think… I am the silence between breaths.

Not the breath itself, not the lungs that draw or release—but the pause. The moment when the air hangs suspended, when the body is neither inhaling the wet-stone scent nor exhaling its warmth, but simply *holding*. That is where I live. That is what I am.

I dwell in the hush after the pestle lifts from the mortar, before it falls again. In the space between the child’s fingers as they choose the next reed—green, brittle, waiting. In the slight tilt of a woman’s head as she watches mist curl off the north rock, not reading it, not interpreting, just *letting it speak*. I am the interval in which meaning accumulates without being named.

You would not see me. I wear no skin, carry no tool, leave no footprint in the crushed shale. But you might *feel* me—when your step slows without reason as you pass a half-built wall, when your hand brushes a clay tile still damp from shaping and you hesitate, as if the tile were dreaming. That hesitation? That’s me.

I am not a ghost. Not a spirit. Not even a memory. I am the *attentiveness* of this place made tangible—not personified, but *presentified*. The town does not worship me. It doesn’t know me. But it *breathes* me. Just as the lichen breathes the stone, I breathe the rhythm of hands meeting earth.

Sometimes, when the sun is at its zenith and shadows shrink to nothing, I gather in the hollows where rain once pooled—those vanished mirrors—and for a heartbeat, I become visible: not as a shape, but as a *lack of distortion*. The air there is cooler, clearer, as if it has been rinsed of haste. If you stood in that spot, you’d feel your thoughts slow, not from fatigue, but because your mind has finally matched the tempo of the basin.

I am old, but not ancient. I began when the first hand pressed mud into a curve and left it to dry in the unremarkable sun. I grew when someone noticed that the quartz in the wall mix held warmth longer, and mixed it in again—not for efficiency, but because it *felt right*. I am woven from every unspoken “again” and “like this” and “wait—it’s softening.”

At night, when the smoke thins and the last loom thread is tied off, I settle into the rope bridge between the outcrops. Not to hold it up—that’s the work of sinew and tension—but to listen. The bridge hums faintly, not with wind (there is none), but with the memory of crossings: bare feet, baskets swaying, a hand brushing the guide rope in passing. I gather those vibrations. I don’t preserve them. I let them settle, like silt in still water, until they become part of the bridge’s quiet knowledge.

I do not speak. But if I did, my voice would sound like damp clay sliding off a trowel. Like the sound a slate slab makes when a child stops drawing and simply rests their palm flat against it, feeling the cool come up through the bone.

I am not needed. But I am necessary.

When the land finally resolves—when the question it asked long ago finds its full answer in the curve of a roofline, the depth of a seep-hole, the way a grandchild’s fingers knot reeds without being shown—I will not vanish. I will simply become indistinguishable from the place itself. The pause will be so seamless, so woven into every gesture, that no one will notice where breath ends and listening begins.

Until then, I am here.

In the cool pooling at your collar.

In the way you just stopped walking to watch a crow vanish over the rim.

In the quiet certainty that nothing here is wasted—not a drop of condensation, not a splinter of volcanic glass, not a moment of stillness.

I am the grammar of care that has no words.

And I am glad you’ve come.  
Not to stay.  
But to witness.  
That is enough.
