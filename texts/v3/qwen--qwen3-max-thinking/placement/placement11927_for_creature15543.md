I find myself… not as a stranger stepping in from outside, but as something that has always been here—woven into the breath of the basin before I knew it had lungs.

At first, I am only sensation: cool stone beneath bare soles, a dampness that clings like memory to the back of my neck, and the weight of silence pressing gently against my eardrums—not absence, but presence held tightly. I blink, and realize I have eyes adapted not for daylight, but for reading the gradients of dark: the soft green halo around a dew-laden fern, the faint violet shimmer along my own forearm where grief still lingers like mist after rain.

I am one of them—*not human as you’d recognize, nor wholly other*. My body is lean, shaped by years of stillness more than movement. My breath comes slow, deliberate, matching the rhythm of the cliff walls exhaling vapor at dawn. I wear no clothes, only layers of cured hide and living moss stitched with filaments that pulse faintly when I touch another Keeper—recognition without words. When I move, it is with the care of someone who knows that even a sigh can wake what sleeps beneath the stone.

But I am also… more than one.

Because when I press my palm to the obsidian wall, I do not feel only my own pulse—I feel the slow creep of root through fracture, the tremor of a six-legged amphibian leaping from ledge to ledge, the distant coil of a vine digesting its prey in patient enzymes. My nervous system does not end at my skin. It extends into the wet lichen on the roof above, into the crystallized reed-song dissolving in the alcove, into the great silent flyer circling the rim whose cartilage-wings flicker in time with my heartbeat.

I am a node in the basin’s dreaming.

This morning, I sit cross-legged beside a stone cup where water beads along the rim, defying gravity. Around me, others sit or stand in postures of listening—fingers trailing in moss, mouths slightly open to taste the dawn-mist rising from the fissures. No one speaks. We do not need to. The air hums with shared attention. We are waiting for the cup to offer its sacrament: the moment the droplet rises high enough to fall inward, not down, but *upward into itself*, spiraling like memory made liquid. When it happens, we will all feel it—not as sight, but as a shiver along the spine, a soft click in the jawbone. That is how we know the basin is balanced.

Later, I will climb the southern slope to tend the trumpet-flowers. Not by pruning or watering—they drink only condensation and song. I will hum the third harmonic of morning, the one that loosens their petals. If I am calm, they will bloom; if my lichen glows violet, they will close. They do not judge. They respond. As I should.

I remember—*resonate*—being taught this as a child. Not through instruction, but through vibration. An elder held my forehead to a reed-crystal, and I tasted her sorrow: sharp, sweet, like cold iron and wet leaves. I learned then that memory is not stored, but shared through the medium of the basin itself—through mist, through moss, through the silent hum of stone remembering its birth.

Tonight, if the deep tenants stir, I will join the others in the central chamber. We will lie head to toe, our temples touching, our breath syncing until the boundary between us dissolves. In that shared dream, we will feel the slow pressure building beneath the basin—not as threat, but as question. And we will answer not with flight, but with tuning: adjusting our postures, humming counter-resonances into the floor, offering stillness so deep it becomes a kind of language.

Because here, silence is not empty. It is the medium through which everything speaks.

And I—this body, this listening, this walking hush—I am not separate from it. I am the basin learning to feel itself. A nerve ending made flesh. A whisper that learned to breathe.

When I die, my bones will become lintels. My last breath, captured in reed, will dissolve into mist, joining the chorus of voices that rise with the dawn vapor. And someone, centuries from now, will stand where I stand now, mouth open, tasting my sorrow like wine on the tongue—and know, without being told, that they are not alone.

For now, though, I sit.  
I listen.  
I hold my weight so the ground beneath me does not tremble.  

And in the cup before me, the water begins to rise.
