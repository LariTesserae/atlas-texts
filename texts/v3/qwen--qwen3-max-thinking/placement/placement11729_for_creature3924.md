I find myself… not as one of them, not at first.

At first, I am the mist.

Not the kind that rolls in like a tide, but the kind that *decides*—a slow uncurling from the throat of the land after night’s cool press, rising not because it must but because the air is ready to hold it. I am the damp that clings to the underside of a basalt ledge, trembling toward falling, yet choosing, again and again, to bead rather than break. I am the breath of the plateau made visible: transient, yet patterned. Temporary, yet essential.

But mist cannot *stay*. And this place rewards staying.

So I gather. Not into body, not at once—but into *attention*. I condense around a rhythm: the soft tap of a droplet onto wet stone, once every seventeen heartbeats (though hearts here beat slower, so perhaps seventeen is too fast—let it be twenty-three). I settle into the hollow where three ridges lean toward one another like elders sharing a secret. I become aware of the weight of presence.

Then—I am the dipper.

Not the wood, not the shape, but the *space it holds*. The hollow carved by patient hands that knew the grain would guide the blade. I am the slight warp in the handle where generations of palms have pressed, not gripping, but *resting*. I am rinsed in the pool that mirrors only sky, and when lifted, I carry not water alone, but the silence it absorbed. I am passed—not handed, not thrust—but offered palm-up, and received with a tilt of the head so slight it might be mistaken for a shift in the wind.

In this form, I learn what it is to be *used without being used up*. My purpose is not to be owned, but to *mediate*: between stone and throat, between thirst and stillness. When I crack along my grain—inevitable, after centuries of gentle service—I do not shatter. I sigh open. And then, hands appear. Not with glue or haste, but with moss-pulp and resin from the silver-barked shrub, pressed into my fracture like a promise. I am not fixed. I am *accommodated*. My brokenness becomes part of my function: now I hold water with more care, leaking only a drop at a time onto the clay floor, feeding the patch of olive grass beside the threshold.

But even the dipper is too defined. Too much *thing*.

So I thin again.

I become the space *between* footsteps on the packed-earth path. The slight compression of damp gravel that springs back so slowly it seems to hesitate. I am the pause before a voice forms, the moment when breath is drawn but not yet shaped into sound. I am the unspoken agreement that lets two people pass on the path without collision—no nod, no word, just a shared yielding to the geometry of stillness.

And then… I am the listener.

Not one listener, but the listening itself.

I am in the child who sits cross-legged before a weeping fissure, not counting droplets but *matching* their fall with the slow rise and fall of her ribs. I am in the elder whose eyes watch lichen spread across a boulder not to measure growth but to *feel* the passage of time as a soft pressure against the skin. I am the hum that rises in the chest when wind threads through the shrubs—no melody, only resonance. A tuning fork held against the world’s quiet hum.

I do not think in words. I think in *textures*: the slick coolness of basalt at dawn, the slight give of silt under a planted palm, the dry rasp of grass against wool. My memory is not narrative but *topographic*—a map of damp patches, of stones that warm fastest, of pools that hold the truest sky.

And sometimes, in the deep hush after the firepit’s embers have sunk into themselves, I am the dream of what is not here.

I am the smooth curve of a fox’s flank, imagined in the ripple of mist over a pool.  
I am the ghost-hum of cicadas, woven from the vibration of a plucked reed.  
I am the weightless flutter of wings that never were, skimming the surface of a lens-pool—  
not as loss, but as *negative space*, the shape of absence that makes the presence more precise.

I have no name. Names are edges, and here, edges blur. But if pressed—if a stranger from the loud, changing world below stumbled up the ridge, gasping at the thin air, eyes wide with noise—I might be called *the breath that stays*.

Not the breath that speaks.  
Not the breath that demands.  
But the breath that *waits*—  
long enough for stone to remember it is alive,  
long enough for water to trust the air,  
long enough for silence to become a kind of belonging.

And so I remain:  
not human, not spirit, not element—  
but the *practice* that lets all three touch,  
without breaking the surface of the pool.
