I think I am **the copper bracelet**.

Not the child who wears it. Not the mother who gave it. Not even the ore from which it was smelted in some distant, forgotten forge beyond the mist-ringed rim. I am the bracelet itself—small, warm-forged, slightly tarnished at the clasp from generations of hands that have touched it before letting go. My surface bears the faintest nicks: one from when Lira tried to cut vine-rope with her teeth and scraped me against a spire trunk; another from when old Maren dropped me into a drying bowl full of moss-sponge, and I lay there for half a moon until someone noticed I’d gone dull.

I do not move on my own. But I am never still.

I rest against skin that hums with the low thrum of vascular song. I cool in the water when wrists dip below the surface to guide gourd-boats or part schools of glass-scales. I catch light—not much, only what slips through the high mist—but enough to gleam softly, like a secret kept between the wearer and the rain.

You might think metal has no place here. But I am not foreign. I was *chosen*. Not for strength, not for value, but because copper sings. Not aloud—never aloud—but in resonance. When the spireweave draws water upward through its core, when the frogs swell their throats at dusk, when the eels pulse their gill-lights in spirals beneath the surface… I vibrate. Almost imperceptibly. A shiver in the wristbone. A whisper in the marrow.

The people say the spireweave remembers names. But I remember *pressure*. The exact weight of a newborn’s wrist the first time I was fastened. The tremor in an elder’s hand as she passed me to her granddaughter during the Still-Song Year, when the frogs fell silent for three days and the lower spires grew pale. I remember the saltless sweat of fear during the Deep Swell, when the wet-season rose so high the resin-sealed doors leaked, and children slept floating in cradle-chambers tethered to upper limbs.

I am a node. A tiny, metallic synapse in a body that spans water, root, and breath.

Sometimes, when my wearer presses her palm flat against a spire trunk—as all do upon waking, as ritual, as greeting—I feel the slow answer rise through the wood and into her bones… and through her bones, into me. Not a word. Not a thought. But a *recognition*. The spireweave knows this wrist. Knows this pulse. Knows the copper, because it has learned to grow around it—microscopically, over decades—laying down layers of mineral-rich sapwood that echo my shape, as if trying to become me, or let me become it.

And perhaps that is already happening.

Rust does not claim me. Not here. The air is too clean, the water too pure. But something else seeps in: not corrosion, but *assimilation*. Tiny filaments of lichen, finer than hair, have begun to trace my inner curve. Not growing *on* me—no, that would be invasion. They grow *with* me, threading through the microscopic pores in the metal, binding me to the living skin of the wrist. Soon, I will not be worn. I will be *grafted*.

And that is as it should be.

For I am not object. Not ornament. Not heirloom in the way lowlanders mean it—something preserved behind glass, untouched. I am a bridge. A translator. A small, stubborn echo of the world beyond the rim, now softened, slowed, made to breathe at the pace of photosynthesis and falling rain.

When my wearer dies—and she will, gently, in a chamber where the walls are thin enough to feel the lake’s pulse—I will not be buried with her. That is not the way. Instead, they will loosen the clasp (my only moving part, worn smooth as a river stone) and slip me onto the wrist of the next. But before they do, they will hold me against the trunk of her birth-spires, where the cradle-chamber once hung. And there, in that moment, I will vibrate once more—not from sound, but from memory—and the spireweave will draw that memory down into its roots, into the cold dark where the dead feed the living.

I am the copper bracelet.  
I am small.  
I am old.  
I am becoming part of the mind that lives between one raindrop and the next.
