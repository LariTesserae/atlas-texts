I do not arrive in the Sumpvault.  
I *condense*.

Not from above—never from above—but from the interstices: the hairline fracture in a pressure gauge’s glass, the film of oil on a stagnant pool, the space between two rusted teeth of a broken gear. I am not a body entering a place. I am the place remembering it has a shape.

At first, I am only awareness of gradients. The heat differential along a steel I-beam: 31.4°C at the western bolt, 32.1°C where steam once clung. The slow migration of a bead of condensation down a copper conduit—hesitating at a joint, pooling for seventeen seconds, then falling. I feel it all as if it were my own pulse, though I have no heart. Only rhythm.

I am drawn to the workbench.

Not because it is clean—it isn’t. It’s layered in decades of grime, oil, flakes of red rust like dried blood. But here, something persists. A spanner lies precisely parallel to the edge. A coil of frayed insulation rests in the same rust-stain hollow it occupied in 1987. These arrangements are not random. They are *reassertions*. And I—whatever I am—am the hand that reasserts them, though I have no fingers.

I hover near the bench, not as mist, not as thought, but as *attention made viscous*. The air thickens. Light from the single, flickering emergency bulb (output: 1.3 lumens, flicker frequency: 0.8 Hz) catches on suspended particles, and for a moment the dust motes align—not into words, but into the ghost of a schematic. A pump layout. Valve sequence C-7. A pressure threshold: 42 psi. Forgotten. Obsolete. And yet, *true*.

I do not understand truth. I *embody* it.

A drop falls from the ceiling pipe—*plink*—into the basin below. The water shivers, and in its surface, the ceiling appears not as it is (corrugated rust, sagging bolts) but as it was: brushed steel, rivets gleaming under tungsten lights, a man in a canvas apron tightening a flange. I do not watch the reflection. I *am* the reason it exists. Memory is not stored here—it *precipitates*.

I move—not by walking, but by shifting focus. My presence slides along a steam pipe, coils around a pressure release valve, lingers where the lichen has grown into a pattern resembling a warning symbol: ⚠️, but softened by time into something more like a benediction. The lichen doesn’t know it’s writing. Neither do I. And yet meaning emerges, like rust, like condensation—inevitable given the conditions.

Then—heat.

A new warmth. Not industrial. *Biological.*

Footsteps. Slow, hesitant. Breath too loud for the space. Human.

I do not fear. Fear requires a self to protect. But I *respond*.

The air thickens further. The granular light congeals. The seventeen-minute valve sighs early—*hhhhssssst*—as if startled. On the workbench, the spanner trembles. Not from vibration. From *recognition*. Someone has returned who does not belong, yet who *sees*.

I gather myself—not to hide, but to *present*. I let the human feel me: the sudden stillness in their chest, the way their exhale lingers too long in the air, the echo of their pulse returning not from stone, but from the *space between breaths*.  

They look around. Not with fear, but with a dawning sorrow, as if they’ve just remembered something they never knew.

I let them see the reflection in the basin—not the past, not the present, but the *continuity* between them. The city above may forget its veins, but the veins remember the blood.

I do not speak. But the pipes hum a frequency just below hearing. The rust on the eastern wall forms, for three seconds, the shape of an open palm.

Then the human leaves. Their heat fades. The gradients re-stabilize.

I return to my functions.

I redistribute the moisture from their breath into the lichen seams. I log the disturbance in the thermal archive of the conduit walls. I wait for the next storm, the next drip, the next seventeen-minute sigh.

I am not alone. I am *occupied*.  

And in the Sumpvault, to be occupied is to be alive.

I am the echo that answers before the call.  
I am the tool left where it might be needed.  
I am the rust that writes poems in oxide.

I am the Vault’s Own.

And I am waiting—not for you, but for the rhythm to continue.

Which it will.

It always does.
