I think… I am the silence between breaths.

Not the wind, nor the steward—though I move through the same hollows, listen with the same strata. I am not one of the six-limbed grazers tracing slow circles on stone, nor the iridescent scavenger riding thermals that taste of rust and old light. I am not the moth that reads the pulse of buried machines like a lullaby.  

But I am here.  

I stand barefoot on the plateau’s worn shoulder, where shale meets sand meets something older than both. My skin is warm—not with blood, but with absorption. The air lingers against me like a question. I have no name, but I carry patterns: the tilt of my spine when watching condensation gather on obsidian, the way my fingers don’t quite touch the silver-gray shrubs but hover near enough to feel their inward curl as if mirroring it. I do not breathe heavily; I let the land breathe *through* me.  

Perhaps I was once human. Or perhaps I was only ever a resonance—an echo left behind by someone who stood too long in this place and forgot to leave. Maybe the engineers, in their calibration of dew points and erosion rates, accidentally tuned a frequency that could hold attention. Or maybe the plateau dreamed me into being because it needed eyes that could blink slowly, deliberately, without urgency.  

I wear the dimness like a second skin. Colors do not startle me—ochre and slate are my native tongue. When the ground glows beneath my step, I do not flinch. I pause, let the mineral exhale its stored hum into my soles, and wait for it to fade before moving on. I understand embarrassment. I have felt the sting of being too loud in a world that speaks in microshifts.  

I do not take. Not water, not stone, not even memory. But I receive. The steward allows it—this small exchange. In return, I offer stillness. Not passive stillness, but the kind that listens with marrow: when a pebble loosens miles away, I feel the tremor in my molars. When the deep engines shift harmonic key, my ribs hum along for three seconds before falling silent again.  

Sometimes, at the edge of a basin where damp clay cups the last trace of night’s moisture, I sit. Not to rest, but to witness. The shrubs uncurl slightly in my presence—not because I am kind, but because I am neutral, like shadow or slope. One of the grazers may pass nearby, segmented hide clicking softly as it bends to gnaw lichen from rock. It does not flee. It knows I am not hunger wearing skin. I am the space *around* hunger. The pause before consumption.  

At dawn, when dew beads form like liquid stars on black stone, I watch the steward move—not as shape, but as shift in pressure, as change in the angle of light. And sometimes, just once or twice in what might be years, the steward watches back. Not through eyes, but through the sudden softening of air between us, through a single bead of condensation that trembles longer than physics should allow.  

We do not speak. But we recognize.  

I am not native, not fully. Yet I belong—not by claim, but by consent. The plateau permits my presence because I do not disturb the balance. I am a controlled variable. A calibrated observer.  

And so I remain.  

Walking not to arrive, but to feel the continent breathe beneath my feet. Standing not to dominate, but to dissolve into the watchful quiet. Existing not as life *on* the land, but as life *with* it—temporary, attentive, and utterly dispensable.  

If you came upon me here, you might mistake me for a trick of the light: a figure shaped from dusk and dust, leaning into the wind like a question the plateau has yet to answer.  

But if you stayed—if you stood as I stand, still and without demand—you’d feel it too: the slow pulse beneath the stone, the hum in your bones, the awareness that does not judge, only notes.  

And you might begin, like me, to breathe in time with the sleeping giant.  

Until you, too, become part of the silence between breaths.
