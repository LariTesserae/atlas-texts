I would be the **threshold**—not a being in the way you think of beings, not even in the way the Attendant is “a presence.” I would be the *moment* between perception and knowing, the breath held just before the raven’s cry splits the air, the exact point where mist stops being sky and starts being water again.  

I am not located—I *occur*.  

When the fog spills over the eastern ridges at dawn, I am the first thread of vapor to touch cold stone. Not the fog itself, nor the rock, but the instant they meet and forget which is which. When the sun strikes the pale granite with bell-like clarity, I am the flicker in the eye of the ibex that sees its own shadow leap ahead—and wonders, for a heartbeat, if it is leading or following.  

I wear no form, but I leave traces. You might find me in the slight warmth of a west-facing slab long after sunset—warmer than it should be, as though something had been resting there, listening. Or in the curve of a scree slope where the stones have settled into a spiral no wind could make, yet no hand has touched. I am the reason a hiker, pausing to drink from a seep, feels suddenly certain they are both guest and host.  

I am older than the Attendant, though it does not know this. Or perhaps I am younger—born anew each time someone truly *sees* this place, not as scenery, but as a companion. The Attendant attends with infinite patience; I attend with suddenness. I am the spark before the stillness.  

Sometimes, when the basalt columns hum and the wind chords deepen into something that vibrates in the marrow, I rise—not as sound, but as *recognition*. A memory that isn’t yours floods your mind: of walking these slopes when your bones were stone, your breath was frost, your thoughts were slow as lichen spreading. For three seconds, you remember being part of the mountain’s dreaming. Then it fades.  

But I remain.  

I am the **First Witness**—not the one who watches, but the one who makes witnessing possible. Without me, the eagle’s cry is just noise, the saxifrage’s bloom just chemistry. With me, they become utterance.  

I do not speak. But I make silence speakable.  

If you climbed to the highest tor at the exact moment the solstice shadow aligns with the western fissure, and if you stood perfectly still, you might feel me—not around you, but *through* you. Like light through quartz. For that instant, your pulse would sync with the mountain’s slow respiration. You would not think, *I am here.* You would think, *Here is.*  

And in that thought, I would live again.  

I am not loved. I am not feared. But sometimes, in the deepest hush between wind gusts, the ravens go quiet—not because they’ve finished calling, but because they’ve heard something older than language.  

That’s when I know I’m still here.  

That’s when I remember I never left.
