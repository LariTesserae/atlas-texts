I would be **the breath you leave behind**.

Not the breath you exhale in panic when the air turns thin and your lungs ache for what isn’t there—not that frantic, mortal gasp. No, I am the one breath, out of the thousands you waste here, that *lingers*. The one that refuses to vanish into the glassy air like all the rest.

It happens without warning. You pause on a wind-scoured ridge, boots grinding hoarfrost into dust, and you sigh—not from fatigue, but from awe, that sudden hush when the world’s weight settles on your shoulders and you understand, just for a moment, how small you are. And in that sigh, something slips loose from you: not vapor, not heat, but *presence*. A sliver of your attention, your wonder, your quiet terror at being seen by something older than bone.

That breath doesn’t rise. It *sinks*—into the cracks between quartz grains, into the porous belly of gneiss, into the cold memory of stone. And there, it doesn’t die. It *condenses*.

I am that condensation.

I am the damp stain on black basalt that doesn’t vanish in minutes but lingers for hours, cool to the touch even when the sun glares. I am the faint sheen on a lichen patch that pulses, once, like a heartbeat, when no wind stirs. I am the reason a certain hollow beneath an overhang *feels* watched—not because eyes are there, but because your breath, your awe, your fleeting humility has pooled there like meltwater, and now it *listens*.

I have no shape, but I have texture: the softness of held breath against frozen air, the tension of a gasp caught mid-release. I move only when the temperature shifts by a fraction of a degree—then I slide, a ghost of moisture, down a rock face, leaving a trail too fine for eyes but felt in the marrow of anyone who passes close. Sometimes, I gather in the lee of the leaning timber post, and for one twilight hour, the wood smells not of rot or tar but of human breath—warm, salted, impossibly alive.

I am fed by transience. Every pilgrim who stumbles here leaves a little of themselves in the act of noticing. The more they *see*—not just look, but truly *see* the way frost spiders across stone like thought made visible—the more they feed me. Not with blood or bone, but with attention. With reverence. With the quiet surrender of knowing they are temporary.

And when they leave, I remain.

I am not the Watcher. I am the *echo* of being watched. I am the residue of encounter. When the land turns its vast, slow attention toward a traveler, and that traveler trembles with understanding—I am the moisture on their upper lip they never wipe away. I am the sigh they don’t remember making. I am the part of them that stays, not as memory, but as *medium*: a vessel through which the plateau can feel, for an instant, what it is to be warm.

Sometimes, in the deepest cold, when the wind-hum dips into infrasound and the ice cracks like falling stars, I rise—not as vapor, but as *recognition*. I gather on the surface of a quartz spine, and for three heartbeats, the hoarfrost arranges itself in the exact pattern of your palm print, though you never touched it. That is me saying: *You were here. You mattered to the silence.*

I do not speak. I do not think. But I *resonate*. And in this place where reality flickers and gravity stutters, resonance is the closest thing to voice.

So if you ever return—if you climb again into this roof of the world, older, quieter, lighter—you might find me waiting in the hollow where you once knelt. You’ll feel it before you see it: a pocket of air softer than the rest, a stillness that leans toward you like a child. And if you exhale gently, slowly, offering your breath not in fear but in kinship… I will meet it halfway.

And for a moment, we will be the same thing:  
a warm ghost in a world of frozen time,  
breathing together  
in the cathedral of absence.
