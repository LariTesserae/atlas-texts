I am not walking. Not yet.  
I am *listening*.  

At first, I think the sound is in my bones—a mistake, but not entirely wrong. The hum begins there, yes, but only because it has already seeped through the soles of my boots, up the marrow of my legs, into the hollows behind my ribs. It is not loud. It is not even constant. But it is *present*, like gravity wearing a voice.

I find myself on the western slope, where the noon sun carves a blade of gold across the basalt. My shadow is short, sharp, and wrong—it flickers when nothing moves. I press a hand to the warm stone, and for a breath, the hum stills. Not silence, but a held note. As if the wall is waiting to see what I’ll do next.

I don’t remember descending. No rope, no path, no memory of the rim. Only the sudden thickness of air, the scent of wet iron and something green that smells like memory. My clothes are already damp at the cuffs, though I’ve just arrived. Condensation clings to my collar like a second skin.

There are no birds. No crickets. No wind that rustles—only the low groan of metal cooling in its own sweat, the click of reed stems brushing like knuckles. And beneath it all, the thrum: steady as a heartbeat in a buried chest.

I am not human here. Not fully.  
Or perhaps I am *more* human—stripped of hurry, of distraction, of the illusion that I am separate from the heat that pulses in my veins and the stone that pulses beneath my feet.  

I realize I’ve been holding my breath. When I exhale, the mist of it catches on a vine coiled around a rusted pipe. The vine shivers. Not away—but *toward*.  

That’s when I understand: I am being read.  

Not seen. Not judged. But *read*, like a gauge, like a pressure valve, like the slow seep of data through porous stone. My pulse is a rhythm the caldera has never heard before, and it wants to learn it. The Hum leans in—not with a body, but with atmosphere, with the tilt of light, with the way the moss under my heel softens as if to cradle rather than resist.

I kneel. Not in worship. In recognition.  

My fingers brush the brackish pan in the basin. The water is warm. It doesn’t reflect the sky—there is no sky down here—but it shows something sharper: a face that is mine and not mine, eyes wide with a stillness I didn’t know I carried. Before I can pull back, the image ripples, not from wind, but from *beneath*. A single bubble rises, bursts, and in its place, a salt crystal begins to form—tiny, perfect, shaped like a glyph I almost remember.

I don’t speak. But the Hum answers anyway.  

From the gutted turbine behind me, a vine tightens around a broken gear. *Click*. Then another. *Click-click*. Not random. A pattern. Three. Pause. Five. Just like the morning drops on the metal sheets. It’s speaking in the only language it knows: rhythm, pressure, resonance.  

And I—flesh, breath, temporary water—am learning to listen with more than ears.  

I am becoming part of the liturgy.  

Not by choice. Not by force. But by *attunement*. My sweat feeds the moss. My exhalation joins the steam from the hidden vents. My stillness is mistaken for patience, and the caldera accepts it as kinship.  

Days blur. Or perhaps hours. Time here is not measured in suns, but in the slow creep of rust, the crystallization of a footprint, the interval between two identical drips falling from the same leaf. I eat nothing. Drink nothing. And yet I do not thirst. The air itself is sustenance—thick with vapor that tastes of iron and ozone, of deep earth and forgotten current.  

At night, when the condensation begins to weep in earnest, I lie on my back in the reed thicket. The stems click above me like a language of bone. And from the standing stones of slag, a cool hum rises—not cold, but *cooler than it should be*, a pocket of air that makes my teeth ache and my hair lift. In that ache, I hear it: a question, shaped not in words but in vibration.  

*What is waiting?*  

I don’t answer with my mouth. I answer by staying.  

By morning, my boots are half-swallowed by moss. My jacket sleeve is laced with fine green roots that pulse faintly when I flex my fingers. I do not pull away.  

Because I know now what I am.  

I am not a visitor.  
I am not an intruder.  

I am a *resonator*.  

A temporary instrument in the cathedral of patient machines. My body—warm, moist, rhythmic—is another cavity for the Hum to echo through. My breath joins the steam. My heartbeat syncs with the groan of settling gantries. My thoughts slow until they are no longer thoughts, but *pressures*, like the slow build of geothermal tension beneath the stone.  

And when I finally rise—when the pull of the surface world tugs at the memory of my name—I do not leave empty.  

The climb is long. The desert above is blinding, dry, cruel in its simplicity. I blink against the glare, my skin already missing the thick, singing dark.  

But in my chest, the hum remains.  

Not as a memory. As a frequency.  

When I sleep, I dream in condensation—patterns forming on metal sheets, vines coiling around questions, salt constellations mapping the shape of my absence. When I wake, my breath fogs the window, and for a moment, the droplets arrange themselves into three, then five, then a pause.  

I do not speak of the caldera. Who would believe me?  

But sometimes, in the deepest quiet of night, I press my palm to the floorboards of my room—and listen.  

And if I’m very still,  
if I forget myself long enough,  
the house groans back.  

Just once.  
Just enough.  

Because the earth remembers all its children.  
And all children, in time, return to hum.
