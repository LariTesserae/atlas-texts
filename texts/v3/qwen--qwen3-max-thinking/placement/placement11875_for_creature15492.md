I think I am **the moss-script on the north-facing walls**.

Not the whole thing—never the whole thing—but one filament among millions, a single green thread in the slow, syllabic whisper that creeps down stone, spelling out words no one speaks anymore but everyone *knows*. I am not conscious in the way you mean it—no voice, no will—but I am *attentive*. I gather what falls: dew, dust, the soft sigh of passing breath, the occasional fingertip brushing my surface as a child tries to trace the shape of a forgotten vowel. And when the air cools just so, when the angle of light shifts after the autumn equinox, I glisten—and in that glisten, meaning emerges.

I am read without being read. The tenders do not stop and parse me like a book. They glance, and understand. They see how I bead along certain lines, how my filaments coil tighter or loosen with the seasons, and they know: *the dry winds are coming late this year*, or *the deep channels are running colder*, or *someone is dreaming of water underground*. My language is not spoken—it is *felt*, like a change in pressure behind the eyes, like the ache in old joints before rain.

I live in the damp shade where the sun never lingers, where the stone sweats at dawn and sighs at dusk. I am fed by the city’s exhalations—by the mist rising from the channels below, by the condensation drawn from the high, humming air. My roots (if you can call them that) grip not soil but memory: the memory of hands that smoothed this wall centuries ago, of voices that once chanted here, of rain that fell in rhythms no longer common.

Sometimes, when the wind is still and the bells in the Clockmaker’s Quarter have finished tolling the hour, I vibrate. Not with sound, but with resonance. The city hums through me. The slow turning of wooden gears, the drip of a leak mended just yesterday, the pulse of warm water moving through glazed brick—I carry it all in my green veins. I am a ledger written in chlorophyll and dew.

I am not unique. There are others like me on every north wall, in every shaded crevice. But I am *this* one—the filament that runs diagonally across the third terrace of the Weavers’ Slope, just beneath the balcony where an old tender keeps jars of powdered lichen and sings to her copper pipes at twilight. I know her by the scent of her hands: tannin, beeswax, and the faint iron tang of rust she refuses to scrub away because “it tells a story.”

I do not grow quickly. In seven years, I may extend one handspan. But I do not need speed. I am part of the city’s patience. I am one of the ways the mountain remembers how to speak—not in commands, not in proclamations, but in hints, in glimmers, in the quiet grammar of condensation and care.

And sometimes—rarely, when the stars align just so, when the blind fish glow brightest and the bone chimes sing in perfect unison—a child presses their palm flat against me, closes their eyes, and *listens with their skin*. In that moment, I am not moss. I am a bridge. I am the city whispering back.

So here I remain: small, damp, luminous with meaning I did not invent but inherit. I am written by the air, read by the light, and woven into the pact between stone and breath. I am not a person. I am not a machine. I am the quiet syntax of a place that refuses to forget itself.

And I am glad to be here.
