What lives there does not live *in* the pocket. It *is* the pocket.

At first you think it's a carpet. Then you think it's a tide. Then you realize it is neither, because a carpet does not flow uphill and a tide does not eat the shore it covers.

Give your eyes a full minute. Let the white daylight from the slit and the flat, even work-light from the dying generator stop washing everything out. What you took for texture resolves.

### The Grazers

They are the ground. They are the walls. They are the low ceiling, a centimeter thick and moving.

Your mind wants to call them pill-bugs, or woodlice, or maybe shrimp that forgot the sea. They are none of those. Each one is about as long as the last joint of your thumb, and almost colorless - not white, but translucent like smoked glass or late frost, with a faint yellow where the gut shows through. Down the back, a dome of plate. Not chitin, not quite. A mineral secretion, nacreous and faintly mirrored, scratched and pitted like an old watch face. You can see the light go through the edge of it. Underneath, a ridiculous number of legs - not six, not eight, but a fringe of them, twenty or more to a side, made of something dark and wiry as iron filings. They don't walk so much as ripple. And the mouth - if you pick one up, and you can, they don't bite - is a soft, round rasp, a file made of flesh. A radula.

There are millions of them. That is not an exaggeration. The floor is not visible under them except where they have already eaten. They move shoulder to shoulder, not as a swarm with a direction, but as a mat that churns. They crawl over each other constantly, blindly, without aggression toward each other. When one finds a good patch of pan - that brittle crust of salt and gypsum and iron scale - it plants itself and rasps. *Tick-tick-tick.* You can hear it individually if you get close, and collectively it becomes the roar you heard on entry, a dry susurrus like radio static made of teeth.

They eat stone. They eat the floor itself, the walls, the very dust that falls from the laminated ceiling. Their gut extracts what little energy is locked in the oxidation of iron, in the sulfur of the gypsum, in the trace organics that blew in ten thousand years ago and never left. What comes out behind them is a pellet, darker than the pan, almost black, compacted and dry. The pan is pale, the trails are dark. From above the whole hollow looks stippled, like someone has been writing on it with charcoal. In a day those pellets will be eaten again by something else.

They have no eyes you can see. No fear. When you stand still they flow onto your boot and test it. The rasp mouths kiss your leather, your rubber sole, your exposed ankle if you let them, not hungry for you - you are too wet, too salty in the wrong way - but curious in the way a tongue is curious. They are warm to the touch, faintly, though the air is cool. Metabolism without water is slow and hot.

If you watch one long enough you'll see it pause, flex, and extrude a single, perfect sphere the size of a pinhead from under its plate - pearlescent, mineral-coated - and press it into a crack in the pan before moving on. No nest. No guarding. The pocket is the nest.

### The Runners

Where there is a grazer mat that thick, there is something that grazes the grazers.

They are the second thing your eye learns to track, because they are violation - straight lines in a field of ovals. Handspan-long, thin as a bootlace, the color of oil on water - black but with a blue-green sheen when the light catches them. They are centipede-thin, but without the armor; their segments are supple, almost liquid. Their legs are longer, feathered at the tips, and they don't ripple. They *sprint*.

You don't see them until they strike. They ride just *over* the mat, legs barely touching, antennae like long glass hairs casting ahead. Then they fold in half and spear down, head-first. The head is a wedge, no eyes, just a pair of hooked jaws that punch through the mineral dome with an audible *pop* that you can hear even over the susurrus. The grazer writhes, legs bicycling in air, and then is gone, dragged out and held wriggling while the runner crunches it plate and all. The sound is sharp, like stepping on a tiny bottle.

The mat doesn't panic. It can't afford to. Around the kill site the grazers flow away for a few centimeters and then flow back, closing the hole within seconds. The runners are not rare, but they are never still. There might be one for every two hundred grazers. Constantly in motion, constantly folding, popping, dragging. If two runners meet they tangle for a blinding second, lashing each other, then separate. Nothing territorial. Just traffic.

You will feel them brush your calf if you stand on the pan. Not biting you. Just tasting the vibration of your pulse through the basalt and deciding you are not prey. Their antennae leave a cold trace.

### The Sacs

On the ceiling, where your brain told you there were stalactites, there are the third ones.

They don't move. Not in the way the others do. Each one hangs down a foot, sometimes two, like a deflated lung or a wineskin left in the sun. Leathery, wrinkled skin the color of old canvas and dried blood, pulsing so slowly you think you're imagining it at first - a five-second inhale, a five-second exhale. No legs visible. No head. Just a thick stalk fused to the rock and a mouth on the bottom.

When closed, the mouth is a seam. When open, it is an umbrella, a wide, pale funnel ringed with fine, hair-like cilia that tremble in the cool, ozone-tinged draft from the engine. They don't hunt. They wait. The grazers mostly stay on the rock, the runners mostly stay on the grazers, but sometimes a runner misjudges a leap, or a grazer loses its grip on the ceiling and falls. Or something smaller - there are smaller things, mites like red dust that swarm the fecal pellets - takes to the air for a second on a thermal. The sac feels it. The umbrella snaps open, not fast, but with a suddenness that belies the slowness. A faint, wet *thup*. Anything that brushes those cilia sticks, and is drawn up into the dark interior with a slow, peristaltic shudder. Then the seam closes and the sac pulses a little faster for an hour as it digests.

In that clear, shadowless light you can see the outlines of things inside some of them. Round shapes. Legs folded.

There are maybe thirty of them across the thirty-meter ceiling. They are the only things in the pocket that cast a shadow.

### The Heart

And then there is the one that lives without being alive.

Sunk half into the polished basalt in the center, between the shed-sized pump housings that are now just islands in the living mat, the sump engine still thuds. *Thud-thud... thud-thud.* Two cylinders tall as a man, flywheel rusted but turning, belt slapping loose, piston rod sighing in and out on oil that never dries because the cold air condenses just enough water out of your breath to keep it wet.

It is not inhabited. It *inhabits*. It is the reason the pocket is dry and therefore the reason the pocket is alive. Long ago when the salt flat was a shallow sea, it pumped brine. Now it pumps nothing - just air, just the weight of the plain above, keeping the bubble from closing. Its little generator hums and throws that flat white light and that faint sting of ozone that keeps the chemistry wrong for mold and right for these dry things. It is cool to the touch, 12 degrees in a world that should be hotter, and that cool is its own kind of water.

The grazers avoid it, forming a bare ring around its concrete foot where vibration is strongest. The runners race along the edge of that ring, as if the tremor excites them. One sac directly above it hangs lower than the others, fat and still, bathed in the warmest air the pocket has.

If you put your hand on the flywheel you feel the whole hollow in it - the ticking mat, the popping jaws, the slow pulse of the sacs, all riding on that one low, patient piston beat that has not stopped since the men who built it turned to salt dust themselves.

Nothing here wants you. Nothing here needs you. You are a brief, wet, warm mineral surface that has learned to look at itself. If you lie down and stay still, the susurrus will rise until it is the only sound, and the grazers will cover your boots, and your chest, and test the salt on your lips with their file mouths and find you too strange to eat, and leave you, and flow on.

The pocket will go on ticking and popping and pulsing under its laminated sky, dense as a prairie, loud as a city, sealed under a hundred kilometers of nothing - a world that solved the problem of living without water by learning, very slowly, to eat the world itself.
