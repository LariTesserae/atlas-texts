They live low, the way the land lives low.

Not that they are short — though they are — not that they are low in number — though there are not many — but that they have learned to live *within*, tucked. If you came upon them quickly you would misread them at first, the way you misread the sink itself.

At first you think: a few families camping in a hole.

Then your eyes adjust and you see it is not a camp. It is a town that has decided to stay small.

There are perhaps sixty of them. Perhaps eighty if you count the infants tied to chests and the very old who no longer come out into the plaza. You can hold the whole census in your head after a day. That is part of how they live — everyone within calling distance, everyone knowable. A voice raised in the plaza can cross the entire pocket without shouting, so no one ever really shouts. They speak the way the stone speaks — quietly, and only when necessary.

They are people of the warm damp. Their skin tells you that before anything else does. It is brown the color of wet bark and river clay, with an undertone of red that shows in the palms and the soles of their feet. It never really dries here. The air in the sink is a bath, and their skin stays beaded as the limestone walls do. The sweat does not evaporate, so they don't fight it. Hair is black and straight and cut short with a shell edge — men, women, children alike — to keep it from holding water. They go barefoot almost always, because the causeway stones and the ribbed mud and the fallen blocks are all meant to be read by feet. You can see the soles thickened and pale, creased with limestone dust.

They are not tall. The ceiling of their world is low — that ragged oval of sky thirty feet above, the fig roots descending like ropes — and they have, over generations, stopped reaching up. Children grow compact, broad in the shoulders and chest from climbing the blocks, from hauling water in the hollowed-stone cistern. Elders fold neatly onto sleeping platforms that would be too short for a northern person. It is not stunting. It is fitting. Their houses fit between blocks; their bodies fit their houses.

You expect, if you think of a town fitted into a single room, that it will be crowded, that privacy will have been surrendered. It hasn't. That is the second thing you misread. They leave emptiness on purpose.

Between any two houses there is a gap that has been allowed to stay wild — a bank of ferns, a papaya that came up on its own and was not cut, a stand of helicon with its red claws, a tangle of helicon that no one harvests except the hummingbirds. The street is not a street. It is a path that widens into garden and narrows into forest and widens again. They could have filled every flat block with a dwelling. They don't. An old woman will tell you, without ceremony, that the pools need somewhere to appear. That the peccary that falls in during the night needs somewhere to pass through without being cornered. That the frogs need a wall that no one touches.

They are not sentimental about the animals. They do not keep them as pets, and they do not worship them from a distance. They live interleaved.

Look, if you stand still long enough on the causeway, to see who else lives here:

The frogs first — dozens, hundreds, the same brown as the mud, plated flat against the damp walls of the sink where the condensation beads. Each male has a territory the size of a palm and defends it with a single soft *peep* every minute or so, so sparse you thought the place was silent. The people know them individually by the pattern of their throats.

The anoles, bright green when they run across the thatch, brown when they sit on limestone, flicking their pink dews. The children do not chase them. They watch them and in watching learn how to move without startling.

The swifts that live in the pitted pockets of the raw wall, in the unopened sea-bubbles in the young stone. They drop from the rim at dusk like thrown stones and stitch the air of the pocket with no sound at all. The people track time by them — when the swifts go up, the cookfires are lit.

The blind white fish in the deepest pools, eyeless beads that have been trapped since the collapse opened. The people did not put them there and do not eat them though they could. The fish belong to the pools. The boys crouch for an hour at a time to watch them turn.

And then the people themselves, moving through this at the same pace.

A woman sweeps the plaza earth with a broom of twigs, not to clean it but to read it — the overnight tracks of beetle, snake, child. A man crouches by the hollowed-block cistern and, with a gourd, transfers wall-drip, drop by drop, without spilling. Two girls sit on the lip of a fallen block and split a ripe hog plum with a flake of chert, passing it back and forth. An old man, whose house is built half-under an overhang where bats fold like dead leaves, mends thatch by tying fresh palm over rotted palm and letting the living vine he stitched in last season do the holding. No one hurries. Hurrying would make you miss where the water will be tomorrow.

Their tools tell you how long they have intended to stay, and how lightly. Everything is cut, woven, weighted, grown-together. Wattle of thin sapwood, mud plaster mixed with chopped leaf, thatch so thick it holds the shape of the forest floor, stone flakes knapped to a clean edge and then discarded when they dull. A shell adze. A fire drill. A loom made of two sticks and tension. No nail, because nail would mean they thought the stone was finished and could be fixed in place. They know it isn't. While you watch, a hairline crack ticks open in a white block as water finds a new seam — a soft, almost inaudible tick — and a house platform a few feet away, which had been set level last year, now lists half an inch. No one gasps. In the afternoon a few people will come with poles and lift it and slip another sliver of limestone underneath.

That is their philosophy, if you can call something so unspoken a philosophy: build as the vine builds. Hold, don't pierce. Be ready to move a wall.

They garden the way they build — in patches, not fields. Where the oval of sky throws its column of light down into the plaza for two hours at midday, they have packed earth and grow squash and beans and chili, low and bright. Where the shade is deeper, they grow nothing and let the ferns do it. They fish the pools only after the heavy rain when the tadpoles are thick and the water will vanish in a day anyway. They keep a sow and her piglets under the houses, not penned, because the piglets eat what would otherwise rot too fast in the wet. Nothing is stored for long. The damp would take it. Everything is used fresh, or smoked for a day over a small, straight-rising fire whose smoke hits the rim and spills like water.

At night the town becomes most itself.

The sky oval goes lilac, then quickly indigo, then black. The air, which has been warm and still all day, finally moves — a cool breath falls from the forest rim down the weeping walls and stirs the thatch. The bats unfold all at once, hundreds of them, and go up in a smoke that for a few seconds blots the stars, and then are gone into the canopy to feed. The people do not follow them with their eyes. They are lighting small fires, one per household, not for heat — there is never need for heat — but for light and to keep the whine of mosquitoes down. The fires are tiny, tended, fed with dry heartwood that burns without much smoke.

And then the frogs resume, one note at a time. *Peep.* ... *Peep.* ... *Peep.* From wall to wall, sparse as the houses, patient and utterly active.

If you speak to them — and they will answer, politely, in a low language that has many words for kinds of wet stone and only one word for *north*, because north means nothing in a room — they will not say they are hiding. They will say they are living where it is easy to live, where water appears even when it does not rain, where the walls keep the wind off and the forest keeps strangers off. They will say the sink opened when their grandparents were children, raw and bright and dangerous, and their grandparents came down because a pocket that has just been made is a gift if you know how to be small inside it. They will say, if you ask if they will stay, that they will stay as long as the stone lets them, and when it shifts again they will shift with it.

From the rim, with the hanging roots curtaining the lip and the thatch the exact color of leaf litter, you would walk past and think the forest floor was unbroken. Below, in the dim, a whole town breathes quietly inside the earth it has not finished becoming — sparse, intimate, lush where it wants to be lush and empty where it needs to be empty, never still, never loud, exactly fitted to its new room.
