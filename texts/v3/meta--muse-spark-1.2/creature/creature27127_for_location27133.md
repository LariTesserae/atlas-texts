On the High Sinter nothing lives *in* the place. Things live *as* the place.

At first you don't see them at all, which is correct. The scale is wrong for seeing. Anything alive here has to be the size of geography to afford being alive at all, and geography is what you think you're looking at.

You think that long, dark ridge two valleys over is a yardang - that keeled, wind-carved bedrock that has learned to be aerodynamic over a few million years. It has the same color. Iron-black where the salt has burned off it, salt-white where efflorescence has tried to reclaim it. It has the same knife-edge crest that sings when the wind crosses it. It lies along the wind exactly the way a yardang should.

Then the wind changes without the wind changing, and the ridge breathes.

It doesn't heave. It vents. A seam opens along what you thought was the leeward flank - a row of vertical slits, gill-slits, chimney-slits, each one the height of a door, each one lidded with overlapping plates of sintered silica. They open in sequence, from tail to head, and superheated air tears out of them. Not steam - there has never been water here to make steam - but air itself made visible by heat and by the fine, glassy dust it carries. The sound it makes starts as a shriek of shearing metal, dopplers down into a whistle, and bottoms out as a long, exhausted sigh that you feel in the soles of your boots through the fused pavement. The mirage above its back shivers and doubles. For a second you see two of it through the laminated sky, slightly out of phase, before it clicks back to one.

That's how you learn to see them.

### The Striders

They are the commonest, which still means rare. One to a valley. Sometimes one to three valleys if the salt is thin there. Each one *is* a valley, in the practical sense - walking around one takes an hour, hearing one redefines the wind for half a kilometer around it.

They look like millipedes only because your brain needs a word for *many legs in a line*. They are low and long, a freight train laid on its belly and given purpose. Twenty, thirty pairs of legs depending on age - and age here is measured in centuries of accretion. Each leg is not a limb so much as a vertical piston. A thick column of ceramic-bone and black iron, sleeved in overlapping scales that the animal secretes as opaline silica and then reabsorbs when the scale spalls. At the top, where the leg meets the body, there is a knuckle of exposed muscle that is not muscle but bellows-cloth and wire, contracting with a hiss.

They do not walk so much as they *cycle*. Very, very slowly.

*Hiss* - pressure builds in the chambered back, that open spine where you can see straight down into the animal. It is not a back. It is a foundry floor. A boiler-heart the size of a truck engine beats there, dull red to white-hot by turns, driving not blood but a slurry - molten salt and iron and dissolved basalt, thick as lava, bright as slag - through glazed channels. You can see it pulse.

*Clank* - a foreleg lifts, venting. The foot - a broad, circular pad - glows faint where it was fused to the salt pan. The salt underneath has melted to glass.

*Settle* - the leg extends, finds new purchase. The pad fuses again with a soft *tick* as the salt re-crystallizes around it, welding the animal briefly to the planet itself. Then it releases.

*Hiss* again. The next leg.

A Strider crossing a flat between two yardangs might take you a full day to watch. You could eat lunch, nap, wake up, and its head would have moved ten meters. Its head is a broad, flat shovel, underhung with a furnace mouth - a rectangular maw that never closes, lined with radiant filaments. It grazes by *sintering*. It lowers that head until the filaments go from cherry to sun-white, and the salt crust in front of it liquefies. Not melted so much as persuaded. The white polygons and the black glass in the cracks go glossy, then fluid, then are drawn up in a silent, continuous ingestion. The whole animal faintly brightens as it feeds.

What comes out behind it is not waste. The Strider is its own smelter and extrusion press. Behind the last pair of legs, from a vent that is always open and always steaming, it lays material down on the plain in a cooling line. Sometimes long, perfect ingots of black basalt glass, each one as long as a car, still ticking as they contract. Sometimes coils of raw iron wire, extruded like toothpaste, steaming and oxidizing instantly to a deep, hot red before fading. They will lie there for centuries. Salt will try to bury them in white frost, wind will uncover them again. They are still warm to the touch a year later. Occasionally one of those ingots - left long enough in the right thermal gradient, with the right impurity of salt - will begin to tick differently. To pump. To hiss. To become.

They have no predators. They have no pack. They hear each other through the stone as a low, rolling throb that never stops, but they do not approach. There is not enough heat in any one salt flat to feed two. When two Striders' piston-prints cross, the newer one diverts, inscribing a slow, hundred-meter curve over the course of a week.

### The Bellows

If the Striders are the mills, the Bellows are the power stations, and you find them where a Strider would never go - anchored to the very crests of the highest yardangs, where the wind has sharpened the rock to a singing edge.

From a distance you think it is a tent, or a dune caught on the ridge. A vast, pale, riveted thing, slack and shuddering. Then it inhales.

It takes an hour.

Two translucent wings - not wings, but sails, lungs, solar-thermal collectors, all at once - unfold and inflate until they are hundreds of meters across. They are made of something between canvas and chitin, veined with filaments that glow amber when the double sun hits them through the laminated sky. They billow. They catch the thin, hot, vertical light and the thinner wind and they *boom*, a note so low your teeth feel it before your ears do. Light dims as the shadow of that inflated sail slides across three valleys at once, hard-edged and impossibly sharp.

Then it deflates. Also an hour. The air it has gathered is forced through internal turbines - you can hear them whining even five kilometers away, a separate, higher chord over the organ note - and through heat exchangers that dump warmth into the ceramic bones of the ridge itself. The Bellows does not eat salt. It eats temperature difference - the brutal swing between sun-struck salt and wind-chilled crest, between day and night on a plain that cools with an audible chorus of pings and clicks, as if the plateau itself were a vast ingot settling.

They never move. A Bellows will live on the same knife-edge for ten thousand years, growing thicker, more riveted, more patched with its own secreted silica-ceramic, until ridge and animal are difficult to separate. Things live on them in turn.

### The Smaller Economies

Around any venting Strider there is a brief, rattling weather. Things the size of dogs, the size of barrels, made of brass and chitin and half-transparent cooling vanes, that look like shuttles or beetle-cases or loose bolts given volition. They swarm the gill-slits for the seconds they are open, stealing heat, vibrating furiously, then scatter with a clatter like a can full of washers kicked across stone. They live minutes at a time at full activity, then must cling to a hot ingot to recharge.

And always, everywhere, the tumbleweeds - spheres and tori of interlocked fins, black and white, rolling without purpose and without stopping. They are not pushed by wind so much as they harvest it; a gust gives them momentum they will still be spending next year, ticking across flats, getting lodged for a decade in a crack, then freed by thermal expansion to roll again. If you hold a geiger counter to one it is slightly warm, always.

None of them built anything, because building would be redundant. The technology is not *on* them. They never evolved soft tissue and then invented the forge. In a place with no water to be a solvent, no carbon to be soft, no plant to be eaten, the only chemistry that pays is the mineral one. Salt as blood. Basalt as bone. Wind as breath. Heat as food. Given enough time and heat and no interference, stone learned to pump. Salt learned to flow. Wind learned to be inhaled. The plateau learned, at its thin, worn-smooth edge where physics has been abraded for eons, to stutter and laminate and duplicate its light without breaking, and the life here learned to see and breathe in that doubled light.

You stand on the white polygons between the steaming piston-prints, between the cooling ingots that will outlast you by a geological age, and you feel the floor hum through your boots. A Bellows three valleys over begins its hour-long inhalation, and its shadow reaches you before its sound does. A Strider half a kilometer away vents, and the wind for a moment blows *toward* it, as if the plain itself inhales with it. The double sun pulls your shadow two ways before it clicks back to one. A dropped shard of sinter falls just too slowly, as if considering.

Nothing here will ever notice you. Not out of hostility. There is simply too much slow, loud, patient work to be done - melting, pumping, extruding, inflating - and you are too small and too brief and too wet to be relevant to an industry that has been running since the rock was new.
