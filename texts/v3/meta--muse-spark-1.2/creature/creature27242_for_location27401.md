What lives there does not *live* there the way you were taught to live somewhere.

At first you think - nothing could. Because you are looking for a figure against the stone, a body that walks out of a grove and stands in the white light and looks back at you. You wait for that silhouette.

Nothing comes. For a long time you think the mesa is empty except for the plants and the swarms, that the builders left and the groves and the kites and the glass-winged things just kept going on their own, feral.

Then you realize you have already been looking at them for minutes without seeing them.

They do not live *on* the mesa. They live *in the rule* of the mesa: abundance packed tight into islands, with vast, hollow emptiness between.

### The Groves Are Not Groves

The first inhabitant is not an animal at all. It's the thicket itself.

Each of those cathedral-scale rooms of black-violet, crimson glass - you called them plants because you didn't have another word - is a single composite creature. Not many plants crowded together, but one body with thousands of mouths.

Think of a coral that decided to become a forest. From a distance it looks like overlapping leaves. Up close, each plate of smoked glass is a lung and a lens and a leaf at once. Underneath, they are all fused into a columnar trunk that is not wood but laminated photosynthetic mineral, and that trunk plunges into the stone not with roots but with acid-feet, slowly dissolving the basalt to make the scooped bowl it sits in deeper. The translucent fins around the trunk that hum in the wind are not decoration - they are radiators and condensers. At night they go death-cold, colder than the 4-degree air, and the ammonia and trace brine in the thin atmosphere frost onto them in a white rime. At dawn the whole colony shivers at once and wicks it inward.

The hanging felt you thought was moss - that black filament that crackles when you touch it - is its nervous system extruded into the air. It dangles from the overhangs of its own body, fishing for static. The wind rubbing salt dust over stone charges everything. The filaments harvest that charge, store it in bladders at the base like a battery.

The grove is sessile, but it is not still. If you sit quietly - quietly enough that the organ-roar of the arches nearly deafens you - you can see it breathe. The glass plates track the pale sun without moving, their internal lenses shifting oil-dark inside. The whole room exhales its used, dust-filtered air in a slow, warm sigh that you only notice because the cold bites harder everywhere else.

There are maybe forty of these beings on the entire mesa. Each one is an oasis that *is* the oasis. And they are old in the way the stone is old - resolved, in balance with their own erosion. They do not grow. They maintain.

### The Swarms Are One Animal in Many Pieces

You thought there were many species. There are, but on the mesa the distinction between species and organ is messy.

The things you called kite-birds: ribboned, translucent membranes over filament frames, never landing, screaming as wind tears through their skeletons. They are not birds. They have no muscle for powered flight. They are *harps*. Their frames are hollow, resonant, piezoelectric bone. The wind plays them. Their calls aren't made - they are filtered. Each kite tunes its body to amplify a different slice of the mesa's endless chord, and other kites hear that amplification miles away. A single kite is deaf and dumb and doomed; it can't feed or reproduce alone. A swarm of two thousand is a larynx and an ear and a mind.

They lay nothing. They bud. When the wind is right, two kites collide gently in midair and tangle filaments, exchange a packet of salt-wet genetic paste protected in a wax, and later one of them will fray at the trailing edge and a dozen coin-sized nymphs will peel off like confetti and be caught by the felt-moss of a grove below. The grove raises them, not as young, but as *cleaners*, until they are big enough to be thrown back to the wind.

The mirrored grazers - those six-legged hares that move in a sudden synchronized flood, click-click-clicking across the salt crust - are the grove's blood cells. They don't graze on the glass leaves. They graze on the *efflorescence* - the white and ochre salt flowers that bloom and shatter between groves. Their mirrored backs are not camouflage from predators. They are mirrors to keep from cooking under the sharp white sun while they sprint across the void between islands, where there is no shade and the stone throws heat back at you. They move together because alone they'd be picked off by the wind, tumbled and lost. In a flood, the swarm makes its own boundary layer.

And the clouds of glass-winged insects that make your teeth ache - each one is the size of your thumbnail, each wing a flawless plate that buzzes at a frequency that resonates the salt crust and makes it briefly deliquesce, just wet enough to lick. They are tongues. They drink the bloom before it shatters.

All of them - kites, mirrors, glass-flies - are not separate ecologies. They are the mobile parts of the grove-creatures. They are how an island reproduces and talks and eats across emptiness without ever leaving emptiness. The void is not empty to them. It is a sea they cross in swarms, precisely because the wind between groves is so lethal to one.

That is why it is so loud. They have to be loud. The mesa roars, so to be heard over it you don't shout - you *tune* the roar.

### The Attendants

And then there are the ones who tend the built things.

You missed them at first because they look exactly like part of the grove. You walked past three.

They are small - when they finally unfold you realize they are not larger than a dog, though long. Imagine a low, many-legged thing - ten or twelve legs, jointed wrong, more like a flexible centipede than anything vertebrate - with a back covered not in fur but in that same black-violet glass plate, overlapping like armor. But between each plate rises a comb of taut filament, a dorsal harp.

They live *inside* the groves, in the dense, choking interior where air is still and warm and smells of ozone and brine. During the day you don't see them because they are tucked under the plates, folded flat, photosynthesizing with the colony, drinking from its internal bladders. They are not parasites. They are the colony's hands.

At dusk, when the plates turn inward and the mirrored hares come home to fold, the Attendants come out.

They walk on the bare stone where nothing else walks, and the wind doesn't take them because they are heavy, dense with stored salt and mineral. They walk from grove to pylon to wall to bowl, always alone, never swarming. One per grove, maybe. The whole mesa might hold thirty.

Watch one reach the pylon - that thirty-meter thing that swallows light out of phase, with the dust torus trembling around its base. It doesn't touch it. It rears up on its back legs and *sings*.

Not with a mouth - it has no mouth you can find, only a radular slit underneath that laps salt. It sings with its back. It tightens and loosens those filament harps and lets the wind play them, but *tuned*, precisely out of phase with the pylon's shimmer. The dust torus stutters. The air between two pylons thickens into that brief lattice of solid light, and then collapses with that molar-cracking bang. When it does, a puff of fresh dust - new matter, precipitated out of the thickened air - settles around the pylon's base, and the Attendant walks forward and laps it, stores it, carries it back to its grove.

They are gardeners. The pylons are not machines left behind. They are *looms*. Beyond-modern not because they have power sources but because they *are* the physics. They hold a standing wave in the air between them the way a rock holds an eddy in a river. The Attendants don't operate them. They weed them, tune them, harvest the fallout. That low wall that runs fifty meters and stops, its script rearranging when you're not looking - trace it with an Attendant nearby and you see: the script is footprints. The wall is a plow, a windbreak that makes a specific turbulence on its lee side, and the script is the trace of dust settling in that turbulence, letter after letter, endlessly rewritten.

The shallow bowl where the air is heavier and luminous and the glass leaves grow *away* from it - that is a nest. Or a womb. Every few years, when the resonance is right, the dome of transparent force flickers on by itself and traps a vortex of those glittering insects inside. The Attendants gather around the rim and wait. Inside the dome, the insects go frantic, buzzing until their glass wings shatter from harmonic feedback, and the fragments snow down into the bowl and melt into a slick. The Attendants drink that slick. Later, back in the grove, they will secrete it as new filament, new plate, new felt.

They don't have cities because they don't need them. The mesa *is* the precinct, and one hamlet is enough. They don't have language the way you think - they have the wind. To talk, two Attendants will climb opposite pylons and play their back-harps until the space between them rings, and the interference pattern *is* the sentence. You heard it as a crack in your molars.

They are not loud themselves. In a place that never stops roaring, they are the quietest things in it. That's how they survive. They let the kites do the screaming, the glass-flies do the glittering, the groves do the breathing. They move in the silences between gusts, in the hundred-meter stretches of bare, ringing stone where there is nothing at all. That's their road.

If you stay until the light slants and the pylons begin to sing lower, their tone so deep you feel it through your boots, you will finally see them all at once - one dark shape perched on each pylon, one moving like a slow ripple between groves, their dorsal harps catching the last white sun and throwing it back, not as light but as sound, a low note that makes the dust torus spin faster, getting ready for another cold night where no dew will ever fall.

They will not look at you. You are too big, too water-soft, too transient. You are a weather event that will pass. They will simply keep tuning, while around them the salt blooms and shatters and blooms again, hyperactive, restless, deafeningly, perfectly alive.
