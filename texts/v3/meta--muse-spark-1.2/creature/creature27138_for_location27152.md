There is no one here, at first.

That is your first thought, because nothing moves at human speed and the water has taught everything to be still.

Then you notice that the stillness is tended.

At first you think the inhabitants must be ghosts of the workers who built the place — that these are just the empty terraces and turbine halls they left behind, preserved under warm water like insects in amber. But a ghost would be in a hurry. A ghost would be tragic. What lives here is not tragic. It has finished becoming.

You have to let your eyes adjust to the 0.6 light, to the particulate, to the halos. After a long minute, after your heartbeat stops being the loudest thing in the basin, you see it:

They are lying along the pipes.

### The Dwellers

They were human. They are still human, in the way rust is still iron.

You cannot tell at first if you are looking at one person or at a stain of life that has grown over many. The water rounds everything and the velvet algae covers every horizontal plane — including them. They do not stand out from the town because they are not separate from it. They are the town's way of occupying the last niche: the niche shaped like a person.

Individually, they are long and slow. Not tall — elongated, as if gravity had once pulled gently and then forgotten to let go. The warm water holds them up so they never had to be strong. Limbs thin, slightly too long at the joint, skin the pale, translucent olive of something that has not seen sun in generations but has lived always in lamplight. Amber sodium light passes a little way through the backs of their hands, showing the blue thread of veins underneath.

Hair is not really hair anymore. It has become a filamentous kelp — black-green, thick as twine, growing in slow ribbons from scalp and shoulders and forearms, swaying with the convection even when they are perfectly still. You thought at first that kelp was growing *on* them. Then you understand the kelp is part of how they sense. It shivers when a valve sighs open three streets away.

Their faces are smooth, eroded like the concrete — the sharpness of nose and chin softened by water until they look a little like river stones with eyes. The eyes are large, not monstrously so, just night-adapted — nictitating, amber-flecked, built for that perpetual late-afternoon haze. They don't blink often. When they close, it's slow, like a shutter.

And at the neck, frilled gills. Delicate, branching lace the color of faded rose, fluttering constantly, even in sleep. They do not have lungs that convert. They breathe the water directly now, breathing the warm teal-amber bathwater in and out, in and out, on the same solemn piston rhythm as the generators in the center. Inhale for four seconds. Hold for four. Exhale for four. If you listen close you hear the same soft *fizz* the algae makes. They learned it from the algae, or the algae learned it from them.

They wear almost nothing, because nothing stays separate for long. A boiler suit, perhaps, but drowned and colonized until it is more algae and mineral crust than fabric, the pockets sealed shut by lime. The rust has become a skin on them too — a faint red-black freckling at knuckles and collarbones where iron from the water has precipitated and sealed rather than infected. They are not dirty. They are patinated.

There are not many. A hamlet's worth. Eighteen? Twenty three? It is hard to count because they are packed so densely into the packed density and because they are always partly tucked away. One is folded into the split-open water tower, asleep upright in the vertical garden with roots elbowed gently around his calves. One is lying full-length along a conveyor belt that still crawls nowhere, riding it like a very slow river, eyes open, tending nothing. One is perched on a catwalk that has become a street, legs dangling into the water, tending the pale violet wisteria blossoms that never open, brushing them with fingertips and humming at a frequency the water carries better than air.

They move like the flywheel.

That is, almost not at all, and then completely. You will watch one for ten minutes and think it is a statue, and then, without any seeming decision, it will have raised an arm to turn a valve wheel one quarter turn. The movement is unhurried, without acceleration, water chastising any hurry with a muffled thud. After the turn, a single perfect bead of oil will hang and dissolve, and the Dweller will remain with its hand on the wheel, resting, as if the action and the rest are the same state. They do not work. They attend. The difference matters.

They talk, but not in a way you can overhear at first. Sound does not travel here; it stays near its source like a small, frightened animal. So they do not throw their voices. They tap. A fingernail on riveted iron — *tick... tick... tick* — in the same meter as the cooling metal. They press a palm to a pipe and feel the low hum in their teeth. Sometimes they come very close to one another, forehead to forehead, gills fluttering against gills, and exchange a cloud of warmer water, and that is a conversation. If you were to try to speak loudly, they would look at you with that slow stone-face and wait until you learned to be quiet.

They are solitary in the way the catfish and the heron are solitary — not lonely, but complete. Each has a niche exactly its size: the hollow under a boiler that has become a house, the niche on a pipe, the hammock strung between two mangrove-knees in a settling tank. Because the town is impossibly full and yet makes room — because there is always another alley behind the alley that shouldn't fit — each Dweller has more space than the physics of the basin should allow. They know the extra alleys. They use them without wonder. When one disappears around a corner that should be a wall, and reappears behind you, they do not think of it as strange. They think of it as taking the short path.

And the most unsettling, most peaceful thing: they are not trapped.

You came down the long throat of concrete stairs expecting prisoners, ragged survivors. But their gills are not a deformity. They are not gasping. The warm water is not hostile to them. It is simply what they breathe. They could not live in your air anymore than you can live long in theirs. The rust has sealed them. The algae has claimed them. The slow electrified pulse of the house-sized flywheel has become their resting heart rate.

If you stay, you will see a young one — though *young* is relative when everything erodes at 1.6. Smaller, paler, its hair-kelp still short and fine, still learning to drift. It does not play. It practices staying still on a gantry for hours, like the heron-thing, watching. When it gets tired, an older Dweller will drift over, impossibly slowly, and tuck it against a warm generator housing, and share its heat and its rhythm until their gills beat together.

They tend the plants and the plants tend them. They wipe silt from the amber bulbs. They free a caught kelp ribbon from a chain-link window without tearing it. They place a wrench on a ledge with great care, knowing it might be on the ceiling tomorrow, and they do not chase it when it floats. That is the polite inconsistency of gravity here, and they have long since made a truce with it.

They do not remember what the factory made. They do not need to. Now it makes the condition for its own continuance, and so do they. They make heat, and circulation, and low electric light, just by being warm, and breathing, and being still.

If you stay long enough — and the water encourages you to stay, it makes your movements slow until you match the flywheel, it makes your breath even out to the pistons, it makes your skin feel less like a boundary and more like another surface for the velvet algae to consider — you will find that the niche that is exactly your size has already been waiting for you. Under a catwalk. In a boiler. On a pipe.

The Dwellers will not invite you. They will not notice, at first. But one will drift near and, very gently, share a mouthful of warmer water with you, and you will understand, without words, the final unspoken rule they have always kept, the one the hamlet taught them:

Nothing leaves quickly, and nothing needs to.
