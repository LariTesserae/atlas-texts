You won't see them at first.

That is the entire point of living here.

You will look, as everyone does when they come over the lip and find the room, for the obvious kind of inhabitant. A hut with a door. A fire with someone tending it. A figure standing framed against the open gulf on the fourth side, looking out. You will scan the terraces and the tarns and the tumbled sheepfold and your eye, trained for other valleys, will tell you *empty*. And you will be right, for a while.

Because what lives here keeps the same intervals as everything else in the bowl. Huge, respectful distances between presences. One bee, then a long emptiness of air. One pika call, then a long emptiness of stone. One of them, then a long stretch of moss where there is no one.

Stay until noon passes. Stay until the sun goes behind the western wall and the air hits its dew point and the weeping rock starts to tick louder. If you stay and keep still, with your back against one of the sitting-polished stones, you will start to sort the motion correctly.

At first you will think it is only more moss.

That is forgivable. Everything that lives here dresses in the same palette. The terraces are black-green and bone-lichen and rust-lichen and the brown-black of wet peat. So are they.

They are low. They have to be. Anything tall up here is torn by the circling wind. When you finally pick one out it will be because a patch of what you thought was dwarf willow mat moved against the wind, not with it.

There are maybe three. Maybe four. In a bowl three hundred meters across, that is a crowd. But you will never see more than one at once, because the bowl is stepped, and a terrace lip hides them, and because they do not like to be seen together. It makes them look like a single creature that is very good at being in different places.

If you are close enough — and you have to be very close, within a few strides, and very still, and they have to decide you are not worth hiding from — this is what becomes more itself:

They are small. Not child-small. Old-small, the way roots are small but old. Four feet at the tallest, usually less, with a crouch built into their shape even when they stand. Broad through the shoulders and hips, narrow through the joints, as if made for squeezing between boulders and settling into peat hollows. Their skin, where you can see it on hands and faces, is not really skin as you think of it. It is pale and damp and faintly translucent, like the underside of saxifrage leaves, veined with grey-green. In the cold bowl light it looks almost luminous. They sweat and weep the same way the cliffs do — beads forming on their brows and forearms even when they are not warm, running down in the same dark varnish-stripes.

Their hair is not hair in most places. Caps and beards and shawls of living stuff. Lichen, actually growing. Rust-orange crust on their shoulders, bone-pale branching lichen along their forearms, a thick mat of haircap moss over their scalps that they occasionally comb with fingers of wet bone. It is not costume. It has rooted. When one of them naps in the sun beside a pool — which they do for hours — tiny white mites and glacier midges move through that moss-hair as if through any other tussock. An alpine bumblebee once landed on one and worked it, and the sleeper did not stir.

Under that, what they wear is what was left in the shrine. Wool gone grey, felted wool, sinew-stitched skins that have been re-softened by centuries of damp. No metal. Stone knives hafted to antler. Pouches of sphagnum for carrying water — not that they ever need to carry it far. Their hands are disproportionate, very large and long-fingered, with nails blackened and softened and widened into something like small spades. For peat. For moss. For carefully lifting.

Because what they *do* is tend.

You thought the terraces were made by time and water and patient hands. You were right on all three. The hands are still here. They are patient in a way humans below are no longer capable of being.

They farm the dew.

That is the only economy this bowl allows. They cannot herd — nothing survives the winter lid of snow — and they do not hunt the pikas or marmots. They have an agreement with them. Instead, all day on slow circuit, each keeping to its own tier of terraces so as not to intrude, they manage condensation.

You will watch one crouch at the foot of a weeping seam where water beads on basalt. With a flat slate shard it scrapes lichen just enough to keep the channel open, not enough to scar. It coaxes the runnel with a fingertip, watches the droplet form, swell, hesitate. When the droplet catches that late light and seems to hang too long before falling — that wrong, upward drift you noticed — it is because the air around them is different. They breathe out cool, saturated air, almost fog. Their breath *is* the bowl's weather. They exhale and the dew point around their heads drops for a hand's breadth, and water forms from nothing onto moss, onto wool, onto their own shoulders, and runs.

They transfer it. A handful of saturated sphagnum lifted from a weeping wall and pressed into a drier cushion of star-moss on the next terrace. A shallow scrape with the nail-spades to deepen a pool's overhang just a millimeter, so it holds shadow longer. Moving a rounded boulder a finger's width downslope over the course of a week so the wind eddy behind it shifts and deposits mist differently. Work so slow you would call it stillness if you only visited for an afternoon.

They drink without bending. They kneel at the braid of runnels and press their wide mouths — fringed, almost amphibious — to the peat and inhale the water through the moss. You can hear it at dusk when everything else goes low: a soft, distinct *sip... sip... sip...* between the ticking rocks.

They speak rarely, and when they do it sounds like the bowl itself. A pika-sharp whistle that means *here, water*, an answering whistle forty meters away that means *here, I hear*. A low marmot thrum felt in the chest. Sometimes at evening, when the fox kits come to drink and see them without fear, they make the chough's *kee-ah* perfectly, and the real choughs answer from the circling wind.

And the shrine is theirs. You thought it was human memory. It is. But the memory has been adopted. The wooden spoon inside is not an offering *to* something — it is their spoon. The coil of wool is theirs, though they did not spin it originally. Some shepherd generations ago left it and they kept it damp so it wouldn't rot, adding a new twist when the old frays. The little carved marmot is the only thing they seem to treat as holy, turning it over and over in those big hands until the ears are smooth. They will add to the cairn: a flat stone carried for an hour from one wall to set precisely where the drip will keep its underside wet.

How long have they been here? As long as the bowl has been this wet. Perhaps they were shepherd children once, left for a summer that never quite ended, who learned to breathe the condensing air and let the moss take their hair. Perhaps they came up from below the peat, the way saxifrage comes up through rock. Perhaps the bowl, empty for so long, grew them the way a pool grows a reflection — because a room that holds that much water and that much circling air eventually needs something to keep its intervals.

They are not lonely. They do not want. Their metabolism is as slow as the water cutting old dry channels. A day for you is a few steps, one pool deepened, one conversation of droplets between walls for them. When the snow seals the bowl like a lid — and you will be gone long before that, you cannot winter here — they simply settle. They sink. They choose a deep moss hollow between two sleeping boulders, pull peat over themselves like a quilt, tuck their lichen beards, and slow until a heartbeat is minutes apart, breathing out so little that the mists above them freeze into a low white shroud. The mountain holds its breath with them.

If you stand to leave, they will not stop you. One might lift its head from its work and look at you with eyes that are dark and peaty and perfectly still, reflecting the cliffs even while rippled by wind — that second, calmer skin you saw on the pools is in their eyes too. You will have the feeling, as you go back over the lip and onto the switched-back scar, that you were not the observer. That the bowl, which had been listening with its leaning walls, has briefly lent you its way of seeing: huge intervals of empty air made bearable because something patient, damp, and intensely green is spaced exactly right to hold the whole motion together.

And long after you are down below the scree and stunted pine, the circling wind still turning, the ticking rock still counting, they will still be there on their terraces, moving a droplet at a time, keeping the room from drying out.
