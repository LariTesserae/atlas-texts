Nothing lives here the way something lives in a forest or a city. Nothing *inhabits* it. Everything that is here *belongs* to it, the way sweat belongs to skin.

They are not visitors. They stayed, or they grew here after the light left. They are built for a world rendered in heat and pressure and touch.

There are layers to them, the way there are layers to the wet air.

### The ones you touch first

The bottom layer is not one thing but a film. It lives on everything.

On every inch of basalt that hasn't been kept hot by a cable, there is a velvet of liverwort and cyanobacteria and iron-breathing bacteria so thick it has become a tissue. It is black-green and rust-red and it sweats. If you press your palm to a ridge, it gives slightly, warm, and your hand comes away smelling of iron and sweet rot. Under a lens - if a lens worked here - you would see it is not one mat but a city: filaments weaving, sheaths inside sheaths, mites grazing on the bacteria that graze on the rock itself, and smaller mites grazing on them. It is already softening the knife-edges of the young ground, not by erosion but by burial. The rock is not being worn down. It is being eaten and made into more living mat.

This is the first resident. It covers the ground, it covers the reeds, it covers the concrete pilings of the pump houses until the concrete is no longer concrete but a living, respiring hull.

### The ones in the water

The water is not empty. The water *is* them.

Crouch by any pan - those warm, black sheets only inches deep and miles wide - and be still. Be very still, because they feel vibration before they feel you. At first the surface looks still. Then it dimples without being touched.

They are pale and they have forgotten eyes.

There are amphipods the length of your finger, translucent white-pink so you can see the dark line of their gut, swimming on their sides in slow, deliberate flicks. They crowd each other, dozens to a square foot, but they never collide. They sense the thermal bow wave of the animal next to them and adjust by a millimeter.

Beneath them, flatworms wide as your spread hand glide over the silt and over your boots if you leave them in. They are black on top, to eat what little heat there is, and milky blind underneath. They move without seeming to move.

And the shrimp - colonies of them, no bigger than rice grains, that make the only intentional sound in the water. A single soft *click*. Wait a minute. Another *click* from twenty feet away, answering. They are speaking in pressure. That is how they keep the pan as one territory.

But the most numerous are the ones on the ridges, half in water, half in air. The salamanders. They are not like salamanders outside. They are blind, their skin so thin it is clear, their heads fringed with huge, feathery external gills that spread like ferns to breathe the wet air itself. They pile together in the seeps where water beads out of the rock, dozens layered flank to flank, gill to gill, breathing each other's moisture. They are warm to the touch. Warmer than the water. If you pick one up it does not struggle, it just slowly reorients to the new heat gradient - your hand - and settles. They have no need to hurry. Food falls on them.

None of them sing. Nothing here calls for a mate across distance. You find a mate by touching the next body over.

### The ones in the air

The air is crowded too, but you have to feel for it.

The gnats are not a swarm. They are a property of the air, like humidity. A cloud of them hangs permanently over every pan, so dense your skin registers them as a change in texture before you realize they are animals. They don't bite. They have no mouths worth the name. They drink condensation off each other's wings and off the cables. They hang motionless until your heat passes through them, and then they part and rejoin behind you without a sound.

Above them, the moths. As big as your hand, sometimes as big as a small bat, but impossibly light. Their fur is not fur but a felt that wicks water straight out of the air, so they are always heavy and damp. They fly without vision, on wingbeats so slow and broad you feel the air compress against your face a half second before the wing brushes you. They are deaf and blind and they navigate by the heat of the pump houses, by the faint electrical fields that leak from the shielded cabling. Their caterpillars live inside the reeds, hollowing the wire-stiff stems and filling them with warm silk.

They are silent because silence is cheaper here. Sound is swallowed by the wet air. To make a sound that travels here costs more energy than to stay quiet and wait to be touched.

### The ones that tend the machines

And then there are the ones that made the ridges parallel.

They are still here. Or their children are. Or what they became after working for generations in a place where photons die within inches.

You will not see them approach. You will feel that the density of presences near one of the stilted sheds has changed.

They are tall and very pale and very slow. If they were human once, they are human the way the salamanders are salamanders - adapted past recognition. Their suits - heavy rubber, canvas, riveted boots - are no longer suits. The condensation and the biofilm and the warm sweat have grown into them until the boundary between skin and garment is gone. Their faces are smooth, not disfigured but simplified: eyes clouded to pearls or sealed entirely over, nostrils widened to taste humidity, ears recessed. They have practiced blindness until it became an anatomy.

They do not use lights. They trail a hand along the cable gantries, reading vibration. They press a cheek to a transformer to judge its hum through bone conduction. They move barefoot through the warm pans, and the flatworms part for them and rejoin behind, and the salamanders do not startle, because they register as warm water that has learned to walk.

There are not many. One per pump house, sometimes two. They do not speak. When they need to communicate across the impossible distances - where a ten-minute wade can be no distance at all - they do what the shrimp do. They knock once on a pipe, *tok*, and wait a long time for the answering *tok* to come through water and steel. That is enough.

Their work is not repair. It is tending. The boilers and turbines and substations were built mid-century, heavy and simple enough to run with intermittent attention. They make heat and pressure and they move water from pan to pan in slow, patient circuits that have no purpose now except to keep the water from going still. The tenders oil the bearings that never cool, scrape the barnacle-like filter feeders off the warm cables only where the crust gets too thick to pass current, redirect a sheet of water that has decided to flow uphill back into its pan by nudging silt with a boot. They do it without haste. Erosion here is measured in the bloom of rust, and so is their labor.

The buildings are not homes. They are bodies. The tenders sleep on the concrete that is warm from the turbines, curled together under the throb for warmth, breathing the same wet air as the salamanders piled outside. They are layered into the ecology, another film over the concrete.

### The one that is the place

And finally there is the resident you cannot isolate, because it is not an organism but a condition.

The darkness itself lives here.

It is not an absence. At 0.0, it has presence, weight, appetite. It eats light at birth, so that a filament inside a pump house makes heat you can feel on your face from a foot away but throws no illumination onto the water. The fauna do not evolve eyes because there is nothing for an eye to do - the medium itself forbids it. The tenders do not miss sight because the dark has taught them to render the world as a thermal map, a tactile map: here is the hot line of cable, here is the cooler sheet of pan, here is the dense, crowded, breathing mass of gnats, here is the empty gap where the next ridge should be but isn't yet.

This resident causes the loose rules. It puckers space at the corners where the ridges meet at the wrong angle. It lets you arrive without crossing the middle. It lets water flow uphill in a glassy sheet because gradient is a suggestion here, not a law. Everything that lives here knows how to move through that. The moths already fly through air that swallows sound; the tenders already walk toward a hum that may be a quarter mile or two steps away without being surprised when distance misbehaves. They are all natives to a place where physics is still negotiating.

If you stay long enough, you stop being a visitor to them too. Your skin sweats and does not dry. Your breath is muffled. Your hand, resting in the black warm water, is brushed, then brushed again, and then left alone when the swarm has mapped you as warm, unthreatening, already crowded. You become another warm surface for condensation to bead on and drip from, *tick... tick... tick*, another pressure in the dark.

That is how you know they live here. Not because you see them, but because the warm, wet, absolute dark is not empty when you stand in it. It is full, and it is touching you back.
