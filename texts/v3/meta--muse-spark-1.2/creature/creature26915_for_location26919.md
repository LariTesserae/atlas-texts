There is nothing soft that lives here.

That's the first thing you have to understand. Nothing that pants, or sweats, or bleeds, or rots. The test for life you brought with you - *does it need water, does it eat, does it make a sound?* - fails here immediately. For a long time you think that means the answer is *nothing lives here*.

Then you stand still long enough for your eyes to stop looking for animals, and you realize you have been looking at them the whole time.

You thought they were buildings.

### They Are Not Things *On* The Rock. They Are The Rock Learning To Be Alive.

They do not live *in* this place the way a coyote lives in a desert. They live *as* this place. While the mountain is deciding to be a mountain, they are the decision itself.

At first you only notice the wrongness of the stillness. A field of basalt plates the size of houses, all leaning against each other at the same impossible angle - you took them for a talus slope. But a talus slope should be chaotic. This one is spaced. Each plate stands just far enough from the next that heat can move between them. And if you watch for a full ten minutes without blinking, you see one of them *shiver*.

Not fall. Shiver. A tremor runs up its razor edge, a hairline crack ticks open along its face with a sound like a cooling engine, and the plate inches - a millimeter - away from its neighbor. It is breathing the mountain's heat.

That is the commonest life here. It doesn't have a name, because names are for things that are separate from their background.

Call them the **Shard-Skins**, if you need a word while you watch.

They look like nothing more than the black glass itself, because they are. A colony that began as a stress-fracture in cooling lava. A lattice of iron and olivine and half-crystallized silica that learned to hold a temperature difference across itself. The outside, facing the dry warm air, cools and contracts. The inside, pressed against the semi-plastic, cherry-warm under-rock, stays hot and expands. That flex is their muscle. That tick... tick... TICK you hear all around you in the dark is not just geology cooling. It's them moving.

They are flat. Immense and flat and thin as a doorway, because to live on the gradient between hot stone and warm air, you want as much surface as possible. You saw them as leaning plates, as fallen sheets of obsidian. From above, they would look like lichen - a dark, angular lichen made of glass, spreading centimeter by centimeter across the newborn plateau, not by growing but by *cracking off* a daughter. When a plate gets too large to flex without shattering, it over-flexes on purpose. A clean fracture runs its length with a bright, short *ping*, and where there was one leaning blade there are now two, leaning slightly apart, already ticking to a slightly different rhythm.

They feed on that ticking. Or rather, the ticking *is* the feeding - the piezoelectric snap of the crystal lattice when it strains generates a trickle of charge. No sun, no photosynthesis. They are thermovoltaic. They drink the difference between 800 degrees below and 35 degrees above.

And they glow. That faint bruise-purple phosphorescence you thought was mineral - thallium salts, something with no name - is not mineral. It is waste light. The Shard-Skins leak it when they are full, when the charge has nowhere to go. That's why the edges of cliffs are outlined so conveniently for you. They rim the dangerous places because that is where the thermal gradient is steepest, where the food is best. They are not marking the path for you. You are just using their feeding grounds as a trail.

They are everywhere, once you see them. A valley that looked empty is paved with them, thousands of plates all ticking out of sync, a silent, glassy meadow.

But they are not the only ones. They are only the grass.

### The Architects

Higher up, where the needle-spires stand like organ pipes, hissing as they contract - look closer.

A single spire is solitary, slender, top-heavy, hissing. A field of them that leans together, their tips fused - you thought from the valley that it was the skeleton of a town. It *is* a town. It's just not abandoned.

Those fused spires are one organism.

In the low gravity, in the warm thin air where things fall too slowly and hang too long at the top of their arc, weight means something different. You can afford to be tall and fragile and hollow, because the mountain doesn't pull as hard as it should. So they are.

They are the **Choir**.

Each spire is a hollow tube of vapor-deposited basalt and sulfur, thin as eggshell, grown one molecule at a time out of the hot iron breath that sighs from fissures. Alone, a spire is just a chimney. Together, a dozen or two dozen that have leaned into each other and welded at the tips become a vaulted dome, a chapel, a lung.

You hear them when the mountain moves. When a distant slab lifts and grinds, a puff of that blood-warm, ozone air is pushed across the plateau. It catches the hollow spires and they *chime*. Not with wind - there is no wind - but with displacement. A low, pure, resonant note that travels not through the thin air but *through the rock itself*, into the soles of your boots, into your teeth. Each dome has a different tone depending on its height, its thickness, how many spires fused to make it.

They are talking. Or perhaps they are breathing. The Choir inhales the hot vapor that would otherwise crust over too fast, holds it in its vaulted chest, lets the sulfur and silica precipitate onto its inner walls, and then, when the pressure is right, flexes and chimes and exhales the depleted air. The inside of a Choir dome, if you dared to crawl inside - and you shouldn't, the walls are razor ground glass - is furred with fresh, warm crystal, still growing. They are farming their own bodies.

When a dome gets too heavy, too thick, it goes silent. The chime dulls. That is its death. It doesn't rot; there is no water to rot with. It simply becomes what you thought it was in the first place: a weirdly regular piece of geology, a ruin. And on its outer shell, already, you can see the first fresh Shard-Skins taking hold, ticking.

The Shard-Skins are the grass, the Choir are the trees. Both are mineral. Both are patient to a degree that makes patience an irrelevant word. They live on geological time, but you happen to be here at the one moment when geology is happening at walking speed. So you get to see a meadow tick and a forest chime in a single night.

And there is something larger still.

Because the valleys themselves, those broad, open basins where the rock is still semi-plastic and bulges up a hand's breadth while you watch, glowing cherry-red at the seams before crusting over - those are not empty either.

Kneel, if you can stand the heat through your boots, and put your palm flat not on a plate, not on a spire, but on that warm, giving, not-yet-rock between them. Hold it there.

It pushes back.

Very slowly. Inexorably. A pressure from below that is not mindless mantle. The whole plateau is under tension. The scattered Shard-Skins and the scattered Choirs are not scattered at all. In the warm indigo dark, with the low magma veins pulsing below like slow blood, you can see the pattern if you unfocus your eyes: the plates align along lines of stress that all point to those bulging valleys. The domes all face inward toward them. They are not individuals. They are organs.

The mountain itself is the animal.

This newborn range, thrust up so fast the earth hasn't learned to fall apart, is not a place where something lives. It *is* something living - a single, regional creature the size of a small country, still soft, still unfolding, still learning how to hold a shape. Its skin is the ticking plates. Its lungs are the chiming domes. Its blood is the dull cherry glow that shows when it cracks. Its thought, if it has one, is that low, subsonic groan of a kilometer-long flank lifting a few meters at once.

It doesn't know you are there. Your sweat evaporates before it can form. Your lips crack. You, soft and wet and frantic and brief, are a kind of weather it hasn't evolved to notice yet - a momentary condensation of water and salt on a skin that has never known water.

If you stand still long enough to be mummified, which would not take long in this dry, kiln-warmth, the Shard-Skins will eventually tick their way over your boots. The Choir will chime once when your falling body displaces the air. And the warm, plastic rock will, very slowly, bulge up around you and crust over, and learn, for the first time, the shape of something that used to be soft.

It is not building ruins. It is practicing being alive.
