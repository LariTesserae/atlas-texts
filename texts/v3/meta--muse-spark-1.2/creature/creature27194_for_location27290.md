It is not empty. That is the first mistake you make.

From a mile out, between the trunks, it looks empty. Cold open water, too clear, too still, dusted with marine snow. You think *nothing lives here except the trees themselves.* Then you hold still long enough for your ribs to stop vibrating with the hydraulic groan of the forest, and the water resolves.

It was never empty. It was just operating at a scale where you didn't know how to look.

Nothing here lives *in* the forest. Everything here lives *as* the forest. There are no tenants. There are only organs.

### The Engines Themselves

Start with what you thought was the scenery.

The trunks are not trees. That was a word you brought with you. They are not kelp, not reef, not plant, though they wear all those shapes like a uniform. They are the inhabitants in the oldest sense — the ones who do not leave, who *are* the place.

Each one is not an individual. You understand this when you watch two of them breathe out of sync for a few hours and then, slowly, without hurry, fall back into the same rhythm. The water they jet from their high vents does not just drive the currents; *is* the current. The low, rib-shaking groan you feel before you hear is not wood creaking. It is peristalsis on a geological scale. At their bases, the intake mouths — sphincters the size of subway tunnels, fringed with baleen-like strainers made of living kelp-steel — dilate. Cold, thin, plankton-poor water from the open cathedral is drawn in. You can see it go milky at the threshold, as if the water itself is being chewed. Inside — and you only ever glimpse it when a valve gapes and the interior glows amber with refracted light — there are chambers that are not chambers. Boilers that are stomachs. Mills that are lungs. Lamellae stacked like gills and like heat-exchangers, pulsing, pressing, cooking. The water is held, heated by a slow chemical fire that has no flame, enriched, re-oxygenated, and then expelled in those endless, perfectly steady warm plumes that never vary, never sputter.

They do not grow. They do not need to. They have finished growing in the way a perfect machine is finished. Their bark peels, but it peels *as product* — long, warm ribbons of fiber extruded from seams that open like lips. The fiber is buoyant when it first sloughs, pale green-gold and still soft, hardening as it cures in the cold until it rings when something brushes it. It drifts free in the current. It is not waste. Nothing is waste.

Their canopy leaves — those mile-wide lily pads that make the false sky — do not photosynthesize the way you expect. They transpire industry. Their undersides are bearded with root-forests that hang down a thousand feet and stop, and their upper sides sweat that thin oil-sap that makes rainbows on the interface. And ceaselessly, from fields of pores on every trunk and leaf and hanging vine, they exhale. Not a few bubbles. A manufacture of atmosphere. Whole rivers of white, roaring fizz that make the water thunder and turn opaque, that rise and never break, only dissolve and are inhaled again at the bases. The forest breathes itself.

If the forest thinks, it thinks here, slowly, with water pressure and chemistry and the timing of valves. Not a mind. A consensus.

### The Blood

Before anything else, there is the snow.

It never stops falling, in light shafts, in darkness between shafts. It is not detritus. It is the plankton, but "plankton" is too small a word. Clouds of diatoms the size of snowflakes, each a glassy, oil-filled pillbox catching the sun; copepods like translucent commas; bacteria in such uncountable numbers the water between trunks has a faint, sweet, metallic taste. They are not drifting. They are being *circulated*, farmed by the plumes. Warm nutrient water jets out, cold clear water falls back, and in the gyre between, the plankton blooms, dies, blooms again without ever collapsing. They are the blood cells of this place, the point of the whole engine. All the larger life is just a way of keeping the blood moving.

### The Grazers

And then you see the fish, because they are the only thing that moves the way an animal should.

They are everywhere and nowhere — this forest is sparse by design. You can swim a mile and see only snow, then you round the shoulder of a trunk and the water is suddenly solid with them. Silver, blind, translucent as frosted glass. No eyes, or eyes reduced to a single light-sensitive smear, because in the endless midday filtered through a thousand feet, and in the abyssal dark near the basalt, eyes are a rumor. They have no fear. They have never been hunted.

They school not in spheres or bait-balls but in tori — immense, slow, perfect donuts, hundreds of thousands thick, turning forever around the same plume. The warm jet from a trunk hits the cold, they tilt into it, mouths open, straining the plankton-enriched water through gill-rakes fine as silk. Their exhalation is fine particulate that feeds the snow. Their feces, if you can call it that, is a clean, sand-like pellet that falls straight down to the basalt where something else waits. They never leave their torus. A given school has held the same orbit for longer than any mountain on the world below has existed. When one dies — and they do die, though rarely, though without violence — it simply stops swimming, goes pale, and drifts outward until the current peels it away and it is caught by the scavengers. The gap closes. The torus does not thin.

You feel their sideways gravity most acutely. Stand neutral in the water near a torus and you will drift *toward* it, not down, as if the mass of that much coordinated life has learned to tug. It hasn't. It's the water. The forest's other physics. The fish have grown into it the way a bird grows into air.

### The Artisans

Nothing rots here, nothing is left to waste, because of the small ones.

You have to get close to a trunk to see them, and then you wish you'd seen them sooner because they explain the silence where echo should be. They are everywhere on the bark — if bark is the right word for that interwoven mat of sail-sized kelp fronds, moss meadows, and root-buttresses. They look at first like lice, like isopods, like crabs made of the same kelp-steel they tend. Some are the size of your hand, some the size of a door, all low and flat and many-legged, the color of old kelp and basalt.

They are the loom-workers. When a ribbon of fresh fiber extrudes and hardens, they come. They cut it with mandibles that spark faintly in the cold water, and they carry it. Not away. *Into place.* They weave it back into the trunk, or span it between hanging vines that have stopped in midwater, perfectly horizontal, held by that sideways pull. They suture leaves together in the canopy. They patch the places where the endless flexing would otherwise crack. Their movement is deliberate to the point of maddening — each leg placement considered, each cut made with a surgeon's slowness, because the water is so cold everything is viscous with purpose. They eat the fiber as they work, and excrete more fiber. They are not building. They are maintaining. There was never a construction; there is only upkeep that has become indistinguishable from growth.

Listen close to a trunk wall and you hear them: a faint, endless clicking and snapping under the great groan and hiss, like a million tiny rivet guns.

On the basalt floor between trunks, their cousins work. Sessile, barnacle-like, but not barnacles. They are dark glass mounds with a single, constantly opening pore, cemented to the scoured basalt by holdfasts older than species. They take the falling pellets from the fish tori, the occasional fallen lily-leaf the size of a field, and they dissolve them with a cold acid sigh, pumping the reclaimed nutrients back into the uptake currents that feed the trunks. The floor looks barren until you realize it is paved with them, a living circuitry, each one exactly far enough from the next that their intake fields do not overlap. They do not move. They have never needed to.

### The Bellows

The large ones move slowly enough you think they are part of the trunk until they detach.

They are manta, they are whale, they are nothing you have a name for. Twenty, fifty, a hundred feet across, flat as pressed leaves, mottled green-black on top and pale, gilled white underneath. They hang in the plumes, perfectly still relative to the jet, letting the warm water wash over them. Their great mouths are open, not to feed — though plankton sticks to their gill curtains in comical mustaches — but to *breathe for the forest.*

When a trunk exhales its roaring column of bubbles, the water becomes dangerously supersaturated, fizzy enough to kill the grazers and the artisans if it pooled. The Bellows swim through the white columns and they *comb* them. Sheets of cilia along their ventral sides beat and break and dissolve the bubbles, turning thunder into a fine, drinkable effervescence. Their backs exhale a counter-plume of cooler, de-gassed water that sinks. They are the pressure valves, the lungs' lungs. They migrate between trunks on a schedule you can set a clock to, though there are no clocks here — one trunk's plume to the next, across the open cathedral, moving with a slow, manta-wing flap that displaces tons of water without hurry. You feel the sideways tug again when they pass; water piles up in front of them in a way that should not happen.

They never vocalize. Or rather, their vocalization is the absence of echo. The forest's roar should reverberate. It doesn't, because their vast, flat bodies absorb and re-emit the sound at exactly the same frequency, canceling reflection. They are acoustic baffles with hearts.

Inside the trunks, where you cannot follow without being taken in and refined, there are smaller versions of them — pale, eyeless, wormish things that live their whole lives in the boiler-stomachs, eating the buildup off the lamellae, keeping the mills from seizing. They are born there, extruded like the fiber, and they die there, and their bodies are refined. They have never seen light.

### The Canopy Walkers

If you could somehow breach the surface — that wind-chopped, misted interface that never breaks — you would find the last ones, walking upside down.

On the upper side of those mile-wide lily pads, where the thin air meets the water-skin, there is a world that is almost dry. The pads are thick enough, buoyant enough, that rain pools on them in shallow, cold ponds. And on them, and between them where the weave is tight, live things that are insects the way the trunks are trees: only by analogy. Striders the size of dogs, with feet that dent but never pierce the skin, skating on the interface with legs like kelp-steel stilts. Beetles with elytra like solar panels, farming the film of algae that tries to grow on the air side and scraping it clean. They drink from below by stabbing a proboscis through the leaf. They never go in the water. To them, the ocean below is sky, and the Bellows are clouds.

On the underside of the canopy, hanging in the root-forests that never reach the bottom, are their inverted mirrors — shrimp-like, translucent things that cling to the hanging vines and filter the snow that collects there, their antennae so long they trail in the current like prayer flags, tasting the chemistry of the next plume before it arrives.

None of them knows there is a world below the plateau. To them the universe is water between a basalt floor and a green sky that breathes.

---

What lives here, then, is not a collection of species. It is a single, distributed, cold, loud, patient organism that has mistaken itself for an ecosystem. Or an ecosystem that has achieved the perfection of an organism. The distinction stopped mattering here a very long time ago.

There are no hunters and hunted the way you understand it. There are only flows. Plume to plankton to grazer to floor-pore to intake to plume. Fiber extruded to woven to patched to extruded. Bubble exhaled to combed to dissolved to inhaled. Everything moves, everything is used, everything returns. Nothing changes, because change would be inefficiency, and inefficiency was selected out eons before your continent finished cooling.

And you — neutrally buoyant, warm-blooded, loud in a different way, shedding heat and skin and carbon in water that has not had to deal with novelty in a billion years — you are the only thing here that is not part of the loop. The fish will part around you without seeing you. An Artisan will pause, antennae tasting your strange chemistry, and then decide you are not fiber and go back to its seam. A Bellows will pass overhead and your ears will pop with its pressure shadow.

If you stay long enough, the water will try to include you. The sideways pull will find you. The snow will try to settle on you. The floor-pores will open, expectantly. But you ache with the cold, and you are too fast, too warm, too irregular. The factory will keep thrumming at full operation around you, perfect, purposeless, forever, while you float in the open water between pillars and finally understand that wilderness and machine were never opposites. They were just two words for something that, given enough time and cold light and pressure, learns to keep itself.
