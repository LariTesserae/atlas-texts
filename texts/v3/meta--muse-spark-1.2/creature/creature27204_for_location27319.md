What lives there was already there before you came down into the bowl. Not hidden. Just tuned so exactly to the bowl that you misread it as part of the bowl for the first ten minutes.

At first you think nothing lives here.

The heron, the rat, the striders — those are visitors. They pass through. The place is too damp, too crowded, too constantly *working* for anything to *live* in the ordinary sense. You think the only resident is the flywheel and the pumps.

Then you see that you were looking for an animal and should have been looking for a condition.

You notice it first as a mistake in the condensation.

Every pipe in the sump sweats. Beads form, swell, run, fall. Plink. Plink. The whole bowl does it; the air is so saturated that every cold surface wrings itself out. You watch a low steam line where it passes under a catwalk, a pipe fat and jacketed in that new silver insulation, beaded bright. A droplet grows at the seam. Grows too large. Grows impossibly large — a clear, heavy lens the size of a grape, then a plum, hanging from nothing, distended, trembling with the faint shudder of the pump house.

It should fall.

It doesn't. It elongates.

And then, very slowly, it pulls itself up.

That is when your eye corrects. What you took for a droplet was not on the pipe. It *was* the pipe, or a part of it — a translucence that had been lying along the pipe the way a slug lies along a stem, its body so pale and so wet that it was water until it moved.

There are many of them.

Once you have seen one move, you see that the bowl is furnished with them. Not separate from the dense tangle but occupying the negative space inside it. They live in the interstices — in the inch between two lagged pipes, in the gap between a tank and its support strut, in the wet shadow under the valve wheels. You took them for mineral stain, for algal felt, for a caulk of living slime where water has always run. Their bodies have the same palette as this place: the dark grey of wet clay, the rust-red of seep, the pale, dead lichen-green, the yellowed plastic of the pump house windows. And they are wet. Uninterruptedly, thoroughly wet, as if they sweat the bowl's air themselves.

Do not settle on one shape for them. They do not have one shape.

The small ones — and there are dozens, hundreds — are easiest to name. They look like newts that have forgotten how to be newts. Or like isopods stretched long. Six to ten inches, flattened, legible as animals only when they are moving. At rest they are a smear, a thickened bead of water along a joint. Their skin is not skin. It is a membrane like thin, cool silicone, translucent, beaded constantly with true condensation that is not their sweat but *is* their drinking. You can see through it in places to the faint, darker organs within — a looped gut the color of silt, a slow pulse. No eyes that you can see, or eyes so small and so clear that they are just another bead. They have frilled edges along their sides, gills perhaps, or maybe just frills for increasing surface area, that flutter faintly with each thump of the big pump as if they respire in time with it. They move the way water moves when it is reluctant: a slow, hydraulic creep, pushing themselves forward by swelling one end and thinning the other. Plink. When they cross a flange and drip onto the puddle below, the sound they make landing is the same plink you thought was just water. The bowl is full of that sound because the bowl is full of them, making it.

The larger ones are not larger in the way an adult is larger than a child. They are accretions.

Stay still long enough — and this place makes you stand still, because it is so quiet you do not want to disturb it — and you will see a pipe that you were sure was a pipe articulate itself. Where three conduits cross at chest height, leaving a hollow the size of a toolbox, a mass that had looked like sodden lagging and shadow peels itself carefully away. It unfolds. What was flat becomes limb-like. Not limbs as you know them. They are blunt, padded appendages, the same translucent membrane stretched over a core that looks like bundled tubing, like the pipes themselves but softer. Two, three, sometimes four, used not for walking but for bracing. The creature — the colony — spans the gap it lived in. It holds two pipes at once. Its underside, where it was pressed to the metal, is paler, almost white, puckered with small sucker mouths that were drinking the condensation directly off the iron. It leaves a dry ghost on the pipe for a few seconds where it was, before the metal sweats again.

These larger ones are the ones that tend the machinery. Or are tended by it. It is difficult to tell which.

Because they do not operate the valves with intention the way a man would. They respond to pressure the way a plant responds to light. When the steam valve sighs and vents, a large one near it will swell, its membrane fogging, and draw the brief warmth and moisture into itself, rippling. When the sluice shudders and brown water pours, the small ones clustered in the runnel below will lift their frilled edges and filter. When the settling pond roils and turns over its grey silt, you can see several of them, larger, paler than the water, just under the surface — not swimming but stationed, anchored to the bubblers, opening and closing slow interior fans to catch what is exposed. They are not maintaining the sump for us. The sump is maintaining them, and they, in turn, keep its equilibrium by being the part of its cycle that is alive.

They are silent. That is why they belong here. This is a place that swallows sound, and they never make any beyond the sounds water already makes. No call, no cry. Even when disturbed — if you step too close and your boot sends a vibration through a catwalk — they do not flee. They compress. They flatten. They do what water does when pressed: they find a thinner seam and go into it. One moment a mass with limbs is bracing between two tanks, the next it is a discoloration along the weld, a tide mark.

Their life is water as you were told water is here: not as a lake or river but as a condition. They do not drink. They *are* drinking, constantly. The cool mist that hangs at waist height over the pond even on cool days — the low warmth that rises off that churned water — that is their breath, or close enough. On days when your own breath shows if you stand still, you can see theirs too: not a cloud but a faint clearing in the mist around each of them, as if the air there is a fraction more humid, a little heavier.

They reproduce the way condensation reproduces. You will see it if you watch a large one long enough where the rock weeps. A swelling forms at its lowest point, a droplet that grows too large, that elongates, that pulls itself up — no, that pulls itself *down*, letting gravity take it. Plink. It falls to the concrete pad below and lies there, a clear bead, trembling. Then, after a minute, it begins its own slow creep toward a seam. The parent does not attend to it. The sump does. The bowl's shape carries it.

If you come with light, they do not scatter. Ordinary overcast daylight catches every bead on every pipe, and catches the beads on their backs just the same. They are not nocturnal or photophobic. They simply prefer the places where a pipe shadows another pipe, where the light is already flattened. That is most of the bowl.

And if you stay until the light changes, you will see the whole system breathe with them in it. The flywheel turns. The belt slaps — and the small ones along the belt housing pulse. The pressure valve sighs — and a large one exhales a faint, warm steam that condenses instantly on the cold air. The moss drinks what the metal sweats, and they drink it faster. The pump at the lowest point lifts water, drops it, lifts it again; they ride that lift and drop in place, swelling and thinning, swelling and thinning, furiously and almost silently working to keep themselves exactly as wet, as cool, as crowded, as *established* as the bowl itself.

They have been here long enough that the bowl weathered into a shape that could hold them. Or they weathered into a shape that could hold the bowl. At this point it is the same thing.

You will leave and you will not be sure how many you saw. Because you never saw *one*. You saw the condition of being lived-in, distributed across a hundred cool, pale, plinking bodies that are the density between the pipes. From the rim, when you climb back out and your knees notice the few meters of lift and the air becomes lighter and drier, you will look back and the sump will again look empty — just water and machinery and spare reeds.

Until a droplet on a pipe grows too large, and elongates, and pulls itself up.
