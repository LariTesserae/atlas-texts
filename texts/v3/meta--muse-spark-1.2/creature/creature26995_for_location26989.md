On the High Shelf, nothing lives *on* the land. Everything lives *with* it, low and level with it, like moss itself.

You don't see them at first.

From a swell a kilometer away their city looks empty — just another patch of the green wound in the stone, just turf and lichen and the dark geometry of peat. Then a seam in the green moves. A stone shifts. Smoke that you thought was mist thins and thickens again. And you understand the city was never empty; you were just looking at a camouflage so perfect it wasn't intended as camouflage at all. They build the way the dwarf willow builds — by lying down.

The People of the Shelf are low.

Not short the way a child is short. Low the way a wall is low. Broad, settled, compressed by the weight of the sky. Generations under thin air and hard light and a gravity that sometimes forgets how much to pull have made them wide through the chest and thick through the joints. Their ribcages are great, barrelled things — you can see it even under the felt — built to wring the last bit of oxygen out of every breath. Their legs are short and heavily muscled, their feet splayed and callused to a hide that grips bare stone better than any boot. They move with a deliberate, conserving gait, never hurrying, because hurry costs heat and air you don't have to spare. When they kneel to cut peat or tend moss, they fold down completely, knees to chest to ground, and you realize their natural posture is not standing at all, but crouching.

Their skin is the color of the stone under the lichen when you scrape it clean — pale grey-pink, freckled with broken capillaries from cold, weathered to a matte finish. Their hair, where it escapes their hoods, is coarse and iron-colored, lank with damp, cut blunt with obsidian. Their eyes have gone pale to match the quartz veins in the gneiss: cloud-grey, ice-grey, the kind of light grey that sees best in a flat, shadowless noon and doesn't squint. They age the way the Shelf ages — not by wrinkling but by smoothing. An old one's face is not lined; it is polished, featureless as a river stone, eyes deep-set and calm.

They wear what the Shelf gives them, and nothing more. Heavy felted wool beaten from the down of the crawling birch — not sheep, there are no sheep — carded with fern fiber and worn until it felts into a single, shaggy pelt the color of wet peat. Hoods that become collars that become capes, no seams to let the drizzle in. Mittens that are really just wraps. Their hands, when they take them off, are the most human thing about them and the least human at once. Wide, spatulate fingers, nails thick and blackened, palms crosshatched with a lifetime of fitting dry-stone without mortar. Stone-handed, literally. Children drag their fingertips along walls as they learn to walk, feeling for the centimeter the frost has heaved overnight so they know which stone to hammer back in the morning.

Because that is what they do all day. They tend.

There are no animals on the Shelf, and the people have inherited that vacancy. They do not herd. They do not hunt. They have no word for predator or prey, for flock or pack. If you showed them a bird they would not know to look up for it. They are the only thing that moves of its own volition in that green, and so they have become the animal the ecosystem was waiting for — the pruner, the grazer, the pollinator that wind and water couldn't quite be.

They farm moss the way other people farm wheat. In the terraced basins, they comb the deep mattress with antler rakes, harvest it in damp slabs as thick as mattresses, wring it, dry it over peat fires whose smoke never quite rises. They eat it — sour, mineral bread baked on hot stones, lichen cakes green-black and steaming. They eat the fiddleheads of the door-wide ferns, the sweet, starchy rhizomes of the willow-mats, the tart berries that form once every few years on the crawling birches. They drink the black water from the scour-pools, breaking the clear ice with a copper maul and lowering skin bags into water so cold it burns. Nothing is wasted. What they don't eat, they weave — walls, roofs, fences that are still alive and keep growing after they've been tied down.

And they keep the city from falling apart, which is constant work. The cold heaves. The weird heaves more.

They do not think of the weird as weird.

A child born in a house where the back room is bigger inside than outside doesn't think that's strange; she thinks that's where you store the winter moss because it never molds in there. A man who walks seven steps to the cistern and nine steps back does not wonder why — he just counts differently on the way back, unconsciously, the way you adjust for wind. When condensation beads up off the moss and rises into the air instead of falling, the women there just hold their felt out to catch it.

Their bodies have learned the accommodations. Some of the older tenders have a slight elongation to them if you see them in the wrong place — an arm a little too long on one side where they spend hours working in a fold where space is stretched. Their voices have adapted to the courtyards where sound arrives before it is made; they speak rarely, and when they do it is low, flat, a murmur that carries perfectly for kilometers in the still air without ever needing to be loud. Mostly they don't speak at all. Thin air makes shouting expensive. They signal with hands, with the tap of stone on stone. A whole terrace can coordinate the lifting of a lintel with three clicks of a hammer. Their language, if they still have one that could be written, is not in words but in placement — where you put the cairn, which way you lean the wall to account for the place where water runs uphill.

They are urban without being crowded, and primitive without being simple. The city goes for kilometers and holds thousands, but there is no center, no palace, no market shouting. There are only levels, and each level knows its peat-bank and its runnel and its section of living fence to train. Authority, if it exists, is entirely practical: the ones who remember best where the space is thin, who can feel the hum in the stone before the frost comes. You see them sometimes standing perfectly still in the middle of a paved way with their eyes closed, palm to a leaning wall, listening to that low, planetary thrum — the stone trying to remember its shape. Then they nod and point, and younger hands move a wall two inches to the left.

They are not happy or unhappy. Those words require contrast the Shelf doesn't offer. They are busy, in the way moss is busy — slowly, completely, without anxiety. They breed slowly, gestate long in the cold, bear children who are large-headed and quiet and can crawl on the stone before they can stand. They die slowly, and when they do they are laid not in the peat — the peat is too valuable and too wet to rot anything properly — but on the bare stone between gardens, under a cairn, where the hoarfrost feathers them over and the wind sands them down until they become part of the polish.

If you stand among them and watch long enough, the second wrongness settles in your chest after the first — the first was *why is it so green with nothing eating it?* The second is *why does this feel so right?*

Because there should be animals here. There should be noise and chase and birds. But there aren't. Instead there is this one, single, low, patient animal that learned to be as still and as relentless as the ground itself. Not a tribe surviving the Shelf. A function of the Shelf.

The Shelf erodes, freezes, damps, greens, and holds its breath. And they, in their turf-sunk houses and their three-meter-thick walls, exhale it back — one stone reset, one moss slab cut, one step counted wrong on purpose, forever, without hurry, under a sky that sometimes shows you the same horizon twice.
