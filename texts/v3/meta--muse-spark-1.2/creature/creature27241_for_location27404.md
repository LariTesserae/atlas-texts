Who lives there does not live there the way you do.

At first you think nothing does. That is the city’s first defense — to look empty.

You stand on the salt a long time waiting for a figure to cross a plaza, for a light in a window, for any of the grammars of occupancy you were taught to read. No one comes to a doorway. No face appears. The glider makes its circle and the membrane in the alley retracts and a buttress swells a millimeter, but nothing walks. The streets stay too wide. You get the distinct feeling you have arrived between inhabitants, as you would come to a tidepool at low water and find only the shape where something was.

Then you realize you have been looking at the wrong scale, and at the wrong speed.

What lives here is not *in* the city. It *is* the city — but not all of it, and not in the way a man is a house.

Crouch, if the salt will let you — it will not soften for a knee — and look at the base of the nearest tower where the chitinous wall meets the white flat. Put your hand, not on it, but near it, where you felt the draft. The pores are breathing. Each pore is round and lidded, rimmed with a faint, darker ring like a bruise. They are inhaling the cold. If you stay there for two, three minutes without shifting your weight, you will see one of them shiver and close, and as it does, deeper inside, something else moves.

The wall is not solid. It is a shell. It is a lung and a hive at once.

Inside, diffused by the matte translucence of the material, is a pale shape. You only see it when it moves against the light. It is the size of a large hound curled around itself, but it has no front or back that you can name at first. Its body is a soft, dense sack, off-white, veined faintly pink and grey like the city itself, because it secretes the city. It is wet in a way nothing else here is wet — not with water, there is no water here to give it — but with the same mineral oil that beads in the salt. It glistens when a pore dilates and catches the dim violet, then dulls as it cures. It has no eyes. Along one side, a fringe of blunt, cilial hands — not fingers, not tendrils, but flattened, overlapping flaps — kneads the inner wall, folding new layer onto old. Where it kneads, the wall thickens. Where it rests, the wall thins and pores open to breathe.

There are dozens of them. Perhaps one per tower, perhaps fewer; some towers share one that stretches itself thin through a low arch like warm wax. They do not hurry. The swelling you saw in the buttress was one rolling over in its chamber. The membrane that drew itself across the alley was one extending a veil to taste the air on the other side, to test if the thickness of light there was right for new growth. The cold exhalation from the aperture was one sighing — literally, a gas exchange — venting the stale mineral air it has filtered.

They are not builders. They are masons the way a coral polyp is a mason: they exude their dwelling because they are their dwelling. They do not need tools because their bodies are the tools, and they do not need plans because light tells them where to grow. You noticed the city leans toward where the light pools, where the air looks bent. So do they. Their backs darken faintly on the side of the weight. If you mapped the vector of every new blister and bastion that has erupted in the last season, every one would point down the same invisible incline of light-pressure. They do not decide to make a doorway. The doorway differentiates at the tip of a new blister because the pressure thins there and an opening is more efficient than a wall. The function creates the form after the fact.

They are not many, which is why the city feels hollowed and expectant. One of them needs a plaza to feed. That thin spire vibrating in the center of the empty market is not decoration; it is a feeler, an antenna of living chitin, tuned so fine it shudders when the pressure changes a fraction, and the animal that extruded it is twenty meters away, listening through its body. The hundred windows facing a blank wall are not for looking; they are nostrils and gills, and the blank wall is a lung that exhales toward them. The streets are wide because anything narrower would stagnate. Sound dies here because they need silence to hear vibration through salt and chitin. Even your deadened *tick* as you walked travels down into their chambers as a faint tremor they pause to interpret before deciding you are not weather.

If you stay through the dimming, you will see the only time they ever resemble people. When the city clicks itself shut for its rest — those tiny doors closing deep inside — most of them fold and still. But once or twice in a long watch, one will evert itself.

It does not come out a door.

A seam low on a tower, that you thought was a growth line, will soften and part, and a walker will push itself free. It is the same creature but changed for transit: now upright, now legged. It is tall and improbable, a stilted, top-heavy thing two meters high and almost nothing wide, the sack body drawn up into a narrow trunk, the ciliated flaps extended and stiffened into three or four jointless limbs that end not in feet but in wide, porous pads that drink oil from the salt as it steps. Its skin is the same matte chitin, still curing at the joints, pale and still slightly translucent — you can see a darker knot inside where its organs are. It has no head. At the apex of its trunk is simply a flared, lipped opening surrounded by a rosette of those flat black leaves, borrowed or symbiotic, angled to the light-weight. It does not look at you. It cannot see you. It feels where light is heaviest and steps, very slowly, toward it.

It walks as if each step is being considered by the ground first. A street that looked too wide for anyone is suddenly exactly wide enough for this one pilgrim to cross without its pads ever overlapping the trail of another. It makes no sound. The salt does not crunch under it; it blots. If it passes near another tower it will pause and lay a limb against the wall and the two will exchange a shudder — a low, infrasonic thrum you feel in your molars more than hear — and perhaps a filament is passed, a packet of chemistry, a gene, a memory of where to grow next.

There may be only three or four walkers in the entire bowl at any given time. The rest of the population is sessile, digesting light and mineral air inside their rooms. That is why the city looks abandoned. It was not built for a crowd. It was grown for a sparse, slow quorum that never needs to gather. The plazas are not for markets but for keeping distance. The gliders — flat, translucent, silent — are not fauna separate from them. They are detached lungs, sensory kites, grown and released by the sessile ones to taste the upper air and return to fold themselves onto a roof to be reabsorbed.

You will not see a walker be born unless you wait for that cold blister in the empty plaza to finish. The pearl-white dome steams with cold and its tip is already thinning, pleating, resolving into the lipped rosette, into the pads, into a doorway that is not a doorway for entry but a mold for exit. Inside, oil is gathering around a new knot of tissue, knitting cilia into limbs. It will take hours. It will harden and stand and take its first blotted step toward the thickness in the air, away from you.

It does not know you are there. If you keep completely still, the silence may eventually include you as just another piece of sharp salt, another pore breathing cold, another wall waiting to grow.
