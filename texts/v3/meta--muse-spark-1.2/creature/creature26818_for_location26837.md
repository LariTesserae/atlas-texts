Who lives here has learned how to live in an interval.

You don't see them right away. Not because they hide. Because they are spaced the way everything else is spaced here — like water, like green. One pocket and then a long bare distance, one pocket and then another. If you expect a village you will miss them entirely. If you expect a wilderness you will miss them too.

First, the body that can live here.

It is a human body, but it has been edited by the height for a very long time. The air is warm so it doesn't need to be bundled or stunted. It is long-limbed and spare, dark from the sun which is closer here and unfiltered by much atmosphere. The chest is broad, not barrelled like a cold-mountain person, but wide and deep, with a slow bellows to it. You notice it when they are at rest — how they breathe. Four seconds in through the nose, a pause, six seconds out through the mouth, never hurried, even when they walk fast over bare rock in the heat. Children learn it before they learn to speak. *Breathe before you need to.* If you pant here you waste yourself.

Their blood is good at holding thin air. Their hearts are unhurried. They move like people who live on a drum skin that is always vibrating — no sudden moves, no waste. When they run, and they can run for a long time across the polished stone without shoes, their feet fall flat and soft, toe and heel together, the way you run when you don't want to make more sound than the wind already makes.

They see differently. Eyes narrowed not against glare but against distance. The plateau is always forcing you to look far and then suddenly very close — a half-mile of nothing and then a split in the rock with a frog in it smaller than your thumbnail. So their gaze has that habit, too. They squint at horizons and then crouch for ten minutes looking at a pothole.

### How they occupy emptiness

They do not live *on* the plateau. They live *between* its invitations.

A family — rarely more than four or five blood-linked people, sometimes only two — holds a territory that would be called absurd anywhere else. Maybe thirty square miles of mostly bare rock, within which are six or seven places that can be lived in. A basin where soil has collected deep enough to coax tubers. A fin of stone with a spring at its foot that never quite dries. A mesa-top where the wind is less cruel and the tall grass can be cut for thatch. A cliff with a south-facing overhang pocked with wind-flutes that stays dry in the afternoon storm.

They do not stay in one. They move in a circuit, and the circuit is timed to water, not to season.

You can read their year in the stone if you know how. That hut with four posts and no roof is not abandoned. It is waiting. In three weeks when the potholes on that dome refill, they will be back and re-thatch it in an afternoon with grass they cut and left bundled under a ledge months ago. That terrace wall holding back soil is not a ruin. It is a garden that is fallow, its soil covered in living mulch, its yam vines sleeping as tubers underground. That cairn is not a monument. It is an address and a calendar. *From here, walk toward the notch until the wind sounds like it does in the big pipe, then down.*

They are expert holders, the way the bromeliads are. When the ten-minute storm comes they are already out with every vessel they own — gourds, tight-woven baskets lined with resin, even the hollows of the rock itself which they have deepened slightly with patient pounding, not to make wells but to make cups. A family can catch and keep enough in those ten minutes to live for ten days. The children know every kettle and bowl for miles, which ones go brackish fastest, which ones the blind fish have claimed and should be left alone.

They carry almost nothing between pockets. A stone adze, a copper knife green as lichen, a coil of fiber rope, a fire-stick, a net bag of seed tubers. Everything else they cache. The plateau is their house; the bare rock is their hallway. They don't need to own the hallway, only to know how long it takes to cross.

And they know. Time here is not clock time. It's walk time and dry time and *how long until that succulent pops* time. They will tell you a place is "two storms away" or "as far as a flower's life." They are never late because they never agreed to be early.

### How they live in the deafening

This is the part an outsider can't stand, and why outsiders never stay. The wind organ never stops, and the forest when you are in it never stops, and the storm when it comes erases thought.

They don't fight it by being louder. They became quieter than the place, and more precise.

Their speech is low and close. Not whispered — a whisper is lost. They speak in chest tones, in vowels that can ride under the hum the way the raptor rides thermals. A mother calling a child across a canyon doesn't shout. She makes a sound like the wind makes in one of the larger tubes — a hooting, rounded *ooo* that the rock itself seems to carry. The child knows which pipe is hers.

Most of their communication isn't speech at all. Between pockets they signal with the cairns, with a stacked stone that means *water here, still good*, with a bent branch that means *don't take this basin, we left seedlings*. On the rock flats they walk with a staff and tap as they go — not for balance, but to listen. The pitch of the tap tells them how hollow the stone is underneath, where a cistern might be.

When they are together in a sheltered basin at night — and this is rare, a few nights a month — they sing, but not the way you think. One person will start a low humming and hold it until the wind in the flutes outside shifts to harmonize, and then another will join a fifth above, and then they will stop singing and just listen to the rock sing back the note they gave it. Their religion, if you can call it that, is almost entirely acoustic. They believe the plateau was not eroded but tuned, and that their job is not to carve but to keep it in tune by not filling the wrong holes, not blocking the wrong pipes. A landslide that blocks a flute is mourned the way you would mourn a deafened elder.

That is why their technology stayed primitive on purpose. Metal and quarrying would close the instruments. Even their fires are small, quick, and made in places where the smoke will be shredded by wind before it can stain the stone.

### The other lives they live among

They do not think of themselves as alone here, because they aren't, even if you see nothing for an hour.

Each family has an arrangement with the other rare presences.

The iridescent beetles the size of fists — they farm them, in a way. They leave a certain resinous tree alone because the beetles bore in it and the larvae can be roasted. But they never take all the larvae. They know a forest where the beetles are gone goes silent in a different way and stays silent.

The gliding lizards are messengers. Not trained. Just watched. If a glide-lizard launches from a particular copse and catches a thermal in a certain direction, it means there is a warm updraft there, which means a storm is building on that side. The children can read the lizard like you would read a weather vane.

The raptor that never flaps — they call it the Turner — is never hunted, never chased. To see it circling above your basin is to know your place is well-placed; the Turner only turns where air is moving correctly. If it leaves, something in the wind has changed.

And the blind white fish in the potholes — those are elders. You do not eat them unless you are starving and the pool is large enough to spare them. They have lived in that same bowl of water since before your grandmother's grandmother learned the circuit. They are proof that waiting works. A boy coming of age will be taken to a pothole and told to sit and watch the fish until he understands that stillness is not doing nothing.

Even the plants are treated as co-residents with jobs. The succulents that pop and creak are water-bearers; you can cut one open in emergency but you plant two somewhere else. The vines that grow turbulent and fast — impossibly fast, an inch an hour after rain — are not weeds to be cleared but ropes to be guided. They train them across chasms where they want a living bridge, or away from a cairn they want to keep visible.

They don't dream of making the plateau more fertile, or more full. That would be a misunderstanding of what it is. An ancient table that has been rained on until it became an instrument — you don't flood an instrument, you play it.

So what lives here is a human that has become interval-shaped. Spare, patient, low-voiced, wide-chested, moving between plenty and nothing without panic. A human that knows how to be as rare as the fauna and as loud as the stone only when invited to be.

If you sit still long enough on warm, bare rock that looks poured, with the sky huge and the wind humming in your sternum, and you have learned how to breathe before you need to — you might see one. Not coming toward you. Just crossing far off, from one pocket of green to another, staff tapping, not hurrying, disappearing into a cleft that from where you sit looks like it could not possibly hold a person.

And then the wind will shift and fill the space where they were with sound, and the emptiness will be complete again, and you will understand that they have not left. They are simply in the next interval, waiting with perfect competence for the next ten minutes of rain.
