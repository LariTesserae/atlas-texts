Nothing lives here the way something lives in a forest.

At first you think nothing lives here at all. You stand on that floor with the roar in your molars and the cold pooling around your ankles, and you think *empty*. The scale is wrong for life. Animals need shelter and quiet and edges to hide in, and this place has only one huge edge, too big to hide behind. People need purpose and warmth and a way to leave, and there is no reason to stay. 

Then you realize you're standing on it.

### The Occupant

The occupant is not *in* the trough. It *is* the trough.

It's a single, continuous organism, but it has agreed to look like many separate ones so you don't get overwhelmed.

From a distance it's a carpet. Up close it's a city. Up even closer it's a country of individuals all doing the same slow work.

**Moss, first.** Because moss is first everywhere. Not the soft lawn moss of a shady yard, but the northern, industrial kind. *Polytrichum* and *Sphagnum* and black-green liverwort that has learned to eat stone. It is a foot thick in the flats, thin as paint on the vertical walls. It grows over everything that stays still long enough - over the rusted ore carts until only the top lip shows like a mouth in the green, over the concrete footings until the rebar is just a set of black quills, over the warm water pipes where it grows absurdly lush and steams faintly. It doesn't need soil. The crushed basalt dust, the slag fines, the thin grit that collects in every blast-mark is enough if you are patient. And it is patient. A millimeter a year. A hundred years to make a hummock you could sink your arm into.

Under it, if you peel it back - which you shouldn't, because it will take a decade to heal - the floor is moving. Mites the size of poppy seeds, ivory springtails that flick themselves like dust when you breathe on them, black beetles with no eyes that live their whole lives in the wet dark between moss and stone. They don't make sound. The roar would drown any sound they could make, so they never evolved one.

**Then the trees that have agreed to be lonely.**

They look like a forest until you notice the spacing. Black spruce and larch, no taller than a two-story house, no wider than your shoulders. Each one stands in the exact center of its own circle, ten or fifteen meters from the next, as if planted by a careful, lonely gardener. They are not stunted. They are precise. Their crowns are perfect, narrow cones, rimed with frost that never melts on the north side even near a steam vent. Their roots are the opposite of precise - they shatter rock. You can see where a root-tip, no thicker than twine, has found a drill line in the basalt and pried it open a centimeter a year until the whole face has flaked off. Lichen covers them so completely the bark looks grey-white, like fur. Old man's beard hangs in frozen tassels and tinkles faintly when the big fans move the air.

They don't grow together because there isn't enough light to share. Each one needs its own slice of that dim, one-hour sun and the amber sodium wash. To crowd would be to starve. So they keep a respectful distance. The forest is everywhere and completely open at the same time - you can see for half a kilometer between trunks across a rolling plain of moss, and still not see the far wall for the steam.

In the warm halos - and this is where the living thing has learned to use the dead thing - there are ferns. Not scattered, but in perfect rings. Every steam vent, every hot pipe joint, every sun-struck south face of a pump house keeps a circle of ground thawed, maybe two meters across. In winter, when every puddle is ice and every railing is furred, those circles stay black, wet, and violently green. The lady ferns and wood ferns there never quite go dormant. They are the only soft things in the trough. If you put your hand there, the air actually feels alive.

### The Other Occupant

The second occupant never left.

It thuds. It howls. It hammers. It was built to empty this wound and it is still emptying it, long after anyone needed it empty. You could call it not-alive and be technically right - iron rusts, concrete spalls, bearings need grease - but that would be missing the ecology.

There are eight of them, maybe ten, spaced along the kilometers of floor. Brick and poured concrete blockhouses with no windows that matter anymore, each with a flywheel taller than a man turning at thirty revolutions per minute. So slow you can count. So heavy you feel it through your boots before you hear it. They pull groundwater out of the shattered floor and slug it up the hammering pipes to the plain. The six-meter ventilation fans on the rock walls do the same with air, beating the cold fog down so the moss can breathe. The cableway overhead still hums. The sodium lights still buzz.

They are simple, stupid, and nearly immortal. No computer to crash, no fine sensor to frost over. A piston, a shaft, a thermostat, a float switch. The maintenance crew comes down once a month, checks the oil, leaves. The rest of the time the machines and the plants have made a deal: you keep the water moving and we will keep your feet warm and your roof from caving in. Roots will not pry at a wall that vibrates. Moss insulates a pipe that would otherwise burst. It's not symbiosis by design. It's symbiosis by neglect. The trough has become a new kind of organism - half boreal bog, half pumping station.

You can tell where a pump has died. There are two of them, dark, their flywheels locked, their throats full of ice. Around them the moss is different - blacker, wetter, already swallowing the doors. In ten more years they will be hummocks.

### The Visitors

Animal life is not resident. Animal life is trespass.

A raven lives here in the way a person lives in an airport - it passes through because the trough is shorter than going around. It crosses the slit of sky high above, a black fleck against the overcast, and its call is instantly shredded by the organ note of the wind over the rim. It does not land.

A fox lives here in the way a rumor lives. You never see it. You see the straight, purposeful line of its prints at dawn in the snow-dusted moss, perfectly spaced between two pump houses, not wandering, not hunting, just commuting from one end of its impossibly long territory to the other. It knows the warm circles. It knows the mice that live nowhere else. It is probably deaf by now.

And very rarely, a human lives here for an afternoon. Not inhabits - visits. The crew in insulated coveralls who come down the switchback with oil cans, who speak to each other in shouts and hand signals under the roar, who check the gauges and look at the moss encroaching on the catwalk and scrape it off without malice. Or someone like you, standing on the floor, hand on a vibrating rail, realizing that your breath is the only quick thing in the valley, and that the place would like it very much if you breathed slowly, too.

Nothing here is in a hurry. Nothing here needs to be. Cold and damp and dim is a recipe for longevity, not abundance. A spruce will stand eighty years to add six inches of girth. A fern will hold the same thaw-circle all winter without growing a new frond. A flywheel will turn at thirty revolutions per minute until its bearings finally wear through sometime next century, and then it will stop, and moss will take it, and the water will rise a little higher around its base, and the trough - the big, slow, green, iron-lunged thing that lives here - will adjust by a millimeter, and continue.
