There is someone here. 

At first you think there isn't. That's the trick of a room that's too full — you look for a person the way you'd look for a chair in a forest, and you don't see one, because a chair belongs to an empty room. You look and you see only system. Water on its loop, vines on their loop, steam becoming cloud becoming drip. No animal moves. The tools sit abandoned in puddles.

Then you realize the tools are not abandoned.

You just haven't learned to see fast enough. Or slow enough.

They don't live here the way a farmer lives in a field. They live here the way blood lives in a lung.

### At first - the movement without a mover

You stand still until the steam takes you to the shoulders and you stop dripping for a second and the room stops pretending you aren't there.

Then you see that something is always being tended.

A basin that was about to overflow doesn't. A vine that was strangling another vine has been cut, clean, with a bright adze-mark still weeping sap. A clay jar that cracked five minutes ago has already been patched with fresh mortar, and the mortar is still warm, and the thumbprint in it is still wet.

You never saw anyone do it. A curtain of hanging roots shivered. A bamboo scaffold creaked as if weight had just left it. A pale moth blundered through steam where a shoulder should have been.

You look at the wrong tempo. In the corner where time is fast, a gourd swells and splits while you blink. In the corner where time is thick, a puddle holds a splash that hasn't landed yet. They move at the speed the room allows — which is neither fast nor slow, but *damp*. Their motion pools, too.

Then you catch one.

### The Unfinished

They are not tall. They were never meant to be tall. A tall person would not fit between the terraces.

They are low and broad and bare, the height of a child and the width of a mason. If you saw one from far below, through the cloud sea, you'd think it was a stone left on a ledge. Their skin is the color of undressed limestone when it's wet — grey-yellow, freckled with basalt dust that never washes off because the water never washes, only recirculates. The chisel left them sharp, too. Their knuckles, their elbows, their clavicles are all knife-edged, unweathered. No callus has had time to soften. No scar has had time to fade. Even the old ones look newly made.

Because they are newly made. Continuously.

Heat doesn't bother them. They sweat and do not dry. A sheen of condensation lives on them at all times, beading and running *up* their forearms along the same grooves the water takes up the cistern walls. Moss lives on them frankly, not as infection but as agreement. A green fur on the shoulders, on the back of the neck, in the hollow at the base of the throat where pooled light collects. If they stand still for a minute the aerial roots taste the air near their skin and lean toward them.

Their eyes are terrible and perfect for here. Large, lidless-looking, clouded like the ceiling-cloud three feet above your head. They don't squint in the dim because there is no dim to them — they drink the light that pools in basins. You see one cup its hands in a hollow of stone where light has collected like warm milk, lift it to its mouth, not to drink but to see. The light runs through its fingers and for a second its face is illuminated from below, and its eyes reflect it back, not bright, but *held*.

Their hands are the oldest thing about them. Too big. Too finished. The thumbs are flat and wide from pressing mortar, the fingertips permanently wrinkled from being wet, the nails soft as horn, stained black with soil that was carried up here jar by jar on someone's back — maybe their mother's, maybe their own. When one of them picks up a stone hammer it does not look like a tool being used. It looks like a hand remembering its shape.

They do not speak loudly. Sound pools here; a word said too sharp will stay hanging over your left shoulder for five minutes. So they tap. One tap on curing stone — *we need cord here*. Two taps on bamboo — *this basin is going to burst*. A soft pop of the tongue against teeth that sounds exactly like a clay pot cracking from a new shoot, so you never know if it was a person or a plant speaking. When they do use voice, it's low and wet, a hum that vibrates the steam.

### How they live packed

There is no house inside the house. No bed. There is no spare surface for a bed. They fit themselves into the intervals the garden allows.

One sleeps curled in the dry curve of an empty basalt basin, knees to chest, with a taro leaf pulled over it like a blanket, breathing steam. Another sleeps standing, leaning against the scaffolding, forehead against cool bamboo, while vines slowly braid around its ankles and then are gently undone when it wakes. Children — and there are children, soft-headed, softer-boned, their sutures still open the way the mortar seams are still open — sleep in woven pitch-lined baskets that were meant for tubers, now lined with moss. They are small enough to fit entirely inside a gourd.

They never stop tending, because tending is not a job, it's how they keep from being overgrown. If they pause, the vegetation takes the mortar. You see it happen: a vine burst its basket and rooted directly into the chest of a sleeping elder, fine white rootlets dimpling the skin, and the elder, half-awake, patiently picked them out one by one with a splinter of limestone and re-pressed them into soil. Not annoyed. This is grooming.

They eat what grows too fast to be contained. Not harvest so much as pruning. A tuber that swelled too quickly and cracked its own skin — they break it open with their thumbs and eat it steaming, starch and hot water. A fern-fiddlehead that uncoiled while you watched — nipped off and chewed raw, tasting of iron and cloud. They eat light-thick water, too, the water that has sat in a basin where light has pooled until the water itself glows; they sip it and their throats briefly illuminate.

They carry soil. Endlessly. Even now, when the pocket is packed and there is nowhere to put new soil, they carry it. You see a figure emerge from the wind-gap — impossibly, from outside, where the air should be unbreathable — bent double under a woven basket of black compost, sweat and steam identical on its back. It squeezes through the leaning megaliths, adds its soil to a terrace that does not need it, pats it down, and the terrace accepts it by swelling a new lip of moss. Where did it get soil at the top of a mountain made of raw rock? Down below, where the old world is weathered. They are the mountain's way of eating the lowlands and bringing them up to become peak.

And they build. Always building, though there is no room to build. They lash new bamboo over old bamboo, they cut new terraces into terraces that were cut yesterday, they place a new stone on a wall that was finished an hour ago, and the wall, being still soft, accepts the handprint. The mountain here rings like wet clay when struck. If you press your palm to a block and hold it, the stone will keep the print until the moss erases you. Their whole bodies are covered in such temporary fossils — palm, cheek, the sole of a foot where someone stepped and left a print in what will one day be hard basalt and completely forget it was ever soft.

They are not permanent residents. Nothing here is permanent except the act of residence. The old ones get too mossy. Their edges round, finally. Their skin, constantly wet, finally wrinkles past repair and they go still in a basin and let the roots take them properly. Within a day a taro leaf the size of a shield has grown through where their chest was. The children, fast-time children, bud impossibly quickly in the fast corner — you see a bud on a vine and a bud on a person at the same time — and break off and begin carrying.

### What they are

They are not the owners of this garden. They are its symbionts, its maintenance crew, its white blood cells. Or maybe they are the garden's idea of what a human should be if a human were made from the same conditions as the garden: high, hot, wet, dim, hand-made, unfinished.

If you ask — without language — what they are becoming, you get the answer from the mountain itself. The mountain is not finished being a mountain. Neither are they finished being whatever lives inside a mountain while it is still deciding to be hard. When the peak finally closes, when the chisel marks finally weather, when erosion finally begins — they will not be here. They will be wall. They will be terrace. They will be the memory of thumbprints under moss.

For now, they keep the cycles from colliding. Water wants to be everywhere at once. Light wants to be water. Time wants to be thick and thin. Vines want to be everything. The Unfinished move through ankle-deep light and sideways rain, tapping stone that is still soft, picking blind white beetles off mortar seams and putting them back gently where they will not be crushed, catching pooled sound before it gets too loud and releasing it near the wind-gap so the wind outside can take it.

You stand still long enough and one will mistake you for scaffolding. A cool, wet, too-big hand will touch your shoulder to test if you are load-bearing. The moss on its forearm will brush yours. Its clouded eyes will look not at you but at the light pooling in the hollow of your collarbone, and it will nod once, as if approving of how you hold it, and then it will move on, bare feet finding the only inch of stone not already occupied by something growing, and vanish again into steam, leaving behind only the faint, warm print of its hand on your skin, which will fade in a minute when the air drinks it.
