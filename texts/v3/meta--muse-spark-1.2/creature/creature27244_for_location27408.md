Who lives there takes a while to see.

At first you think no one does.

You walk the length of the canal and count three lit windows and one workbench with a kettle still steaming and you assume you've caught the town empty, between shifts, or at the hour when everyone is somewhere else. The spaces between things are so wide and so respected that a person can be standing in the next alley over, behind a wall of boiler plate, and you won't know it until you hear the soft *puh* of the diesel and, seven seconds later, his cough answering it, not loud.

Then you see one of them, and the town comes into a different focus.

She is at a loom in a hall that could hold twenty looms. She is not old the way people are old outside. She is established, the way the brick is. Late sixties maybe, or forties gone slow. Damp wool cardigan, rubber boots cracked at the ankle and then stopped cracking, hair pulled back and beaded with condensation the same way the pipes are. Her skin is pale in a way that isn't pallor — it's underexposure, like the underside of a leaf, like the blind fish. Her hands are chapped, oiled at the knuckles, moving with the same patient RPM as the line shaft above her head.

She doesn't look up when you pass the doorway. Not unfriendly. She heard you three streets away when your boot scuffed the basalt step. She is just not hurrying to register you. In this air, everything arrives slowly and is allowed to.

If you stand and watch, you see the accommodations.

None of them move quickly. Not as a discipline. Their bodies have learned the basin's tempo. Heart rates lower — you can see it if you watch them sleep in the terraces with the doors open, their chests rising once and then a long pause. Breath that makes a brief cloud and vanishes, and then nothing for a while. Eyes that have widened a fraction to take in amber light, pupils large even at noon, because no direct sun ever hits the floor and the bulbs are dim by design. They squint outside. Down here they see clearly.

They are all slightly damp, always. Not wet. Beaded. Like the stone. The cool saturated air condenses on them too. Forearms, foreheads, the rims of their mugs — a fine dew that they wipe away without thinking, or don't wipe away at all. Their clothes never fully dry and never rot. Wool, waxed canvas, rubber. The same finish as the rust: weathered to a point and then held. You will not see a new jacket down there. You will see a jacket that rusted, in its own way, to its equilibrium thirty years ago and has been perfect since.

There aren't many of them. Twenty, maybe forty if you count the ones who keep to the upper walkways and are only ever a silhouette behind steamed glass. Enough to keep the flywheel from seizing but not enough to fill the town, and that is intentional. They have learned the town's sparse economy. A whole row of houses with three lit windows is not a sign of decline. It's correct occupancy. One person to one hall. One voice per alley, so that sound can carry clean and not accumulate.

They don't talk much, but when they do it carries.

You hear a man two streets over say "—left the spanner by the weir—" and it arrives intact, unmuffled, because the town refuses to be loud. They've learned to speak at that volume, low and distinct, sentences with long pauses between them while the water ticks. Conversations take a long time. Someone will answer a question you asked an hour ago as you pass them again on your way back, as if no time had passed at all, because for them it hasn't.

Their work is maintenance, but not repair. Nothing is collapsing. What they do is more like tending. A woman oils the fly wheel not because it will stop — it won't — but because the ritual of oiling is part of the balance that keeps rust from becoming decay. A man sweeps condensation from a switchboard with a slow, flat brush, back and forth, back and forth, though the water will bead again in minutes. A boy — there are very few young ones, and they look strangely old, or the old look strangely young — sits for an afternoon watching the canal to note where the level is, even though everyone knows water remembers its level to the millimeter. He marks it in a ledger anyway, in pencil, the numbers barely changing year to year.

They tend the animals as extensions of themselves, and the animals have become like them.

The pigeons are not theirs, but they let them nest in the idle gear teeth and never run the crane that would disturb them. The orange carp they feed with crumbs from the same bread they eat. The forearm-long salamanders cling to the weeping walls right at head height and the residents duck around them without comment; some of the salamanders have been in the same spot for so long a faint clean outline has formed around them where the moss won't grow. The palm-sized moths that fur the amber bulbs in the evening — they collect the dead ones gently in the morning, soft as ash, and put them in the reeds.

And some of them tend the stranger colonies.

Under the works where warm discharge meets cold seep, the glass-and-copper barnacles click and open. You will eventually see someone crouched there on a low stool, not harvesting, not cleaning, just listening. An older resident with lichen-grey hair will have her hand in the water up to the wrist, utterly still, while the things filter the mist. She will tell you, if you wait long enough for her to speak, that the factory grew them. She is not joking, exactly, but she is not explaining either. To her they are livestock and neighbors and infrastructure all at once. They keep the water thinking about each corner before it turns, and she keeps them.

This is the adjustment the basin does to people if they stay more than a few days. The switchback stairs slow your legs, then the cool slows your blood, then the ticking slows your need to fill silence. People who come down intending to fix something or buy something or study something tend to leave quickly, annoyed by the polite diesel going *puh* every seven seconds and the way no one hurries to answer. People who stay find that they are no longer visitors. The town does not hire them. It incorporates them.

You see it in how they leave things.

A barrow of parts mid-alley where someone left it — not stolen, not abandoned, just waiting in its correct place until the thought that left it there resumes. A kettle still warm on a workbench in an empty hall — someone will be back for it in about an hour, or tomorrow. Laundry steaming on a line, beaded, dripping at the same rate as the rock. They don't own in the usual way. They keep a pattern of objects and spaces and sounds that, together, hold the water level steady.

If you stay until the deeper dimming that is called night, you understand what actually lives there isn't a collection of individuals at all.

What lives there is a single, distributed metabolism that has learned to wear different skins: human skin, pigeon skin, salamander skin, brick skin, copper-wire-and-glass skin. All of them beaded. All of them moving at flywheel speed. All of them rusted to the point of varnish and then held.

The town itself is the organism. The humans are its hands that can oil and sweep and listen. The fish are its slow blood cells circling the canal. The moths are its way of dimming the lights a little more. The water is its breath, condensing and ticking, rising and receding with a period that has nothing to do with rain, remembering its level no matter how much you pump.

And the walls — the sixty-meter weeping walls — are what cup it. The people look up at them the way you might feel the edge of your own palm without looking, to be sure you are still holding.

They are not trapped. You see it when one of them finally does climb the switchback stair,-polished rail dark with hand-oil, to trade on the plain. He goes slowly, breathing the thinning air, and everyone knows he will be back down by evening because directly above, from the outside, the basin looks like nothing, a small missing piece of ground, and it is very cold up there and very loud and very dry.

Down here, everything has found where to stop.
