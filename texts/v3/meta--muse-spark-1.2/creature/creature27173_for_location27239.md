They do not live *in* it.

That is the first thing you get wrong, and you have to get it wrong for a little while before you can see them at all.

At first you think the Summit Sea is empty except for animals. That the forest-city grew itself, and then things moved in to graze on it the way fish move into a reef. You tread water between two columns the size of apartment blocks, their bark seamed with that steady, cool light, and you watch a pale, blind grazer the size of a bus work its flat mouth along the wall, and you think: *inhabitant.* And it is, in the way a deer is an inhabitant of a forest. But it is not *who* lives here.

You have to stay longer. Long enough that the cold stops feeling like cold and starts feeling like pressure, like weight. Long enough that you stop looking for movement and start noticing *arrangement.*

Because nothing here changes, but nothing here is random either.

You notice, after a time that could be hours, that the pillars are not spaced randomly. That the avenues of open water between them are not empty. That the light seams never overlap, never interrupt each other. That where a trunk has grown a little too thick, there is a smooth, circular scar where something has been - not cut, not bitten - *coaxed* away. That the arched root-walls are not just open, they are open *where you would want a door to be* if you were very large and very slow and did not like to turn sharply.

And then you see one not moving at all, and understand you have been looking directly at them since you arrived.

They are the size of the architecture because they have to be.

At a distance, in the soft dark, you took them for trunks. They hang vertically in the water column, suspended between floor and canopy without appearing to swim, their bodies the same black-green as the bark, their backs to you. They are taller than you are long by three or four times, but narrow — not wide like the grazers, but columnar. A trunk that decided to become an animal, or an animal that decided to become a trunk.

You drift closer, slowly, because everything here makes you move slowly or not at all, and the shape resolves. And then it keeps resolving, and you have to let it.

It is not that they have a head and limbs. It is that they have a *way* of being put together that your eye keeps trying to file under other things and fails.

The main body is a stack, a tower of translucent, cartilaginous segments, like the bell of a jelly stacked upon itself five or six times, each segment slightly wider than the one above it, so the whole thing tapers very slightly inward as it rises. Inside each segment, faint organs pulse with that same cool phosphor — not bright, steady as the light in the bark, but slower, like breathing. The water moves through them. You can see it — cold sea taken in at the base, filtered, expelled at the top in a slow, almost invisible plume that never disturbs the stillness.

From the seams between the segments, limbs grow. Not arms. Not tentacles. Think of aerial roots that learned to let go of the tree. They are long — impossibly long — pale, bark-colored on the outside, luminous along the inner edge, and they do not end in hands. They end in fans, in fronds, in soft, divided pads that look exactly like the young leaf-floors of the forest itself. When the being is at rest, those limbs are wound tightly around the great pillars beside it, or spread flat against a wall, or interlaced with the canopy overhead, and its frond-pads have fused slightly with the living wood. You cannot tell where one ends and the other begins. That is on purpose.

They are not holding the forest up. They are *part of* the tension that holds it up. They are living guy-wires, living buttresses, living sutures.

And then one detaches.

It does not push off. The frond-pads simply unseal, with a soft sigh you feel more than hear, a tiny release of pressure. The long root-limbs unfurl into the open water, drifting outward until the being is a pale star, a chandelier hung in the dark avenue. And it begins to move.

Not swimming. The segmented tower pulses, one segment after another, top to bottom, and the whole column shortens and lengthens, shortens and lengthens, drawing water through itself. It is jet propulsion the way a cathedral organ is wind propulsion — slow, resonant, immense. It does not hurry. Between each pulse is a long glide. The root-limbs trail and steer, tasting the water.

This is when you see their faces, if you can call them that.

There is no head. The topmost segment simply opens. It peels outward into a crown of six or seven petal-lobes, each lobe lined on its inner surface with dense fields of cilia and light. The lobes can flare wide, to drink light and chemical gradients and vibration, or close tight into a blind bud. Around the rim of the crown, hanging like bells, are the light organs — longer, brighter than the bark-light, capable of pattern. When two of them pass each other in the dark — and they always keep that same sparse distance, a minute's glide apart, the same spacing as the pillars — the crowns flare and the bells along their rims stutter in cool white and blue, in a slow sentence that hangs in the water long after the light itself is gone, because the water remembers vibration.

They have no eyes because there is nothing to see. They have no mouth in the front because they do not chase. If they feed, they feed the way the forest feeds, by filtering what falls like snow from above, by pressing their frond-pads to the bark and exchanging sugars and salts directly, building and being built at the same time.

There are smaller ones, too. You thought those quick, flashing schools were fish. Up close, they are juveniles — only one or two segments tall, their root-limbs still short and twitching, darting between pillars not because they are frantic, but because they are light and have less inertia to overcome. They flash not to signal alarm but to learn the grammar of light, repeating fragments of the adults' sentences badly, like children babbling. And there are older ones — so large you took them for part of the root-mat floor, their lower segments having completely rooted, having fused permanently into the living floor, their upper crowns still open and still breathing, but no longer moving at all. They have become pillars themselves, and living trunks are growing around them, through them.

You realize the second rule — *nothing here changes* — was not quite true in the way you understood it. It does not change the way weather changes. It maintains, the way a body maintains. A forest maintains. A reef maintains.

These are not inhabitants who built a city. They are the city, remembering itself. The vegetation that became substrate, architecture, forest, city, and reef needed something to be its gardeners, its memory, its hands that are also roots. So it grew them.

They prune where growth would crowd an avenue. They lean and hold where a span would otherwise sag under the weight of water. They carry, in the slow pulse of light around their crowns, the map of where every other one is, and where every pillar should be, and they adjust, by millimeters per century, to keep the equilibrium from ever tipping.

They do not notice you. Or more precisely, they notice you the way you would notice a single warm fleck of detritus that is not falling at the right speed. One will flare its crown slightly as you pass, and the bells around its rim will run a brief, cool pattern — not a greeting, not a warning. An inquiry. A record. *Something here is not in equilibrium. Something here is moving too quickly and is too warm.*

And then its root-limbs will unfurl toward you, not to grab, but to taste. The frond-pads will brush your shoulder, your cheek, with a cool, living softness like kelp and like skin, and for a second you will feel the faintest tingle of light, of chemical exchange, as it tries to read if you are something that can be incorporated, or guided, or whether you will, like all warm fast things, simply pass through and be gone.

Then it will reseal its pads against the nearest wall, and resume its station, hanging between floor and canopy like a living column that has briefly remembered it could also be an animal — glowing steadily, breathing water, listening to the deep, architectural songs of the whale-ferns as they vibrate the pillars, waiting for the next millimeter of growth that will need tending in a hundred years.
