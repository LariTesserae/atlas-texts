Nothing lives here alone.

That is the first thing you understand, and the last. At first in the warm dark you think the city is empty — that it was made and then abandoned, that these towers and domes and fused arches are ruins. Because nothing moves quickly. Nothing announces itself.

Then your eyes learn the violet-black, and the amber bead on its stalk opens again a few feet from your face, and you see it is not a light at all. It is a lure. And something lives behind it.

### The Builders

They do not live *in* the city. They *are* the city.

Lowest and most numerous are the ones you would miss if you were looking for an animal. The coral polyps - not the branching, sun-hungry corals of shallow water, but the deep, slow, blind kind. Each one no larger than the pad of your finger, a soft, anemone-like mouth ringed with six or eight tentacles, set in a cup of its own limestone sweat. Individually, nothing. Together, everything.

They do not decide to build. They secrete. A thin, gritty milk of calcium that hardens in the warm broth. One lip after another, one ring after another, for centuries. A tower is a thousand generations of identical mouths, each one building precisely where its parent died, slightly askew, slightly outward, until a spire thirty feet high has risen where there was once flat silt. You can see them if you hold still - the living skin of the city, a faint, speckled pelt of translucent polyps over every wall and roof, each with its tiny crown of tentacles combing the water for falling snow. When you brush one, the whole patch flinches, a wave of contraction moving outward like wind over wheat.

Among them, the tube-worms. Thick as your wrist, thick as your thigh, they do not make stone but make tube — parchment and mucus and cemented sand, long vertical chimneys that cluster in organs. Their bodies are hidden, you never see more than the crown: a plume of red filaments, feathery gills that unfurl to breathe and feed, then snap shut at the faintest pressure change. Some of their tubes have grown together for so long they have become the city’s plumbing, hollow causeways and upright colonnades, still lined on the inside with living worm-flesh.

The sponges are the houses. They do not build, they inflate. House-sized, barrel-shaped, or low and encrusting like spilled dough. Their outer skin is tough, pitted leather, pierced everywhere with inhalant pores. Their great oscula — the doors, the windows — gape on their summits, a slow, patient pulse drawing water in through a million mouths and exhaling it in a great, silent *whuff* that you feel more than hear. Put your hand near one and the current tugs at you. Inside, if the wall is young enough to be translucent, you can see the slow peristalsis, the canals and flagellated chambers beating, sorting bacteria from broth, and the young budding off inside the mother, pale commas swimming in her hollow heart before they find a place to settle.

And the barnacles make the tenements. White, sharp, artless little volcanoes, each with its own hinged doors. They settle on anything — on coral, on sponge, on each other — and then never move again. They stack, they crowd, they cement their calcite walls to their neighbor's wall until a cliff of them becomes a single mass, hundreds of tiny jaws opening and closing, kicking feathered legs into the current. An entire district of the city is just this — a white, stippled accretion fifty feet long, humming faintly with the clicking of a thousand opercula.

None of them has a face. None of them knows it is building a city. And yet the city has avenues.

### The Keepers

Because the walls would die without the tenants.

They are everywhere and they are small, so small you first mistake them for part of the water itself.

The shrimp clouds — each animal the size of a grain of rice, transparent, with black pepper eyes and endlessly flicking swimmerets. They move not as a school but as smoke, a single fluid that pours along the boulevards, splits around a ray, reforms. Their work is cleaning. They pick at every polyp, every worm plume, every barnacle mouth, removing parasites, dead mucus, stray algae. You see one hang upside down from a coral lip, picking, picking, while the polyp tolerates it completely.

The crabs, flattened and pale as the silt, click along on tiptoe, their legs so long they seem to measure the street as they go. They are caretakers and scavengers both — scraping the felted algae off domes, carrying fragments of shell to plug a hole, snipping away a dead sponge before it can foul the water.

On every ledge, the anemones: bloated columns topped with a ring of thick, blind tentacles, white and pink and rust-red. They do not build, they anchor. They claim a spot and then spend their lives waving, stinging. The clown-white fish — eyeless, or nearly — live between their tentacles without being stung, darting out to nip and returning. The feather-duster worms: sudden, shy plumes of red and orange that bloom from every pore in the city wall and vanish the instant you exhale too hard, retracting with a soft *pop* into their tubes.

None of these are citizens. They are maintenance. They keep the living stone from being smothered by its own living.

### The Transients

Above the rooftops, where the water opens up again, the larger things move.

They are unhurried to the point of solemnity. Gravity is loose here, and they use it.

The rays come first — long, flat, the color of old linen, wider than a doorway, with no tail spine and no hurry. They glide a foot above the spires, mouths open, filtering. Clouds of shrimp and particulate snow vanish into them and do not come out. Under their wings, small remoras and isopods cling like ornaments.

The glass bells — not jellyfish, but close. A hard, transparent bell, faintly prismatic, pulsing once every ten seconds, carrying on its back a garden of hitchhikers: algae, small barnacles, even tiny anemones that have made the bell their island. They bloom with their own light, a slow pulse of blue along the bell margin, answering another pulse far off across the valley. They do not hunt. They drift, and the city drifts beneath them.

The shoals of white fish — blind, lateral lines exaggerated into luminous seams along their flanks — turn as one, without a leader. Twenty, fifty, a hundred, reflecting the scattered bioluminescence. When the eel passes they part without panic.

### The Eel and the Other Mouths

Predation here is not a chase. It is an arrival.

The eel — if that is the right word — is not an eel at all but a long, muscular ribbon, dark above, pallid below, with a head like a shovel and a mouth that opens too wide. It does not thrash. It slides through an avenue that seemed too narrow for it, its body conforming to the turns, its skin barely brushing the living walls. The shrimp smoke does not flee; it simply ceases to be where the eel is. The fish hold still. No one makes a sound that water will carry. The eel noses into a sponge osculum, or into a crevice in a barnacle tenement, and a soft, terrible suction happens out of sight. Then it slides on, and after a long minute the avenue fills again.

The cone shells do the same thing slower. A great, cone-shaped shell the size of a cask, its foot a pale, spreading sheet that moves with impossible slowness over a dome, dissolving a path of living polyps as it goes, leaving a clean, white scar that will be recolonized by tomorrow.

And below all of them, the recyclers you almost never see: the blind brittle stars with arms like knotted rope, unfolding from cracks to gather the perpetual snow of detritus — scales, feces, dead larvae, marine snow from the forgotten sun — and fold it into their central mouths. The worms in the mud beneath the city, turning the ash-silt over and over.

---

There is no mayor in this valley. No queen. No central mind that planned the arches or decreed where the avenues would run. The intelligence is in the current. Chemical whispers on the warm broth — spawn signals, alarm mucus, the taste of calcium — tell a polyp to build here and not there. Hydraulic pressure tells a sponge to open or close. The shape of the flow, trained by centuries of growth, trains the growth in return.

If you stay, as you are staying, suspended in the warm, breathable dark, you realize the most honest way to say it: the city is not a place where things live. It is a thing that lives, by being many things that live. It grows, it heals, it re-routes itself when the slow tide shifts. It breathes — that low pulse you felt at the edge of hearing is not fish tails, it is water being drawn through ten thousand sponges at once, and expelled. It digests the light-world’s endless falling snow. It reproduces, not by splitting, but by budding — a tower sends out a fragile, finger-like extension toward another tower until they touch and fuse into a bridge that will thicken for a hundred years.

You, warm and heavy and sinking very slowly in water thick as broth, are not separate from it for long. The shrimp investigate your skin. A polyp near your hand extends its tentacles toward the heat of you. The city, which has never seen a sky, does not know you are a visitor. To it, you are just another warm current, another brief, large animal moving through its avenues, to be cleaned and tolerated and, eventually, if you stayed forever, to be grown over.
