Who lives in the Bowl is not a single thing. That's the first mistake you make when you climb down into that wet heat and your ears fill with the drone.

You look for *an* animal. A resident. Something that owns the place.

But nothing owns the Bowl. The Bowl owns everything in it, and what lives there lives *by permission* - spaced out, like the plants and the machines, each given exactly enough room to be perfect at what it does.

You have to learn to see them in layers, because they don't all live in the same second.

### The Air That Is Alive

The loudest things are the smallest.

You called them dragonfly-things from the rim. Down here you see they're wrong. A dragonfly is a hunter, all needled jaws and hunger. These are cattle.

Each one is as long as your palm, as heavy as a sparrow. The body is not chitin but something like varnished wood - segmented amber, translucent where the light hits it, with a dark stripe down the back that pulses when they breathe. Their wings aren't wings. They are *feathers* made of glass. A hundred overlapping vanes on each side, clear as mica, that don't beat so much as shiver and rattle. That rattle is the drone you heard from the plain.

There are thousands of them, but they never swarm. That's the eerie part. Real insects crowd. These keep distance. Every one has a circuit. You can watch one for a full minute and see it trace the same oval: up the sweating east wall where the algae slick is thickest, grazing with a soft, file-like mouth that scrapes the stone clean, across the open air over the tree, down to drink where the droplets hang suspended by the white arch, then back up again.

When they drink at the arch, they misbehave. They stutter. One moment a glass-feather is here, the next it is six inches to the left without having crossed the space in between, wings still in the same position. They don't seem to notice. They use the fold the way a trout uses a current - a little free momentum. Sometimes two collide mid-skip and spiral down, locked together, furious and clattering, until they separate and resume their ovals as if nothing happened.

They are the base of everything. The air is their pasture.

### The Gliders

Above them, in the warmer, wetter column of air that the Bowl exhales straight up, live the things that eat them without ever hunting.

You only see a glider when it moves over you, because at rest they are almost nothing. When they cling to one of the tree's dinner-plate leaves, they fold themselves into a pale crease, translucent, veined like a jellyfish, the size of a folded sheet. But when the heat thermals correctly - and in the Bowl, it always does - they let go.

They don't flap. They can't. They open.

A manta of living cellophane, four or five feet across, thinner than paper, with a fringe of hair-fine cilia that trembles. The sun shines right through them and throws a rippling shadow on the moss below. They ride. In the Bowl's light gravity, the warm wet air is thick enough to hold them. They circle for hours, mouths - which are just a widening in the leading edge - open, and the glass-feathered cattle fly right into them. You hear a faint *tick, tick* as they strike the membrane and stick, then slowly dissolve.

They never fight. They never hurry. Two gliders can occupy the same thermal and pass through each other's shadows without acknowledging one another. If the wind from the plain ever got in here, it would shred them instantly. They can only exist in this one pocket where the air is heavy and still and lifts for them. They are prisoners of perfect conditions, and perfectly content to be so.

When one finally gets too heavy with water and food, it sinks slowly, like a falling leaf, back to the tree, folds itself up, and digests for a day.

### The Gardeners

The ground is slower, so you forget to watch it, until something moves under your hand.

The pill-bugs are not bugs. They are more like tortoises that decided to become isopods. A mature one is the size of a bread loaf, or a baking stone, low and domed, slate-gray underneath and living green on top. Its segmented back is not shell but soil - a shallow, pitted carapace that holds moisture, and in that moisture grows the exact same deep viridian moss that carpets the Bowl floor. Tiny white roots thread into its back. Tiny white flowers bloom there, no bigger than pinheads.

They are the reason the Bowl looks tended.

Each gardener has a territory: one between the ferns and the lattice, one along the damp runnel by the white arch, one slowly circumscribing the tree. They move at the speed of a clock's hour hand, their fourteen soft legs leaving damp prints in the black earth, their blind heads down. They eat earth. They process it. Behind them they leave a trail of fine, aerated, slightly steaming castings, rich as coffee grounds. Ahead of them, the moss and the ferns lean, infinitesimally, toward the path they will take tomorrow.

If you sit still long enough, one will approach your boot, test it with filament antennae as thick as twine, decide you are not earth, and go around you with immense, unhurried politeness. They have been doing this since before your grandparents' grandparents were concepts. Nothing hurries them, because nothing needs to. In a place where water appears from the air itself, patience is not a virtue. It's a physics.

### The Singers

And then there are the ones you never see at all, until you think the Bowl is empty of anything that thinks.

You hear them first as a percussion under the rattle. A single, huge note.

*GONG - ah.*

A pause.

*GONG - ah.*

It vibrates your molars. It vibrates the droplets hanging by the white arch. The glass-feathers pause mid-air for a fraction of a second when it sounds, as if listening.

It comes from under the man-high ferns, where the black earth is deepest and wettest and the roots make a cavern. There, half-buried, lungs pulsing, is the singer. You will only find it if you kneel and part the fronds and stare until your eyes adjust.

It has no business being in the same Bowl as the others. Where they are spare and spaced, it is concealed. Where they are silent or rattling, it is loud. It looks like a throat. A sack of wet, dark-red muscle the size of a dog, with no head, no legs, just a wide, fleshy funnel that opens and closes. Around its base, the moss grows in a perfect circle, as if it has been singing there so long the plants have learned not to encroach. Its skin is slick and translucent and you can see a slow, greenish light moving inside it, in time with the notes.

It sings, and another answers from the opposite wall - hidden under the boulder by the lattice. Then a third, fainter, from near the dark glass circle, where the shadows fall wrong.

Three singers. Maybe four. Never more. They are spaced like the trees, like the machines. Each one gets its acoustic territory. They call and answer all day, not to mate, not to warn. The sound seems to *do* something. When the low note holds, the thrumming of the black lattice steadies. When it cuts off, the lattice wavers and the condensation on the walls sheets a little faster. They are not communicating with each other.

They are communicating with the Bowl.

### The Caretaker

Because the last inhabitant isn't an animal at all, and you only notice it after you stop looking for animals.

No doors. No inscriptions. But someone left the machines on, and the machines are still tending. The white arch that folds space, the lattice that pulls water out of dry sky, the dark glass that shows another sun - they are not ruins. They are organs. And organs need a body.

The body is the Bowl itself.

If you put your palm flat on the sweating stone wall for a long time, you feel it: a faint, very slow pulse, not of heat but of *attention*. The temperature in the shade corrects itself when a cloud passes. A trickle that was about to erode a fern's hummock diverts sideways into a runnel. A fallen dinner-plate leaf half-eaten on the moss is moved, overnight, a few inches to where a gardener will find it in the morning.

There is no little man in the wall. There is no ghost. The intelligence that lives here is distributed, slow, and uninterested in you the way a greenhouse is uninterested in a fly that got in through the vent. It keeps the gravity a little lighter so the gliders can fly. It keeps the shadows bending near the glass so the light is even. It keeps the dew forming, the song in tempo, the territories respected.

You might, if you stare too long into the dark glass, see what it sees: the Bowl from very high up, the way a gardener sees a seed tray, and for a second you realize you are not a visitor here. You are a weed seed that blew in over the lip. The Bowl is deciding whether you belong - whether you will be watered or weeded.

It usually decides to let you leave. That's why the wind hits you again so hard when you climb back out. That's why the drone cuts off as if a door has shut behind you.

Nothing that lives in the Bowl can live on the plain. The gliders would tear, the singers would dry, the gardeners would starve on that thin dust. And nothing that lives on the plain, that wind-scoured, ancient, finished plain, can stay in the Bowl for long without feeling like the air is too rich and the sound too close and the light too intentional.

The inhabitants know this. They keep their distances. They let the empty spaces stay empty, so each perfect thing has room to be itself, under warm, wet, artificial, lovingly maintained physics, humming to itself forever in its hidden pocket in the tired old stone.
