It is not empty. That is the second mistake you make.

At first you think it must be - because nothing *should* live here. Light without heat is a contradiction your body understands before your mind does. Brightness is supposed to mean warmth. Here the brightness is surgical. It comes down through five hundred meters of clear ice in hard, unmoving columns, too white to be sunlight and too steady to be day. It throws shadows with sharp edges and no warmth at the edge. The air itself pools cold - your own breath drops out of your mouth and falls to the moss in a splash of crystals. You think: this is a sterilized place. A beautiful, mineral desert. A place scrubbed by cold and light until nothing can hold.

Then you notice the moss is breathing.

It was the floor the whole time but you thought it was frost. It isn't. It's a felt, a carpet, a skin - a pale boreal green so washed with white it almost disappears against the basalt. It covers everything that isn't vertical. Rock, fallen bark, the stones of abandoned hearths, the bones of grazers. It is deep enough to swallow your boot to the ankle and springy, wet when you squeeze it. It drinks sound. It drinks water. It drinks light. It is not one moss but a nation of mosses and lichens and liverworts woven into a single continuous pelt, and it is the only thing here that seems content to be small. It does not reach for the ice. It stays low, where the cold pools heaviest, and makes its living from what falls: pollen snow, leaf-glass, melt that trickles down from the pillars, and the faint geothermal sigh coming up through the young basalt. It is antifreeze and patience. If you lie on it, it steams slightly around your body, not from your heat but from its own quiet, chemical work. It is everywhere and it is the first true resident - the one that taught the rock how to hold water.

And then you realize the pillars were never pillars.

You took them for geology because of their scale. A trunk a hundred meters thick and two kilometers tall does not register as a tree. You look up the flank of one and your brain files it under cliff. The bark is grooved in vertical channels deep enough to run rivers, the buttress roots are ridges you could build a village on. But it is alive, and once you see it you cannot unsee it.

Call them Crown-Pillars, because we have no better word.

They do not have wood as you know it. They have a kind of living basalt - dense, fibrous, saturated with a sap that will not freeze until forty below, sweet and sharp with salts and sugars that act like antifreeze. Strike the trunk and it rings, low and bell-like, because the whole column is under tension. They grow not by adding rings but by inflating. You can watch it if you are still long enough. A seam in the bark will sigh open and a new rib of pale flesh will push out, wet and steaming in the cold, and harden in an hour. Tendrils thick as a man, pale green-white and blind, uncoil from the upper flanks at the speed of a stretching arm, tasting the air for more light, for the wet streak of a water channel. When they find nothing they lignify and hang like dead cables. When they find the light-pool off the ice, they flatten and burst.

Their crowns are the reason the air is so bright. Two kilometers up, each pillar spreads against the underside of the ice itself, a vast plate of photosynthetic tissue pressed flat like a leaf held to a windowpane. From the floor they look like pale continents, mottled green-black, veined with the silver tracery of the water-rivers they catch. They drink the hard light directly. They don't need to make a canopy to compete for it - there is nowhere else to be. Each plate is the size of a town. When one gets too heavy, or too frozen at the edge, the pillar aborts it. It detaches along a clean line and comes down. It does not fall quickly. It sails, it folds, it autorotates for an hour, a continent of translucent leaf-glass the thickness of paper but wide enough to cast a moving eclipse. When it lands it covers hectares of moss like a fallen sky. Within days it is white - not with snow but with fungus. The decomposers come.

The decomposers are the second nation. They are not subtle. Where a crown-plate lands, where a limb shatters from its own weight and explodes on the basalt, where a sheet of bark sloughs off and sails down ringing against the rock, the white mold comes up out of the moss in a foam. It is not mushroom, not quite. It is a rapid, hungry felt that can dissolve a house-sized leaf in a week, turning it to black slurry that the moss drinks. It smells like cold mushrooms and iron. The whole rift smells like that after a fall - wet stone, green sap, ozone, and the sweet rot of that white hunger. Growth and collapse *are* the weather here. The forest makes itself and unmakes itself constantly, violently, without wind. Seeds the size of sails drift down always, slow as parachutes. Pollen falls like snow you can see in the light columns. Flakes of leaf-glass chime when they hit stone. It is a loud place made of quiet sounds, and it never stops moving.

So the plants are the place. They are the architecture and the weather and the soil and the river system. To live here as an animal is to live *on* a plant the way a barnacle lives on a whale.

And there are animals. Fewer kinds than you expect, because this world is only a few thousand years old - nothing has had time to specialize very far. The ones that are here are all practicing the same lesson: conserve.

First, the drifters. You will think they are scraps of the crown-plates coming down, until one changes direction. They are moths in no earthly sense except in their stillness. Blanket-large, translucent as thin ice, with veins that carry that same antifreeze sap, they never flap. They cannot afford to. They ride. Warm air, by which I mean air that is one degree less murderously cold, rises slowly off the faint geothermal warmth of the basalt floor. Cold air sinks from the ice. Between the pillars this makes a slow, permanent convection, an invisible elevator. The moths hang in it with wings spread thirty feet, rippling only to steer. Their undersides are covered in fine down that combs pollen snow out of the air. They are almost silent. When the light catches them from above, you can see the green of their last meal glowing through their wings.

Where the moths drift, the grazers climb. Slowly. So slowly you have to mark their position against a lichen scar to be sure they are moving at all. They are pale - white, bone, lichen-grey - six-limbed, low and broad, with heads that are mostly mouth. They do not have eyes worth naming; the light is everywhere and there is nowhere to hide. They have chemical pits and vibration pads in their feet. They climb the pillars at a pace that loses heat as slowly as possible, scraping the living bark and the lichen that crusts it, leaving bright, wet scars the size of doors that weep sap. The sap flash-freezes at the edge of the scar into a sweet, glassy rind that the grazer will come back to lick later. They do not call. Nothing here calls - sound costs moisture and heat and is swallowed by moss anyway. They communicate, if they do, by leaving those scars, by scent, by the slow tremor of their feet on the ringing wood.

There are things that eat the grazers, but you almost never see them. They are not faster, just more patient. A kind of flat, white hunter that lives in the deep grooves of the bark where the water braids down, folded like a cloak, waiting for a grazer's foot to pass. When it moves it is sudden and then still again for days.

And then - much later than you expect, because you have been looking for a city - there are people.

You find them first by their absence. By the things they left that the forest hasn't taken back yet.

A lean-to lashed between two buttress roots, made of a single sheet of fallen crown-plate propped on basalt chips. The vine they lashed it with is still alive and has continued to grow up and over the shelter, pulling the roof slowly, inexorably apart. A cairn, but not for burial - just sharp, fresh basalt shards stacked to mark: *we were here, water this way*. A rope bridge now slack and furred with moss, its ropes braided from the down of those sail-seeds, hand-twisted, impossibly strong and soft. On a root-face someone has scraped the lichen away with a stone - a child's drawing, or a map: stick figures under a pillar, the blurred coin of the sun behind ice, a wavy line for a bark-river.

They are not a civilization. The forest does not permit civilization. You cannot clear a field when the clearing is filled in weeks by shoots you can watch grow. You cannot build a town under a pillar that sheds continents. You cannot store food that the white mold will take first. So they live like the moths live, like birds in a redwood forest scaled up until the trees are mountains - small bands, two or three families, moving.

They are pale and short and heavily clothed in felted down-seed and cured grazer hide, their faces wind-cut even though there is no wind. They move along the buttress ridges and the bark-rivers, following water and falls. They drink from the silver sheets running down the pillars, collecting it in bladders made of that same translucent leaf-glass, which stays flexible even frozen. They eat what the forest will spare without fighting them for it: the sweet rind at the edge of grazer scars, the soft heart of new shoots before they harden, the jelly that forms under the white mold where it has dissolved a crown-plate - sour, protein-rich, quickly harvested before it turns. Sometimes they take a grazer, but it is dangerous work on a vertical cliff of living wood.

Fire is their only miracle and they keep it tiny. Resin that weeps from a wounded pillar will burn even half-frozen, with a small, hard yellow flame that gives almost no heat but is visible for kilometers between the trunks. They keep it in stone-lined pits, moss-damped, and carry it as a coal in a basalt box. At night, when the ice above holds its stored twilight and the air between the pillars goes a deep, clear indigo, you might see one. A point of warm yellow, impossibly small in that cathedral void, impossibly steady. You will not reach it. The moss swallows your shout. The distance between pillars makes liars of your eyes. By morning it will be gone, and where it was there will be only a blackened ring of stones already filling with pale moss, and a few footprints that the rime is erasing.

That is who lives here, then. A carpet that eats sound. Pillars that are rivers that are forests that are mountains. Fungus that comes like weather. Moths that never land. Grazers that write in scars. And people who have learned the only way to live in a place that is still learning how to be is to be light, to be temporary, to leave cairns instead of houses and drawings instead of roads, and to keep a small fire burning while the huge, cold light pours down through the ice without ever warming anything at all.
