What lives there was not meant to be seen. At first you think nothing does.

That is the first impression the place offers you, and it is honest. Nothing *grows* here. Nothing *visits*. Nothing you would call an animal could stand that chord for an hour without bleeding from the ears, without going mad from the heat that has no source and the thirst that begins a minute after you arrive. And so you look at the pressed-together, shoulder-tight hive of rooms and think: empty. An abandonment. A town built until it had filled its table and then died of its own completeness.

Then you realize the town is not empty. It is *full*. And what fills it has learned to be indistinguishable from the fullness itself.

They are still there. They never left, because there was nowhere to leave to. When the last wall met the last cliff, they didn't stop living — they stopped *needing* to change.

You will not see them at first because they are the same temperature as the wall you lean against, and the same color, and almost the same stillness.

They are small. That is the first concession the body made to a place that has no streets and no empty floors. Not small the way a child is small, but small the way something that has lived for many generations in cracks is small — compacted. If one stood — and they rarely stand upright, there is no room to — it would come to your sternum, perhaps your solar plexus. But you will never see one standing. You see a shoulder where a wall has a shoulder, a curve of back in a sleeping-mat courtyard that you thought was a low bench, a knee tucked into a chimney pot.

Their skin is what happens when skin stops trying to be skin and tries to be brick. It is dry beyond any human dryness, not cracked but *cured*. A warm, matte red-brown, dusted over with the same fine alkaline powder that furs the cliff edges, salt-pale at the joints. No sweat, because there is no water to waste on sweat. No hair, because hair would catch the grit that has been sanding every corner for ten thousand years. Their heads are large and smooth and rounded, like river stones, like the corners of the mesa itself that the wind gave up on. The eyes — you expect eyes — are there, but they are not for this light. In the perpetual amber dim, under the haze that never clears, eyes are a liability that dries out. Theirs have filmed over, lidded shut with a translucent scale the color of unglazed terracotta. Blind, or nearly so. They do not need to see walls that are always a foot away.

What they have instead is hearing that has gone beyond hearing.

You covered your ears and still heard the roar in your teeth. They did not cover their ears. Generations ago they could not. The wind that shreds itself through a thousand stone throats is not noise to them. It is the medium. It is the air and the water and the light all at once.

Their outer ears are gone — smoothed away into the side of the skull, leaving only a whorled, hardened pit, like the mouth of one of those empty storage jars set into the wall. The wind plays them and they feel it not as sound but as pressure, as topography. A change in the chord means a doorway two houses over has shifted a millimeter. A harmonic trembling in the low drone means another body is moving through a crack three roofs away, belly to brick. They do not shout. Shouting is for people who live where sound fades with distance. Here a whisper in a crack and the wind at the cliff are the same volume, so they learned to be silent. When they need to speak to one another they press a palm, or a forehead, to the warm brick and let the tremor travel.

And the brick trembles. You felt it give slightly under your hand, not like stone but like dense clay still curing. That is because it is still curing, and they are curing with it. Their bodies are warm — the same kiln-warm that radiates from the mass of the walls. They do not make heat; they *keep* it. The hamlet is a kiln that never opens, and they are the pots left inside. Their blood, if you could call it blood, is thick, salty, almost fat — like the rendered fat desiccated into the mortar between bricks. They do not drink. There is nothing to drink. The white and pale-yellow crust at the mesa's lip is not just mineral, it is their cistern. On the coldest edge of night — though night never truly comes — when the warm air finally condenses for a breath against the cooling basalt, it beads on that salt fur. They come — slowly, so slowly you would think they were not moving — and press their cheeks, their flattened forearms, to the crust and let the deliquescent salt pull the trace damp from the air into their skin. One bead a day is enough when you do not sweat, when you exhale through a nose that reclaims every molecule on the way out. Their mouths are small, pursed, almost sealed, lined with that same salt.

They do not eat as you eat. There is no plant, no fungus, no insect to eat. They eat the town. Not in bites. They scrape a fine dust of fired clay and limestone from the sill where countless hands have worn it smooth, and hold it on a tongue like a flat stone, and let the minerals and the ancient, mineralized fat dissolve. They rasp the black, iron-hard lumps that were once timber beams — now more stone than wood — for the sparse carbon still left in them. It is enough, because they hardly move.

That is the other thing you will not understand until you have sat — though there is nowhere to sit — and watched for a long time. They are in equilibrium, just as the place is. The wind does not gust, the light does not shift, the heat does not cool, and neither do they. If you stare at what you think is an empty alley that is not an alley but a crack between houses, you will see at first only walls. Then, after many minutes, you will realize one of the walls has a rhythm. A rise so shallow it could be the brick itself humming. A fall that takes a full minute. Breath. One breath every long, long while. They can remain pressed into a niche, folded into a stairwell that is also a roof, for hours. For days. Because to move is to make a new sound in a chord that has been held forever, and to create a brief, hotter friction in a warmth that has been held forever, and that would be wasteful. When they do move, they flow rather than walk — shoulder through a gap, hip through a gap, on hands whose fingers have widened into spatulate, thumb-printed pads that fit exactly the hand-marks left in the sun-baked brick. They were the hands that made those prints, or their grandmothers were. Hammerstones and green-copper chisels are still clutched sometimes in those hands, not as tools now but as ballast, as heirlooms, warm from being held.

They live utterly packed. A courtyard the size of a sleeping mat is an apartment, a clan house, a country. Three of them will occupy a room you thought could hold one, fitted together like honeycomb, breathing each other's reclaimed air. There are no doors that open because every door opens onto someone else — onto a back, onto a forehead, onto another door a foot away that will never open. It is not claustrophobia to them. It is completeness. The mesa was finished when there was no room left to press another room, and they were finished when there was no room left to press another body.

If you stand at the very edge, where the outer bricks have fused into a single mass and you cannot tell geology from craft, and you look down into the warm haze that has no bottom, you will feel them notice you — not by sight, not by hearing you over the deafening pressure, but by the wrongness you bring. You are wet. You are loud in the wrong way — your heart is a gust, your joints click, your skin gives off water. You are cool in places, and you *move*.

One by one, without turning what you would call a face toward you, the warm, brick-colored backs and shoulders in the alleys, on the roofs, in the jar-niches, will adjust by a fraction. Not to look. To feel the new tremor you make in the chord. To consider, with a patience that has outlasted erosion, whether there is room for you.

And there is not.
