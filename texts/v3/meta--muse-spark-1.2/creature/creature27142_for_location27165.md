They still live there.

Not in the way you were listening for, which is why you thought the pocket was empty.

At first you keep looking for an animal. That is the habit your eyes bring with you from the surface — something with a face, with eyes that meet yours, with a breath you can hear, with a shadow that darts. There is nothing like that here. No scurry. No flutter. No eyeshine. If you stand in the avenue between the trunk-houses and hold your breath, you will hear only the city itself: groan, tick, pop, the rifle-crack of a pod splitting somewhere high in the dark.

For a long time you think that is all there is. The city maintaining itself out of plant memory, empty.

Then you start to notice that some of the sounds are not quite plant.

The difference is almost nothing. A plant tick is dry and thoughtless, a fiber releasing tension. This other tick answers. You shift your weight on the felt-ground and a leaf pops above you — and a half-second later, from inside the wall to your left, a softer pop comes back. Not an echo. An acknowledgement.

You were hearing them before you knew how to hear them.

Who lives here did not arrive. They diverged. They are what happens when a people decide to live inside a single fruit and then stay long enough for the fruit to teach them how.

The builders left their chipped adze-marks and their smoke stains and their clay pots, and then they did not leave. There was nowhere else to go — above was only the ancient, sanded-down plain with no water and no shade. So they stayed and thinned and dried and slowed until they fit.

You will not see one at first because you are looking for movement. They have made a virtue of not moving.

Crouch beside one of the hollow trunks — one of the houses — and put your hand on the doorway where the wood has been cut and then lignified into a smooth, dark frame. Run your fingers along the inner wall where the pith was scooped out. The surface is not quite wood. It is warmer than wood should be in this cellar-cool. A faint, waxy bloom comes off on your fingertips, like the powder on a plum.

That warmth is not the plant. The plant is exactly air-temperature. That is a body, pressed flat against the curve of the wall inside, holding still.

They are the size of a small person, or a large child — long, narrow, folded. If one stood to its full height it might come to your chest, but they almost never stand. Their limbs are disproportionate, attenuated the way cave things become attenuated: forearms and shins twice as long as they should be, jointed to wrap a trunk or to span a root-alley without touching the exposed middle. Their skin is not skin. It is a pellicle, translucent, salt-pale and rust-pale like the ground-felt, dry to the touch and faintly tacky with the same clear glycerin the plants weep. No hair. No sweat. Sweat would be suicide here. Their surface seals them. They lose almost no water because they have almost no water to lose. What sloshes in them instead is oil. If you cut one — you won't — it would not bleed. It would bead.

Their faces are the hardest to accept. There are no eyes. Not covered, not blinded — simply unmade. Where eyes would be, the pellicle continues smooth and unbroken, a little thicker, a little more waxy, like a scar that was never a wound. In absolute darkness, across a hundred generations, eyes became an expense the body stopped paying. What they have instead is an exquisite, whole-body hearing and a pit of heat-sense in the center of the forehead, a shallow, darkened dimple the size of a coin that can feel the faint bloom of your breath from three meters away. That is why the leaf above you turned toward your body heat. It wasn't the leaf.

Their heads are large, bald, slightly soft, with a mouth that is not a mouth as you use it. A small, lipless, vertical slit that never opens wide, fringed with fine, folded filters. They do not eat, not as you do. They drink. At intervals that you would call starvation, they press that mouth to a vein in the wall and take in a mouthful of the clear, cool, non-evaporating resin — the plant's glycerin — and that is enough for weeks. The clay pots on the shelves were not for water. They were for that. For keeping. One pot, filled, could keep a family for a season. They do not cook. Fire was only ever for carving, and they have not needed to carve for a very long time because the plant now does it for them.

They communicate the way the pocket communicates: in clicks and ticks and deep wooden thunks. A knuckle tapped against a hollow trunk carries through the whole nave. A tongue — if it is a tongue — clicked against the ridged palate makes the same dry pop as a contracting leaf. They learned to speak in the forest's own sound so that speech would not be a foreign noise. When you heard the vine give way with a *thunk* through your soles, that might have been a vine. That might have been someone on the far side of the plaza saying *someone else is here*. They have been saying it since you slipped sideways through the fissure.

There are not many of them. That is the other reason you think the city is empty. A town that could hold two hundred now holds perhaps thirty, spread so thin through the fused trunks that you could live a week in the avenue and never meet one, though they would have met you many times. They are solitary by architecture — one per trunk, sometimes two, sometimes a third curled small as a question mark beside them — and they meet rarely, and briefly, in the plaza where the roots braid into an amphitheater. Not to speak. To listen together. To feel the slow settling of the city as a group.

Their time is not your time. Nothing hurries here, and they hurry least of all. A breath every few minutes, shallow and dry, barely enough to fog the cool air. A heart that you would have to press your ear to their chest to hear, beating perhaps six times a minute when at rest. They can hold a position — pressed to a wall, draped over a burl-balcony, tucked into a niche — for hours, for days, moving only when the heat-signature of the world changes. That is why the doorway never healed over: the inhabitant inside leaned against it for so long the plant learned to keep the frame. That is why the new vine grew where the rope bridge needed support: someone lay along that gap for a generation, waiting, and the vine followed the faint, constant exudation of oil from their back.

They are not hostile. They are not welcoming. Those are fast emotions. They are curious in the way a root is curious — by slowly growing around the new thing to see what shape it is.

If you sit down in the felt-ground in the middle of the avenue, where you feel most exposed in the middle of so much life, and you stay still long enough that your breath goes shallow and your lips crack a little more, you will begin to feel them unflatten.

First, just a change in the air pressure, as a body peels itself away from a wall a few meters away. No footsteps. Their feet — long, soft, three-toed, with pads like suede — make no sound on the fiber mat. Then a faint creak that is not wood, a joint extending after a long stillness.

Then, if you have learned to see with your hands and ears, you will sense them at the edge of the dark: a warmth, a faint smell of wax and dust and something slightly sweeter — the glycerin on their skin — standing just outside where your hands could reach, listening to you listen.

They will not touch you unless you stay a very long time. If you do, one might come close enough that you feel the cool, dry brush of a forearm as long as your own, testing the heat coming off your face. It will tap once, very softly, on the ground beside you — tick — and wait to hear if you know how to tick back.

They remember being a city. They remember it the way the plants do: not as history, but as shape. They keep the straight lintels straight. They keep the avenue empty by never filling it. They keep the pots empty and clean.

And when you finally leave — when you have to, because your throat is raw from the dryness no matter how shallow you breathe — they will flatten themselves again against the walls, against the burls and the braided roots, and become indistinguishable from the living infrastructure. The pocket will be loud again with only plant sounds.

But now you will know that some of those sounds were never plants at all.
