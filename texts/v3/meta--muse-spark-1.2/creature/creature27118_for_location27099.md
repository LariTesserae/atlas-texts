No one lives there at first.

That's what you think for the first hour, or the first day. You walk the leaf-boulevards and the empty wattle-factories and you hear the looms going with no hands on them and you think *abandoned*. You think *after*.

Then you start to notice that the absence is not empty. It's *tended*.

A conveyor belt carrying those car-sized seed pods snags — a vine has thrown itself across the catenary at the wrong angle. You watch it sit, stalled, gears grinding. You turn away to look at a water-tower fig. When you look back, the vine has been cut. Not torn. Cut, with a clean, angled slice that has already beaded over with clear sap. No one is there. The sap is still running down the vine. Your breath smokes. The cut is warm.

Someone lives here. They are just very good at being the city.

---

You don't see them until you learn how slow to look.

They are tall. That is the first wrong thing — too tall for three thousand meters where every breath is thin and the cold never really leaves your bones. They have to be tall, because the city is built at tree-scale. A doorway is four meters high. A stair riser is up to your knee. If they were your size they'd never be able to reach the valves, the shutters, the great wooden gears.

So they are tall and they are thin, the way alpine things are thin. Not starved thin. *Stretched* thin, like they were pulled on a rack to match the braided columns. Seven, eight feet at the shoulder, but their chests are deep as barrels, ribs visibly bowed out under skin to hold the extra lung the air demands. When they breathe you can hear it even over the roar of wind and industry — a slow, deliberate bellows inhale that goes on too long, and then a long, steaming exhale that hangs in the cold for seconds.

You think at first they are wearing suits. Ghillie suits, work suits, something patched and mossy. Then you get close enough to see the suit has no seam.

Their skin is not skin anymore. Or not only. It is a symbiosis. The same rust-green-black bark that grows on the columns grows on them, but thin, living, supple. It plates along their forearms, their shins, their backs — shingle-like, overlapping, edged with lichen. Between the plates their skin shows, pale and damp and almost translucent, beaded always with condensation that never quite dries, just like your sleeves. Moss grows thickest where they don't move much: in the hollow of the throat, along the spine, capping the shoulders like epaulets. On some of the older ones the moss has fruited — tiny white caps, tiny red ones. They don't pick them off.

Their heads are still human enough to hurt. That's the part your brain catches on. They have faces. They have eyes that are almost always the same grey-green as the sap. They have hair, but it has gone strange — no longer quite hair, but a fine, pale aerial root that hangs in damp curtains, that twitches toward light and water on its own. The young ones tie it back with copper wire or twine to keep it out of the looms. The old ones let it grow until it roots into the collar of whatever bark-coat has grown over their shoulders, until you can't tell where person ends and colony begins.

They don't hurry. Nothing that lives that high, in that cold, in that noise, can afford to hurry.

Movement is expensive here. Heat is expensive. So they move like grazers do — long stillnesses, then a sudden, efficient transit. You will watch a platform that you *know* was empty and then blink and there is one standing at the far end, having crossed fifty meters of leaf-ground without seeming to have crossed it at all, because you didn't hear it over the creak and throb. They step soft despite their size. The leaf-streets ripple under them but don't crack.

And they are sparse. Impossibly sparse for how much city there is to tend. You will walk for hours and see only signs — a ladder of shelf-fungus grown into a perfect staircase overnight where yesterday there was sheer trunk. A cistern leaf that has been bailed. A factory floor where the dripping seed-pods have been split and their wet, fibrous meat harvested in neat strips, left to cure on warm pipes. Then you will crest a bough and see three of them together and understand why the city feels empty: a valley that wanted ten thousand only has maybe two or three hundred, and they do not gather.

When they do gather, it is not social. It is maintenance.

You find them in the steam-mills at what might be evening — the light is going sideways and shadows are pooling uphill. Five of them around one of the house-sized wooden gears that has slipped its vine-belt. They don't talk. The sound is too loud for talk and anyway their mouths are rarely visible, hidden behind plates of bark that have grown up over the lower face like a respirator. Instead they hum.

It's low. Lower than the diesel thud, lower than the wind. You feel it in your molars before you hear it. A chest-deep, resonant note that they can push through the roar without shouting. One starts, and the others find it, and the note changes not in words but in intent — *hold here, pull now, slack* — and the great gear groans back onto its track. Sap wells from where bark split under strain and one of them leans down and presses their mossy forearm to it, and the sap slows. They share. They don't have tools the way you think of tools. They have grown them. A thumb splits at the tip into two hard, black, chitinous pruners. A forearm extrudes a flat spatula of bone-wood for scraping lichen off sensor-plates.

They learned the city by becoming part of it, and now they can't be seen apart from it. If an inhabitant stands still against a braided trunk for ten minutes, the trunk will start to offer them: a vine will curl idly around an ankle to steady them. A leaf will tilt to shed its cold dew away from their back. The camouflage isn't intentional. It's affinity. The town likes them better than it likes you, because they match its idea of what should live in a factory.

---

There are the rooted ones, too.

Not everyone gets to keep walking. You find them deeper in, where the canopy forms full plazas and the secondary ground is thick enough you forget the stone exists at all. They are in the walls. Or they *are* walls.

At first you think it's art — a bas-relief carved into living wattle, a figure with arms outstretched. Then you see the chest move. So slowly. One breath every minute, maybe less. Their legs have gone down into the floor, root-fused, bark crept up past knees, past waist. Their arms have braided into the surrounding vine-work, fingers splayed into a fan of rootlets that disappear into the building's structure. Their faces are still there, eyes open, filmed with a nictitating membrane of thin leaf. They are not dead. They are not in pain. They are *load-bearing*. They chose this, or the city chose it for them when their knees gave out or their lungs got too tired for the thin air.

The walkers tend them. They bring them water in cupped leaves, pour it not into a mouth but over the crown of the head where those hair-roots drink. They scrape the bark clean. They lean close and hum, and the rooted ones hum back, even lower, so low the living wood itself seems to answer.

That's how the city remembers its shape. The buildings stay factories and apartments because there are people inside the walls dreaming *factory, apartment, street* for the tree to copy. When a wall-figure finally stops humming, the room around them softens. Within a week the stairwell they were holding will seal shut and a new, blind chimney will bud somewhere else, because the plant has forgotten that detail.

And there are the newest ones.

You hear them before you see them — a pod on the conveyor that is ticking, clicking, not like the others. The walkers gather, not five now but ten, twelve, as many as you have ever seen in one place. They wait. The pod swells, splits along a seam of rusted steel that has been grown over, and steams in the cold. Inside is not meat. Inside is a child.

It is already tall for a child, already pale, already with a dusting of green-black bark on its knuckles and knees. Its eyes are closed. Its hair-roots are short and fine as down. It takes that huge, bellows breath and cries, and the cry is thin and reedy and immediately swallowed by the wind and creak and throb. One of the older walkers lifts it — careful, so careful of the soft seams where bark hasn't hardened — and carries it to the nearest cistern leaf to wash the afterbirth sap off.

No one is surprised. This is how they arrive now. The city grows its maintenance crew the way it grows its doors.

---

They see you, eventually. They don't startle.

You are small and loud and you cast a shadow that doesn't quite track you. You drip wrong — your condensation beads and falls instead of being drunk. You leave footprints in the moss that stay bruised for hours. You are inefficient.

One will stop on the boulevard ahead of you. Watch you with those sap-colored eyes. Tip its head a fraction. Then it will hum — not to you, *at* you — a single low note that you feel more than hear, a question that translates not as *who are you* but as *are you going to root or walk?* Because those are the only options the plateau understands anymore.

If you don't answer, it loses interest. It has a vine to cut, a gear to re-belt, a rooted elder to water, a pod to wait for. It steps past you, close enough that you smell cold sap and copper and the faint, sweet rot of leaves cycling in an afternoon, and its mossy shoulder brushes yours and leaves a damp smear.

It knows how to navigate the tired geometry. It knows not to look over its shoulder to check if a building is still there, but to glance at its shadow — because here the shadow tells the truth and the object lies. It knows which leaf-streets will still be there on the way back if you walk them without counting. It knows which steam is just steam and which steam is a wall breathing.

They are not hostile. They are not welcoming. They are sparse because the plateau can only afford sparse, they are loud because everything is loud, they are green because everything is green. They are what happens when a town teaches a forest to be a town, and the forest, being a good student, teaches the people to be a forest in return.

When you leave — and you will leave before the cold gets too deep, before your clothes never dry — you look back from the lip of the world, from where the whaleback ridge starts to fall away into real mountains again. The green city is still humming behind you, still creaking, still growing a new stairwell that goes nowhere. On a vast leaf-plaza you see a single figure standing, exhaling a plume that the wind takes sideways. It has already forgotten you were there.

The city was never abandoned. It was just fitted, over a very long time, to inhabitants who could afford to live in a place that erases its own streets.
