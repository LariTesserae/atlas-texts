At first you think nothing lives here.

That is the first conclusion the place wants you to draw. It's too loud with non-animal sound, too wet to be comfortable, too empty between the towers. You stand on the warm give of the cliff-mat and scan that luminous, dripping emptiness and see only plant building itself, falling, rebuilding itself. No birds to cross the gap. No insects to cloud the light. No tracks, no burrows, no nests. If something lived here you would hear it — and all you hear is the valley growing.

You are wrong, but slowly.

The life here has solved the problem of being heard by not making sound at all. The life here has solved the problem of the high, thin, hot air by not fighting it. The life here has solved the problem of there being no ground by never needing it.

You start to see it when your eyes adjust to the fact that movement does not mean what you think it does here. A pop and a shudder runs through the tower opposite you and a whole crown of leaves unfurls, pale and wet, with a sigh of steam - and something *else* moves on it that is not the leaf.

Not crawling. Flowing.

### The ones on the skin

They are everywhere once you see the scale. The first inhabitants are almost not animals. You took them for water.

On every waxy upper surface, on the underside that glows yellow-green with reflected light, there is a film. Look closer and the film resolves into bodies. Thousands of them. Little more than blistered air.

They are flat, translucent, the size of your palm, the size of a coin, the size of a fingernail depending on the leaf. Like stingrays pressed thin by humidity. No legs. No front or back that you can find. Just a frilled margin and a mouth underneath that never leaves the leaf. They are lung and stomach at once. 

In this air you have to work to breathe - thin and heavy at the same time, sweet rot in every inhale. They don't. They open. Their whole dorsal surface is a gill, a fern of filaments that drinks the steam directly. They don't drink; they are *drunk* by the air. Water condenses on them and passes through them. Sap beads at the leaf's edge and they flex towards it without lifting, skating on a micron of water the leaf exudes for them.

When the leaf tips - and every few minutes some roof-sized leaf somewhere in the valley finally tips its collected load - they ride it. You see a sheet of water spill and one of them briefly become a sailing thing, carried down six feet to the next level, then reattaching without a splash. They never fall. If they do fall, it doesn't matter. The air is so thick and they are so light, they parachute on themselves.

They are the color of whatever they are on. Green on top, pale luminous green-yellow underneath. When they move they leave no trail because the leaf heals behind them. They are not eating the plant. They are drinking its sweat.

They are silent. Entirely.

You thought the valley was empty because you were listening for buzzing. This is louder than buzzing but invisible - the valley is paved with them.

### The ones in the weave

Then you see that a cable is moving wrong.

The great braided trunks - the fused roots and vines as thick as your body - creak and tighten in the heat. But one segment is tightening in the wrong rhythm. It pulses. It reaches.

You realize you are looking at a limb.

The second inhabitants live *in* the architecture, and at first you cannot tell them from it. They are long. They have to be long here, because the world is vertical and sparse and to live you must cross emptiness.

They look like someone stretched a spider monkey and then forgot what a mammal should be. Four to six limbs, you can't count because they never all show at once. The limbs are exactly the diameter of the vines they live on. The skin is bark-fibrous, damp, mottled green-white, covered in fine root-hairs that are actual root-hairs - symbiotic, growing from them. Their torso is small, almost vestigial, tucked between joints. Mostly they are reach.

They move without stepping. They flow hand over hand over hand along the underside of causeways, along the vertical cliffs that weep water. Suction pads at each hand and foot - not suckers, but rings of cilia that create a seal on wet wax without breaking it. They distribute their weight so perfectly that the woven mat doesn't dimple. You weigh too much. You feel your mat sag warm under your boots. They weigh nothing.

Watch one closer: its head is nothing but a frill. No eyes you can see. A ruff of the same filament-gills as the flat things, spread wide to breathe the steam, trembling. Between the frills is a soft beak, not for biting but for piercing. It leans down and kisses a young shoot - one of those pale spears punching upward - and drinks the pressurized sap inside before the shoot can unfurl. It farms tension. It knows which cable is about to snap before it does, because it feels the vibration through its whole body. You see one freeze, spread its limbs wide, and hold - and a second later the vine next to it gives with a roar and a whole platform sags, water thundering down. The animal rides the sag. It doesn't flee. It *stays* spread, holding the next two vines together with its own body until the plant, fast as it is, threads new rootlets through its hair and knits around it, then lets go.

They are stitchers. They are not parasites. They are not separate. They live by keeping the weave taut, and the weave feeds them for it. When a tower collapses - and you will see one collapse, slowly folding, browning - they are already there, white rootlets threading through the dying limb before it stops bouncing, as per the rule: nothing rots. Neither do they when they die. If one falls, if one is crushed between two creaking trunks, you don't find a body. By the time you look again white exploratory rootlets are already threading through its filament-ruff, stitching it into the floor. The valley remembers them as added thickness.

They communicate the only way you can in a place that is this loud with growth: not with sound, but with tremor. A tap on a vine travels hundreds of feet through living cable. You start to feel it under your feet after a while, a faint pattern under the drip and groan - tap. tap... tap-tap. The whole valley thrumming with low conversation you can't hear, only feel in your soles.

They watch you. The frills turn.

### The ones between

You will not see the third inhabitants until the light pillars shift and one passes through a pillar and disappears.

Between the sparse towers is nothing but deep, wet air - and that is itself a habitat, if you are light enough to live in air that is almost water.

They are the reason the space stays sparse. They keep it empty by occupying it.

They are vast and they are almost nothing. Imagine a jellyfish that decided to live in steam instead of sea, then grew a sail. From wingtip to wingtip wider than any of the lily-pad platforms, but so translucent you see straight through them to the green glow beyond. A bell that is not a bell but a loose hood of membrane, pulsing not to swim but to wring water from air. Underneath, trailing filaments as fine as spider silk and as long as the drop between levels, beaded constantly with condensation.

They do not flap. They cannot. The air is too thin to beat and too heavy to need to. They *drift*. They rise on the plant's exhale - because the mountain drinks itself, remember: water lifted in impossible volumes through the columns and transpired out the top in clouds that never leave. The whole valley breathes up. The veils ride that breath up as high as the haze, trailing their threads through cloud, collecting it, growing heavy, then slowly, majestically sinking as they become rain themselves, bleeding what they've gathered in a silent curtain that feeds the platforms below. Then lighter, they rise again.

They are the water cycle made animal. Or the water cycle learned to make an animal to carry itself.

When one sinks near a tower, the stitchers reach out and briefly hold its filaments, trading - sap for salts filtered from cloud, nitrogen fixed high up where the sun is ordinary and indifferent. You see a stitcher clinging to a vine with three limbs and with a fourth gently stroking a filament thicker than its body, and you understand this is husbandry.

They block the sun for a second when they pass. Their shadows on the waxy surfaces are faint, like cloud shadows, and the flat things below flex toward that brief dimming. They are utterly silent. Even the water that falls from them makes no sound of its own - it just joins the general percussion.

You feel the groan in your chest when a tower half a mile away shudders and extends another six feet. The nearest veil, huge and invisible until then, ripples. It feels the same shudder through the air pressure and tilts, spilling its gathered load early to avoid being snagged on new growth. Everything here grows away from everything else, sharp and unweathered, and the things that live here grow *with* that rule. Nothing crowds. Even the veils keep their distance from one another, each in its own clear span of humid air, like the towers themselves.

That is who lives here, then - or what. Not a fauna on top of a flora, but a gradient where you cannot find the edge. A film that breathes steam on every leaf. A long, many-jointed stitcher that is half vine. A veil that is half cloud. All of them pale, all wet, all weightless or weight-distributed, all silent, all directly drinking the air and the sap because there is never ground to stand on and never still water to find. 

They have no nests because the place *is* the nest, continuously made and unmade. They have no fear of falling because there is nothing to fall *onto* - only more weave to catch them, more filaments to tangle gently and lower them. They have no voice because the plants are already too loud with becoming, and they have learned to speak in tremors and shadows instead.

Stand a little longer on your warm, giving, faintly pulsing mat and you will feel it: you are the anomaly. You are heavy, you are loud with breath, you drip salt instead of fresh water, your skin doesn't breathe steam, your weight makes the weave sag, your footsteps make a rhythm that is not a growth rhythm. The frills all turn. A veil far out tilts just enough to look - if a cloud can look.

They are not hostile. There is no predation as you know it here. What dies is immediately knitted back in. To kill is to feed the floor.

They are simply waiting to see if you will be woven in, too.
