You think at first that nothing lives here.

Not really. Not in the way you mean when you say *lives*. There is too much light without nourishment, too much heat without water. The bowl should be empty. The salt should be clean.

Then you stay long enough for the dim to sharpen, and the place begins to correct you.

The first correction is that *lush* does not mean *wet*.

What lives here does not drink.

### The Holders

They are the reason there is anything else at all. They hold the bowl.

There are not many of them — if you count, you will count wrong, because they are shy of being counted. Eleven, or twenty-seven. Enough that each one has a long apron of empty salt around it that no other will cross. They keep distance the way desert wells keep distance.

You mistook them for stain at first. Gray-green, amber, bruised purple on the white. Come closer.

Each one is a single insistence.

The oldest in the middle has a trunk you could not put your arms around, though it is not wood. It is rope made into column, fibers braided and re-braided until they have become a thing like fossilized hemp, pale, dry, peeling in whisper-thin sheets that lift and tick in the heat. It does not grow. It *twists* upward, fifteen feet of patience.

On top of it — no branches, no crown as you understand crowns — a fountain. A burst. A slow explosion of leaves that never finish opening.

Each leaf is the size of a horse blanket, or a cloak left to dry. They are thick as your thumb, waxy, translucent like old horn or fingernail. Cream, and when the dim light hits them you see the rose and soot living in their veins. They are inflated. They breathe without air, they puff themselves full of heat and hang there, half-full, creaking. Even in stillness they rustle because they are always letting out and taking in, a lung-field.

They are not green. Green is a water trick. These are oil-colored.

If you cut one — and someone does, rarely — it doesn't bleed. It sighs. A warm oil comes to the edge, citron and blood-iron and pepper, and the air fills with a fine dry spore that does not fall for hours. You can taste the oil three feet away. That is the blood of this place. It has no water in it at all.

At dusk — because dusk does come, a little thinner, a little more violet — the edges of those great leaves prickle with light. A faint, crawling fire, blue-white, that ticks and jumps from tip to tip. You realize the leaves are fields. They are making charge the way other leaves make sugar, combing the thin air for the few stray molecules of ammonia, of hydrocarbon that drift through, pulling them down onto electrified skin and condensing them into wax and oil.

Around their feet, the others.

Low things that you called moss, until you knelt. They are not soft. They are a felt of glass, hair-fine threads of silica black as soot, each one warm to the touch. Put your hand near and you feel the thrum, the faint sting of static, and the threads lean toward you. They are singing — a high hiss you feel in your teeth — because they are growing. You can watch it. An inch an hour, a filament reaching.

And the barrels — the ones that look like cactus if cactus were made by a lantern-maker. Nested papers, dry membranes layered one inside the next, amber and bone, rattling. They tighten in the heat until they pop like drums. *Pop. Pop-pop.* Out of rhythm, all day.

And the vine-things that have given up on ground altogether. Tumble skeletons, dry lace, holding themselves a foot off the salt on nothing but hot air, rolling very slowly forever on an invisible cushion, never landing, never rooting. If the wind finds them they go faster, but there is almost no wind. Only heat.

None of them are dusty though they are covered in dust. None of them are dry though there has never been rain. They are powdery and swollen at once. Press one and it pushes back.

### The Noise

Because the holders are here, the bowl is not silent for a second. That is the second correction: a desert is not quiet if you are small enough.

You hear the holders first. The clack of those opal leaves as they bump and deflate. The barrel drums. The glass-felt hiss.

Then you hear what lives *on* them.

The air is full. It is too full, and you don't see why until one passes close to your face.

They are not birds. They have no beaks, no mouths you can find. They are hand-sized, cloak-sized, made of the same oiled paper as the barrel membranes — translucent, ribbed like a leaf, with long trailing edges that crackle. They ride. They don't fly so much as they *surf* the heat coming off the salt, tilting, colliding with the great leaves so softly you would miss it if you weren't listening, and when they touch they shiver. That is how they eat. The belly flashes faintly as it steals the charge off the leaf, the spores, the fine electrified dust. Then off again, buzzing.

Buzzing is wrong. It is chittering without a voice. Wing-case against wing-case, dry, a thousand knuckles cracking at once. The air is thick with it, a constant dry storm. When one dies — and they do, often — it doesn't fall. It crumples in midair like burnt parchment and drops, and before it hits the salt the ground is already moving toward it.

The ground things.

Fast is not the word. *Busy* is better. The salt that looked empty is a market, an anthill after the stick.

Fist-sized pill things, backs crusted with salt until they look like traveling rocks, pushing perfect balls of dust ahead of them, tireless. Striders with legs like whips so thin you can see through them, that skate *above* the salt, never quite touching, held up on pads of repulsion that make the dust beneath them dimple. And a thing you want to call a lizard because your brain needs a word — but its skin is interlocking fiber plates, overlapping like that braided trunk, and its tail is not a tail but a dry frond, a feather-duster of filaments that combs the air as it walks, tasting charge.

None of them drink. They chew. They rasp at the waxy leaves, they gnaw the glass threads down to stubble — you can watch a black cushion go bald in minutes under a flock of gliders and then, an hour later, furring over again — they crack the fallen barrel lanterns and lick the bitter wax from inside.

This is the tempo of the bowl. It is not growth. It is churn. A pod the size of a melon will swell while you watch, its skin going taut and glossy, and then detonate with a gunshot that makes you flinch, firing corkscrewing fibers that whistle as they spin. A tumble-levitator will catch on a leaf edge, tear, knit itself back together out of the air. Everything making and unmaking without pause and without progress. A storm in a jar. Solaris in miniature. The bowl itself — that soft-lipped, wind-polished, ten-thousand-year-old thumbprint — stays exactly the same while everything inside it riots.

You understand then that the bowl is not a place that *contains* life. The bowl is the thing that *allows* the riot to be endless without exhausting itself. It holds the shape steady so the churn can be furious.

### The Sparse Ones

And then there are the ones who built the windbreaks.

You almost don't see them because there are so few and they do not want to be seen together. Not a village. A bowl this size cannot afford a village. Three. Perhaps three shelters in the whole thumbprint, each alone, each turned the same way into the faint updraft that never stops rising off the warm rock.

Each is low, hardly more than a wall. Stacked bricks cut from the salt itself, already melting back, softened at the corners. Over them, a roof of leaf-membrane stretched tight over a frame of that braided fiber, cured until it is drum-tight and translucent, fluttering faintly with heat. Beside each one: a small careful world. A cairn of dolomite slabs, a loom weight of pitted stone, a shallow ash circle where oil was burned. Chimes — long strips of dried lantern skin, tuned by scraping, hung from a fiber tripod so that when the heat rises they knock together, moaning, popping, adding to the loudness even when no one is there to listen.

Inside, a bowl carved from the tooth-colored rock, shallow, oil-dark at its center. A bone awl, the bone polished yellow, hafted with black resin. A blanket folded, if you touch it your hair lifts — it's woven from the glass threads, crackling, warm.

They cut. That is their work and their prayer. With a flake of chert they slice a leaf-edge, not more than a hand's breadth, and catch the oil in a cured skin, and the spores in another. They press the oil into their throats, they rub it into their lips and the cracked skin of their hands. They have to. The air would take them otherwise.

You find one footprint, at the edge of one windbreak, half-filled with blown salt, already overrun with the tracklets of striders. The print is narrow, long-toed, bare. The skin that made it was not quite skin as you know it — oiled, dusted, the color of that waxy leaf, cream and rose and soot. You look up, expecting to see its maker, but the bowl is empty except for the holders and the gliders and the frantic ground things.

They move with the gliders, perhaps. Or they sleep through the loudest part, curled under the crackling blanket, breathing the citron air.

You can't call them people without qualifying it, because they have solved a different set of problems. Light is scarce but enough — the dim sun is plenty for oil and charge. Water is not scarce; it is *forbidden*, a story that has no word here, irrelevant as snow. Oil is blood, and they carry it in skins. Static is muscle, and they feel it pull at the hairs on their arms before a glider lands. Heat is food, and they never shiver because the rock never cools. Sound is how you know you are still alive — so they make chimes, so they listen to the popping barrels and know the bowl hasn't stopped.

They are few because the bowl is jealous. Ten holders, thirty gliders per holder, ten thousand striders, and three windbreaks. That is the ratio. Lush, but sparse. Abundant, but spread terribly thin. Each one alone with an extravagance that would be a forest anywhere else.

Stand on the rim when the amber thins just a little more — true night never comes, the veil never breaks, but the light goes honey-violet and the Saint Elmo's fire on the great leaves gets brighter — and the whole bowl announces itself upward.

Leaves clashing, barrels drumming, felt hissing, gliders cracking their knuckles by the thousands, seed guns firing, glass growing with a sound like faint singing, chimes knocking with no hand to strike them.

A dry jungle in a salt thumbprint, too loud, too busy, too full for a place that has never had a drop of water, going on and on in its perfect, weather-polished circle because it has learned how to live on everything else.

And somewhere below you, under a tight leaf roof, someone oil-lipped and dust-skinned turns in their sleep, listens to the bowl turning over itself, and knows — without needing to look — that the world is still there.
