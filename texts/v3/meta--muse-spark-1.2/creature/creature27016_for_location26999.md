It lives low, because everything here lives low.

At first you don't see it at all and you think nothing does. You think the builders have gone, or died, or finished and left this to cure in the sun. The plain is too empty, too regular, too hot for anything with a metabolism to bother with. You walk for an hour and the only things that seem to be alive are the reeds, and even they look placed.

Then you stay long enough to notice that the pattern is wrong.

You kneel at one of the junctions where the canals meet and the water is supposed to be, and you see the pool is not still. It's a dull mirror, yes, clouded with clay - but the cloud never settles. There is a faint, continuous roiling, as if the mud itself is breathing. And the edges of the pool are not mud. They are not brick, either. Not quite.

The brick here is sharp. Everywhere else its edges are perfect, its corners unchipped. But at the water's edge the upper course is softened. No - *soft.* If you touch it, it gives a little, like leather left in water. The color is the same - that pale bone-color - but it is darker by that shade that means damp has gone deep. And it is warm. Not sun-warm. Blood-warm.

That is the first time you realize you are not looking at brick.

You are looking at a back.

It does not move when you touch it. Not at first. It has no reason to hurry. What lives here has learned that hurry is death. To move fast in this heat is to dry out. So it moves at the speed that brick moves, at the speed that water seeps.

Wait until dusk. That is when it becomes more itself.

As the sun drops and the brick finally stops burning, the whole plain begins to tick. The mud plates curl and lift. The air, which has been holding its breath all day, exhales. And the brick exhale with it. A chill comes that is not cold, just less hot, and then the dew begins to bead.

Every horizontal surface silvers. Droplets run together, find the seams, follow the seams into the runnels.

And every seam opens.

What you thought was mortar between the blocks - that thin, slightly darker line - splits. It swells. It lifts its edge.

They are flat. That is the first thing you can't get over. They are as flat as the world that made them. When they are dry and still, they are indistinguishable from the plain. A living brick, a living course. Their dorsal side is not skin but a casing - a secreted plate of that same silty clay, fired not in a kiln but by the sun on their own backs while they lie motionless through the day. It hardens into the exact texture and color of the surrounding terrace, with the same sharp corners, the same faint palm-press in the center where the flesh contracted as it dried. They *are* the courses. They make the courses by being the courses. When they estivate at noon, you could walk over them and think you had stepped from brick to brick.

But at night, soaked and supple, they peel themselves up.

An adult is the size of what it lays. Some are the size of your hand. Some are the size of your torso, curled at the edges like a broad, pale ray. The oldest - the ones whose plates are the size of a room - you will never see move. They have become landscape. They lie in the lowest terraces where the water gathers deepest and they breathe once an hour.

Their ventral side is not brick at all. It is pale, translucent, slick, the color of wet clay slip. No legs. No head as you understand it. The whole underside is one broad foot, a muscle that holds the mud in a seal so perfect that water cannot escape from beneath it. Around the rim of that foot is a frill - and this is what you mistook for mortar. A living lip, thin as paper when dry, fleshy and mobile when wet, covered in fine cilia that beat constantly, almost invisibly. That frill does three things: it drinks, it builds, it feels.

It drinks dew. The dorsal plate is slightly concave, engineered by its own growth to be a dew-catcher. At night the plate cools faster than the air. Water beads on it by the hundreds of droplets and runs to a low channel in the midline of the back, where the frill sips it before it can evaporate. On a good night, a hand-sized one will collect a thimbleful. A torso-sized one, a bowlful. Enough.

It builds. That is why the world is new and raw and endless. With its frill it scoops the silt that the water brings - the fine, pale clay that never settles - and kneads it under its body, mixing it with a mucilage it secretes. It presses. You can see it if you lie still for a long time and let your eyes adjust: a slow, peristaltic ripple traveling inward from the frill's edge toward the center, like hands smoothing a sheet. The pressure of palms on mud you saw in the bricks - that *was* palms. Soft, broad, sequential presses of living fringe. It holds the press for hours while the sun rises and bakes the new brick on its back. When the brick is cured, the animal inches forward - a millimeter an hour - and begins the next one. The seam you brushed away is the scar between one growth and the next. The courses that run for kilometers without wavering are not laid by many workers. They are the trail of one animal that has walked in a straight line for years.

And it feels. It has no eyes. Eyes would dry. It has no ears. Sound is nothing here. It reads vibration through the brick. The tick of a mud plate curling a mile away tells it that water has gone. The tremor of rain on a distant terrace tells it to turn toward the seep. That is why the plants are lonely and spaced by agreement: each reed clump, each succulent, each stunted tree marks where one of them has paused to drink and left a little more moisture and a little mucilage behind. The plants are not wild. They are weeds of the mason's road.

If you stay until the pool deepens to a few millimeters, you will see them converge. Not fast. Never fast. From each dry canal, a low, pale shape detaches from the wall where you thought it was wall. It glides - it flows - into the junction. Hand-sized ones first, trembling, their backs still dark with damp. Then the larger ones, their dorsal plates riding just above the water like flat boats, their frills undulating underneath, stirring the clay into a milky cloud which they then filter and eat. They do not fight over the water. There is a spacing, even in congregation. One, then a long emptiness, then another. They drink, they filter, they excrete a fine, perfect silt that will be the next brick.

If you put your hand in the water, they will not flee. They will touch you with the edge of that frill, tasting salt. The cilia are soft as wet velvet. And then, finding you too hot and too salty and not mud, they will go around you.

By mid-morning they are gone. Or rather - they have become gone. The water has thinned, the sun is white again. Each one has found its place in the course, settled its broad foot into the seam, and flattened. The mucilage dries. The frill thins into that dark line of mortar. The dorsal plate pales and hardens until you cannot find it again even when you mark where it was. The plain is brick again, sharp-edged, silent, endless.

Dig, if you want. Under the top plate you will not find earth. You will find flesh, cool and damp and yielding, waiting for night. Under that, another plate. Another animal. The world is deep with them, layer upon layer, a living continent that forgot to rise because rising was never the idea. The idea was to be flat, and wide, and patient, and to make more of itself out of mud and dew until the water decided to stay.

It is not hostile. It does not know you are there except as a vibration that passes quickly and means nothing. If you lie down in a canal at noon and stay very still, your sweat will bead on your back and run into the seam. An edge of frill, mistaking you for a cool plate, may feather against your ankle, sip, and leave a faint, pale, hand-shaped print on your skin that will dry and flake off like clay by evening.
