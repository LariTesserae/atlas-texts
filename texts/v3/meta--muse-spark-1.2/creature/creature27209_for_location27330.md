There is no one living in the Bowl the way someone lives in a house.

At first you count absence. You walk the soft street that used to be Main and you think: *abandoned*. Kettle left on a hot plate, still warm. A workbench with pliers open around a half-stripped wire, untouched for days except for the faint orange dust of spores settling in the jaws. A cot in the back of a brick workshop with a blanket kicked down to the feet because it's too warm to need it. Laundry strung on a catenary wire between two amber bulbs, shirts gone stiff with humidity, nobody coming to take them in.

Then you realize your scale is wrong. You're counting humans and this place doesn't.

### The Sparse Ones

There are humans. Few. Fewer than the buildings want.

Maybe a dozen. Maybe twenty when someone comes down from the surface and stays through a wet season and then decides to stay longer. Not a community in the way that word is usually used — no mayor, no shop, no schedule. A hamlet of custodians who never agreed to be custodians together.

They don't look like survivors. They don't look feral or hard. They look acclimated, which is different. Skin gone a little pale and a little green at the edges, not from sickness but from lack of direct sun and from the permanent damp that never quite dries. Hair always soft with condensation. Eyes used to low amber light, so they squint up at the oval mouth of the Bowl like it's too sharp. They speak quietly, because the Bowl is so loud. After a while your voice drops to just under the engine throb or you don't bother speaking at all. They nod a lot.

You can tell one by what they carry: rags that never dry, a tin cup hooked to a belt for dipping from the troughs, a flat-bladed knife not as a weapon but as a weeding tool. Their hands are stained. Rust won't wash off them anymore, neither will the violet-black juice of the fig-philodendron leaves when you have to cut one back from a belt. Their fingernails have a faint line of green under them always.

They don't own anything, even the buildings they sleep in. They *tend*. That's the word they would use if you asked, and you would have to ask a few times because they are not quick to explain themselves. They oil the flywheel that doesn't need oiling anymore because the root through it keeps it from seizing. They clear the felt of static-seed from the generator cages so it doesn't spark and catch. They ladle fish out of the black cistern and put a few back. They do not farm — the Bowl feeds them without being farmed. Bananas that were never bananas, big starchy pods off the feral engineered trees, tubers that swell where a root hits warm concrete, the blind fish if they want protein, and always, always water that tastes of iron and leaf.

They sleep shallow and not all at once. Someone is always awake because something is always dripping, hissing, opening. Their sleep is stitched into the Bowl's breathing. They nap in hammocks strung between root-pillars, or on the felt-matted roof of the mill where it's warmest, with the bats pouring out under them at dusk. They don't really live alone and they don't really live together. You might not see another human for a whole day and then find three of them sitting on upturned crates by the condensers, not talking, just watching the water bead and run.

Children are rare. When there are children they are quiet in a way surface children never are. They learn to walk on root-floor that gives under their feet. They learn not to swat. They learn that if you stand still long enough on the old main street, the cloud of iridescents will decide you are a pillar and move around you.

If you stayed, you would become like them by degrees. First your clothes would never fully dry. Then you would stop minding the noise. Then you would stop checking the time.

They are not the inhabitants. They are the most recent ones.

### The Air That Is Inhabited

The real population is the air itself.

You thought the Bowl was empty because you were looking at ground level. Look up. Look at the space between the root-pillars that hold the vault open like a church.

It is occupied to saturation.

The midges move as a single weather system, a low cloud that tightens and thins around the warm bulbs. The beetles — lacquered, green-black, the size of a thumb — carry gardens on their backs. Living moss, deliberate, cultivated by mites they farm. When they hit the sodium lights they click and fall and are instantly taken by ants. The moths are too big, dusty as hands, and they have learned to ride the slow swing of the catenary wires instead of fighting it.

At the dimming hour — when the column of sun thins and the amber lights become the sun — the bats come out. Not from cracks in rock, but from inside the roots themselves. The big aerial roots are hollow now, warm chambers that pulse faintly. You can put your hand on one and feel the heat of a dozen bodies breathing inside. They peel out in a soft pour, hand-sized, leather-winged, and the swifts cut through them from above, diving down the light column like stitches.

Everything that can sing does, all at once, and never stops. Cicadas saw until your skull vibrates. The translucent-throated amphibians in every trough and pipe-joint sing a round that has no beginning — *koonk koonk kreek* — over and on top of the sixty-cycle hum and the clank of the condensers and the slow sigh of belts turning. The birds — not many species, but many individuals — shriek territorial arcs that echo off the sheer concrete and come back doubled. The rustle is constant, papery, a thousand leaves the size of doors shifting a degree toward the work lights.

They live *in* the town, not around it. A rat watches you from a girder with perfect composure, because the girder is its branch. The heron stands in the cistern on legs like rebar because that cistern is its estuary now. A line of ants, each as long as the last joint of your finger, carries pale leaf cuttings along the factory conveyor in a procession so orderly it looks like work. It is work. They use the belt because it moves.

You are the sparse thing there. They are the city.

### The Green That Became Infrastructure

And then there is what lives there that you cannot count at all because you keep trying to separate it from the building.

The vegetation is not cover. It is the resident that ate the premise of residency. The figs and philodendrons and the third thing that was designed to be both — fast-growing, waxy, engineered for biomass and then left to argue with itself for a century. It learned.

It learned to be architecture. The roots that came down like pillars from the rim hit the floor and kept going, became floor, became wall, became the way you get from one workshop to another. Vines thick as fire hose traced the catwalks because the catwalks were the straightest line to light, and now you can't tell where steel ends and vine begins. In the mill, the sawtooth roof holds soil on its glass and grows a meadow upside down, roots dangling in the dim like chandeliers.

It learned to be machine. That is the part you acclimate to too fast. A trumpet vine that opens one white flower per piston stroke — *pff* — on the compression, pollen puffed in time with the engine. The giant stomata-leaves that track amber instead of sun, opening and closing on belt-driven shafts. Roots grown *through* flywheels, phloem around iron, steel and wood fused without fracture. Seeds like cotton that love static and felt themselves to generator cages and spark faintly, living insulation. The plant does not use the factory. The factory is a trellis it has incorporated.

It drinks the same water the humans drink and the fish drink — condensation beaded on every cool pipe, dripped on a metronome, gathered in troughs, steamed off the warm engines, condensed again. It drops a leaf, beetles pulp it, it becomes soil on the roof, a new leaf comes. It breathes so heavily the air tastes sweet and metallic.

If you press your palm to a root-pillar you feel warmth that is not sun-warm and not engine-warm. It's both.

### The Hum

Nothing here lives alone. That is the final law.

The rust lives — a bacterial sheen that eats iron and exhales that faint perfume of blood and ozone. The film on your cup of water in the morning is alive. The concrete sweats and the sweat is alive. The heron eats the blind fish that eat the larvae that eat the leaf litter that was made by the light that is half sun and half buzzing amber.

The Bowl itself lives, not as metaphor. A hamlet-sized hollow with edges you can always see, warm, damp, humming, weeping for two days after it rains up top and then going back to steady transpiration. It holds heat like a body. It breathes through that ragged oval mouth. It keeps time with pistons and flowers and frog throats all layered until you stop hearing noise and start hearing a single, continuous note.

The people who live there understand this without saying it. They don't maintain the Bowl so it will work. They move through it the way blood moves through a heart that was already beating.

You don't find the edge between who lives there and where they live. There isn't one anymore.
