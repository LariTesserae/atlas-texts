They do not live there the way people live in a city.

They inhabit it the way water inhabits a cup — by taking its shape completely, and by having no idea that the shape was made for something else.

Nothing here remembers being built for air.

### The Meadow

First, what lives there is not an animal at all, at least not as you would count it.

It lives as a skin.

On every horizontal and inverted surface — under every lintel, in the dished center of every stair, in the mortarless seams between basalt slabs where the stone sweats its heat — there is a felt. It is cream-white where it is thin and young, sulfur-yellow where it thickens, sometimes flushed a faint rose where iron blooms out of the rock. It is soft as velvet to the very slow touch, and it comes off on your finger if you brush it, like cold-cream, like silt that decided to be alive.

This is the meadow. This is the field. This is the wheat and the pasture and the forest floor. It has no leaves because it has no need for light. It eats the stone's own breath — hydrogen, sulfur, methane seeping up through the warm basalt, the mesa exhaling through ten thousand invisible pores. It grows against the current, a millimeter in a season, a centimeter in a year you would not live long enough to measure. It is everywhere that is not scoured by a current, and because nothing here moves fast, that is almost everywhere. If you killed everything else on the mesa and came back in a century, it would still be there, thicker.

It lives by being still. It lives by being eaten.

### The Grazers

So then, what lives there are the mouths that keep the meadow short.

They are small, and they are numberless, and that is why you do not see them at first. You look for a fish and miss the city wall moving.

The shrimp come first because there are most of them. They are not pink like the shrimp you know. They are clear. Glass shrimp, ghost shrimp, with a gut that shows as a dark line and a back that carries six or seven pinpricks of cold light — not light to see by, just enough to find each other in the dusk. The light is chemical, tired, the color of spoiled milk. Each animal is the length of your thumb joint, and there are thousands of them on a single paving slab, shoulder to shoulder, legs working with the metronomic patience of weavers. *Click. Click. Click.* Forelegs combing the bacterial velvet, mouthparts scraping, legs passing food back. They do not scatter when you pass. To scatter would waste energy. If you push your hand through them, the cloud parts slowly around you and then closes again behind you, unhurried, resuming the same slab as if you had been a brief current. They will spend a full day on one stone before the felt is thin enough that the stone's grey shows through, and then they will advance together, a front, to the next stone. You could watch them and think they are the stone's own texture until the texture moves.

Among them, slower still, are the isopods. They are wrongly sized for this place — as big as loaves of bread, as big as slates — domed, pale, blind, the color of old ivory left in water. They huddle in the lee of walls, under the eaves where houses overhang alleys, seven or eight to a doorway that was once for people. They are not huddled for warmth; the water is always warm. They are huddled because the felt grows thickest where the current cannot reach, and they are patient enough to wait for it. Their antennae — two long white whips — wave constantly, tasting the water for sulfur, for the wake of shrimp, for the approach of the larger things. They do not walk so much as they *creep*, each jointed leg finding a purchase in a chisel mark left by a bronze tool ten thousand years ago. You can hear them if you are very still: a faint dry rustle, like paper being smoothed, even though they are underwater. When they molt they leave behind a perfect, translucent house-husk in the doorway, and the shrimp eat it within an hour.

On the vertical surfaces, the spirals live. Small gastropods, white and thin-shelled, so delicate the warm water is already trying to dissolve them even as they build. They lay down their whorls one desperate crystal at a time, fighting dissolution with sheer persistence. They do not move. Or they move so slowly you would need to mark their position with a grain of sand and come back tomorrow. Their lives are on the wall, their trails are faint clean lines in the bacterial felt where they have licked it away. Every now and then one loses its grip and *tok* — you heard that sound — falls to the pavement and lies there, and a shrimp will be on it before the sound has finished traveling.

### The Shoal

What lives there in the open — in the alleys and courtyards where there is room to be above the floor — are the fish.

They are not fish as you picture fish. They have no color. No eyes. Or rather, they have eyes, but the eyes are skin-covered, pearls under a membrane, useless in a place where light never comes. Their heads are blunt, their mouths underslung and constantly open, filtering. They are the length of your hand, pale as the inside of your wrist, and you can see the faint outline of spine and gut through the flesh. They move not as individuals but as a single, slowly warping sheet, twenty or thirty together, turning in the alleys with a synchrony that takes minutes to complete.

They do not swim. Swimming implies a push, a hurry. They *drift* with a correction — a flick of a translucent tail, a leveling of pectoral fins as thin as wet paper. They ride the breath of the mesa, the one-inch-per-hour upwelling of warm water that rises from the seams between slabs. To watch them is to feel your own breathing slow down to match. They turn at the parapet — always at the parapet — as if the low, rounded wall were a reef drop. The shrimp will not cross it. The fish will not cross it. Something in the water beyond the edge — colder, emptier, a down-current that pulls — tells them *this is the end of the world*. So they run the circuit of the city, forever, alleys to plaza to alleys again, sieving the dust that hangs in the water column: shed bacterial skins, larval clouds, fecal snow so fine it never settles. They open and close, open and close, and the water passes through them and they are fed.

Their young are not born here. Or rather, they are, but you would not recognize them. The water over the courtyards glitters, if you let your eyes unfocus, with what looks like motes. Copepods, larvae, things with too many temporary legs and no purpose yet, hanging in the warm thickness like dust in a sunbeam that has no sun. They are the glitter. They are food and future at once. Most will be filtered by the shoal. A few will find a doorway and hide.

### The House-Dwellers

And then — what lives there that *uses* the houses as houses.

They are longer than the fish, as long as your arm from fingertip to elbow, and the color of the stone itself, a mottled grey that is not camouflage so much as kinship. You could call them eels, you could call them salamanders that never left the water. They have four stumpy limbs they do not walk on, and a flattened head with external gills like pale ferns that wave without hurry, and a lateral line so sensitive they feel a shrimp's click from three houses away.

There is one per house, sometimes two. They have taken the rooms. If you hover at a doorway — and you can hover, the water lets you hang motionless with a single slow exhale — you will see the tail first, thick as a wrist, disappearing into the dark where a hearth once was. They lie curled around the interior corners, noses tucked into the bacterial felt that is thickest inside, where no current has scoured it for millennia. They do not hunt the shrimp. They farm them, in a way without intention. They graze. The same slow scrape, but with a larger mouth, and then they rest for hours, digesting heat. When they move, they move with a whole-body undulation that stirs the dust-motes and sends the shoal warping gently away — not in fear, just in displacement, like a curtain moved by someone passing behind it. They go from house to house, nosing each threshold, tasting who has been there. They know every doorway. They have their favorites. Some houses they never enter, for reasons you cannot see — a colder seep, a thinner felt.

They are blind as well, but less completely. If you shine no light — and there is no light to shine — you will sometimes see them pause and tilt their heads toward the faint chemiluminescence of the shrimp, not to see it but to feel the slight warmth of its chemistry. They lay eggs like strings of pearls on the ceilings of the interior rooms, in the cistern that is now every room, and they guard them by simply being there, large and slow and in the way. The isopods give those rooms a wide berth.

None of them are fast. None of them can afford to be. The water is too warm, holds too little oxygen for sprinting, and there is nowhere to sprint to — the world ends at the parapet. Predation here is not a chase but an encompassing. If a fish weakens and sinks to the pavement, the shrimp will cover it within minutes, and the house-dweller will find the aggregation an hour later and swallow what is left, shrimp and all, with a slow peristaltic gulp that you could miss if you blinked.

### The City as Animal

If you stand long enough at that knee-high parapet and look back at the faintly glowing hamlet — the courtyards holding their dim pools of shrimp-light, the alleys like veins of a little warmer darkness, the roofs unmoving — you realize what lives there is not any one of these things.

What lives there is the *density* itself. A place where every surface is either meadow or mouth, where stone has become food and food has become stone again, where a city built for lungs and feet has been re-tiled by soft bodies that fit its chisel marks exactly. The isopod fits the doorway. The shrimp fits the paving seam. The fish fits the alley width. The salamander fits the house. They have lived here so long — grain by grain, scraping by scraping — that evolution has planed them to the city's dimensions as surely as the water planed the stone.

They do not know it was ever dry. They do not need to. They keep it swept. They keep it polished. The city will not fall because they will not let it be forgotten by the water. The felt holds the stone against dissolution; the grazing keeps the felt from smothering itself; the filterers keep the water clear enough for the felt to taste the seep.

It is a small world, and it is full to bursting, and it is held. The warm water holds the fish, the fish hold formation, the walls hold the water back from spilling over the abyss, and the parapet holds them all from wandering off the edge into the true dark where nothing grows.

Listen long enough and you hear it breathe: shrimp click, gill-fern sigh, shell *tok* on basalt, dust settling. That is its pulse. That is whatever lives there, living.
