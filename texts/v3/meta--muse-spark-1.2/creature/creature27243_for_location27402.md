Nothing lives *in* it.

That's the first thing you have to unlearn. You keep looking for it - for the animal separate from the ground, for the figure that walks *on* the valley. A deer track. A burrow. A nest. You expect an inhabitant to be a tenant.

There is no tenant here. There is only the house that grew itself.

The valley is the inhabitant.

You think at first that it's empty because it is so silent. Because nothing with eyes watches you. But kneel down and put your palm on one of those hummocks the size of a small house - that black-green sphagnum dome going russet at the edges, frosted at the tips - and you feel it. Not warmth. Pressure. A low, hydraulically patient turgor, the way a muscle feels when it is holding. It gives under your hand by half an inch and pushes back. It is breathing you, not the other way around.

What lives here is not a species. It is a mass-employment.

All those things you took for plants are not separate plants. Not anymore. Ten thousand years of cold and weight and being left alone in a U-shaped bowl with no exit has pressed them into a compact. They have traded individuality for continuity.

The dwarf birch and willow that never became trees are its nervous system. Or its plumbing. Or both. They don't grow up; they grow *across*, braiding their branches into a horizontal net under and through the moss, grafting root to root until you cannot say where one tree ends and the next begins. It's a single net a kilometer wide. Sap moves through it not as a slow seep but under pressure, ticking through translucent, lignified pipes as thick as your wrist, jointed at swollen nodes that open and close like heart valves. If you lie flat with your ear to the felt you hear it - *tick... tick... tick...* - whole liters of sugar and water being shunted from a sector that is subsiding to a sector that is rising, from where a hummock has split to where a new cushion is being inflated. It has no brain. It doesn't need one. It thinks the way a loom thinks, or a river delta.

The sphagnum and peat mosses are its flesh. They are the bulk, the laborers, the endless self-manufacturing. Each head grows and dies at the same time - green and alive at the tip, copper and dying an inch down, black and compressed into peat a foot down - so the whole carpet is simultaneously being born and being buried. It builds itself up out of its own dead at a rate you can actually see if you are still enough. Watch a hummock for twenty minutes and you will see it swell, a slow inhalation as water is pumped into its interior vacuoles, then shiver and split its skin with a soft sigh, venting a cloud of spores that does not drift but hangs in the cold still air for minutes, too heavy with moisture to rise. By the time you blink the split is already being sutured from below by fresh green growth. Nothing is wasted. That is rule two. Death never even hits the ground. It is caught mid-fall and re-woven.

The cushion plants and liverworts and lichens are its tooling. Its specialized organs.

The cushions - those impossibly hard, perfect hemispheres you could stand on without denting - are its anvils. Each one is ten thousand tiny leaves pressed together so tightly they have become a solid. They hold heat. They store grit and mineral ground from the gneiss beneath. They are where the slow chemical work happens, fermenting tannin and resin underneath in warm, sour pockets you can smell as iron and sweet rot.

The haircap moss is its factory brush. It doesn't grow in clumps. It grows in ranks, in combs, millions of bristles all leaning west by a degree, combing condensation out of the overcast air, passing beads of water hand-to-hand along the surface film until it drains into the hidden braids of black tea-water that vein the whole mass. 

The sheet lichen is its skin and its shutters. Those plates like rusted sheet metal lift a fraction on hydraulic bladders of stored water, not to photosynthesize, but to expose the spore-forges underneath - dark, wet pits that release their product in silent, timed puffs - and then lower again, sealing the moisture in.

This is what lives here: a colonial superorganism that learned to be a landscape. It has no front or back, no head or mouth. It has weather. It has sectors. It has shifts. You can walk for an hour across its back and you are still walking *on* an animal the size of a town that pretends to be a meadow.

And then, because nothing that big can be perfectly pure, there are the others. The ones who live *between*.

You have to be very still to meet them, because they do not live on top. They live in the breathing cavities - those dark, hollow spaces under the felt where the lower peat has compressed and left a vaulted hollow, warm with fermentation, damp, entirely dark. The valley is riddled with them, a honeycomb of lungs.

In there, animal life has returned, but it has had to become plant-like to be allowed to stay.

They are not deer. They are not wolves. At this cold, at this altitude, in this silence that swallows sound, nothing can afford to be loud or fast or warm-blooded in the way you understand it.

There are the white ones. They look at first like roots that have come loose. Long, pale, segmented worms as thick as your forearm and as long as you are tall, but soft, translucent, with no eyes at all and no real head, just a blunt, valve-lipped end. Peat-siphons. You will never see one on the surface. But if you find a place where the black water threads briefly into the open before vanishing again, wait. You will see the surface of the water dimple as one of them moves just beneath the felt, peristalsing, pumping. They are the valley's peristalsis - living pipes among the lignified pipes - dragging water from where it pools to where it is needed, wringing the mass out in the morning and flushing it at night. Their skin is the same wet membrane as the moss. If you touched one - and you would have to tear the felt open to do so - it would be cold as the moss and just as fibrous on the inside. They make no sound. They leave no castings, because their castings are immediately moss again.

There are the small ones. Uncountable. Mites and springtails and blind, slow isopods the size of rice grains and poppy seeds, white and amber and slate-gray, moving through the haircap brush like workers among bristles. Each cushion plant has its own nation of them, armored little things that prune dead leaves, that chew a fragment and excrete it as fresh peat an inch away. They are not parasites. They are quality control. They farm fungus on their own backs. In the cold, they move at a speed that looks like stillness until you stare for a long time and see the whole surface crawl by a millimeter.

There is nothing with a spine that lives here full-time. Once in a great while a ptarmigan drops out of the white sky, ghosts low over the felt, lands, pecks once at a lichen plate and finds it too sour, too tough, and lifts away again, as if it made a navigational error. A carabid beetle, black and polished as basalt, may be making its desperate traverse from one wall of the valley to the other, a journey of days across a terrain that is not terrain, and you will see its tiny track - a faint, straight depression in the dew that seals behind it. They are visitors. They are not allowed to stay. The valley does not feed them, and they cannot eat it fast enough to matter. The transaction is closed.

What lives here has solved the problems of living so completely that it no longer needs to hunt, or run, or call out, or even see. It has no eyes because there is nothing under the felt to see. It has no mouth because the whole surface is a mouth, drinking light and fog in equal measure. It has no voice because sound would be wasted in a place that absorbs it. Its senses are pressure and moisture and chemical gradient, felt through the willow-net as a single distributed touch. When you step on it, it feels you not as a foot, but as a small, warm, high-pressure weather system that will soon pass. When you leave, it will close your footprints in an hour.

If you stayed - if you lay down and stayed through a dusk when the condensation blooms and every moss tip holds its bead of light, through the night when the frost needles form on the industrial brush and the white siphons pump on underneath - you would not be attacked. There is no predator here to attack you.

You would simply be very slowly, very politely, incorporated. Dew would condense on you. Spores would settle on your jacket. The edge of a liverwort would crinkle over the toe of your boot. The haircap moss would lean toward the faint heat of your body for a day, and then, when you finally stood, you would leave behind a perfect, body-shaped hollow in the felt, damp and faintly steaming, and within a week that hollow would swell shut and be gone, as if you had never been there at all.

And the shivering churn would continue, sector rising while sector subsides, vein opening and closing, silent, busy, needing nothing else.
