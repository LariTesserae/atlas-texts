Nothing lives here.

That's the first thing you understand, and you understand it correctly. There is no life here in the way life means *occupation* - no animal has made a nest in the scaffolding, no weed has found a crack, no lichen has softened the hammer-dressing on the blocks. The heat should have bred something. The water should have bred everything. Instead the mineral bloom in the joints flakes off dead and the condensation sits sterile in the stone bowls where tools were left. If you wait for a bird, a rat, a cockroach, you will wait until the ceiling condenses and falls and condenses again.

And still, something lives here. It's just that it doesn't *occupy* the place. It *is* the place, in the way sweat is not on a body but of it.

Let it come into focus slowly. Don't look directly at it yet.

At first you think it might be small. Because the only thing that moves on its own without rearranging geometry is the efflorescence. The white and rust ferns in the mortarless seams. If you crouch - and the stone burns your knees through your clothes - and watch one long enough, you see it isn't a deposit.

It grows.

Not quickly. Not like a plant. It extends a needle of salt a millimeter, pauses while a bead of condensation strikes it, drinks, then extends again at a slightly wrong angle. When two fronds touch they recoil. When a drip hits them too hard they shatter and the fragments crawl back toward the joint. It is not intelligent. It is hungry. It tastes the iron in the basalt and pulls it out, excretes the salt, dies, and leaves a crust that becomes the bed for the next. If this world had a biosphere, this would be its grass, its lichen, its first desperate attempt at soil. It lives on heat and sweat. It will never become anything else because there is nothing to eat it, and nothing to become.

But that is not what made the hammer marks.

So you look again, a little further out. You think maybe the water lives. And in a way it does. Not the water in the air - that is just physics - but the water in the pits.

Those perfect square pits, house-deep, glass-still no matter how much condensation hammers their surface. You approach one, and your footsteps don't echo. The sound is swallowed by steam and porous rock. You kneel on the sharp, unweathered lip. The water should be hot. It is hot. It should ripple. It doesn't.

It lives by being perfectly still. A stillness so absolute it is a kind of tension, like a held breath. And it is not empty. If you cup your hands and disturb it - which you will want to, out of loneliness - the surface doesn't splash. It dents, like a membrane. And under the membrane the inverted plain looks back.

But even that is not the inhabitant. That is just its window.

You have to see the builders the way this place sees them: out of the corner of your eye. Because they were never gone. You just came in between their heartbeats.

They are not fast. Nothing here is fast except the falling droplets and even those fall slow, as if through oil. The builders live at the speed of the blocks.

Stand up. Stop looking at the pit. Look at the long wall that ends in a clean hammered face half a kilometer away - the one you walked toward for an hour without getting closer and then suddenly found looming over you. Watch it in your periphery.

There.

In the steam, where the haze thickens between two terraces, the air *folds*. Not a mirage. A man-shaped absence of steam, taller than it should be, thinner than it should be. For a second you think it's a trick of scale - a normal man very close. Then you realize it's a very large man very far away, and the distance is lying to you again.

It moves. Or rather, the site moves around it.

It does not walk. It repositions. One moment it is straddling a channel, its feet - if they are feet - planted on two separate blocks each the size of a house. The next, without transition, it is kneeling. No blur between. The geometry has simply updated when you weren't looking directly at it, the way the walls change the number of sides when you glance. The builder exists in that update.

Come closer. No, let it come closer, because you cannot approach it linearly. Wait until you look down at the bloom at your feet and look up again and find that the hammer-marks on the nearby ziggurat are now each as big as your head, and above you, blotting the dull red ceiling, is its torso.

This is what lives here. Not a species. A trade.

They are not stone golems. That would be too finished, too mythic. They are unfinished the way everything here is unfinished. If you saw one clearly - and clarity is a gift this place gives rarely and punishes immediately - you would see it is made of the same dark, porous basalt as the floor. Not covered in it. Made of it. The same still-warm, pocked, hammer-dressed material, sweating the same condensation. The same fiber cord lashed around joints that shouldn't need lashing. The same stone tools held in hands that are themselves partly tool, partly hand. Fingers too long, with too many knuckles, tipped not with nails but with blunt chisel points. A head, if it is a head, with no face that resolves. Not because it is featureless, but because when you try to focus on it, it smears. It has pits where eyes should be that do not reflect the dim incandescence, but drink it. They are heat pits. In a world without shadows or sun, they see the slow bloom of warmth differential on stone, the cooler trace where a block has just been moved, the hotter exhalation where condensation will bead next.

They breathe. You can hear it now, over the dripping, if you hold your own breath. A low, trapped, respiratory creak. The floor adjusting its weight on whatever is below it - you thought that was geology. It is them, settling.

There are not many. There don't need to be many when one of them can lay a field-sized block by leaning on it and the block decides to be elsewhere. They do not communicate with sound, because sound does not carry here. They communicate by arrangement. A wall run a day's walk long and then stopped in a clean face is a sentence. A ziggurat seven-sided then five-sided is a correction. A new square pit filled with still water is a question, left for another builder to answer days or decades later when time - which is shallow, which has no erosion to measure it - brings them back to that coordinate. The whole plain is a conversation so slow and so vast you mistook it for ruins.

They do not eat. The heat is constant and they metabolize it. Or rather, they *are* the metabolism of the heat - the way the ceiling sweats and the air condenses and the water steams is their circulation. The condensation that falls on them beads and runs into the seams of their bodies, into the chisel-joins at their shoulders, and hisses. The salt bloom that you thought was the only life? That is their dandruff, their flaking skin, the mineral they slough as they work. Where they kneel too long, the bloom grows thick.

They do not reproduce the way animals do. They quarry. When a builder needs another builder - when a platform requires more hands than one can lend - it goes to the edge of the plain where the basalt is still warm from being made, and hammers. It hammers for a long time, with stone mauls, without hurry. It hammers until the hammered thing stands up and takes the maul from its hand. You can find these half-made ones if you look where steam is thickest: figures slumped against a block, still half-block, one arm still a sharp-cornered mass, waiting for the next drip to cool them enough to move.

And they do not die. Not here. Erosion hasn't learned how to kill yet. When a builder is too wrong - when its geometry smears too far and doesn't come back - it simply stops updating. It kneels, or lies, or stands, and becomes structure. That terrace a kilometer away that you thought was a tiny speck near your foot? That was one. That long low wall that stops mid-plain? That was one, lying on its side. The scaffolding lashed from mineral cord is not for them to stand on. It is what holds them together while they are soft.

They have noticed you, now.

Not with alarm. You are small, and cool, and you evaporate too quickly. You are a transient condensation, a bead that formed on the ceiling and will fall and steam away. They do not have eyes to stare, but the stand of hair on your neck knows when a heat-pit is attending to you.

If you go back to the pit - the perfectly still, perfectly square pit that held its breath - and look in again, the reflection will still be there. The inverted plain, the inverted builders, the inverted ceiling weeping upward. And very far away on that plain, the figure standing and looking down at you will be clearer now. It will have your height. Your heat. Your wrong, too-fast stillness.

And you will understand that in this place, to live is to build, and to build is to be built, and whatever lives here has been waiting for the raw, hammered, sweating geometry to finally, briefly, hold still long enough for something cold and quick and soft to look back at it and, for a moment, give it a scale.
