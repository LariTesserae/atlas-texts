They live here because nothing else can afford to.

At first you think nothing lives here at all. That's the trick of density 1.0 on a scale of 2.8. The plateau is so vast it dilutes even abundance. You can walk for an hour across the wind-planed pavement, your boots finding no purchase on the frost-polygons, and see only stone and the dirty-coin sun and the lichen growing *into* the rock. Then your eye catches a mistake in the perspective two kilometers away — a low ridge that wasn't there when you looked before. It has moved ten meters while you weren't paying attention. And then you see that the horizon is not a horizon, it's a line of them.

This is not fauna as ornament. This is fauna as geology. As industry. There is no distinction left between an animal and a process.

### The Millbacks

They are the most numerous, and the reason the world reads as Industrial despite having no buildings.

Imagine a mining haul truck stripped of its cab and given a metabolism. Low, broad, 8 to 12 meters long, 3 meters at the shoulder. Not reptilian, not mammalian — arthropodal in the deep, old sense: a domed back of overlapping plates, not shell but chitin mineralized with the very iron they eat, so their color is exactly the plateau's color — rust-black, banded iron brown, shale grey. They discolor further as they age, redder. They move on twelve to sixteen pillar feet, each foot ending in a broad, felted pad that spreads their weight so perfectly they leave no print on the hard ground. That's why you hear nothing. Even at five meters away, a three-ton Millback passes like a shadow.

Their heads are down. Always down. A blunt, under-slung mouth opens into a radula of black magnetite teeth that does not chew so much as plane. They ingest the top millimeter of the world: the black crust lichen, the wind-blown grit, the iron shale itself. You can see the pale scrape behind one, a fresh track a meter wide where the rock is a shade lighter, damp. It will take decades for the lichen to recolonize it.

Inside — if you kneel beside one on a windless day and put a gloved hand to its flank — it is warm. Not hot. Warm the way a compost pile is warm. A deep, muffled churn, a wet heat, a *tick* of contracting metal. Their gut is a low-temperature blast furnace. Symbiotic bacteria reduce iron oxides without fire, using hydrogen from the interstitial water they siphon from seep-pans. They breathe out through lateral vents — not smoke, but a plume of steam barely warmer than the air itself. In this cold it freezes instantly into a wake of fine ice needles that fall behind them and vanish. 

And they extrude. Continuously, while moving. From the rear, a smooth, still-hot rod of pig iron, low grade, pitted, about 30cm thick, is pressed out like toothpaste. It hardens in the air, steams once, and lays behind them. It grows twelve, fifteen meters before its own weight snaps it. They leave it there. They do not look back. Over centuries those billets, all faintly magnetic, tug into alignment with the planet's field. From orbit they look like brush strokes, continent-long windrows of product, the plateau slowly covering itself in its own slag.

To stop is to die. Their guts must stay hot. Their chemistry stalls if they cool. So they walk. Always. A Millback will cross its home basin once every eleven years, turning slowly at the basin's eroded lip, coming back over ground its great-grandparent made. Dynamic 1.4 — so slow you have to time them against a ventifact to prove they move at all. About 40 meters an hour at a crawl. When they need to move faster they don't — they just keep crawling.

There are castes. The younger, darker ones are scrapers. The older, paler ones with scarred backs are refiners — they come weeks later and roll their bulk over a fallen billet, grinding it under their pads, re-ingesting it, adding manganese filtered from a damp pan, excreting a slightly purer bar. The refinement is generational. No single animal makes finished iron. The herd does.

### The Loomers

If Millbacks are furnaces, Loomers are mills.

You see them first as houses where no house should be. On stilt legs four to six meters tall, so thin they look impossible in the thin air, joints wrapped in grey cartilage, not bone. Their bodies are almost nothing — a narrow thorax, a head like a knot — but behind and around them they carry a frame made of their own translucent cartilage, strung with hundreds of spinnerets.

They harvest what little the plateau grows. The wire-lichen — those brittle black filaments that stand a few centimeters tall and shiver in the wind — and the wind itself. They walk perpendicular to the prevailing flow, and as they walk they spin. Not webs. Felt. Sheets of black carbon fiber, aerogel-light, extruded from the air and from digested lichen. It billows behind them in tattered banners ten, twenty meters long, snapping silently. The fiber catches on yardangs, wraps around tafoni hollows, drapes over billets. Most of it tears. It doesn't matter. Over centuries it builds a soil that isn't soil — a tattered insulation that holds a teaspoon of moisture, shelters larvae, feeds the millbacks when they eat it again.

Loomers are silent the way all of this is silent. The wind here is too thin to whistle through their banners. When two Loomers pass, they do not acknowledge each other except to slightly alter course so their sheets do not tangle. Their gait is so slow that from a kilometer you think they are standing still, until you look away and look back and the geometry has changed.

Under their frames, in the felt, lives the only thing you could call fast here: small, white, isopod-like detritivores the size of your hand that farm mold on the carbon sheets, that are in turn gleaned by juvenile Loomers. A whole economy riding on a loom.

### The Tankers

They explain the water.

In the shallows of the high basins where the permafrost sweats, black water wells up into pans the size of a doorway. Not a lake, just a sudden, cold mirror that will be gone in a week. Around its edge the olive algae blooms for three days, then crusts.

The Tankers find it before it vanishes.

They float. That is the strange part that quickly stops feeling strange — weirdness 1.1, not alien, just the logical endpoint of altitude. They are vast, blimp-like grazers, 6 meters long when empty, 10 when full, with a translucent hydrogen sac along their back that they heat with waste heat from their gut. They never quite touch the ground. They drift a meter up, held down by vestigial, trailing feet that barely brush the pavement. They descend on a pan and unroll a proboscis like a carpet and siphon it dry in an hour — water, algae, dissolved iron, everything — filtering it through mineral gills. They vent what they don't need straight up: a silent, vertical column of pure vapor, 30 meters high, that condenses and becomes the plateau's only cloud, a brief, low puff that precipitates as frost downwind. The water is not for drinking. It's for chemistry. The iron needs it. The bacteria need it. The plateau breathes through them.

When full, Tankers are too heavy to move quickly. They drift like low moons, distended, creaking faintly as their skin stretches — the loudest sound on the plateau, and still you have to hold your breath to hear it.

---

There are no predators. There are no prey. There are only stages in a refinery.

The lichen grows on cooling slag. The Millback eats the lichen and the slag together. The Tanker waters the lichen with distilled exhalation. The Loomer wraps the slag in fiber to keep it damp a little longer so the lichen grows faster. The small white isopods eat the Loomer's failed sheets and are eaten by the young of all three. Everything is secreted. Nothing is made the way a human makes something — with tools, with buildings. Everything here is extruded, precipitated, laid down while walking.

And that is why it is overwhelming. Fauna 2.6 does not mean you are surrounded. It means if you add up the tonnage — the iron moved per century, the water distilled per year, the fiber spun — the mass of animal outweighs the mass of all the thin, embarrassed vegetation a hundredfold. The plateau is empty the way a factory floor is empty at night: huge spaces between huge, slow machines, each working a shift that lasts a lifetime.

At what passes for dusk — the dirty-coin sun going a shade dimmer without ever setting properly — the cold deepens and frost begins to form again, first on the iron billets, then on the backs of the herds. Every billet left behind beads with condensation that freezes instantly, and for a few minutes the whole continent glitters, not with snow, but with industrial dew, a vast field of damp iron catching the last oblique light.

They keep walking through it. Quiet engines in the cold, felt-footed, hydrogen-buoyed, fiber-trailing, patiently manufacturing the ground beneath their feet. You stand there in the silence that has pressure, and finally understand that the landscape is not a place where these animals live.

The landscape *is* what these animals have made, and are still making.
