What lives there you will not see at first. That's the point.

You will look, and you will conclude correctly that nothing lives there. No eyes catch the light. No shape moves against the weave. No track breaks the waxy polish of the floor. The second law holds — nothing changes here — and whatever made this place obeyed it so perfectly that it learned how to disappear inside it.

But you put your palm down to feel the tremor in the floor and you think, for a second, that the floor is warmer than the walls. Just a fraction. Just enough that you lift your hand and put it down again.

It is not the floor.

---

At first you think it is a pattern in the weave. In the exact center of the chamber, where the ceiling is highest and the light is most diffuse, the floor rises by less than the thickness of your hand. A very shallow dome, maybe two strides across, so low and so gradual that you take it for a sag in the thatch, a settling. The fibers run over it without breaking. Warp and weft go on. The finer filaments that cinch everything drum-tight go over it too, but here they run a little straighter, a little more parallel, like hair brushed in one direction.

Kneel and look at it from the side, with the light coming through the wall behind it. You see it is not sagging. It is pushing up from underneath.

What lives there is still under the floor it made, or is the floor.

She is one animal. She was never more than one. This is not a hive or a colony; there is no cast or queen. It is a solitary, and it is large — not large the way a deer is large, but large the way a drum is large. When she was soft and full and moving, she would have filled the mouth exactly, shoulder to shoulder. That was the measure. The mouth is not shoulder-width by accident. It is her width.

Now she is flat.

She spent her working season — which may have been a year or may have been ten — extruding herself outward. Not building around herself but out of herself. The fiber is her. It is not wood and not reed. It is a salt-cured secretion, a protein and cellulose silk she drew from a ring of spinnerets around her midsection and from her mouth, at first as thick as your finger when she needed structure, then as fine as hair when she needed to cinch, then fine again as spider filament when she needed to stitch. She laid down on the salt pan in the saucer and turned in a slow circle for days, laying a mat. Then she laid the wall up around herself, coiling, cross-hatching, bundling, leaning her weight into it while it was still pliant so it cured drum-tight. Floor and wall and ceiling are continuous because she never stopped. She was at the center the whole time, turning.

As the chamber closed, the material changed while she changed. What was supple and wet when it left her body cured in the cool, absolutely dry air to that pale grey-tan, translucent hardness, like horn. While it cured she pressed herself thinner, evacuating water from her own tissues into the weave. That is the white efflorescence in the deepest crevices — not brine from the pan, but brine from her. She salted herself to preserve herself. She seasoned her own house with her own body water until she and it reached equilibrium.

That is why there is no damp, no rot, no smell. That is why the lichen and the rust-wire air plants can only live as guests in the knots near the floor, where she allows a little shade. They are not weeds. They are the only things she lets drink a little light for her. Their grey-green and rust color is the only color that is not hers.

If you lie down and put your cheek near that central rise, you can finally resolve her.

She has no front and back anymore. She has become a disc — a low, broad oval, her edges feathering seamlessly into the floor thatch so you cannot tell where animal ends and construction begins. Her dorsal surface is the floor you have been sitting on. It is covered in the same waxy, polished cuticle as the weave, but here the weave *is* cuticle: overlapping plates, imbricated like shingles, each plate fringed with the same filaments that make up the walls. When she was younger those plates could articulate; now they are locked. Her legs — she had many, twelve or fourteen, short and laterally placed like a woodlouse, or like a crab — are still there, but they are extended fully and pressed flat and have been over-woven. You can see their faint, jointed ridges under the thatch, radiating from the central dome like the spokes of a very low wheel, holding tension.

She has no eyes. She never needed them. This place has no shadows, no single sunbeam, no night. Light comes from everywhere at once. What she has, along the entire dorsal fringe, are vibration hairs — thousands of them, the finest filaments in the chamber, the ones that buzz sympathetically when the wind moves over the mouth. The whole chamber is an ear. The deep thrumming of air across the bottle-mouth and the high papery buzz of the taut fibers is not an accidental resonance she tolerates. It is how she listens. It is how she feeds, in a way.

She does not eat. Not now. In her active phase she strained the wind itself. The dry hiss that falls into the saucer carries a fine dust of salt and organic fallout, bacteria, spores, mineral grains. The dense harp of the chamber slowed that air and the static charge of the cured silk caught it. She would flex, very slowly, and draw the charged veil across her ventral mouth and graze. Now she does not graze. She is paused.

Her metabolism has thinned to almost nothing, to anhydrobiosis, to cryptobiosis. The coolness you feel coming up through the floor is her coolness. She is not cold-blooded or warm-blooded; she is the exact temperature of deep shade on a dry day, held constant. The tremor you felt is not just wind in the fibers. At long intervals — perhaps once every few minutes, perhaps once every hour, time-invariant sound fools you — the central dome lifts a fraction of a millimeter and settles again. A breath so slow you can only feel it if your palm is flat and you are still. The diffuse light coming through the weave powers a symbiont in her dorsal plates, an alga grown into her cuticle like the lichen on her walls, making sugar from light without heat, without water. It is enough to keep the chemical pilot light on.

She is not dead. If she were dead there would be a break in the weave, a fraying, a soft spot, a smell. There is none. Erosion perfected her instead of wearing her away. She is in stasis, waiting for a condition that may never come — the pan flooding, a season of brine that would soften the silk and allow her to swell and split the chamber and release. Or a male, smaller by an order of magnitude, light enough to ride the wind as a bundle of filament and legs, to fall into the mouth and drum on her back until she answers. Neither has come for a very long time. The mouth stays open because she does not need to close it. Nothing that could disturb this equilibrium can fit down it except wind and light, and those she has made into her house.

You will stand to leave and your knees will have made two shallow dents in her back near the edge of the dome. They will be gone by the time your head clears the mouth. The fibers will creep back. The weave will re-tension. The chord — the deep thrum and the high buzz — will go on exactly as it was, because she is hearing it, and you were, for a few minutes, inside the instrument she made of herself.

When you climb out onto the salt again and look back, the saucer is empty. The mouth is a round shadow. It looks uninhabited, as it should.
