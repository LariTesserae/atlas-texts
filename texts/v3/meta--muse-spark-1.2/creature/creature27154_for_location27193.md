No one lives there.

That's what you think at first.

You stand on that high braided platform under the vault and you look rim to rim across the basin — kilometers of green infrastructure steaming under ordinary white-gold noon light — and you think: *abandoned.* A city with avenues and plazas and aqueducts and second stories and no citizens. Pollen hanging in the air uncollected. Petals falling straight down and rotting where they land. No foot traffic. No voice. Just the roar of wood growing against itself.

Then you stand still a little too long and you feel a runner slide over the toe of your boot, and you understand you've got it backwards. It's not empty. It's full to bursting. It's just that the residents are the city.

There is no distinction here between inhabitant and habitation. No one moved in. No one built. The population *is* the architecture, and the architecture is alive.

### The Citizens

They are all plant. Only plant. That is the entire census.

But if you watch for an hour — if you let your eyes stop looking for animals and start seeing what is actually moving — the mass differentiates. Like any city, it has castes, professions, neighborhoods. Not because anyone assigned them, but because in a place this dense, this fast, every form of growth becomes a way of life.

**The Pillars** live in the center, where the vault lifts.

They are what you first took for trees. Figs and ironwoods and things with no name outside this basin, trunks thirty, forty meters tall, fluted, buttressed, their bark warm and tight like skin over muscle. They don't stand. They *hold*. They have decided, collectively, to be vertical. To be structure. Their crowns have grafted together into that interlaced roof-sky, filtering the daylight green but not dimming it. You can read by their shade. Pollen motes hang under them like dust in a cathedral.

They are the old ones only in a relative sense — a week older than everything else, which in this city is ancient. They groan constantly, a low nave-creak of wood under hydraulic pressure, water screaming up their xylem fast enough to hiss. You hear them splitting. Bark cracks open with a rifle-shot as they put on a handspan of girth in a minute, wet red wood showing, then sealing over before the echo fades. They compete by being still. That is their ethic: to stay, to thicken, to not be moved while everything else whips around them.

At their feet, in the straight avenues they leave between them, their children detonate.

**The Detonators** live everywhere, but they parade in the avenues.

They are the flowering bodies — not separate species so much as a temporary office any branch can run for. For hours a bud will swell, fat and closed and sweating resin, until the pressure inside becomes unbearable and it bursts. Not opens. *Bursts*. A soft concussive thump you feel in your sternum. A cloud of heavy, warm, golden spores hangs in the damp 30-degree air for a long minute before settling.

They are the reproduction, the gossip, the mail. The spores land on any damp surface — leaf-courtyards, trunk-cisterns, the back of your hand if you linger — and immediately throw out white thread-roots that tickle. Most will be dead by the time you brush them off, outcompeted, reabsorbed. A few will find a crack, a membrane-window, a fresh split in a Pillar's bark, and do what everyone here does: start growing, frantically, into whatever shape the gap asks for.

There is no bird to carry them. No bee. The plants have taken over pollination by ballistics.

**The Weavers** live in the walls and between the floors.

These are the vines, the aerial roots, the runners, the epiphytes the size of blankets. If the Pillars are the citizens who decided to be buildings, the Weavers are the citizens who decided to be *between* buildings. They are infrastructure as a lifestyle.

You see them as cables thick as your waist, screaming as they slide against each other, as plumbing along the colonnades, as shag curtains of root that drink vapor straight from the air. They never stop moving. While a Pillar groans in place, a Weaver whips a new length across an alley in the time it takes you to blink, fuses, flattens, becomes a new floor. The upper stories weren't built. They are Weavers that reached out horizontally for light, thickened, flattened, learned to be load-bearing. Balconies are just wrists of wood they exuded and left behind.

They make the city negotiable. An avenue that was straight when you looked down it is a loop when you look back because two Weavers on opposite sides decided to meet in the middle and shake hands. A chamber that had three Pillars now has five because Weavers dropped from the ceiling, rooted in mid-air, became Pillars themselves. They are the reason you can't map it. They are the reason gravity is a suggestion — they grow up as happily as down, and water will run along the underside of one of their leaves for meters before it remembers to fall.

They are also walls within walls. A translucent membrane of leaves stretched between two trunks is not a window someone hung. It's a colony of Weavers that agreed to be thin and let light through. An archway where two great roots almost meet is two Weavers that stopped growing at the precise moment they created a door.

**The Cups and Cisterns** live low and drink.

They are the broad-leafed ones, the ones that make the plazas. Leaves the size of courtyards, cupped and waxy, holding shallow pans of rainwater that wasn't rained but made — transpired by the vault itself until the air collapsed into warm grape-sized drops that hammer for twenty minutes at noon and vanish. Hollow trunks that hold dark, tannin-sweet water inside. Mats of moss that are not moss but a tight felt of seedlings waiting.

They are the wells, the reservoirs, the public baths. The whole basin drinks the air, and they are how. They sit heavy and damp and still, breathing out that cloying peppery smell of green sap and crushed flower, and they do not move fast. Their job is to be still enough to hold water. Every other citizen comes to them — a Pillar's roots will snake across a street to sip, a Weaver will dip a tendril. You can drink from them too, but never twice from the same one. By the time you come back it will have budded a lid, or become a mouth, or simply walked away on new growth.

And underneath and over everything, the **Sheath**.

That's what you call it after a while because you can't count it. The ten-deep overlap of leaves like shingles, the epiphytes on epiphytes, the seedlings that sprout on dying limbs before the limb has finished dying so that rot never touches the ground. It's the crowd. The density that makes you turn sideways to pass. It ensures nothing is ever single-use. The floor of one level is the ceiling of the next. A root descending through open air becomes a pillar before it lands.

It is always moving, always in light turbulence. Leaves unfurling with a papery snap that runs in waves down an entire street. Flowers detonating in sequence like applause. Wood splitting, fusing, splitting again.

That sound is their conversation. They don't have voices. They have hydraulics. The constant hiss of water being hauled up ten thousand throats at impossible speed, the groan of wood under its own acceleration, the soft thump of blooms — that's how they argue and agree about where the next street will go. If the roar ever stops, the old rule on the basin wall is right: *the air is about to change.* Something is about to transpire, literally, and make its own weather.

So who lives there?

A city that decided to be its own population. A forest that got so crowded and so fast and so warm and so wet with its own breath that it differentiated into urban professions without ever inventing an animal to fill them. Tens of thousands, if you count individuals, one, if you count the organism — the valley itself, a low, steaming, deafening, sunlit basin between raw young hills, building and unbuilding itself at the speed of thought, drinking the air, trying loudly and without hands to be a city, and succeeding.

It is not hostile. Hostility requires noticing you.

It simply has no concept of *animal* left. Every niche an animal would have held — carrier, builder, digester, shelter — is already held by a leaf, a root, a trunk, a vine that moves just enough to fill it.

That's why the last rule is the only one that matters, and it is not about them. It is about you:

Keep moving. If you stand still, you are not a visitor. You are a damp surface. You are substrate. And substrate, here, is always immediately inhabited.
