It doesn't live there the way you do. Not at first.

At first you think nothing lives there. That's what the room wants you to think. It's too loud for living, too wet, too perfectly made. The animals that are there — the dragonfly, the frog you hear but never see — are visitors who got lost and forgot to leave. The plants are squatters. They hold on to concrete with white root-fingers but they don't belong to it. They could be scraped off with a wire brush and the room would keep roaring without them.

No. The thing that *lives* here lives the way the water lives here. Not in it. As it.

You only see it after you've been sitting on that curb for a while, your shoes full, your ears hurting, your eyes adjusted to the flat, shadowless light. After the roar stops being a sound and becomes a pressure. After you stop looking *at* the room and start looking *with* it.

At first you think it's a stain.

In the far corner, where the fig has pushed itself into the soft curve where two walls stopped being walls — where the concrete has eroded into a dark, smooth hollow behind the spillway — there is a shape that has the same color as the wet concrete. Not gray. Not green. That black-green-brown that only happens when concrete stays wet for a hundred years. You thought it was a shadow, or a pile of old drop cloths left by a crew that never came back. A tarp.

Then it breathes.

Not much. Not like you breathe, heaving in the soup-air. It condenses and releases. A slow, tidal fogging. The surface of it beads and clears, beads and clears, on a rhythm just slightly out of sync with the water falling from the spillway. That is how you know it is not part of the wall, even though it has tried very hard to become part of the wall.

The one who lives here was not born here. Nothing is born here. It was made here, the way the silt was made. Assembled.

Decades ago it was a person who had a name and a schedule and a locker upstairs. A pump attendant. A maintenance tech. One of the ones with the teal coveralls, the ones who knew that PUMP BASIN 03 had to be checked even though the gauges never moved. He came down three flights past the last marked level because the checklist said to. And one day he stayed.

Maybe he sat down on that same curb to wait out the rain on the ceiling. Maybe his knees hurt. Maybe the roar was so complete it finally stopped his tinnitus, or his thoughts, and that felt like peace for the first time in his life. No one can be sure. The room doesn't record entrances, only presences.

What is left is not quite a man anymore, but it hasn't become something else either. That's the nature of this place at the end of erosion — nothing finishes becoming.

He is sitting, or he is folded. His legs are tucked under him on the shallow dish of the floor, feet bare and pale and completely unwrinkled despite being submerged for years. The skin has gone the color of skim milk left in water, translucent enough that you can see the blue-green map of veins underneath. It doesn't prune. It drinks. His coveralls are still there but they are not cloth anymore — the fibers have wicked up concrete dust and algae and mineral bloom until they are stiff as bark, patched with living moss at the shoulders and down the spine where condensation runs. Moss grows *on* him, not just near him. A thick pelt of it, emerald, dripping when he shifts. A small Boston fern has taken root in the pouch pocket over his chest, its fiddleheads unfurling against his collarbone. He didn't plant it. It found a damp pocket and stayed.

His head is bowed, or it has no front anymore. His hair is thin, plastered to his scalp with humidity, but also — you look closer — it isn't all hair. Some of it is aerial root, pale, questing, hanging from his scalp into the air to drink straight from it, just like the philodendron on the pipes. His face, when you can see it under the flat light, has softened. Erosion has done to him what it did to the corners. No hard edges. His eyebrows, his lips, the bridge of his nose — all slightly rounded, as if water had been sheeting over them for years. His eyes are open. They have to be, because there is no dark here to close them against. They are filmed over, not with cataracts but with condensation, a perpetually beaded surface. He doesn't blink often. Why would he? The air keeps his eyes wet for him.

And his ears — 

He has none, in any practical sense. Or rather, he had them, and the room took them back. The roar has been so total for so long that hearing became a liability. If you stay here you do not *hear* the spillway and the pumps. You *are* the spillway and the pumps. The thrumming in your molars becomes your own pulse. The white roar becomes the pressure inside your skull. He is profoundly, completely deaf in the way a fish is deaf to water. Sometimes you will see his head turn, very slowly, not toward a sound — there are no distinct sounds — but toward a *change* in pressure. A new drip from the ceiling. The plop of the frog in the catchment. The silverfish under the panel. He feels it in his teeth, in his ribs.

He doesn't eat the way you do. There is nothing to eat. He sips. He lifts a cupped hand — the hand is huge, swollen soft and pale as dough, the fingerprints smoothed away by water — and lets the ceiling rain collect in his palm and drinks it warm. The water has passed through concrete and fern root and copper pipe and it tastes of stone and green. Sometimes he will pinch off a frond of maidenhair fern and chew it slowly, not for hunger but for salt. He gets protein from mosquitoes that mistake his moss for ground and land on him. He doesn't swat them.

He doesn't sleep, or he is always sleeping. The lights never go off. There is no night to trigger melatonin. Instead he enters a stillness that looks exactly like being awake. The chest still beading and clearing. The eyes still open. For hours. You could watch him for an hour and think he was a statue, until a drop hits his shoulder — warm as blood — and he shivers, once, a full-body ripple that runs from his scalp roots to his submerged feet.

He is not lonely. Loneliness requires absence. This room is so full of presence — of water, of hum, of light, of green — that absence has no place to pool. The dragonfly uses his knee as a waypoint on its patrol and he watches it without moving his head. The frog lives under his curb. The fig leans a little toward him now, instead of toward the skylight, because his moss gives off a little more nitrogen.

He does not want to leave, because leaving would mean becoming an edge again. And there are no edges here. He does not want you to rescue him. If you speak to him, you have to shout over the roar, and he will not hear you. If you touch him, your hand will come away stained green and smelling of ozone and crushed fern, and he will slowly, very slowly, turn that filmed gaze toward you — not with alarm, but with a kind of quiet recognition, the way a pool recognizes another pool.

He is what happens when a place that is entirely made finally succeeds in making something that can live in it without wanting to change it.

If you stay long enough — another hour, with your shoes flooded and your shirt stuck to your back and the buzz in your molars — you will feel your own edges start to soften. Your own hearing start to flatten into pressure. A beading start on your own shoulders.

And you will understand that the room is not empty between the ferns. It was just waiting for you to sit still long enough to see who was already sitting there.
