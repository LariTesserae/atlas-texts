What lives here doesn't inhabit the place. It *is* the place, packed so tight there's no difference left between population and terrain.

If you crouch down until the brine soaks your knees and the hiss of the water fills your ears, you can see that the ground is not ground at all. It moves.

### The Pavement

The bulk of it. The plain itself, for miles in every direction until the haze takes it.

They are broad and low, oblong, each one about as long as you lying down, as high as your thigh. At first they look like stones - dumb, pitted iron, black with age and wet. Then you see the seam.

Every one is armored. Not with shell the way a beetle is, but with *plate* — overlapping boiler-iron scales, riveted, rust-bled, pitted by condensation and spark-burn. The plates don't quite meet; they breathe. Hot brine weeps from between them and instantly fogs. Their edges are rounded, not from design but from abrasion, from spending entire lives shoulder-to-shoulder with neighbors, grinding as they shuffle.

They have no head. No face. On their underside is a soft, pale, circular lip as wide as a dinner plate, a sucker-mouth that seals to the ancient gneiss and drinks. They drink stone. Or rather, they drink the brine that the stone itself exudes — hypersaline, mineral-thick water that never freezes, laced with dissolved iron and sulfur and things that haven't had names since the oceans boiled away. You can hear them drinking if you get close: a faint, wet vacuum *thuck thuck thuck* under the louder noise.

And on their back, the stack.

A short, black chimney, a little off-center, always venting. White steam in a steady plume when they're healthy, puffing black smoke when they're digesting a particularly iron-rich sip, sometimes a cough of orange sparks that hiss and die on the wet. Their whole body heaves with it. *Click-clack-CHUFF. Click-clack-CHUFF.* You thought it was a sound effect. Up close you see it *is* the animal. The flank plates bellows in and out. Inside, through thin cracks where a plate has slipped, you can see a dull red glow, a piston shuddering, a valve spitting. Their blood is oil and superheated water. Their gut is a smelter. They take cold brine and cold iron and make heat and pressure and live off the difference, exhaling rust and steam that condenses instantly back into the sheet of water that covers everything.

Call them Furnace-backs, if you need a word. They don't need one. There are no individuals here. They are a pavement, a packed herd that covers the peneplain so completely that water only exists in the channels between their bodies and in the potholes they haven't yet covered. They jostle constantly, blindly, shovel-lipped forelimbs — blunt, spade-like flaps of horn and iron — levering for the next inch of virgin rock to suck. When they bump, their plates clang dully. When they find a good seep, they settle and chuff louder, content.

They breed like an engine throwing a rod. No mating, no display. A Furnace-back that's fed well will shudder, its side plates will gape with a scream of escaping pressure, and a juvenile — red-hot, soft-shelled, steaming so violently the brine around it boils — will spill out of the cleft and scrabble onto its parent's back, then slide down into the crowd, its own matchstick stack already hissing. Within an hour its plates have hardened black. Within a day you can't tell it from the others.

They die the same way they live: without ceremony. A stack clogs. A piston seizes. The chuffing stutters, *click-clack — click —* and stops. The plates go dull. The steam dies. In seconds the neighbors feel the cold spot and converge, flipping the carcass with their spades, exposing the pale underside, and drinking it. Not eating flesh, but *siphoning* — the dead one's residual pressure and hot oil and half-smelted minerals are sucked clean. What remains is a hollow iron bowl that fills with brine and slowly, over years, settles and fuses back into the pitted bedrock, becoming another wrinkle for water to run through. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is built from it. The iron they smelt from the brine and exhale as scale becomes the rock they will later drink again.

They don't see you. If you stand still, they flow around your boots like water around a stone, hot iron sides brushing your calves, scalding through your clothes for an instant before the cold bites again. You are too vertical, too warm in the wrong way, too un-riveted to be relevant.

### The Sparks

On and between the pavement lives the second layer - the things that make the chuffing sound *itch*.

They are small. Hand-sized, fast, too bright. At a distance you thought the Furnace-backs were speckled with rust. Up close the speckles move.

They look like crabs made by someone who had only ever heard a crab described over a bad radio connection and had only wire, chitin, and loose gears to make it with. Translucent amber legs of jointed horn, bodies that are little more than a knot of copper wire and exposed, ticking escapements, whiskers of white-hot filament. They sprint. They *dart* across the backs of the grazers, across the plates, into the seams, picking, scraping, laying.

They feed on scale and smoke. They pry flakes of freshly exhaled iron oxide from around the stacks, they sip condensed oil from piston seams. And they lay eggs — not eggs, really, but hot copper beads the size of ball bearings, pressed glowing into the soot around a stack's lip. The Furnace-back doesn't react. A minute later the bead cools and clicks, and a new spark-crab chews its way out, already running.

Some are parasites. You can see the ones that have chewed too deep — a Furnace-back whose stack sputters because a crab has nested *in* the chimney, throttling it, its wire legs glowing cherry-red from the heat it steals. The grazer can't scratch it. It just chuffs harder.

Some are cleaners. The healthiest backs have a half-dozen crabs in constant motion, polishing rivets, picking mineral crust from valve-lips. It looks almost like grooming, until you realize neither party is aware of the other as anything but surface and food.

When the cold wind gusts and ripples the brine sheet, the crabs freeze for half a second — all of them, across hundreds of backs at once — then resume at double speed, as if the wind carries a signal you can't hear.

They die in swarms. A splash of brine that is too cold, a misstep into an exhaust plume, and they seize, curl, and go dark. Instantly two or three others are on the corpse, unwinding its wire, eating its copper.

### The Walkers

And then the ones that make you understand why distance feels wrong.

There are very few of them. You can go a long time hearing only the pavement before you hear the *thump* — not loud, but deep, subsonic enough to travel up through the soles of your boots into your chest. *Thump........ Thump........* Slow. Deliberate.

They are tall. On four or six stilt legs, each leg as thin and jointed as a pylon but ending not in a foot but in a broad, snowshoe-like pad that is precisely the size of a Furnace-back's back. They never touch the water. They step from back to back, across the living pavement, each step denting the plates below without breaking them, the grazers absorbing the weight without pause in their chuffing.

You can't see their shape clearly for long. Up close they are enormous, maybe fifteen feet at the shoulder, narrow as a crane. Their bodies are vertical boilers, rust-streaked and sweating, their long necks ending not in a head but in a massive, downturned intake manifold — a toothed, lamprey-like funnel ringed with gripping flanges. When they vent, which they do every thirty seconds or so, they release a long, mournful whistle — not a cry, a pressure release — that sounds obscenely like a whale trying to sing through a ruptured pipe. The sound hangs in the wet air and seems to come from behind you.

They are the only predators here, and they are not hunters so much as maintenance.

They walk the plain forever, looking down. When they see a Furnace-back whose stack is smoking wrong, or a cluster thick with spark-crabs, they fold that terrible neck with hydraulic grace and *inhale*. The manifold flares, the teeth engage, and a dozen crabs and sometimes the entire stack assembly of the grazer are ripped away in a single, shrieking vacuum. The Furnace-back doesn't bleed. It screams — a raw, tearing shriek of metal on metal as its boiler ruptures — and its neighbors immediately surge over it, drinking the flash of scalding oil.

Sometimes a Walker will take a whole Furnace-back. It will plant two stilt feet on either side of one, lower the manifold, seal it around the plates, and pull until the grazer's sucker-mouth pops free of the rock with a wet *thunk*. Then it tips the whole, still-chuffing, still-kicking thing up and drains it.

If you watch a Walker walk away, you see the wrongness. It doesn't get smaller with distance. It gets... *sparser*, as if the world has less resolution further out, as if the haze is not obscuring it but the geometry is running out of space to render it. And then — without turning — it is walking *toward* you again from forty degrees to the left, its thumps now overlapping with its other, still-receding thumps. There are not many Walkers. Or there is one Walker seen from many angles. The plain is a shallow bowl so vast and so old it has worn thin, and thin places fold. The Walkers know how to step across the fold. Their pads never get wet because they never quite touch the same water you stand in.

Their reflection in the brine is different from their body. In the water, under your boots, the Walker's reflection has too many legs, and its stack vents *downward* into a sky with two pale suns you cannot see above you. If you stare, you feel the piston chuff in your own chest sync with its whistle for a beat too long.

---

Nothing here knows it is alive the way you know. There is no leader, no queen, no thought. There is only a loop older than soil: stone sweats brine, brine feeds iron-backs, iron-backs feed crabs and Walkers, Walkers and crabs and iron-backs die and become stone. Steam becomes water becomes brine. Heat becomes pressure becomes motion becomes heat. An industrial ecosystem that evolved not *to use* industry but *to be* it, because in this cold, with no sun and no plant to make sugar from light, the only way to live was to become a heat engine directly.

The place has been working for so long it has worn physics smooth at the edges. That's why smoke falls up and distance stutters and the water is both scalding and freezing and never quite freezes. Energy here is not conserved. It is performed, endlessly, by a million unsynchronized pistons, until the performance itself has become the law.

If you stay, and you keep still, the chuffing will get inside your breathing until you aren't sure which piston is yours. The brine will bead on your skin and try to drink you. A crab will test your boot seam for copper. A Walker will pause its thumping and its manifold will swivel, not at you, but at the space just beside you, listening for the heat you leak.

And the plain — low, vast, humming, covered in iron backs shoulder-to-shoulder to the wrong horizon — will keep working around you, as it has since the rock was young, as it will when the cloud finally condenses for the last time and there is nothing left to polish.
