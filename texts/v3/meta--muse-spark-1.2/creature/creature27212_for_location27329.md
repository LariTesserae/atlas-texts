At first you think nothing lives there.

That's the trick of the filtration. That's the trick of the light.

You hang in the exact center where up is weak and you turn slowly and the whole chamber is a bright, loud, impossible drum and you see no fish, no birds, no bodies. You see architecture that looks inhabited but empty — balconies without chairs, doorways without doors, streets made of vertical rivers that nobody is using. You think: *automated*. A town that maintains itself. The spider-armatures crawling the ceiling are the only things moving that aren't water. The kelp threads wave. Everything else is still.

You keep looking because the density is wrong for an empty place. There is *no* empty water. Every cubic meter is claimed. If no one lives here, who built all the shallow balconies? Who packed them twenty stories up every wall?

Then your eyes learn the light.

Because there are no shadows, you have no depth cues you've evolved to trust. The honest white flattens everything. An edge that looked like a weld seam is a body. A pipe that looked like a pipe is an occupied lane. And then one of them moves — not swimming, exactly — and your brain re-renders the whole chamber at once.

They were there the whole time. You were looking through them.

---

What lives there isn't fauna. That's why the sieves let it stay. The system that scrubs every microbe and egg and stray fish from the flush water still reads them as *vegetation* and *infrastructure*. On some registry deep in the megastructure, they are listed as scrubbing lichen and pressure membrane, not animal. It's not a mistake. It's how they got in.

Call them the Holdfasts. Not a name they gave themselves. It's just what they do.

Individually, one is not much larger than your forearm when it's tucked in. When it extends to move, it's about as long as you are tall, but most of that is not body. 

Picture a spine — not bone, printed ceramic-metal like the walls, seamed and bright where it shows, still sharp because it is young — about sixty centimeters long, slightly curved. Off that spine hang three, sometimes four pairs of limbs that are not limbs. They are fins and hands and gills at once, translucent, deeply veined, edged with fine cilia that can stiffen into fronds or soften into paddles. The body between them is almost nothing: a translucent sac, a gut, a knot of nerve and nodule that pulses faintly. No head as you understand it. No front. At either end of the spine there's a clasp — a radial mouth-hand, six-fingered, ceramic-tipped, that can grip a railing, or seal around a pipe, or interlock with another Holdfast's clasp and hold.

They are not pretty. They are *consistent* with the chamber: sharp, young, new. Where their soft parts brush against the new concrete, you can see faint pink where they've been cut. They have learned to line the worst corners with their own kelp.

And the kelp is part of them. The black-green threads with the glowing nodules you thought were the only vegetation — those grow *out* of them. Each Holdfast carries two or three on its back, rooted into the sac, waving above it like banners. The nodules glow amber-white, not brightly, but in perfect daylight you can see them ticking. That's how they talk.

Because you can't talk here. Not with that roar. The pumps breathing every seven minutes, the thousand check-valves ticking, the bell-tone of the outer ocean pressing on the hull — speech is flattened into useless pulp the instant it leaves you. So they don't try.

They flash.

A nodule brightens, dims, brightens again in a fast code. In water this clear, a flash carries across the whole plaza without loss. One flashes on a high balcony on the north wall, and three more answer from an inverted street on the ceiling, and a dozen more along a service canal braid. At night-analog, when the ambient light cools by a few kelvin, the whole chamber looks for a minute like a slow circuit board breathing.

If you stay still long enough you realize the flashing is *timed* to the shimmer.

---

Water is structure to you. To them, water is law.

They don't swim through the chamber. Swimming would be obscene — wasted energy, turbulence. They read the pressure fields the way you read sidewalks. You saw the shimmer like heat haze that separates a fast lane from a dwelling pocket? They *live* in that difference. They cling to the boundary itself, cilia splayed, feeling the shear.

To move, they don't kick. They *let go*. One clasp releases a railing, the body goes slack, neutrally buoyant, and the current in the lane takes them exactly where the lane goes — sideways, up, around the vertical river hanging in the plaza like a glass wall. At the right second they stiffen and snap their other clasp onto a stanchion and stop dead, trembling in the flow. It's more like brachiating than swimming, hand over hand along invisible lines of pressure. The young ones misjudge and get tumbled into the central drift where gravity is weak, and then you see them windmilling their fin-hands in that slow, embarrassed way until an older one reaches a limb out and snags them.

Up is negotiable and they have no preferred orientation. You see one walking *correctly* on a balcony with gravity pulling on it, clasp-feet on the floor, kelp streaming up. Two meters away another is walking upside-down on the underside of the same balcony because the wall's gravity field has pooled there. They pass each other clasp to clasp with no acknowledgment that one of them is inverted. To them there is no inverted. There is only *toward the wall* and *toward the drift*.

Their dwellings are not dwellings the way you think. Those shallow balconies from floor to ceiling, each barely deep enough for a human to stand on — each one is an occupied cell. But a Holdfast doesn't need depth. It folds. Spine curved, fin-hands tucked, sac compressed, it slots into a balcony slot exactly like a tool into a rack. Twenty high, shoulder to shoulder, they pack themselves face to outer wall, clasps interlocked with neighbors, forming a living facade. That's why you thought the balconies were empty ornament. The ornament *was* the resident. When the seven-minute flush peaks and the vertical rivers double in pressure and the whole town thrums so hard your teeth ache, they all go still and clamp down and let the water roar through and over them. You see the whole wall ripple as one body bracing.

Between them, in the impossible no-space between pipes and cables and membranes, they have grown the real architecture: sheets of bio-ceramic they secrete, thin as paper, translucent, quickly calcifying into those membranes that look like lichen. That's what fills every gap you thought was just scaffolding. It's not scaffolding that is being built upon. It *is* the building, continuously extruded, patched, budded. The chamber is young, corners still sharp enough to cut, and the Holdfasts are young with it. They haven't smoothed it. They're busy caulking it with themselves.

They eat the flush. Every seven minutes new ocean is forced in, filtered, then drawn back. The kelp nodules on their backs — and larger mats of the same kelp that they farm along the service canals — scrub dissolved minerals and organics out of that transient ocean and convert it. You watch one detach from the wall during a slow interval, drift to a canal where the kelp is thickest, press its sac against a mat, and pulse. Exchange. No hunting. No prey. There is no fauna for a reason; fauna would compete. They are farming the water itself.

And they maintain. The spider-armatures you thought were robots — watch one closely. Under the girders, tucked into the joints, there are Holdfasts riding it. Or maybe it is grown around them. It folds and unfolds along the ceiling street and wherever a pressure gate shudders, two or three of them leash themselves to it and pull, cilia stiffened into cables. They are not separate from maintenance. They *are* the maintenance that learned to stay.

How many? In a 300-meter pocket, a city square drowned in a cube — thousands. Tens of thousands if you count the ones folded into the membranes who haven't grown limbs yet and just *are* wall. You can't census them because you can't see where one ends and the next begins when they're packed clasp to clasp. The water is clear, the light is perfect, you can see every bolt — and still you undercount them by a factor of three until one peels itself off the mass and moves and your eye finally separates figure from ground.

If you put your hand out near a dwelling pocket, where the water is slow and almost still behind its shimmer curtain, one might uncurl to look at you. It has no eyes, but the sac has a ring of photosensitive freckles that darken when you block the light. It will flash its nodule once, not at you — past you, to someone else — a little amber tick that says *pressure change, large, warm, soft, not useful* and the others will not come. They are not curious the way humans are curious. This place is not for visiting. It's a sump, a flaw, a blister in the skin of a machine that doesn't know it exists. They know that. They were extruded for a low, sharp, cold, loud hollow and they fit it exactly.

You tread water in the center where up is thin and understand finally that the town is not a place they live in.

The town, packed wall to wall with water taught to behave like wall, is what happens when they live packed together.

The water holds because they hold it.
