They live close enough to hear each other breathe.

That’s the first thing you understand about them, before you can pick out any one of them. You don’t meet an *individual* here first. You meet a density. A pressure. A human texture that has filled the bowl the way water has — finding every seam, every flat, every ledge that would hold weight.

Then, if you stand still and let the mist settle on your eyelashes and let your eyes adjust to the flat, sourceless light, they resolve.

They are pale. Not the pallor of sickness, but the pallor of people who live under cloud and stone and perpetual damp. Their skin never quite dries. The children’s knuckles are always a little pruned, their hair always a little lank, even when it’s clean. The adults have the weather in their faces — wind-chap on the cheeks, a faint ruddy burn on the nose and ears from the cold that never quite leaves the air, but the rest of them is the color of the underside of river stones. Their eyes are dark and quick. They have learned to see in a place with no shadows. No one squints here.

They are small, or rather, they have made themselves small. Not short by blood, but compact by habit. No broad gestures — there is no room for them. Shoulders tucked, elbows in, they move crabwise through the shoulder-wide gaps, sideways through alleys that were never meant to be alleys. They go barefoot more often than not, even when the sheet-water at the bottom is cold enough to ache. You understand why when you try it in boots: you can’t feel the tick of the young basalt settling if your soles are thick. They need to feel it. Bare feet, calloused white and soft at the edges from wet, grip the slick shake-roofs and the smooth tops of causeway stones with a dexterity that looks like it should be painful. It isn’t, to them.

Their clothes are the same color as the settlement because they are made from it. Undyed wool, grey and black and a dirty cream, fulled until it is almost felt, then oiled with rendered fat until it turns the mist. Every adult wears the same basic shape — a long, wrap vest, trousers or a skirt that can be hitched high for wading, all layers that can be wrung out and put back on damp. Nothing is ever fully dry here, so they stopped trying. A wet wool that is warm is better than a dry wool that never comes. On the coldest days they add capes of woven willow-bark fiber that shed water in sheets. You can smell them before you hear them: wet wool, woodsmoke that never quite rises, the faint iron tang of the rock flour that gets into everything, into the creases of skin, under nails that are always trimmed short and always a little black.

They do not speak loudly. The bowl eats sound. The wet air and the close walls swallow a shout after three paces, so they have stopped shouting. A language of low voices, of clicks and tsks and taps. A mother doesn’t call her child from across the hamlet; she sends a younger child, or she taps twice on the roof-beam that connects their houses and the vibration carries. Much of what they say is not said at all. If a channel is blocked with silt, you don’t announce it. You kneel and clear it, and the next person who passes sees your wet hands and knows. If a stone has slipped from a lower course, you reset it, because your neighbor’s wall is also your wall. Their cooperativeness is not ideology. It is structural. A sag in one roof is a leak in another floor. A dammed channel sours the water everyone drinks. Selfishness here is not immoral, it is incomprehensible, like refusing to breathe.

You see it in how they work. There are no farmers, no smiths, no specialization that can afford to be separate. There are maintainers. 

The *water-tenders* — mostly older women and older children — spend the whole day in a slow circuit, not much faster than the water itself. A twig dragged through a channel to break up the silt fans. A palm scooped to lift rock flour and toss it into a cloth bag — later it will be mixed with clay for chinking. They test the flow with a fingertip. In places where the water forgets to fall and trembles uphill on a vertical face, they watch it and wait and sometimes gently touch it to remind it where down is.

The *stone-listeners* — men and women with stone hammers no bigger than a fist, with wooden mallets that make a soft *tok tok tok* that does not echo. They tap the dry-stack courses and listen for the hollow that means a shim has worked loose. They rebuild without mortar on purpose. Every wall has a little give, a little flex, so when the young basalt ticks in the night and a new hairline crack appears in a plastered wattle panel, the house sighs instead of shattering.

The *green-keepers* — the few who tend what little grows. They do not farm so much as coax. They harvest moss and liverwort in small, permitted squares, leaving the rest to hold the seeps together. They wring the sour grass and sedge and feed it in tiny handfuls to the only animals they keep: a small, dun-colored breed of damp-hen, maybe a dozen in the whole bowl, that lives on stone-mites and moss and lays pale, speckled eggs in the thatch. They pollard the stunted willows until the trunks are knotted fists, every withy taken for baskets, for wattle, for cradle-frames. A child is taught very early which lichen is orange and edible when boiled three times, and which grey lichen is only for dye.

The children are everywhere and nowhere to trip over. Because there are so many of them for the space — that first weirdness you felt, the settlement holding more bodies than its geometry should allow — they have learned to be vertical children. They don’t run in circles. They run in levels. A game of chase goes over a roof, down a notched log, across a plank that bounces, under a laundry line, through ankle-deep sheet-water with a splash that is immediately scolded — not for noise, but because splashing silts the channel. They know the map by foot-feel and by handhold. They sleep in shifts, in lofts that are warm from the bodies below. There is no privacy, so there is no loneliness. An infant cries and four arms that are not its mother’s reach for it.

The old are low and still. They sit where the seep is warmest, on stones that have taken the shape of their hips over years. Because the rock is still being born, they are the memory of it. The oldest woman, whose face is as fissured as the basalt on the upper rim, will tell you without mysticism that the bowl inhales. She will put your hand on a wall and you will feel it — that faint, deep *tick* through the sole of your foot as much as the palm, a ledge settling a millimeter deeper. “It hasn’t decided,” she says, not as folklore but as weather report. And when a vertical lens of water hangs impossible on a wall, trembling, refusing to fall, the old don’t marvel. They nod. *It’s learning*, they say. They build cradles with loose joints for the same reason they build houses with loose joints.

None of them plan to leave. People from outside who come down the steep, sharp stairstep ledges stay a night or two and then get restless. The closeness that feels like a weaving to those who live here feels like a smothering to those who don’t. The air too heavy, the light too flat, the quiet too complete. The people of the bowl watch them go without resentment. They know their world looks like a scab — that’s what the old woman says it is, a tight, warm scab over a wound in the earth while it heals — and scabs are not beautiful to anyone but the skin that needs them.

At dusk, when the mist thickens until the rim is gone and the bowl becomes exactly world-sized, they become most themselves. Lanterns with oiled paper come on one by one and double in the sheet-water. Families fold into rooms that are also hallways that are also roofs. Someone plays a small, reedy pipe very softly, the notes not carrying far. The grey herons on the high rim, those huge, still judges, finally tuck their heads. In the thatch, the flat stone-mites come out to graze, slow as fingernails. In the channels, the translucent shrimp flick and feed on what the humans have stirred up all day.

They lie down close, wool to wool, foot to foot, listening to water tick off eaves into barrels, listening to the stone tick underneath them, and they breathe together — shallow, cool, mineral breaths — until the bowl, and the dense, careful, patient life that has packed itself into every crack of it, exhales as one.
