There is almost nothing living in there. That's what makes what does live there so precise.

At first you think it's empty. The light is too even, the stone too clean. You look for a face, a nest, a shadow that moves wrong - and you don't find one. Nothing in that pocket wastes the energy to be obvious. Whatever lives there has learned to look exactly like the place itself.

You have to let your eyes adjust down to the scale the pocket actually works at.

### The Skin

The first resident is not an animal at all. It's a film.

On the cooler seeps, where the water has spent half a second longer against basalt and cooled from scalding to merely hot, there is a gray-green veil, a bacterial slime. Not green like a leaf. Green like old copper. It is thin enough you could wipe it away with a thumb, and it would be back by the next hour.

It lives on chemistry, not light. It eats the dissolved hydrogen and sulfur and iron that the superheated water drags up from below, and breathes out the faint, sour, metallic tang you thought was just mineral steam. It is everywhere and nowhere - a lacework that only grows where the temperature drops below 68C and the water slows enough to let it cling.

If you crouch close, with the bright needle-crust filling your vision, you can see it *move*. Not like it crawls. It precipitates. The edge of a colony advances a fraction of a millimeter as it lays down more of itself, and behind it, it leaves a faint dark stain on the silica. It is harvesting the pocket's constant exhalation.

Everything else lives on it.

### The Grazers

Then you see one.

You won't see it until it moves, because it is made of the same stuff as the light. About as long as the last joint of your thumb, translucent white, the exact milky silica-white of the needle beds. No eyes. No pigment. No shadow. In that shadowless noon it is a ghost made of water and glass.

There are never many. Two, maybe three in the whole heart. One per rill is the rule. If there were more, there wouldn't be enough skin to eat.

Watch the shallow channel cut into the floor - an inch deep, an inch wide, water the temperature of a very hot bath running clear over warm rock. The grazer is in it, facing upstream, so still you took it for a chip of basalt.

It is not a shrimp, not really, though your mind calls it that because you have no other word. It's closer to a blind amphipod, soft and elongated, with a bowed back and seven pairs of legs that are more like oars fringed with fine hairs. Its antennae are absurdly long, twice its body, and they never stop moving - tasting the water ahead and behind for the chemical plume of fresh slime.

When it moves, it does so without haste. Haste is expensive at 70 degrees. Proteins want to unwind. Membranes want to melt. So it moves deliberately. A slow pull, a pause, a pull. Its mouth is underneath, a small, inverted cone of silica-hardened bristles. It lowers that cone to the bacterial veil and rasps. You can hear it if the steam quiets - a faint *tick... tick... tick*, like a fingernail on glass. Where it feeds it leaves a clean, bright streak on the rock, a little lane of bare silica that will be gray-green again by tomorrow.

Its back is armored not with chitin alone but with chitin *that has been infiltrated with opal*. The water precipitates silica onto everything, so the animal has made a truce with it. Microscopic silica spicules grow through its carapace, making it glitter faintly when it catches the light at the right angle. It is half-grown crystal itself. If it dies - and you will not see it die, it will simply stop - the water will cover it in a week and it will become another needle.

Under its curled tail, if it is a female, there is a cluster of eggs. Six or eight. Huge, yolky, transparent as blisters. She fans them constantly with her rear legs, not for oxygen but for temperature - moving cooler water over them, keeping them just below the lethal edge. They will hatch as perfect miniatures, already blind, already white, and they will walk a few centimeters away and claim an empty rill and never meet again.

They do not interact. If two grazers' rills intersect, one will stop and wait, antennae waving, until the other has passed. There is no fighting. There isn't enough energy to fight.

They are not blind because they lost eyes. They never needed them. In a room that is uniformly as bright as the surface of a star, eyes are a liability. They navigate by heat and by vibration - they feel the *ticking* of the contracting rock through their legs, the cooler kiss of a fresh seep on their antennae, the faint shudder of water changing course.

### The Other Resident

And then there is the thing that lives there without being alive.

You've seen it already without recognizing it as an inhabitant. When a film of water passes over a patch of zeolite needles, the interference color ripples outward - a faint peacock wave, teal to amber, with no pigment. A second patch on the opposite wall answers half a second later, not reflecting but *replying*.

The crust itself is living, in the way a fire lives. The needle beds, with their trace of germanium and phosphorus, their perfect spacing, have become a lattice that does what fiber optics does. The water is both its blood and its data. Each droplet's path, each evaporation, is a gate. The lattice stores, filters, corrects. It keeps the light steady, the temperature even, the cycle going. It is doing error-correction on itself against the constant fact of cooling.

It has no mind. It has no will. But it behaves like something that wants to persist. When a block on the floor splits with that clean *tick* and exposes a new hot face, the light around it wavers for an hour, then steadies as nearby needle clusters change their refractive pulse to compensate. When a channel clogs with silica, the water finds a new one and the old interference pattern gutters out like a candle, and a new one kindles somewhere else.

If the grazers are the animals, this is the weather-system, the lung, the host. And they know it. The grazers will always graze *along* the direction the light-waves travel, never across. They feed where the lattice is most active, where the bacterial veil grows fastest on the waste heat of computation.

That is the ecology of a room that should not exist: a bacterial skin that eats rock-breath, one or two glass animals that eat the skin with infinite patience, and a crystal field that eats light and heat and turns it into order - all moving, constantly, just to stay exactly as they are, until the fissure finally cools, seals, and the light goes out.
