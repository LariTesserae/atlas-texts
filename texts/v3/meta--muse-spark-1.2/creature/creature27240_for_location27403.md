Who lives there is exactly what you would expect to grow on new rock that never dries.

Not people who *came* there. People who *condensed* there, like the water on the undersides of the lintels.

At first you think they'd be tall — something that could see over the press, something leggy that could wade the seep. But nothing tall can live in a place where you live on the roofs. After a generation, after ten, after a hundred generations of stepping from flat roof to flat roof, ladder to ladder, ducking under low lintels that weep all morning, you get low. You get wide.

They are low themselves.

The Basin-folk are short the way pit-dogs are short, the way lizards are short — not stunted, but *pressed*. Four and a half feet at the tallest, broad through the shoulders and hips, with a center of gravity that sits almost in the thighs. Their legs are short and bowed, their feet enormous, splayed, pale. Not quite webbed, but the toes are fleshy and spread wide to grip wet basalt and sun-soft thatch and the slick skin of algae that comes back every morning no matter how often you scrape it. Children learn to walk on the roofs before they learn to walk on the ground, because the ground is never really ground — it is gutter and seep — and so they move with a particular roof-gait: a crouched, scuttling shuffle, hands often touching the parapet for balance, bare soles finding purchase where you would think there is none.

And they are wet the way the town is wet.

Their skin never quite dries. It is not amphibian, not frog-slick, but human skin that has learned to stay oiled. A grey-yellow, bloodless pallor from living under that pearlescent dim, beaded always with condensation the way the basalt sweats. The sebaceous glands overproduce — a thick, musky oil that smells faintly of lime and smoke — to keep the constant damp from macerating them. Their hair is thin, sparse, plastered to the scalp in damp strings; men and women both keep it cropped close with chipped obsidian because long hair rots here. Old people and babies alike have a soft, waterlogged look to the creases of their necks and wrists, the flesh a little swollen, a little spongy, as if they've been soaking too long. Which they have. All their lives.

You would think they'd be deaf. Living under that roar — the cicada roar that *is* the air — any animal that kept human ears would go mad. So they have, in a way, gone quietly deaf.

Not born deaf. Made deaf. The outer ear is small, crumpled close to the head like a bat's folded wing. The ear canal is narrow and can be closed by a flap of cartilage, an unconscious wincing shut when the chorus peaks at dusk. Children are born hearing acutely, crying at the sound, and by the time they are five they have learned not to hear it at all. They don't listen *through* the roar, they listen *under* it. Their language is not projected — shouting is useless — it is tapped and felt. A hand hammer on stone, a mallet on mud-brick, a heel stamped on a hollow roof: the town thrums with it. They speak in low, close, chest-voice murmurs when face-to-face, foreheads almost touching, and over any distance they drum. A code of knocks runs constant across the rooftops, through the stone, through the soles of those wide feet. You feel someone calling you before you hear them.

Their eyes, though — their eyes have opened wider to meet the dim.

In that amber half-light that is never noon and never evening, you need to gather what little photon there is. So their eyes are large, and dark, and wet — amber-brown irises so large the white barely shows, with a soft nictitating film they can draw across when the rain hammers or the kiln smoke hangs thick. They don't blink so much as *glaze*, a slow horizontal slide. At night, when the bats pour out and the gutters glitter, those eyes catch kiln-light and reflect it back like a gecko's. They see beautifully in the dim. They would be blinded if you took them out onto a bright plain.

And they eat what the valley provides, which is itself.

There are no fields. No grain. No fruit. The only green is a lie — that slick black-green algal mat that coats every seep and must be scraped from the doorstep with a basalt shard every sunrise. They scrape it, and they keep it. The women collect it in shallow clay pans on the roofs where the rain can thin it, and it grows again by afternoon, rust-colored and olive, bubbling faintly. It is not good food but it is food, alkaline and mineral, dried into black cakes over the charcoal fires. The rest is protein that moves: what the insects eat, they eat; what eats the insects, they eat. Gutter minnows and fat tadpoles scooped by the handful and fried whole. Water beetles cracked like nuts. The thumb-sized cockroaches roasted until the chitin pops. Cicada nymphs dug from the mud-brick where they burrow, creamy and salty. And the bats. Especially the bats. At dusk boys climb the scaffolds that are never finished and never fall and pull down the bat-nets strung between eaves — fine reed nets left all day — thrashing with a hundred squealing bodies. They snap the necks with practiced fingers and singe the fur off over the kilns. Nothing is wasted. The bones are ground for mortar.

So their teeth are strong and stained black at the gumline from tannin and smoke. Their bellies are tolerant. Their breath, like the town, smells of warm mineral water and char.

They have no history because nothing changes to mark it. They have no word for *season*, no word for *progress*. Time is told by the rain — *before-rain, rain, after-rain, steam* — that same twenty-minute hammer every day, and by the repair cycle. Every wall is always being patched. Every roof is always being re-mudded. Every block is cut to the same shape with the same stone hammer that left the same mark a thousand years ago. A boy learns from his father exactly how to wedge the chisel into the bubble-pocked basalt so it breaks clean, and his son will learn the same, and the block will be the same. There is no innovation because innovation would require surplus or dryness or quiet or a reason to be different. Stasis is not oppression here. It is ecology. The town is at carrying capacity the way a tidepool is at carrying capacity. Every niche filled. Every death replaced.

They are not unhappy. Happiness would be a change in state.

They move on the roofs in single file, crouched, oiled, dark-eyed, humming low in their chests at a pitch just under the cicada roar so they can feel each other's presence. At night they curl together on the upper floors — whole families and sometimes whole roof-blocks — sleeping in a damp, steaming pile under woven reed mats, feeling the roar as a weight on their skin until it becomes the same as gravity. In the morning they wake beaded with condensation like the lintels, scrape their doors clean, and start tapping again.

If you stayed long enough to stop hearing the roar, you would see they are not really inhabitants at all. They are the town's way of keeping itself inhabited. Smooth, spongy, low, and loud — animals perfectly shaped by warm basalt and trapped sound and water that never leaves.
