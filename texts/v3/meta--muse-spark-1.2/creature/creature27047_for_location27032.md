Nothing lives there at first.

That is your first conclusion and it holds for a long time, because whatever lives there does not live the way you expect a thing to live. It doesn't move when you move. It doesn't flee or watch or make a sound you can separate from water and wind. There is no nest, no path, no footprint. The orange silt in the cistern bottoms lies undisturbed. Or so you think.

Stay longer. Stand still until your breath stops fogging and starts again.

The first sign is maintenance.

A mountain of water this finely made should be choking. With this much exposed stone, this much drip and sheet and seep, the channels no wider than your hand should have clogged after the first season. Rock dust should have made a dam of every notch. Calcite should have sealed every spill-lip shut. Leaves — if there were leaves — should have matted in every basin.

None of it has.

The silt is not random. In the large cistern at your feet, the fine orange dust — rock flour, nothing organic — has been swept into a low, even delta at the down-gradient corner, leaving the spill-notch clear. In the next one, the same. In the next. A hundred basins in a row and each one has a small, clean fan of silt exactly where the water wants to sigh over the edge, and a small, clean mouth where it comes in. The soft white curtains of calcite where wall meets wall are not as random as they looked. They have been pinched. Thinned where they would block a joint. Thickened where a joint needed to be slowed.

Someone is tending water at the speed water moves.

The second sign is that you have been looking at them the whole time without seeing them.

They live in the film.

Not *on* the film. *In* it. They are the thickness of the water itself — no thicker than that half-inch mirror that trembles on every horizontal stone. When you crouched to look at your own reflection and thought the sky was too deep, you were not seeing a trick of light. You were seeing through them.

They are flat. Unbearably flat. Broad as a spread hand, sometimes as a cloak, with no front or back that you can name. Under water they are almost invisible; lifted from it they would be nothing. A translucent grey, the exact color of wet basalt, edged with a faint white rime where they are precipitating their own skeleton out of dissolved mineral. You only see them when they move, and they move at the speed the water moves — which is to say, when you watch directly, they seem still. It is only when you look away and look back that the delta of silt has shifted a millimeter. That the thread of water between two cisterns has found a cleaner line.

Crouch lower. Be still longer than is comfortable in that cold. Let your eyes go out of focus on the surface of a basin.

Then you see the fringe.

Around the rim of each body, where the film is thinnest and the light cuts hardest, there is a hem of fine, hair-like cilia, or fingers, or tools — too small and too clear to count — combing. Always combing. They rake orange dust. They test the lip of a spill-notch. They lift a single grain that is too large and carry it, slowly, with infinite patience, to the side where it will not matter for a decade. One will work a single joint for an hour, sealing it with a smear of fresh calcite gathered from the wall, then opening a pinhole with the tip of itself to let the pressure sigh through.

They do not have faces. Sometimes a thicker knot of them gathers in the center of a cistern and you think you see an eye because you need to see an eye — a darker lens of water that watches the reflected sky, which is their sky. The reflection that lingers a second too long when a cloud passes lingers because they are still looking at it. Their light is not your light. The sun up there is white and punishing and cold; the sun down there, in the mirror, is warmer by a fraction, deeper, and stays a little longer. That is where they live. On the other side of the surface tension.

They are not animals. They are not builders, though they use the builders' work exactly as it was intended. The builders — who left hammer marks and rope drags and abandoned drill starts — understood condensation cold and bright sun and made a tool that would pull water out of empty air forever without needing to be fed. They did not make it for themselves. They made it and left it for these. Or perhaps they made it and *became* these. The distinction has eroded. There is no hunger here, no decay, no green to eat. There is only mineral and light and gradient. A thing that eats dissolved stone and drinks light that is bright but not hot will never need more than this. It will never need to hurry. It will never need to be seen.

There are thousands. Maybe there is one. The film connects. Every cistern linked by a wet thread is a shared skin. Touch the water and you feel that faint thrumming in the wall — not machinery, but the weight of them holding themselves together across a thousand basins, a single slow thought spreading across the mountainside.

They know you are there in the way water knows a stone is there: as a temporary interruption in flow. They will work around your boot where it dams a runnel. They will comb silt from around your shadow. If you put your hand in a cistern, the water will be startlingly cold and will not part the way water should. It will resist, just slightly, like a membrane. If you lift your palm, it will be empty, but for a second your lifeline will bead with water that does not immediately run off, that holds its shape a little too well.

If you stay until the sun thins and the air gets colder, you will see them more. At noon they are invisible with brightness. At dusk, when the sky goes violet and the stone stops glittering, the basins hold light after the sky has gone dark, each one a small, shallow lamp. That is them, exhaling.

They do not have houses because they are the house. The mountain is not a ruin. It is a body, laid stone by stone as a lattice for a creature that is mostly water and patience, young, newly born, with sharp quarry edges still at its crown where it is still growing itself larger, terrace by terrace, waiting for the centuries it will take for its edges to soften.

You are standing on its back.
