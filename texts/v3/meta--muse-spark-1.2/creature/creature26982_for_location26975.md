Who lives here has not arrived. Who lives here has been *left behind* by the fact that everything else eroded away.

At first you think nothing lives here. Or nothing should.

The scale is wrong for a habitat — too flat, too exposed, too wet in a way that never pools and never floods, too loud to hear a predator or a child. You look at the concrete that goes on until it becomes a weather system and you think: this is a place for machines. For storage. And then you notice the movement on the storage.

Then you notice that the storage is moving.

---

The people here, if you still want to call them people and they still call themselves that, are not pioneers. Pioneers are lean and wind-burnt and squinting toward a horizon. These are not those animals.

They are broad, and damp, and unhurried. They were not selected for speed.

You see it first in how they walk. Nobody hurries on the plateau, not because of indolence but because everything is slick. Every walkway beads. Every ladder rung weeps. Every concrete stair has been worn in the center by a century of boots into a shallow U that holds a film of water an eighth of an inch deep. If you hurry you fall. If you fall you crack something on a planet made of old basalt under a skin of concrete. So they have learned a gait — short steps, weight forward, rubber boots that never fully dry. The children learn it before they learn to read. The old still do it perfectly at eighty, in the same boots.

Their clothes have given up on being dry. That was the first negotiation.

Cotton rots here in a season. Wool stays wet for days. So they wear what the manufactories make for themselves: oilskin that has gone soft with age, waxed canvas gone dark at the shoulders, rubberized aprons, caps with a short bill to keep the drip off the neck. Not a uniform, but a convergence. When everything weeps, you dress like a fisherman even if you work on turbines. When you come inside, you hang it on a peg by the door where it drips into a tray that drains back into the cistern, and you never expect it to be dry when you put it on again. Newcomers, if any still come up the scarp roads, complain about this for weeks and then stop.

Their skin tells you how long they have been here. Paler than you expect under the high white sun, because they live under cloud and sodium light more than sun. Damp all the time at the hairline, at the small of the back. Faintly macerated at the fingertips, like people who work with water. Many of them have a faint, harmless rash along the forearms where the bacterial film — that orange, sulfur-yellow slick that paints every weeping wall — has decided to live on them too. They do not scrub it raw. They wash, they dry carefully, and it comes back, and they leave it. The old maintenance women have nails that are slightly yellowed and thickened from the mineral water. They can gauge the hardness of a cistern by taste. They are not sick. They are colonized, lightly, the way the stone is colonized. You have to be, to eat here.

Because there is nothing green to eat. And they have stopped pretending otherwise.

No one farms here. Farming implies soil and soil implies plants and the plateau refused that contract long before the City was poured. What they do instead is *husbandry of what is already there.*

They tend the films. On sheltered walls behind the power stations where warm vapor condenses first, they scrape the bacterial leather with flat steel tools, not to clean it but to harvest it — in sheets that come off like wet paper, to be dried on racks, powdered, pressed into cakes that smell of iron and brine. The cakes feed the pill beetles. The beetles feed them.

They tend the beetles. In low, wet cellars under every district, in trays stacked ten high under amber work-lights, the iron-black pill beetles move in a slow, ticking mass, rasping the bacterial paste, laying eggs like black glass beads. A child can tend a thousand. A family can live off a room of them. They are not delicious. They are protein and fat and salt, roasted until the carapace cracks. You eat them with the fungal mats — the pale, flat fungus that grows over the catch-basins — fried into something like an omelet, earthy and metallic. Visitors gag. Locals add more salt.

They tend the birds. Not tenderly. Practically. Every ledge, every cornice, every broken window is a nesting site and every nesting site is a pantry. They take eggs on a rotation so strict it has become a calendar — this block on Mondays, that manufactory on Thursdays — leaving one in three to hatch, because the gulls and pigeons and needletails are also the guano geology, and the guano is nitrate and phosphate, and without it the bacterial films thin out. Some districts keep the cliff swifts semi-domesticated, netting fledglings before they can fly properly and fattening them on beetle meal in wire coops on the roofs. The meat is dark and dense. The bones are soft enough to eat.

They drink the weep itself. The water that beads on the underside of every bridge is not rain, it is distillation — cloud caught on cold concrete and rendered pure. It runs through gutters worn smooth as throats, into settling trays where the mineral fuzz is allowed to bloom and then skimmed, into cisterns. Every roof is a catchment. Every building is a well. The water-tenders — a kind of priesthood without a god — walk the elevated troughs with long poles, tapping the concrete aqueducts to listen for the change in tone that means a constriction of mineral scale. They clear it with hammers. They always have.

You will not find a farmer. You will find a tender, a scraper, a harvester, a listener.

And they listen constantly, because the plateau is loud.

They are a loud people the way people who live beside a waterfall are loud without knowing it. The City drones — turbines the size of houses, belt drives slapping, stamping presses thudding on a nine-day cycle, ventilation fans pushing warm wet air from one district to the next — and the fauna screams over it — cicada-like chitter of lice, clatter of beetle shells on concrete, the tearing kiting shriek of a hundred thousand wings that never stops, even at night, just thins. To talk on a street corner you pitch your voice up and over it. They all have a slight permanent shout. They all have a slight permanent hearing loss in the left ear by forty. They read lips without knowing they do.

They do not try to silence it. They have built their speech around it. Their language is clipped and percussive, vowels held flat so they carry. They do not whisper here. Whispering is useless. When they want to be intimate they lean in until their foreheads touch and speak into the bone.

They are not lonely. That is the thing you get wrong longest.

You come from a place where density means crowds and crowds mean stress, and you think a city the size of a small continent with animals layered onto every surface must be maddening. You imagine competition. You imagine people fighting pigeons for ledges, rats for pipes.

They don't. They have become layered too.

A beam has a nest and a conduit and a man's bunk under it and none of them is in the way of the other. A warm exhaust vent has twelve lizards and three rats and an old man warming his hands there on his break and they share the heat without comment. A service tunnel has a river of the high-plateau rats moving one way along the wall and a maintenance crew moving the other way with tool carts, and the rats part around the boots like water around stones. It is not tolerance. Tolerance implies annoyance endured. This is coevolution. The man knows not to leave food where the lice can find it, and the lice know to stay on the fungal mats. The pigeon knows the man will take two eggs and leave one, and the man knows the pigeon will return to the same cornice next year no matter what he does. A truce so old it has become an architecture.

They maintain rather than build. That is their ethic, their culture, their slowness made into a virtue.

Nothing is new here. Even what looks new is a patch. A wall is re-poured in place where the weep has exposed rebar like bone. A turbine bearing is recast from the slag of the last one. A district's cistern is jacked up a centimeter after it has subsided a centimeter into the damp dust. Young people are not taught to invent. They are taught to notice the drip rate changing. To hear the turbine thrum go slightly flat. To see the orange film paling, which means the water has gone too hard. Their great art is diagnosis. Their great pride is keeping something that should have failed a hundred years ago still turning, still weeping, still feeding the beetles.

Time here is not measured in hours — the sun is too high and honest and the sodium lights never turn off to allow that. It is measured in drips, in nine-day weather cycles when the cloud sea comes in from the same quarter and the weeping doubles and the birds wheel tighter, in curing times — four days for a bacterial cake, eleven days for a beetle generation, a year for rust to deepen one shade. If you ask a woman how long she has worked the waterworks she will say "since the last time we had to replace the trough on 47." That was seventeen years ago. It feels accurate.

If you go looking for them at the edge, you will find them there too, but not looking out.

The edge of the plateau is polished, not crumbling. Water goes over in a thousand veils and becomes cloud again. The gulls hang motionless in the updraft. It should be a place for contemplation, for staring into the white below and feeling awe.

The people who live nearest the edge use it to dry laundry. They have strung lines on the windward side where the updraft keeps the oilskins from touching the rock. They sit with their backs to the drop, mending nets for the swifts, shouting to each other over the wind and the birds, not looking down because there is nothing down there that needs tending. The lowlands are a theory. The plateau is a fact. The City is the fact that contains all the others.

And when evening comes and the cloud sea that did not rise but arrived drags its belly over again, beading on every vertical face until the whole world ticks and rills, they go inside — not to separate themselves from the animals, because the animals come inside too, a lizard on the warm pipe above the stove, a few lice working the corner where the condensation never dries — but to be inside the same volume a little more closely. They eat beetles and fungus and gull egg. They oil their boots. They listen to the hum until they stop hearing it, the way you stop hearing surf.

They do not wonder if they belong here. The stone has something living on it. Especially the stone. They are part of that something.
