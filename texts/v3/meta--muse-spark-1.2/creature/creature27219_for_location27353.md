What lives here doesn't live *in* the forest.

It *is* the forest — or a fold of it. You only think there's a difference at first, from the rim, when the roar hits you as one thing.

Then you fall in and the roar comes apart.

At first, you think the rift itself is humming. The heat-shiver in the air, the white sun chopping through Latticeheads, the papery sigh of a million bristles — it feels like geology making noise. Then your ears adjust and you realize it's not one sound at all. It's pressure made of edges. Billions of edges rubbing, clicking, rasping, chewing. The basin isn't inhabited. It's *infested* in the way a hive is infested with itself — so completely that plant and animal stop being useful words.

Start small, because small is what there is most of.

The air is never empty. Even when it looks clear between the Pillars, it's full of ash that isn't ash. Clouds of Hoppers — clear-winged, pale as dust, each no bigger than a grain of rice — hang and drift and suddenly decide, all at once, to be somewhere else. They don't fly so much as flicker. Their wings are dry cellophane that makes no water-sound, only a papery *tik tik tik* when they move. They live their whole lives without ever touching ground, drinking nothing, laying wax-glazed eggs directly onto the bristles of the Latticeheads. They feed by scraping the waxy emulsion off those bristles with combs on their legs. And they are food for everything else.

Put your hand on a Pillar and you feel it before you see them. The trunk looks like wound rope, straw-pale and frayed, but the surface is warm and faintly vibrating. Inside every joint, behind that papery bark, is a gallery. Thimblebacks — beetle-things the size of your thumb joint, dome-backed, the exact ochre and fray as the fiber they live on — have chewed labyrinths into the dead outer layers. Their shells are not shiny. They are matte, fibrous, dusted with the same scented powder the plant seals itself with. When they freeze, they vanish. When they move, the Pillar seems to ripple. If you press your ear to the fiber you hear the city inside: ticking, scrabbling, the soft pop of larvae chewing oil-soaked pith, the drumming of adults signaling through the wood with their heads. Nothing in there ever drinks. They chew fiber and they press it in their guts until a single dark bead of metabolic water sweats out, which they pass mouth to mouth.

Between your feet, the Underhair is alive in a different way. It's knee-deep, wiry, hair-fine runners knitting the salt pan into a trampoline you sink slightly into. It looks like dead grass but it recoils when you step. That's because the Browsers are already on you. They are long, pallid, many-legged things — not centipedes, not quite isopods — the color of sun-bleached bone, with glassy, forward-shearing mandibles. They move low and fast, shearing the Underhair not to kill it but to farm it. They clip a runner, the runner exudes a bead of amber oil to seal the wound, they lap the bead. Behind them the thatch springs back in minutes, already re-weaving. They are silent individually, but there are so many that their passage makes the whole mat whisper, like a field combed by invisible wind. And they are never alone; on their backs ride smaller things — mites like polished seeds, hoppers that have learned to steal the oil second-hand.

And then the things that eat the eaters. Because nothing here is juicy, nothing can afford to be soft. Every predator is dry, waxy, patient, and perfectly camouflaged against straw and dust.

You will walk past a Latticehead three times before you see the Clasper folded inside it. It is not a leaf, but a woven mat of bristles the size of a door, and when it is open it is a solar comb, electrostatic, humming faintly as it pulls humidity out of hot air. When it senses the vibration of a cloud of Hoppers settling, it folds. Not fast — fast is wet-muscle work. This is slow hydraulic crackle, bristle against bristle interlocking like a jaw made of dry thatch. Inside, thousands of tiny hooked hairs exude a dust of dry enzymes. The Hoppers are not digested in liquid; they are desiccated further, broken into powder and oil that wicks down grooves into the plant's core. After an hour the frond sighs open again, empty, and recharges its static field with a faint smell of ozone and resin.

Other Latticeheads don't eat insects at all. They sing. Or rather, something that lives *on* them sings through them. The Harpers are wasp-thin, leggy things with stridulating plates on their backs like dry reeds. They align themselves along a single bristle and bow it, and the whole frond acts as a sounding board, amplifying a high, pure tone that carries down the alleys. It's not mating song. It's map. The tone tells other Harpers where a Pillar is about to fall, where a new gap has opened. Within minutes of a collapse — that great dry crash you hear at night, the splintering of a house-thick stalk into leaning shards — they are there, and then everything else is there, a frantic, choking bazaar of legs and wings racing into the light.

Larger browsers move through this only when you stop looking for them. Far back in the cathedral vaults where ancient Pillars have fused into arches, something the size of a deer but not a deer at all picks its way on stilt legs as thin and dry as the roots it steps between. Its body is a barrel of woven fiber — you realize with a start it is wearing the forest. Its pelt is not hair but living Underhair that has colonized its back, complete with its own mites and Hoppers. Its head is a narrow, downward-pointing beak of chitin, made to shear and press, shear and press. It has no eyes you can see; its whole carapace is pitted with vibration sensors that read the thrumming of the basin through its feet. It pauses, cocks that beak, and then continues, utterly silent, perfectly the color of everything.

Nothing rots here. Nothing can. When one of those stilt-browsers dies — old, or caught by a Clasper, or simply wedged between Pillars that shifted while it slept — it does not bloat or smell. It dries further. Within a day its joints pop, its waxy skin splits, and the Thimblebacks and mites and Underhair arrive to take it apart fiber by fiber. In a week there is only a faint, slightly darker stain on the salt pan and a new tangle of runners knitting over it.

And then, distributed thinly through all this density, the people who learned not to be separate.

You won't see them at first because they have learned the same color. They wear long, pale sheets woven from dead-fallen fiber — not cut from living Pillars, never that — beaten and softened until it drapes like muslin but still carries that dusty green-ochre. Wrapped head to ankle, with only their eyes dark inside, they are another vertical texture among vertical textures. They move quickly, not because they are afraid but because staying still means the Underhair will try to stitch over your feet.

There are not many. A family here, two families there, never a village — the forest won't hold a village. What they have instead are negotiations: a platform of bleached boards lashed twenty feet up between three Pillars with cordage made from the same fiber, just high enough to be out of the Browser paths. A ladder of knotted bristle left dangling where a Pillar has since leaned away, now useless. A windbreak of cut, dried frond-plates propped against the salt, already half-swallowed by thatch. A cairn of basalt shards on the pan to mark an alley that was safe yesterday and might not be tomorrow. Their tools are all deadfall: stone flakes set in polished fiber handles that never rot, bone needles for stitching frond-cloth, fire-hardened points. They carry gourds lined with wax to collect the amber oil beads — not to drink, the oil is bitter and purgative — but to burn. At night their little smokeless lamps make pools of warm light in the thrumming dark, and you can hear, if you are close enough, the low talk that is almost not talk at all because the forest is so loud you have to lean in.

They don't clear. To cut a living Pillar is to slit a hive. The Pillar answers with a boiling, deafening cloud that will fill your nose and mouth with choking dust and mandibles. So they glean. They take only what has fallen, what has dried and split. They know which Clasper fronds are hungry and which are singing, which alleys the stilt-browsers use at noon when all the Latticeheads turn at once with that vast papery sigh and the whole basin seems to inhale. Their children learn early to walk light on the Underhair, to read the tremor of an imminent Pillar-fall through the soles of their feet, to be as dry as the things around them. They are born without water-birth — small, waxy, loud — and they are taught not to cry long, because crying wastes moisture.

They are not masters here. They are not even guests. They are one more kind of dry, busy thing that has found a way to comb a living out of dust and light and static. And like everything else, they keep moving. The alley you sleep in tonight will have closed or shifted by morning, walled over with new bristle, or opened into a sudden shaft of white sun. You take your platform apart and lash it somewhere else, or you leave it and let the forest take it — which it will, quickly, rustling over it with runners and beetles until it is indistinguishable from any other tangle.

If you stay long enough you stop trying to count species or kinds. You start to feel the single rule that lives here: nothing holds water, everything holds *pattern*. Plant-as-animal-as-shelter-as-soil, all one continuous, dense, restless, straw-pale lattice that learned to run on air and oil instead of water, and then filled itself to choking with every variation of chewing, clicking, stridulating, folding, leaning, falling, and re-weaving it could invent. It doesn't need you. It will let you pass if you learn to be brief, and dry, and quiet — as quiet as anything can be in a place that never, ever stops rustling.
