There is no one living there. Not in the way you mean when you say *live*.

At first the place edits out the possibility. You look for life the way you look for it down below — for a face, for eyes looking back, for a track that intends to go somewhere — and you find none of it. The avenues are empty. The plazas hold only wind-grooves and cold talc. You could lie down in the dust and no alarm would run through the city, because there is no one to be alarmed.

Then you stay long enough that your definition of living loosens.

What lives here does not *inhabit* the city. It *is* a function of the city, the way rust is a function of iron and air. It does not occupy the towers. It explains them.

It emerges slowly, and it refuses to resolve into one thing. Allow it to stay unfixed for a while.

### 1. The Film

The first living thing you notice isn't alive.

On the north faces, where the dim light never quite strikes directly, there is a black branching on the stone. Like fern. Like frost on a window. Like ink spilled in water and frozen mid-bloom.

It is not plant. If you touch it, it does not yield. It is glass. It is a salt crystal efflorescence that has learned the shape of a fern because that shape is good at catching dust. It grows a few microns a day by pulling mineral out of the cold dry air, branching and branching with mindless, perfect mathematics. In the thin salt-sweat before dawn it darkens, as if wet, then ticks and pales again as the frost sublimates straight to vapor.

It is not alive, but it is the pasture.

### 2. The Masons

You will step over the second kind before you see it.

They are the color of the plain precisely. Not camouflaged — *made* of the plain. Low, wide, blind domes the size of a tortoise or a low table, with no front or back until they move, which they do rarely. When they do, it is a slow pouring, a spreading of the edge, like a slug made of wet stone. Underneath, they have no feet. They have a single, broad, ciliated sole that secretes.

You crouch and watch one for an hour because there is nothing else to do at that speed.

It moves along the leeward foot of a salt-white course and pauses. The rim of its body lifts slightly, and from beneath it a sheen spreads — not slime, but a paste: ground stone and salt dissolved in something that should be water and isn't quite, something that smells faintly of iron and old blood. It carries its own solvent inside it, the only liquid that can persist here without instantly hissing away into air. It exudes it, dissolves a little of the rock, mixes it with dust it has filtered for years, and lays it down.

A millimeter. Maybe less. A new layer, warm to the touch for a few minutes.

That is all it does. It does not eat in any way you recognize. It grazes on the black fern-crystals, rasping them with a mouth that is more like a mineral file hidden under its lip, taking in salt and trace iron and the static charge that lives in the dust. It does not drink — it harvests the dawn salt-sweat by unfolding a frill as thin as paper, catching the frost before it ticks away, and drawing the sublimation directly into its gut where pressure keeps it liquid for a few seconds longer.

There are not many of them, because there cannot be. One per tower, sometimes one per plaza. They never meet. If two paths cross, they go over each other without pause, leaving a double-thick seam that will become a ridge in a thousand years. They have no sound, no eyes, no warning display. They have no young you ever see; you find, very rarely, a perfect, empty dome the size of a coin, still soft, already the color of stone.

They do not build the city. They *are* the deposition. The strata in the tower walls — black, rust, white, bone-glass — are their lives叠加, their bodies, their centuries of slow excretion. Erosion takes from the windward side what they add to the lee. The city breathes through them. You press your palm to the fresh paste and feel what you thought was vibration in the rock: it is their very low hum, not a sound but a tremor in the teeth, the work happening.

If you stand still long enough, one will flow over your boot. It does not notice. It leaves your laces stiff with a gritty varnish that will flake off in a week.

### 3. The Gliders

Above, because below is too heavy.

You see the third kind only when you stop looking for movement and start looking for a flaw in the air.

They are pale, almost not there, the size of a folded coat or a door. From below they look like a tear in the pearlescent sky. Closer, like a manta made of stretched, dry membrane and filament, with no head, no tail, only a leading edge that is slightly thicker, slightly darker.

They never flap. There is nothing to flap in this thin air. They warp. The trailing edge shivers and curls, the whole body bowls or flattens, and they turn on currents you cannot feel. They ride the fast dust-veils that race overhead, and the invisible charge those veils leave behind. When a cloud passes, all the hairs on your arm rise, and the gliders tilt and climb without effort.

They are translucent. When one banks over a tower, you can see the tower through it, dimmed. When the dim light hits them at the right angle they vanish entirely except for a faint iridescence at the rim, like oil on water.

They do not hunt. They filter. They open a ventral slit — not a mouth — and let the fast air pour through a comb of salt-stiffened bristles that snag spores, crystal fragments, the infinitesimal mites that live on the fern-film. Everything up here is eating dust. Even the predators, if you can call them that, are just filtering a different size of dust.

You never hear them. Sound falls flat within ten feet here, eaten by the dryness, and they have no need of it. If you shout at one, your voice dies before it reaches them. They may feel you as a pressure change, a warm anomaly on the plain, and alter their warp by a degree to avoid you. Or they may not.

They live a long time, because nothing happens quickly. You may see the same one for three days, circling the same three towers, never landing, never sleeping. At dusk — when the light dims further on its slow, non-daily cycle — they rise very high until they are specks, and hang there, barely moving, as if listening to the single tone the dust makes when it sings through the hollow towers.

If one dies, it does not fall like a bird. It folds. The membrane dries, tightens, and becomes a brittle leaf that the next dust devil picks up and grinds against a tower wall until it is just another white band in the strata. The Masons will eventually lay paste over its dust.

### 4. The Hollows

And then there is the last kind, which you only suspect.

You peer through a shoulder-wide window into a tower that should be solid and see an interior too large for it — a smooth, inward-curving cathedral that goes back farther than the stone allows, with a dimmer, closer sky inside it. You toss a pebble in and hear nothing. You feel the polite, inconsistent gravity tug sideways.

Something lives in that wrong space, or perhaps the wrong space itself is alive.

You never see it directly. You see its effect: dust falling slowly at an angle in an empty arch; a patch of air that is colder by a breath; a faint difference in the way distance lies when you look through one arch versus around it. Some hollows gather dimness, as if they are a little more night than the plain around them.

If you stay too long in one, the nausea comes — the one foot heavier than the other — and you realize you are being *noticed*, not by eyes but by the space paying a kind of attention, the way a throat notices air passing through it.

The Masons avoid those towers. The gliders never pass through those windows, though they will circle the tower for hours.

You come to understand that the city is not empty because it was abandoned. It is empty the way a lung is empty between breaths. Its population is measured not in individuals but in processes: deposition and erosion, filtration and sublimation, the slow warping of space where the rock remembers being a forest, a reef, a cave, and hasn't yet decided which one it is.

Nothing here wants anything from you. Thirst is structural; they have solved it by not needing water. Distance is negotiable; they navigate by not looking straight at what they want. Silence has weight; they have never learned to make noise at all.

If you stay long enough to be honest, you understand you are not an inhabitant. You are a temporary condensation, like the salt-sweat before dawn — a film that will hiss and go straight to air without ever having been wet, and the city will lay one more cool, gritty millimeter over where your foot was, and smooth you out.
