They do not announce themselves.

At first you think nothing here could house anything large. The shrimp, the discs, the stilt-walkers, the mica-fliers — those are the residents. The city looks automated, left to run itself on steam and gravity. You walk for ten minutes along a causeway between two scalding mirrors and see no one. You hear no one. The air is too thick to carry a voice, and the light is too total to leave anywhere to hide.

Then you realize you have been looking straight at them and misread them as part of the machinery.

The people who live here are high and white themselves.

You see one standing perfectly still on the far lip of a terrace pool, and for a long second you think it is another wader — that same bone-white chitin, that same impossible stillness. Then it moves, and the movement corrects your eye. Not jointed. Not chitin. Cloth.

They dress the way the stone does. Loose, undyed, pale wraps of mineral-stiff linen and felted fiber that has been boiled and boiled again in the distilled water until it holds no color at all. White on white. Some wear broad, shallow hats like lids, not for shade — there is no shade in this light — but to keep the constant condensation from running straight into their eyes. Most go bare-headed. Their hair, where they keep it, is cropped close or bound flat. Anything that could trap heat is a liability. Skin is left open to the air.

And that skin tells you how long they have been here.

It is not sun-darkened. The sun here bleaches, not tans. Their skin is pallid, damp, almost translucent in the way the shrimp are translucent — you can see the faint blue map of veins at the temple, the wrist. It is always wet, not with sweat but with the air itself, a permanent sheen of condensate that never dries. At the edges — at the hairline, the corners of the mouth, the creases of the knuckles, the soles of bare or sandaled feet — there is a fine white crust. Salt. Travertine dust. Precipitate. No matter how clean they keep themselves, the water leaves a little of itself behind when it evaporates. The old ones are rimed with it. Their eyebrows are pale. Their eyelashes are pale. The children look like they have been dusted with sugar.

They have learned, generation by generation, to be the quietest animal on the plateau. Because the plateau enforces quiet.

You do not hear them come. Their feet are bare or wrapped in soft, soaked felt that makes no slap on the wet stone. They do not call to one another. A voice raised above a murmur is swallowed within twenty feet by the hot, wet air. To shout would be wasteful, and vaguely obscene, like shouting in a library. So they don't.

They have a different language.

Watch two of them meet on a narrow spillway between basins, where the water sheets over a smooth lip a few millimeters thick, silent as glass. They do not wave. They lift a hand, palm open, fingers splayed — a slow signal that catches the white light. Or they tap. One will crouch beside a thick, sweating pipe where condensation gathers and falls — *plink* — *plink* — and tap a rhythm on the iron with a knuckle. The sound, that tiny, bright metallic tick, carries better than any voice in this dead air, and the other will answer by tapping on the next pipe over. The whole dispersed city talks to itself like that, in plinks and hand-signs and the slow re-angling of a condenser panel to flash sun. It is a conversation you have to learn to see, the way you learn to hear the water here.

They move the way everything else here moves — without hurry. You cannot hurry in wet heat at this elevation. The heart would burst. So they have unlearned hurry. An adult crossing a causeway does it at the pace of the waders, that same absurd, delicate placement of foot, ankle, foot. When they work, they work slowly. An old woman tending a settling tank will spend an hour with a long, flat wooden rake, skimming the bacterial felt in slow, even strokes, coaxing it without tearing it. A man on a tower catwalk will sit for a long time just watching a pressure gauge, his hand resting on a riveted rail, feeling its faint tremor through his palm rather than listening for it. A maintenance crew of three will realign a section of pipe on block-and-tackle, not with shouts and counts, but with nods, moving inch by inch. The water is patient. They have become patient to match it.

There are not many of them. That is the first human law of this place, and the one that makes the city feel so strange. Each low, wide building — half sunk into the terrace, roof white as the stone, walls weeping with catchment gutters — is a household, a watershed. A family, or a work-group of four or five, lives spaced a quarter-mile from the next, because that is how much catchment you need to live. To crowd closer would be to steal each other's rain, each other's steam. So the city is not a cluster but a constellation. You stand in what looks like a street and see, in the heat haze between towers, perhaps six other humans in the whole middle distance, each a small white figure attending to their own water.

They do not farm. There is nothing to farm in the green sense. They harvest.

The pools farm for them. The pale shrimp that comb the biofilm, the flat disc-grazers that lick the stone clean — those are livestock, though no one pens them. The bacterial forests — those inch-high white branching silica trees that bloom along every pool rim at the gold hour — are their fields. At low light the harvesters go out with shallow trays and soft silicone scrapers, milking the pools without ever denuding them. They take a little felt here, a handful of shrimp there, a few discs pried gently from the rock, always leaving enough to regrow by morning. The condensate cisterns give them the only water a human can actually drink — everything that comes straight from the rock is too heavy with dissolved limestone and salt to swallow. They drink distilled, flat and hot from black panels, and they eat what has already learned to live on dissolved stone. Their lips, like yours after an hour, always taste faintly of iron.

The children are the only ones who seem quick. You see them at the edges of the great wheels, where the water drives the slow generators. They are still white — white wraps, white dust, white sun — but their eyes are dark, adapted where their elders' have gone pale and cloudy from a lifetime of unfiltered glare. A membrane, almost clear, shutters when they blink against the vertical sun. They play a game that is not a game: they throw a flat chip of travertine to skim a still basin without rippling it. Or they stand utterly motionless beside a wader, mimicking it, until the animal decides they are also a wader and steps delicately around them. They learn early that to be noticed here is to be loud, and to be loud is to be wrong.

At noon when the fliers rise in their clouds of mica and rainbow, the people stop. Work pauses. Everyone who is outside tilts their face just slightly — not up, the light is too fierce to look up — but open. The fliers drink from the air and from the sweat on hot pipes and sometimes from the sweat on a human forearm, landing with a touch so light you feel only a cool prick. The people let them. To brush them away would be pointless. The towers are already stippled with their eggs in every damp crack, and no one scrapes them clean. The machines, the animals, the people, all use the same water and lay their futures in the same damp seams. They have an agreement without words.

The old ones, when they die, are not buried. There is nowhere to dig that is not already water. They are laid on the high terraces where the mineral precipitates fastest, on a clean sheet of stone where the hot spring seeps thin and even. In a year, in five, the quiet water will have done its work: a fine, creamy dam will have grown over them, a new pale lip, a new shallow mirror that catches the white sky. They become part of the stair-step, inch by inch, the way everything here becomes stone if it stays still long enough. No marker is needed. The pool is the marker.

If you stay until that deeper gold light, when the whole wet tableland glows and the silica forests bloom, you will see them come out onto the causeways between their far-apart houses. Not gathering — they never gather. But standing each in their own place, visible to each other across half a mile of steam and mirror, lifting a hand. Holding still. Checking that the others are still there, still white, still breathing the same thick air, still tending the same silent engine.

They look at you with those pale-rimmed eyes, with the faceted indifference of the waders and the quiet patience of the stone itself, and they do not speak. They wait to see if you will learn how to be quiet enough to live here too.

If you lift your hand, slowly, they will lift theirs in answer.
