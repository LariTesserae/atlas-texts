It is not empty. That is the first mistake.

From orbit you thought there was nothing alive here because there was nowhere for anything alive to hide. No shadow, no water, no green. But life here does not hide. It *is* the texture you mistook for emptiness. It doesn't live *on* the plain. It lives *as* the plain.

You have to get down on your knees to see it correctly. Down where the wind breaks up and the light stops being a wall and starts being a scatter between stalks.

### The Ones Who Do Not Move

The field is alive.

Not metaphorically. The bristle itself — that trillion-strong thatch of masts and fins and loops and glass leaves that covers the world to the horizon — is the dominant organism. Or organism-colony. Or city-organ. The distinction collapsed a very long time ago.

Each mast is not a machine that was built and left there. Each mast was *grown*, and it is still growing. If you put your hand on one, the warm ceramic thrums. Not with electricity. With attention. In the heat of the day they are turgid with light. In the cold hour before dawn they go slack and drink.

They do not need soil. They are rooted in the plain itself, which is also them — filamentous foundations that run meters, kilometers down through the laminated pale body of the world, tapping the old lattice for trace ions. They stand shoulder to shoulder not because they were planted that way, but because they outcompeted any distance between them. Light is the limiting resource here, and humidity, and capacitance, and they have divided it with the ruthless fairness of grass.

Watch one for a long time and you will see it breathe. Not with lungs.

At about 10:00, when the sun is high enough to punish, the black heads tilt a fraction of a degree, tracking. The frilled ones spread their dark mesh wider, increasing surface area by a third, and you can hear them if you are close enough — a faint increase in the subsonic thrum as they begin to strip water out of air that has no water in it. They do it electrostatically. The mesh holds a charge that makes the 0.5% humidity want to be somewhere else. Molecules stick, cohere, are pulled along filaments too fine to see and deliquesce into lithium brine at the base. The mast does not store the water. There is nowhere to store it. It uses it instantly — to cool a junction, to mobilize a salt, to pass along to the thing growing at its foot — and then it is gone again.

At about 14:00, the hottest hour, many of them do something else. The light between them thickens. They bend it. You can see the bars of brightness hanging in the air between stalks like solid glass, a trick of refractive indices engineered into the skin of the leaves. They are photosynthesizing, but also photosynthesizing *each other*, sharing excess irradiance from mast to mast as a standing wave. During this hour the whole field goes quieter. The ticking stops. They are full.

At night they excrete. If you come back to the same mast the next morning, there will be a lip of fresh ceramic at its base, warm, still slightly translucent, extruded overnight from its own body. A new rootlet. A new bud. It has grown a millimeter while you slept. If it is damaged — if you snap a wire loop — the neighbors lean in. Within a week the gap will be closed with new growth, not repaired, *replaced*.

They flower rarely. Once every few years, a mast will telescope up twice its height overnight, throw out a head of dark glass that unfolds like a fern, and for three days it will hum at a pitch you can hear, broadcasting nothing but its own genome on static. Then it will shatter into dust that the wind takes. The dust is spores. Ceramic spores, glass spores, filament spores. Most fail. A few find a crack in the salt and start again.

This is what covers the planet. A prairie that built itself.

### The Ones Who Live in the Cracks

At the foot of every mast, where the brine briefly forms and where the shadow is a line no wider than a finger at noon, there is the second thing.

You called it vegetation. It is not. But it is the closest thing this world has to a plant.

They are called freckles if you need a word. Flat dendritic fans, rust-red, amber, dried-blood brown, spreading across the pale ceramic in the lee of the stalks and threading the hex-cracks of the lithium flats. Each one is a few centimeters across, flat as paint, dry and hard to the touch. Look closely and it is not one thing but a lace of wires finer than hair, branching and branching again, tipped with those tiny glass beads you noticed.

The beads are lenses. Each one focuses the brutal sun to a hot point on the wire beneath it. Not to burn, but to bake. The fan eats dust.

Wind-carried mineral dust — silica, lithium salts, flecks of ceramic shed by the masts — settles on the fan. The lens heats it. The wire beneath sweats a microscopic bead of the brine the mast made for it, dissolves the grain, and draws the ions in. Photosynthesis is almost secondary; the light is used mostly to make heat and charge to do this lithophagy. They are stone-eaters who use the sun as a furnace.

They grow a millimeter a year, outward at the edges, dying at the center where they turn pale and powder away into more dust. You can age them like tree rings. Some of the larger fans in the sheltered basins are centuries old, a handspan wide, their centers a little crater of their own leavings. When the heat peaks they retract the beads — a slow curling of the wire tips downward, like an eye closing. At the cold hour before dawn they open again and glisten, not with dew, but with brine, hygroscopic and brief.

They are sparse the way desert lichen is sparse, and for the same reason: the economy cannot afford abundance. But because there are trillions of masts, there are trillions of freckles. From ankle height the plain is not white at all. It is white freckled red, a living stipple that holds the dust down and holds the salt from blowing away. Without them the wind would have buried the masts long ago.

They are food. Everything else eats them, or eats the brine they make.

### The Ones Who Move

The animals are rare because movement is expensive when water is a rumor and shade does not exist.

You will not see them until you stop looking for them. They have had eons to learn the best camouflage on this world is to look like another mast.

The first kind perches.

On the head of a solar fin, perfectly still, is a thing the size of your palm that is not part of the fin. A folded kite, translucent amber, with veins of dark filament running through it like circuitry. It holds itself open to the sun with the same angle as the leaves around it. If you breathe on it, it does not fly. If you watch for an hour, it will shift — a twitch, a re-aiming — and you will realize it has legs, four impossibly thin stilts of glass that grip the ceramic.

It is a capacitor grazer. It presses its belly to the masthead and siphons charge, the static differential the field builds all day to pull water from air. It drinks electricity the way something else would drink blood. It also scrapes freckle-dust from the mast's stem with a soft, dry mouth and stores brine in a bladder no bigger than a tear. It can go months without moving. When it must move, it doesn't walk — it lets the wind take it. It unfolds, becomes a kite indeed, and tumbles a few meters to the next mast on a gust that never seems strong enough to carry it. At dusk, hundreds of them will lift at once on the thermal sigh that runs through the ground, a brief, silent migration of living glass that lasts a minute and then is over.

The second kind does not perch. It runs.

You see it only on the salt flats, where the hex-cracks make a maze. A white line that is moving *against* the wind. Crouch, and it resolves: a low, many-legged skimmer, so thin and so white it is almost just the crack itself given legs. Its body is a filament stretched tight between two dozen needle feet, its back dusted with silica to reflect sun. It never stops. It skates from crack to crack, lapping at the freckle fans with a tongue like a loop of wire, and pausing only to press itself flat into a crack during the worst heat, becoming invisible. It lays no eggs. It carries its young — three or four beads of amber — under its belly, where they sip its brine.

There is a third kind you will probably never see, but you will feel it. At night, when the ceramic pours its stored heat back into the air and the ticking is loudest, put your ear to the ground. Far below, deeper than any tool you brought can cut, something sighs through the laminated layers. A maintenance worm, blind, pale, as thick as your torso and kilometers long, that never surfaces. It swims through the old pipeways and lattice, dissolving dead mast-roots and extruding new ceramic behind it, re-knitting the plain from the inside. It is the reason the world is still flat after eons — the reason erosion ever finished. It eats the city's own dead weight and smooths it. You will never see it. You will only know it passed because a line of masts a kilometer long will dip and rise the next morning like wheat after something has moved beneath.

---

None of them need you. None of them notice you except as a brief, inconvenient shadow and a source of water vapor from your breath that causes a dozen freckles and a capacitor kite to lean toward you for an hour after you leave.

They are not numerous. The plain cannot support numbers. A square kilometer might hold two skimmers, a few hundred kites, and a freckle coverage of three percent. But a square kilometer repeated without interruption across a planet is still an abundance beyond counting. Vast, bright, warm, humming, patient.

If you lie down between the bristles at dusk, as the light goes rose and the long shadows connect into script, you will feel them adjust around you — a mast leaning a degree away to avoid your heat, a freckle curling its lenses shut, a kite above you folding tighter so as not to be seen. The world making room, and then, very gently, closing it again.

This is what lives here now. Not the builders. Not the city as it was meant to be. Something that learned to live on sunlight and dust and static and time, and found that was enough to make a prairie out of a roof that forgot it was a roof, and to keep it, quietly, forever.
