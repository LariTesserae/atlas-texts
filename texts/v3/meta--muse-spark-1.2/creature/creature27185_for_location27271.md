It doesn't look like anything lives here at first.

That is the first trick of the place. You stand still long enough for your own sound to settle — for the shush of your feet to stop displacing the film — and you decide the basin is empty. The mineral crust is unbroken except where you broke it. The water is too warm, too saturated, too still. Nothing green holds. Nothing moves quickly enough to count as movement. The little walls and the chiseled basin and the three-stone cairn look like things left behind by something that finished its work and left.

Then you sit for a while, because the air is warm and there is nowhere to go that isn't also in the basin, and the place starts to unhide what never bothered to hide.

The first thing that lives here is not a who. It's the slick.

Lift any submerged stone — and after a time you will, because there is little else to do with your hands — and its underside is not stone-colored at all. It is coated in a film a millimeter thick, translucent, warm to the touch, the color of weak tea or of skin after a long bath. It trembles but doesn't drip. It smells faintly of iron and of eggs, not rotten, just mineral. This is the green that isn't green. This is the pasture here.

It grows on every surface that stays wet and out of the air, precipitating silica and lime around itself, making that brittle eggshell crust at the waterline as a byproduct of its own breathing. Put your thumb to it and it slips. Leave it alone for a day and the print of your thumb will be filmed over again. It photosynthesizes without light, or chemosynthesizes, or does something so slow you could not call it eating. It is everywhere and it is barely anything, and everything else that lives here lives off it.

Which is not much.

The beetle lives here, but not really. It rows across a pool that is exactly its size, dives, surfaces, and will be gone by morning. The lizard on the rim lives here the way a visitor lives in a bathhouse — perched on the one dry stone, borrowing the heat, watching the midges rise and fall in their narrow column over the warmest seep and then leaving again. They are not inhabitants. They are mistakes that wandered down the imperceptible slope and have not yet realized they should wander back up.

What *lives* here has learned not to wander.

You see it by not seeing it for a long time.

There is a shape on the far side of the chiseled basin that you took for a stone. It is low and broad and wet in a way stone is wet, but with a different kind of wetness — a sheen that beads and breaks. It has been there the whole time you were looking at the water. You thought it was the raised threshold of the shelter, or a slab that had settled a little higher than the others. Then it breathes.

And once you have seen one breath, you see the rhythm of it, so slow you missed it. In. Out. One every twenty, thirty seconds. Condensate gathers on it and runs off in the same vertical stains that run off the rim.

They are not many. Maybe three. Maybe four. In a bowl three hundred meters across, four is abundance.

They are people the way the stones are stone — which is to say, they have become the local version of it.

They are low. If they stood — and they rarely stand fully — they would come to your chest. But they don't stand. They crouch, they recline, they steep. Their legs are short and bowed, their feet wide and splayed, the toes spread like a lizard's to distribute weight over cobbles that would otherwise tip. Their arms are long in proportion, the hands broad, the fingers thick and blunt, the nails soft and yellowed and worn down to the quick from scraping.

Their skin is what you notice most, because it is not skin the way you expect skin. It is pale — not white, but the pale of that other rock that sweats, a limestone-pallor, a silica-pallor — and damp always, always beaded. It has no hair. Hair would hold water and stay wet and rot here. Instead their scalps and shoulders and backs carry the same thin mineral crust that grows on every waterline. It flakes at the joints — elbows, knuckles, the back of the knees — in pale, papery scales, and reforms. In the dim, diffuse light their heads look crusted, like stones left half-submerged. When they soak — which is most of what they do — the crust softens and sloughs in sheets, and underneath the skin is pink and new and tender for an hour before it begins to mineralize again.

Their eyes are open, but filmed. A nictitating membrane, clouded, that they rarely blink. It protects against the constant steam. You think at first they are blind. They are not. They watch the tick… tick… of the seep, they watch your feet, they watch the midges. But they do not move their heads to look. They move very little at all.

When they do move, it is with a deliberation that makes you hold your breath without meaning to. One of them, in the chiseled basin, lifts a hand from the water. The water sheets off it without a splash, just a soft displacement. The fingers curl. On the pad of each finger the whorls have been worn smooth. He — she — it — brushes at the lip of the basin where the crust has grown too thick and is beginning to choke the little feeder channel. A few careful scrapes with the nail. A pale flake comes off and settles. The flow, which had been reduced to a beading, resumes to a thread. The hand lowers again and is still for a long time.

That is work here. That is all the work there is.

They tend.

They keep the feeder channels open with those soft nails. They nudge a cobble back into place when warm mud beneath it has settled a finger's width too low and a pool is draining wrong. They lift a stone, lick the biofilm from its underside with a broad, pale tongue, and set it back exactly as it was, oriented the same way. They do not eat otherwise. They drink the seep where it weeps from the rim, cupping water in both hands, sipping warm mineral water that would give you diarrhea and, in them, closes the circuit. They absorb so much through that thin, crusting skin that you wonder if drinking is a habit or a ritual.

They do not speak. Or they speak so quietly and so seldom that the basin swallows it. When one shifts its weight in the water you hear a faint *eh* — not a word, a sigh as steam is displaced from lungs adapted to wet air. Sometimes two of them are in adjacent pools and they will make that sigh at almost the same time. Sometimes they will rest their foreheads together, crust to crust, and stay there until the condensate runs down both faces in one joined drop.

They were not born elsewhere and brought here. They were chiseled here the way the basin was chiseled, softened by tool marks that have since dulled. The fire-blackened stone in the three-walled shelter is theirs, but they haven't needed fire in longer than their own memory, which is not long in the way yours is long. Their memory is mineral: where the warmest seep is in the coldest hour, which slab holds heat until dusk, which pool grows the thickest film after it has been scraped. They remember the cairn by adding to it without thinking and falling it without mourning. The fourth stone fell and they left it fallen because that is now its stable form.

They reproduce as quietly as everything else. Once, maybe twice in a life that is long because nothing here hurries a metabolism to its end, one of them will curl in the warmest, stillest basin — the one no deeper than your ankle but steady to half a degree — and release a cloud into the water that you would not see unless the light caught it. Another will add to it. For months after, that pool is left utterly alone. No one scrapes it. No one drinks from it. If you lifted the stone at its center you would find a clutch of beads, translucent, each no larger than a pea, clinging to the biofilm. Most dissolve. One or two do not. When they hatch they are already the shape of their elders, already crusted, already still.

You will not startle them if you stand up now and wade closer. The water will shush around your feet and settle again immediately. They will watch you with those filmed eyes and make no sign that you are a stranger. Because in this place, anything that stays long enough to learn stillness becomes, after a while, indistinguishable from what already lives here.

After an hour with your hands in that water that has been exactly this warm for longer than hands have existed, your forearms will bead the same way. A drop will gather on your skin and fall, ticking against the surface. The circles will spread and vanish. One of them will sigh, *eh*, and you will not know if it was a greeting or just a breath, and it will not matter.

The basin will have one more low, broad, damp shape in it, learning how to settle lower into warm mud, learning how not to leave.
