They do not live *on* it. They *are* it.

At first you think nothing lives here at all. That is the trick of the cold and the dim. The plain looks uninhabited because nothing moves the way an animal should move in water — no darting, no gliding, no single body crossing open ground. There is only the architecture, and the architecture seems to move by itself. A tower shivers. A dome breathes. A wall lengthens an inch while you are looking elsewhere.

Then you hold still long enough for the haze to settle, and the movement resolves.

It was never one movement. It was ten thousand.

### The ones you see first are the smallest

You notice them as a discoloration on the white. A sort of crawling static over the flank of a tower, a pale rash that flows uphill against the current. Only when you drift closer — close enough that the cold finds the seams in you — do you see they are individuals.

Call them masons. They are not shrimp, not exactly, but your mind goes there for lack of a better shape. The length of a forearm, the color of skim milk, almost translucent. No eyes. Where eyes should be there is only a smooth, blank plate of carapace, polished by pressure. What they have instead is a crown of filaments, feathery and constantly trembling, tasting the water for dissolved stone.

Their bodies are lopsided. One forelimb is huge, swollen, ending in a soft, lip-like pad that secretes. The other is narrow, hooked, tipped with a black bristle that weeps acid. This is their entire life: soften, carry, cement. Soften, carry, cement.

They do not walk. They cling and flow. A single mason is clumsy, blindly tapping. Ten masons are a tide. A hundred is a decision. They communicate by drumming on the stone they stand on — a faint *tick tick tick* you feel in your teeth more than hear — and by leaving chemical slicks that say *build here* or *take here*. There is no queen. There is only the slick and the drum.

Watch one tower for an hour. On its current-facing side, a swarm of them has opened a wound. They press their acid limbs to the paper-thin whorls and the calcium foams, softens to paste. Others come behind and scoop that paste with their pad-limbs, roll it into a pellet the size of a pearl, and carry it — waddling on six spindly legs — to the far side of the same tower. There they press it down and vomit a clear cement over it. The wall dissolves and grows at the same time. The tower is not being repaired. It is being moved, molecule by molecule, by a hundred tiny mouths that will be dead before it has shifted a meter. The masons themselves live only a season. The tower has been walking in place for centuries.

They are everywhere but you never see them in open water. To be off the structure is to be lost. If a current plucks one free it tumbles, legs splayed, filaments flared in panic, until it finds another wall or it falls to the silt and keeps walking until something eats it, which is usually just more masons looking for loose calcium.

### The ones you see second are not ones at all

You thought the terraced mounds were earthworks. You thought the palisade of upright tubes fringing each terrace was a fence.

Then one of the tubes withdraws.

The mound is the animal.

Each ziggurat is a colony of worms, thousands of them living head-up in the tubes they've secreted, side by side like organ pipes. Individually they are nothing — a pink thread as thick as your finger, blind, mouthless for most of their life, filtering marine snow with a crown of cilia. Collectively they are a single mason with ten thousand hands.

They build by rippling. A wave starts at the outer edge of a terrace — a blush of pink as every worm on that rim extends a little further and exudes a bead of rust-colored cement and a grain of silt. Then the next ring in does the same, and the next. From above it looks like wind over wheat, except the wheat is made of flesh and the wind is purpose. In an hour they can raise a lip a millimeter high. In a year, a step.

They do not decide to build. They build where the water tastes right — where a neighbor has just built. It is stigmergy, the way termites do, but slower and colder. And they are always building over their own dead. When a worm dies its tube stays, its neighbor widens its lip and seals the corpse in, and that sealed tube becomes foundation. The mound you see is half living fringe, half mausoleum. Cut it open — nothing does, but if you could — you would see strata like tree rings: living pink on top, then grey compacted tube-grave, then older white grave, all the way down to the false bedrock, which itself is nothing but ancient worm-city compressed.

When a dome nearby exhales, jetting silt, the worm mounds all flinch in unison, each crown snapping shut for a second, then reopening. They are shy of turbulence. They like the slow.

### The ones you see last are the slowest

Because you were looking for movement and they barely move.

Among the meandering low walls and the fields of tube-stumps, there are boulders that were not there before. Rounded, lumpy, grey-white things the size of a bathtub, or a small car, prickled with short, blunt spines. They creep a few centimeters an hour. You only realize they are alive when you see the trail behind one — a pale, scraped furrow in the silt where it has dragged itself, and a faint, fresh line of cement at its rear where it has been... paving.

These are the carriers. They are urchins, or cousins to urchins, but their backs are flat and broad like a table. They do not grow their own shell. They steal it.

A carrier will spend weeks grazing at the foot of a collapsing dome, using a hidden, rasping mouth to pick up the choicest plates — intact whorl-fragments, perfect tube-rims, shards of sulfur-yellow sponge-wall. It cements them to its back with a living mortar from glands at the base of its spines, overlapping them like shingles, until it is wearing a mosaic of the city it walks through. When its back is full, it walks. Slowly, patiently, up a swell, along a wall, until it finds a place where the water tastes thin — a current-scoured patch where worms are struggling. There it stops, lowers itself, and with a shrug of its spines, releases. The plates fall. Its back is bare and pale. Within days the worms will have built those plates into a new terrace, the masons will have dissolved an edge. The carrier, lighter now, turns and lumbers back toward the ruins to load up again.

It carries the city to itself. It is the only thing here that seems to have any notion of distance.

You understand, after watching them long enough, that there are no species here in the way you think of species. There is only a single metabolism stretched across the plateau, broken into different mouths.

The masons dissolve what the worms raise. The worms raise what the carriers bring. The carriers bring what the masons dissolve. Even the domes that pulse are not buildings but animals — great colonial tunicates grown into a bowl, inhaling water through skin and exhaling it through those perfect round vents to filter the endless snow that falls from the olive-black above. The snow is their only food, the only thing that comes from the sun-rumor world. Everything else is recycled stone.

Nothing is predator and nothing is prey. To be eaten is to be built into a wall. To build a wall is to prepare your own grave. They have no eyes because there is nothing to see beyond twenty meters. They have no sound because water has stolen it. They feel the city as vibration through their feet and taste it as lime on their tongues.

They do not know they live on a high place. They do not know the abyss drops away at the rim, or that above them hangs a kilometer of weight. For them there is only substrate and not-substrate, current and slack, cement and acid, the faint chemical call to *add here* and *take there*. They have been doing it since the plateau was young rock, and the rock is gone. They will be doing it when the last tower has walked itself into dust and that dust has been carried by the carriers to make the last wall.

If you stay long enough the cold gets into you and you stop trying to count them. The turbulence resolves into a single, slow breath. The whole high, drowned plain inhales silt, exhales stone, and shivers — endlessly, patiently, without ever waking up.
