You don't see them at first.

That's the trick of a place this full. You expect movement to announce itself - a scurry, a splash - but the trench is so saturated that everything living has learned to move without displacing anything else. At first you just feel watched. Not by eyes, exactly, but by occupancy. Every surface you look at is already being used.

Then your eyes adjust to the scale of the small.

The air just above the moss is not empty. It shivers. Clouds of midges and blackflies and caddisflies hold in place in the cold, rising and falling a few inches at a time, their wings making no more sound than dust. Water beetles dimple the black pools and vanish under sphagnum mats. Leeches ribbon under leaves. On every hummock, springsnails bead. The peat itself, if you crouch, is moving - mites, springtails, the pale worms that turn the dead moss into more peat, all working so slowly in the cold that you have to be still a long time to see them.

Under the water, the same density continues. The ponds are dark tea, tannin-stained, but if you kneel on a causeway and wait, the pale shapes resolve. Brook trout and sticklebacks, white-bellied and sluggish with cold, holding in the deeper channels. Tadpoles in clouds. Freshwater mussels half-buried in silt, filtering. Nothing darts. Nothing needs to. The water is cold enough to hold its oxygen and still enough that a fish can hang motionless for minutes and not be carried anywhere.

Above you, the willow-thickets tick.

Another city layered on top. Forty, fifty nests to a single crown - you thought that thatch was dead leaves until one shifts and a white-throated sparrow slips out. Warblers, jays, boreal chickadees, thrushes, ravens that have gotten small and quiet here. They don't chorus. They call in short, flat notes that don't carry far in the damp air, a contact call and an answer, a fledgling's thin beg. The branches are latticed with old nests and new nests and nests that have been repaired so many times they've become part of the branch. Spiderweb and lichen and down felted together. When the wind brushes high above on the rim, a feather falls and never hits ground - it catches on moss, on a leaf, on another feather.

All of that is just the background. The infill between.

The citizens are the ones who made the rooms for everyone else to fill.

You hear one before you see it. A soft displacement, not a splash. A V on the water behind a dam that wasn't there a second ago. Then a head.

A beaver down here does not look like the solitary animal from field guides. It looks like a worker who never stopped. Big - thirty, forty kilos in late summer, even bigger before winter - but low and smooth in the water, only the flat of the head and the back showing, the fur so dense it's beaded with water that never seems to soak in. Dark brown to almost black when wet, scarred on the tail, the incisors a deep, iron orange even at a distance. The eyes are small and set high, always just above the waterline. The tail, when it lifts, is not a paddle so much as a trowel - broad, scaly, muscular at the base.

You never see one alone for long. Where you see one head, there are four more under the water, or behind the lodge wall, or in the canal you haven't looked down yet. They live not as pairs but as extended families that have become neighborhoods. Six, ten, fifteen to a lodge complex, kits from this year still the size of house cats, yearlings with pale new gnaw scars on their teach, adults whose whiskers are crusted with dried mud from packing. The old ones have heads gone grey and fur worn thin on their bellies from hauling. They all look alike until you watch them work and you see the division without rank - one gnaws, one carries, one dives and packs mud with its forepaws like a mason, one sits half-out of the water and combs its fur and watches.

They don't build the way you build. They don't plan. They respond. A leak sings - a higher trickle over a dam lip - and within the hour a beaver is there with a mouthful of willow cuttings, pushing them into the flow, then another with mud scooped from the bottom held against its chest, tamping it in with its chin. They weave living willow through dead sticks so the wall takes root and grows stronger. A lodge wall slumps and they don't tear it down; they plaster over it, and sphagnum colonizes the plaster, and a spruce seed lands and sprouts, and the roof of the house becomes a tree. Some of the oldest mounds have trees twenty feet tall growing from them, roots knotted right through the sticks and mud like rebar. If you cut a cross-section you would count generations in layers - stick, mud, root, stick again.

Between and beneath them, the other mammals have learned to live in the beaver's architecture without ever being invited or refused.

Muskrats are the second citizens. Smaller, quicker, their lodges tucked against the flanks of the larger beaver mounds like lean-tos, their entrances lower, barely above waterline. They run the under-city. Where beaver canals are avenues, muskrat tunnels are the alleys and service corridors, braiding through the peat mat, undercutting causeways, connecting pond to pond where a beaver would have to go around. You see their push-ups in winter ice, and in summer you see the ripple of their wake where no beaver wake should be - too narrow, too fast.

Voles and water shrews and lemmings take what the muskrats leave. Their runways are pressed into the sphagnum on top of the causeways, tiny highways no wider than your thumb, kept open by constant feet. A moose is the exception that proves the rule about weight. It should sink here. Instead you find its prints deep and clear in the peat, each one filling slowly with dark water, and you realize it has learned to walk only on the oldest, most thickly root-bound causeways - the beaver roads that will hold it. It feeds standing chest-deep in a pond, its breath steaming, stripping willow with a prehensile lip, dropping half-chewed leaves that the fish and insects finish. Marten and mink slip along the same causeways, low and liquid, using the dams as bridges. You find their scat full of vole fur on a flat stone that is also a dam cap.

None of them are tame. None of them are wild in the way an upland animal is wild either - flighty, scarce. Here abundance has made them calm and economical. A beaver will surface ten feet from you and look at you with that flat, high-eyed stare, assess that you are not water and not wood, and go back under with barely a ripple. A warbler will feed its young while you stand beneath it. They can afford proximity because there is nowhere else to go and no need to waste heat panicking. Even fear is done quietly. When a beaver does slap its tail, the sound is not a crack but a dull thud, peat and water swallowing it. The kits dive. The birds go still for thirty seconds. Then everything resumes.

At dusk - though dusk is long and pale this far north - you start to understand the real inhabitant isn't any one of them.

It's the weave itself.

No beaver lives alone, no lodge stands alone, no dam holds without the pond behind it and the canal beside it and the willow that roots it and the moss that seals it and the muskrat that drains it and the trout that keeps the insects from choking the water. The trench isn't inhabited by a city. It *is* the city, and the animals are its moving parts. The water decides, the cold preserves, the plants bind, the teeth cut, the paws pack. Each generation doesn't build anew; they maintain and thicken what their grandparents left, raising the floor of their world a few millimeters of peat at a time.

If you stay long enough on that causeway that is also a roof, with water seeping into your boots and midges finding the warmth of your face and the willow leaves dripping cold condensation onto your shoulders, you stop looking for residents as separate things. You feel the whole trench breathing around you, slow and wet and packed, repairing a leak somewhere downstream, growing a little moss over a fresh gnaw-mark, shifting its weight evenly so nothing sinks.

And you realize you are the only thing in it that is not part of it.
