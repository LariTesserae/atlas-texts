Who lives here is not one thing. It's a density.

Nothing that lives here should be able to live here, and everything does.

At first you think - in this cold and this dark, with the cloud clamped down and the rock still raw - it should be empty. A lichen place. A wind place. And if you look only at the high shoulders, it is. Snow and black rock and the beard lichen hanging like wet laundry, waiting.

But down in the bowl, where the ground doesn't freeze, the valley is crowded past carrying capacity. It lives the way a culture plate lives.

#### The Foundation

The real inhabitant - the one everything else stands on - is not an animal at all. It's the mat.

A meter thick and alive through its whole depth, sphagnum and feathermoss and liverwort and star moss knit so tight you can't tell where one plant ends and the next begins. It holds ninety percent water by weight. Step on it and it sighs and springs back. Tear a handful up and it comes away in layers: green on top, then rust-red dying, then black, then peat, then peat that is no longer really plant but not yet coal.

That mat *is* the valley floor. It breathes. Under a hand lens it's a forest in miniature, stems like miniature spruce, leaves that are single cells thick, all of them beading and shedding condensation constantly. In its lower layers, where there is no oxygen and no light, it ferments. Methanogens bubble. The whole bowl exhales slowly, a sour-sweet smell of rot and growth at once. Beetles no bigger than a pinhead - black ground beetles, rove beetles by the million - tunnel through it. Spiders sheet their webs across its hollows. Springtails detonate in white puffs when you disturb it. It never goes dormant. Even at minus three, with frost on your shoulders, if you push your hand into the moss it is warm.

Out of that mat, everything else grows.

#### The Plants That Tolerate Everything

The trees here aren't visitors. They are residents who have learned to be small and close.

Black spruce - *Picea mariana* - and stunted larch, ten meters at the tallest, most half that, trunks no thicker than your thigh but packed stem-to-stem so the wind can't get between them. Their branches interlock and hold snow even when it should fall. Their needles are rimed half the year, dripping the other half, and still they photosynthesize in that sourceless amber gloom because they don't need direct sun, they just need *enough*.

Under them, what should be understory *is* the forest. Dwarf birch and willow that never get above your knee but form a thicket so dense a caribou has to force its chest through. Labrador tea with leaves curled against the wet, crowberry, lingonberry, bog rosemary. And cotton-grass in every pool margin, going to seed in white flags that catch on your coat and on caribou hair and never seem to disperse far enough.

None of this is exotic. A botanist from Labrador or Siberia would name every species in an afternoon. What is strange is the *abundance* in the dark. The way the saxifrage flowers out of season in a perfect ring around a warm spire, white petals open to a cloud that gives no sun. The way ferns unfurl in January because the ground at their roots is nine degrees. The valley is keeping its plants awake. They live tired, and fast, and continuously.

#### The Swarms

If the mat is the body, the midges are its breath.

You notice them last and then you can't stop noticing them. Columns of them, chironomid midges and blackflies, hanging in the air over every active spire where snow has melted and water is open. They shouldn't be flying at minus two. They are, because the spire's heat-haze makes a chimney of warm, still air a meter wide and twenty tall, and in that chimney algae bloom and bacteria bloom and the midges hatch in hours instead of weeks.

They live for a day, maybe two. Just long enough to mate in the column, lay back into the warm pool, die and fall. The air in those columns is thick enough to inhale.

And because they are there, the birds never left.

#### The Flocks

This valley should be silent in winter. Instead it roars with birds who have decided not to migrate.

Redpolls and snow buntings in flocks that move like a single organism - five thousand strong, lifting off the peat with a crackle like static, circling once under the cloud, dropping again into the spruce. They feed on the midge columns and on spruce seed and on the endless drift of cotton-grass.

On the black pools, waterfowl in numbers that look like a mistake. Canada geese, teal, scaup, long-tailed ducks crowded shoulder to shoulder on water that would be ice without the spires' bleed. They gabble without pause, and the low cloud reflects the sound back down until it's hard to tell if you're hearing the geese or the echo of them.

In the sedge, the secretive ones: sora rails and American bitterns calling, leathery, amplified calls that carry absurdly far in cold wet air. Snipe drumming. And above, ravens and a few rough-legged hawks, waiting.

None of them are tame. None of them are unafraid. They are just too numerous and too busy to care about you. You are another large mammal pushing through the willow next to the caribou. They flush at ten meters and settle at twenty.

#### The Herds and the Hunters

The large mammals live as if this bowl is the entire world.

Caribou - not the tall migratory barren-ground kind, but a smaller, peat-adapted woodland form, coats long and shaggy and matted with ice and burrs, antlers still in velvet though it is deep winter. They move in tight groups of twenty or forty along the only walkable terrain: the sharp backs of the basalt ridges where the moss is thinner. Their hooves are splayed wide, made for this sucking ground; each step pops and sucks, leaves a print that immediately fills with black water. They eat lichen by the mouthful, scraping beard lichen off spruce, and they keep the willow browsed to knee height.

Where the caribou go, lemmings and voles have already been. Their runways are everywhere under the moss, a braided highway system of clipped tunnels you only see when you peel the mat back. In a square meter there are twenty entrances. They don't hibernate. They live in the warm thatch and eat the base of the sedges all winter.

And where the lemmings are, the foxes are. Arctic fox, winter-white but stained peat-brown at the paws and muzzle, barking that sharp, almost birdlike bark. They trot the ridges with no real stealth, because they don't need it. There's food under every tussock.

Wolves would live here too, or will, when the valley is older. Right now there isn't enough dry ground for a den that won't flood by morning. You find their tracks sometimes coming down off the shoulders - a single animal passing through, not staying.

Everything that lives here is North American boreal. Nothing has extra legs or extra eyes. If you field-dressed the caribou it would be exactly a caribou. The valley doesn't need monsters. The density is the wonder.

#### The Other Inhabitants

And then there are the spires.

They are not alive, but they *live* here in the way a beaver dam lives in a stream. Inert ones are just piling - matte black, frosted, silent, leaning as the peat heaves them. Active ones are warm to ten meters, humming that subsonic thrum you feel in your teeth, pulsing amber from inside like a slow heart.

They are not mysterious if you watch. They harvest. One kind pulls heat from depth - the gradient between the cold air and the still-warm rock a kilometer down, left over from the planet or from the recent ice. The other kind - the webs you only see when water beads on them - pulls charge from the cloud deck itself, from condensation and friction, and sings when the wind pulls it.

No one tends them visibly. No one lives *in* them. One has fallen and split open on the south slope and its interior is dry and empty but for folded black material like paper, untouched by animals. Birds perch on them. Lichen will eventually cover the inert ones.

They were put here to hold this place at the edge. Without them the water table would freeze solid four months of the year, the peat would lock, the midges would die, the birds would be forced south, the caribou would scatter. With them the valley stays wet and just above zero, a greenhouse running full-blast in a darkness that should be barren.

So the spires don't inhabit the valley the way the caribou do. They *inhabit* it the way a furnace inhabits a house. They make habitation possible and then stand quietly while the house gets loud.

#### What It's Like To Live Here

To live here is to live wet, and crowded, and never quite frozen and never quite thawed.

It is to live tilted toward the warm spots. Everything blooms and hatches and feeds in halos around the spires and then empties between them. It is to live with your feet always wet, your coat always dripping, your breath always hanging and falling again as frost on the next plant. It is to live loudly because the sound never leaves - the dripping, the roar of cloud on ridge, the glassy singing of the webs, the subsonic throb, the gabble of geese that never stops.

It is to live frantically and briefly and then be replaced. The valley is young. No equilibrium has been reached. A pool you waded through yesterday is a peat mat today because a mat slid. A channel carves overnight. A flock eats a midge column to nothing and another hatches over the next spire by evening.

Nothing lives here permanently in the human sense. People come - you can see where a boot has compressed the moss, where a sample core was taken - but they don't stay. There is nowhere dry to stay. The valley is a nursery, an incubator left running. Its residents are the ones who don't need dry, who don't need sun, who can live on diffused light and drained heat and condensation.

Standing still in it long enough that the cold comes up through your soles, you realize you are not watching a wilderness. You are watching a farm that has gotten away from its farmers. A peat bog that has been told to *grow faster*, and has obeyed, and now is growing so fast and so thick and so loud in the dark that it has become its own place again.

It will live whether you stay or not.
