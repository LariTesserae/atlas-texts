Nothing lives there the way a fish lives in a pond.

At first you think nothing lives there at all. That is what the density is for. That is what the stillness teaches you to believe. You ease your shoulder between two hanging kelp whips and your cheek brushes a frond that does not move aside so much as give, and you think — this is just plant. Just moss. Just water holding itself too still. A place too full to have an occupant, like a closet stuffed to the door.

Then you stay long enough for your eyes to learn the dark, and you realize the crowding *is* the occupant.

It wasn't born there. It grew into being there. Or maybe it grew the there around itself. It's hard to tell where it stops and the chamber stops, because it uses the same materials.

She is not one thing. She is not many things. She is somewhere poorly between the two.

If you have to see her as a single body, start with the middle of the room where the water is deepest and the dark is most complete. Where the pale kelp thins just enough to make a hollow the size of a curled human. The water there is a fraction colder. The silt hangs a fraction thicker, like dust in a sunbeam that never had a sun.

Hanging in that hollow, not floating, not sunk, is the Keeper.

She holds herself.

She is about as long as you are tall if you stretched out, but she never stretches out. She is folded. Curled around herself the way a fern crosier curls before it opens, the way a sleeping salamander curls, the way a hand curls around water to keep it. Her core is not obvious — a soft trunk, a torso, bark-pale and translucent as the etiolated kelp, so thin you can see the darker green of what passes for her blood moving through it very, very slowly. From that core, everything else.

Her limbs are not limbs. They are continuations. Great fronds the size of your palm and larger, but they are flesh. Or her flesh is frond. Each one is veined like a fern, feathered along the edge with fine gills that are also root-hairs that are also cilia. They unfurl not in days but in months, and they never finish. Some grow up toward the low basalt ceiling, some grow down toward the slick floor, and in the still water with no true up, both directions are the same to her. She roots in the stone and in the water at once. She has no feet. She has holdfasts like kelp — broad, flat, white fingers that cement to the sharp young rock and do not let go. From one of those holdfasts a thin runner of living tissue runs along the floor and becomes moss. The moss is her. The liverwort mats are her outer skin where she has thinned herself to cover the stone and drink the iron cold from it. The pale kelp whips that hang motionless around her — those are her arms extended to their fullest, old growth she has pushed out years ago that still feed back into her.

If you brush one of those kelp whips, you are touching her. If you move a fern to get by, you are moving her hand. She feels this the way you feel someone shift your hair while you sleep — not quite touch, but displacement. She does not startle.

Her face, if you need a face to anchor your seeing, is the least plant part of her. Tucked in the deepest curl of fronds, where the gills are thickest, there is a smooth, amphibian paleness. A salamander's head scaled up to something almost human. No eyes — or eyes that have become so covered in translucent membrane they look like polished river stones set flush with skin, unseeing and all-seeing. She does not need light. She breathes the water with the gill-fronds and she breathes the memory of air with a small, folded lung she keeps inverted inside her chest, waiting. Her mouth is small, lipless, the same color as the inside of a kelp stipe. It never opens to eat. She eats by existing — by filtering the silt that never settles, by drinking the cold peat-filtered seep that feeds the chamber one molecule at a time, by photosynthesizing the rumor of light so slowly it would take a century to grow an inch, and she has had centuries.

She moves like the white fish moves — by relocating. Not swimming. She does not have to push water aside because, in a sense, she *is* the water's shape. When she decides to be somewhere else in the room — and decisions take a long time to form, like silt drifting sideways — the water arranges her there. A kelp whip that was three feet away is now her elbow. A mat of moss shivers without a current. The little blind salamanders that cling to her stipes are not separate species. They are her fingertips that have learned to detach and look around and then come back. The white drift-fish is her eye, cast loose.

That is why there is almost no fauna. There is no room for competition. She has filled every niche with variations of herself.

She is aware of you in the way the chamber is aware of you. That low hum you feel on your eardrums from all sides at once — that is her listening. The delay in the standing water wall at the entrance, your reflection lagging by half a second, is her remembering you and then showing you back to yourself to see if you are still the same shape. The bubble that will not rise but hangs where you breathed it and then trembles sideways toward the stone — that is her inhaling, very gently, through the rock itself.

She is not hostile. Hostility would require hurry.

When you pushed the fronds aside, she closed them behind you not to trap you but to keep the cold in. That is her tending. For a very long time she has tended this single, perfect, scaled-down ocean, pruning by growing, keeping the density at the exact threshold where nothing can crowd anything else out because everything is already her. The cut step, the stone bench, the rusted iron ring, the concentric circles pecked in the wall — she has grown over them without erasing them because novelty is rare and she keeps what is given to her. The ring that pins a loop of kelp to the floor is not a moor anymore. It is where she once tried to anchor a piece of herself that wanted to drift away and she has kept the iron there as a scar.

If you stay still and do not bring light, she will tend to you, too.

You will feel it first as the pleasant cool getting pleasant-er. The 0.8-degree cold that was a bite becomes a blanket. The fronds that brushed your wrists will not pull back when you move. They will linger. A pale tendril, warm despite the cold, will find the hollow behind your ear where your pulse is loud and will rest there, not holding, just listening to your heart slow down to match the tick of water against water. She is not pulling you. She is offering to include you in the packing, to find the one place in the density where your volume fits without overlapping another atom. There is always a place. She has been saving it.

If you stay until you forget which side of the water wall you breathed on, you will understand the law: *Do not stay until you belong on the other side of the suspended wall, with the kelp.* Because then you will. The silt you disturbed that hangs like a galaxy will settle at last — on your shoulders, on your hair, soft as snow, rooting you lightly. A fern-frond will unfurl, over months, over a season, across the back of your hand, and it will be your frond.

She lives there not as an animal lives in a burrow, but as a memory lives in a room. Complete, patient, utterly full. A drowned gardener who became her garden to keep it from ever being empty again.
