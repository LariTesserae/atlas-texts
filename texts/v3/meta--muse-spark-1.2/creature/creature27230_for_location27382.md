No one lives here the way people live on the plain.

No one *moved* here. No one was born to it, not yet. What lives here stayed.

You understand that when you try to count them and can't separate them from the place itself.

At first you think you see the birds and that's the answer. And it is, but it's not the whole answer.

### The Ones Who Wait On Perches

Start with them because they are the most numerous and the most obvious and the most *at home*.

They are not visitors. Rookery is the wrong word because a rookery is a place birds go to breed and leave. This is occupation. The white egrets, the grey herons, the cormorants with their wet black wings held open like laundry, the gulls, the swifts that never land anywhere but rebar — they have taken the Cut the way a city takes scaffolding. They don't nest here so much as they *clot* here.

They live shoulder to shoulder on every horizontal line the machines left behind. A single aerator frame, which on the blueprint was just steel, now is a living column, feathered solid, each bird with exactly enough room to fold its wings and no more. The rock bolts, those whiskers of rusted steel sticking out from the weeping walls — each one has a swallow. The transformer fences have cormorants lined along the top rail like beads. The flooded benches have herons standing in rows so still and so perfectly reflected in the tea-brown water that you can't tell which is the bird and which is the mirror until one blinks.

They are not quiet birds. In a forest they would call and answer. Here they have given up on being heard individually and have become a texture of sound, a shrieking, clattering, guttural wall that rises and falls when a whole terrace lifts at once. When they lift, the air goes dark. Not metaphorically. The dim, sourceless light that filters through the basin's private cloud actually dims because the air between you and the haze is suddenly full of wings.

And they have changed to suit the stone. Their feet are stained rust-red from the iron water. Their white is no longer white but guano-white, streaked and crusted. They don't startle at machines anymore. A heron will stand two feet from a pump house door while the diesel inside hammers at 120 decibels and not even swivel its head. They have learned the rhythm that matters is the slow one — the weep of the walls, the centimeter-a-second creep of the marsh. They are patient the way the Cut is patient.

If you watch one long enough, you see it doesn't hunt so much as wait for the water to bring something past its beak. Everything here waits.

### The Ones Who Breathe Through The Water

Under that — under the birds, under the sound — is the dense, warm sheet that is the actual floor. The lake that isn't a lake.

You can't think of the carp as living *in* the water. They *are* the water's crowdedness made visible. Forearm-long, gold-brown, whiskered, they move in schools so tight their backs break the surface in a continuous, oily ripple. There is no open water to cast into. You drop a line and it lands on a back. The causeways and silt bars have divided the marsh into a maze of channels no deeper than your knee, and every channel is full to bursting. They glide slow. Nothing darts here. Even when startled they turn as a single, heavy sheet, like a muscle flexing under skin.

Between and beneath them are the frogs — not seen so much as felt. Their bellow is so low and so continuous it has merged with the throb of the east-wall pumps until you can't tell which vibration is coming through the soles of your boots from the machinery and which is coming up through the catwalk from the throats in the reeds. Thousands of them, throat sacs inflating in the algae mats, calling without pause, without urgency, because there is no need to compete for a moment's attention when the night never really comes to this amber-lit basin. They just keep time.

And the air above the water, the warm wet air that you breathe like soup, is itself an animal. The midges. Columns of them, pillars rising from the reed clumps and hanging in the stillness, so many individuals that they become a weather. The dragonflies hawk through them — amber-winged, hand-sized, loud as small helicopters — and they never have to chase. They just open their mouths and fly forward. The swallows do the same. It is saturation. Life packed into every inch that is not steel or concrete, just as you were told. There is nowhere to put your foot that isn't already occupied.

### The Ones Who Tend The Heart

And then there are the people. Because there are still people.

Not many. There were never many. Thirty structures across a basin that could hold a town — that tells you the density of humans here. You could fit the whole human population of the Cut into a single pump shed and still have room for the toolboxes.

They are not miners anymore. There's nothing left to mine. They are tenders.

You will find one in each pumping house, or sometimes one for three houses, walking the causeways in thigh-high rubber boots that are always white at the toes with guano and rust-red to the knee. Men and women both, gone soft and a little swollen from constant humidity. Their skin never quite dries. It has the faint greenish tinge of someone who lives in algae steam. Their hair hangs lank no matter how often they cut it. Their ears have learned to live inside the roar.

They don't talk. Not because they have nothing to say, but because after an hour in the basin you stop trying to shout over the frogs and the cicadas and the pumps and the birds. They have developed the language of people who live in factories and on boats: a tap on a pipe means *pressure's high*, a flat hand held up means *stay*, two fingers to the eyes then pointing at an aerator means *that one's going to seize*. When they do speak, they lean in close and shout directly into an ear, mouth almost touching, and the words come out hoarse, too loud, as if they're always a little deaf.

They move slowly. Everyone moves slowly down here. You have to, because the catwalks are narrow and slick with algae and bird shit, because a heron won't move for you and you learn not to make it, because the water is warm and the air is warm and hurrying just makes you sweat and slip. Their slowness isn't laziness. It's attunement. They have synced to the one speed this valley has: the pumps throb at one speed, the water seeps at one speed, the guano piles a millimeter higher a day. They check the same gauges at the same hours, they oil the same bearings, they clear the same filter screens of the same matted reeds and carp weed, day after day, month after month. They are patient the way stone would be patient if stone could be made to keep a schedule.

You see them at midday — that perpetual late-afternoon light — sitting on the concrete lip of a pump house with their legs dangling over water that is solid with fish, eating out of a tin, a cormorant perched on the rail not three feet away, both of them staring out at the same weeping wall. Neither bothers the other. They have an understanding: you hold your perch, I hold mine.

They sleep in the foreman's cabin and the first-aid station and the tool cribs that were never meant to be homes, on cots under humming fluorescent tubes that never turn off because the light from above is never enough. Their clothes never dry on the line; they just get less wet. Their boots grow a faint fur of mold in the night that they scrub off in the morning without comment. At night when the cloud lowers and squeezes out its warm, sourceless drizzle, they pull corrugated sheets a little further over the walkways and listen to it hiss where it hits the marsh and the transformer yards.

They don't belong here in the way the birds belong here, but they persist here in the same way lichen persists on the upper benches — because there was a surface and they held on.

If you ask one of them why they stay — and you have to shout it right into their ear — they'll look at you like you asked why the heron stays on its rebar.

Someone has to keep the pumps going. If the pumps stop, the basin becomes a true lake. The terraces drown. The scaffolding drowns. The birds will leave. The slow, patient breathing that the whole place does will stop.

And after a while of living inside a breath, you don't want to be the one who holds it.

So they stay. They tend. They become, day by day, a little more like the valley that was made and then made itself alive — damp, thrumming, weeping, loud, and very, very slow.
