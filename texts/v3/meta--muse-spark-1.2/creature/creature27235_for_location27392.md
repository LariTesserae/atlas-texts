Nothing lives here at first.

That's what you think for the first day. Or the first week. You watch the light breathe and the bales drift down too slow and the leaf-sails tilt toward brightness and you think — *this is a place that grows, but not a place that is inhabited.* The beetle-things are too small, too infrequent to count. The stilt-grazer in the distance is a trick of the canopy. The factory tends itself. The plant is its own keeper.

Then you stay long enough that the absorption fades. Your ears adjust to a silence so complete it has texture, and you start to hear what's inside it.

A *tick* that isn't a leaf expanding.

A sigh that isn't the wind turning a page.

You push through a curtain of vellum leaves — each one as big as a tabletop, warm from the light — and the curtain doesn't fall back quite the way it should. Some of the leaves have been lifted, folded, tied back with filament. Not torn. Not wind-broken. Tied. A neat, small knot of dried vine around a girder-stem, holding a passage open at about shoulder height. The knot is old, the fiber bleached, but the knot is *intentional*.

Someone still uses this path.

You start to see the paths everywhere after that. Not roads. Not trails. *Gaps* — the negative space that remains when something that knows the density intimately moves through it every day and never takes the same branch twice, but always takes the same *give*. A seam in the understory where the felted litter has been pressed down to a firmer felt, a few millimeters more compact than the drifts to either side. A place where the air-roots hanging in a maintenance tunnel have been trimmed back at head-height, the cuts pale and dry and callused over. A conveyor's corky side-growth shorn clean where it would have brushed a shoulder.

Whoever lives here is not many. And they are the same color as here.

You don't see one so much as you notice a place where the plant *isn't*, and then it moves.

They are tall and spare and absolutely dry.

At a distance you mistake them for another stem. That's not camouflage, that's kinship. The people — if that word still fits — have thinned to fit the basin. Gravity is light and the floor gives, and so they have gone light with it. Six and a half, seven feet high and narrow as a loom frame, with limbs that look jointed in one place too many. Not extra joints, exactly, but the *give* in them is wrong — like green wood, like fiberboard under tension. When they walk, they don't step so much as they transfer weight from one holdfast to another, placing narrow, callused feet on floor-plate and on leaf and on stem with equal ease. They drift a little when they drop from a middle-story catwalk to the understory, the way the bales do, as if they have to remember to land.

Their skin is not skin. After generations — or fabrications — breathing air with no water in it, touching nothing but cellulose and warm iron, they have become papered. Pale, matte, dry to the touch, with a fine, short down on the arms and shoulders that is not hair but fiber — the same dusty celadon-white as the straw-blonde leaves, catching the bright sky and glowing at the edges. On their backs and shoulders the fiber thickens into a kind of thatch, overlapping plates like small, living shingles that lift and lower to light. You see them in the upper thatch sometimes, prone on a leaf-sail a kilometer across, utterly still for hours, thatch open, drinking. They don't eat in any way you recognize. They were made — or they made themselves — to do what the plant does: fix carbon from the air, drink light. When they do take in mass, they pare a thin curl from a fresh shelf-leaf with a tool made from a sharpened beetle carapace or a splinter of stem-steel, and they press it between dry lips and let it soften there. No chewing. No thirst.

Their faces are quiet. Small mouths, nostrils that flare very little — the air is so dry and full of mineral dust that deep breaths would be wasteful. Their eyes are large, but lidded against the absolute brightness, lensed with a milky, translucent film, like vellum. They don't call out. Sound dies here, eaten by the felt. To shout would be like shouting into a closet full of coats. So they have learned other speech.

If you stand very still in a dimmer layer and wait, you'll feel it through the soles of your feet before you hear it: *tap. tap — tap.* A knuckle or a heel against a hollow stem. *Tick-tick* against a floor plate. The whole basin is a sounding board if you know how to use it low and slow. A long *creak* as someone leans their weight on a tensioned vine and lets it hum. They talk to each other across kilometers of dense thatch, not in words but in patterns, in *I'm here, I'm moving along seam 7-Green, the press at sector 12 is shedding again*.

They are solitary most of the time. The basin is continent-wide and there were never many of them, and now there are less. You might go days between vibrations. But they are not lonely in the human sense. They are *distributed*. Each one tends a circuit — a district of mill and plant that is roughly the size of a old city — walking it, listening to it, pruning it the way you would prune a bonsai that is also your house and your mother. They clear choked grates, they redirect a cable-root that has started to split a load-bearing plate, they guide a gantry crane that has drifted off its silent rail. Not because anyone told them to. Because if they don't, the growth will close their path, and the light will be less.

And because they *are* the growth, in part.

You see a very old one once, if you are lucky, or unlucky, and you understand the rest. It has stopped walking. It has found a place where the light falls clean through a break in the sail-canopy, where the floor-plate is warm, and it has rooted. Its feet have broadened, frayed into holdfasts that have sunk into the composite, into the rebar-bone below. Its thatch has thickened into true leaves, vast and translucent, trembling faintly. Its arms have lifted and branched. Its face is still there, tilted up to the bright dome, eyes filmed and open, but the paper of its cheeks has gone veined like circuitry, like those leaves that show tiny printed floors inside their veins.

It is not dying. It is fruiting.

After a season under the absolute sky, seams appear along its sides, and dry, light, human-shaped husks — pale and hollow and very light — split away and drift for a long time before they land. And somewhere downwind, in a pressed-paper drift against a stamping press the size of a cathedral, a new thin figure unfolds itself, already the color of straw and iron-white, already listening for taps through its feet.

That is what lives here. Not an animal that moved into a factory. Not a plant that ate a factory. A third thing that learned the trick of both: to be built and to grow, to be quiet and to keep building quietly, to need no water and to make its own floor as it goes. The basin inhales light, and they inhale it with it, and the mills press and the spindles spin, and the low, endless, thrumming world goes on being itself, tended by its own dry children who are never far from becoming walls.
