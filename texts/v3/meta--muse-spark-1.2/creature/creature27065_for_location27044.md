Nothing with a face lives there.

That's the first thing you have to unlearn. You go down looking for an inhabitant the way you look for an animal in a burrow - something that sleeps in the center and comes out to feed. There is no center-creature here. Not anymore. If there ever was, it left before the concrete finished curing.

What lives there lives the way a wound lives. Not *in* it. *As* it.

At first you think nothing lives there at all. The emptiness is too precise. A thousand doors and no footprints in the moss. A thousand windows dark except for the amber filaments that brighten when you pass, as if surprised to be noticed. You hear only water hitting water, water hitting stone, water hitting leaf. No chitter, no wingbeat. You think: abandoned.

Then you notice the moss is not growing *on* the walkway. It *is* the walkway.

You crouch and look closer and the green resolves.

It lives in layers, and none of them need you.

The lowest thing that lives there is the water. Not in the water - the water itself. It doesn't behave like water. It clings to the undersides of platforms and creeps sideways, uphill along electrostatic seams until it finds its designated drip-point and lets go. It bleeds out of the basalt halfway up the walls in perfect vertical lines, as if the rock is sweating on command. It rises two centimeters after midnight and is gone by dawn, pulled through root-mats and culverts and mineral filters and exhaled again as cloud by three p.m. It never pools where it should. It never stagnates. Even knee-deep and black, it's clear if you cup it - warm, faintly mineral, full of suspended filament the width of hair that twitches toward light. It's not a lake. It's lymph. It carries dissolved concrete-milk and spore and dead leaf-sugar from one end of the basin to the other and back again. If you stand still long enough on the central causeway you can feel it moving under your feet, a low hydraulic pulse, systole, diastole. The basin breathes water the way you breathe air.

On that water lives the second thing. The green.

It should not be so lush on so little sun. The sun is a coin straight overhead for an hour and then gone. The rest is reflected glow and filament amber. But the green has learned to eat dimness.

The moss that carpets every stilt and eave is not moss. It's a engineered pellicle, dense as felt, warm to the touch. It glows faintly on its underside where it meets the concrete, trading sugar for mineral. It seals cracks as it grows. It brightens the filaments by feeding them.

The water-ferns choke every canal, their roots not in soil but braided directly into the causeway's pores, filtering the black water until it runs clear on the other side of a building.

And the vines - the vines are the trees here. Their leaves are tables, translucent, veined with light-pipes, spread into umbrellas to catch the column of noon and the amber the rest of the day. They climb the stilts of the vertical hamlets and then keep climbing, past roofs, until they flatten against the underside of platforms and become ceilings. They flower without pollinators. Pale lantern-fruits swell and split at dusk with a soft pop you can hear across the basin, exhaling that sweet yeasty breath, and then they deliquesce by morning. Nothing eats them. They fall into the water, float, dissolve, and are pumped up again. The whole green mass grows an inch a day if you mark it. You can watch a tendril move. It is not a forest. It is one organism with many leaves, a lung spread over an empty city, patient, indiscriminate, and utterly uninterrupted because nothing with a mouth lives there to prune it.

On that green lives the third thing. The town itself.

Put your hand on a wall and it is warm. Not sun-warm. Blood-warm. A slow computation lives in the aggregate - not a computer *in* the building, the building *is* the computer. Hairline cracks bead with mineral milk and seal while you watch. The causeways shiver water off themselves with a faint static that raises the hairs on your forearm. The shutters open at 07:00 and close at 19:00 in every empty room. The streets that run *through* buildings inhale, cycle air, exhale.

The light lives there too. Not bulbs but filaments grown into every overhang, dim amber, conserving attention. They brighten a notch when you approach, dim when you leave. Light that gets more useful the less you look directly at it. You turn your head and the corridor ahead is suddenly visible. You stare at it and it softens, as if shy.

High up, near the sharp lip, the hexagonal tether-frames hang empty in the air and make rain pause. Droplets hang for a half-second, suspended, catching the column of sun like glass beads, before the field lets them fall on schedule. No one programmed the schedule anymore. It programs itself now.

And moving through all of this, tending it, are the only things that approximate animals.

They are not many. Two, three at a time. Caretaker drones the size of doors, silent except for the displacement of humid air, matte white and beaded constantly with condensation that never quite falls. They don't buzz. They glide along the causeways a foot off the moss, extending soft manipulators to tuck a vine, to clear a culvert, to smooth a bead of concrete-milk flat. They are not intelligent the way a person is. They are intelligent the way white blood cells are - they know what *not right* looks like and they fix it. They pollinate the lantern-fruits by brushing them, though the fruits don't need pollination. They pause when you are in the way and wait with a patience that is not programmed but grown. If you don't move, they go around you, leaving a cool wake that ripples the black water.

That is the ecology. Water carrying green carrying building carrying light carrying drone, in a loop so closed it has forgotten it was ever meant to be open.

If there was ever meant to be a fourth thing - people - they are now the least part of it. You might find one. If you stay long enough, far back in one of those vertical hamlets where a street runs through a kitchen built for twenty, you might see a cot with a depression still in it, a cup still warm from the filament-heated shelf, a single pair of boots drying neatly by a cistern. A custodian who stayed, or came back, or never left. He won't speak much. He has learned not to compete with the water for sound. He tends a small plot of real soil he brought up from the surface in a bucket, where real weeds - unengineered, dusty, ordinary - struggle in the too-humid air. He is tolerated the way a basin tolerates a pebble. He is not needed.

Mostly, there is no one. The town keeps itself perfect for none. That perfection *is* the inhabitant.

At night you understand it most clearly. The oval of sky goes black and the filaments brighten just enough to double the city in the black water - one right-side up, one inverted, both empty, both humming that uneven 60-cycle that lives in your teeth. The cloud that formed at three has become bead on every leaf, every rail, every drone-shell, and it all drips at once, a second rain falling upward from the undersides of things. The green exhales yeast and warm air. The concrete exhales heat. The water rises its two centimeters.

You are the only animal in the wound, and the wound looks right through you, and keeps breathing.
