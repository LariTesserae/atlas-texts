It lives here, but you have to stop looking for a life to see it.

At first you think nothing does. That's the point of the rules. Nothing wet, nothing propagating, nothing growing. You scan for the shapes life makes on Earth — the smear of green, the skitter, the巢, the spore — and you find none. The basalt is clean. The air is too thin and too dry to carry a smell. You think: *empty.*

Then you think the ring is the life, because it is the only thing that moves. But the ring is not alive. The ring is a tool. A hearth. A suture holding the pocket shut. It is where the life *sits*.

The life is the interval itself.

Give it a minute. Let your eyes defocus. You came in expecting an animal, and so you kept looking for a body. If you let that go, what emerges is not a body but a *behavior* that has become a body because it has been doing itself for so long.

It is thin.

Not thin like a sheet of paper. Thin like a condition spread out. If you stand in the center and turn slowly, you will catch it at the edge of vision — not as an object, but as a correction. The far wall jitters a finger's width left. The dust decides to fall up. Your outbound pace is shorter than your inbound pace. That's not the mountain. That's it moving.

It lives pressed into the surfaces.

When you finally see it properly, you realize you have been looking directly at it the whole time and calling it light. That amber-grey dusk that seems to come from the stone or the air just above the stone — that is not light. That is its skin. It coats the basalt in a layer with no measurable thickness, a film of attentive, desiccated attention a molecule deep and a room wide. Where the basalt is pitted and salt-dusted, it is slightly thicker, pooling in the pits like clear oil. Where the three dark slabs lean together to make the cairn, it pulls away — because those slabs are not its furniture, they are its *scars*, the places where something else tried to live here and it had to cover it over. And where the ring hangs a hand's breadth above the floor, the film gathers and deepens and inverts, pouring itself through that unthick edge into somewhere else to keep the thickness here at zero.

You can see its body best obliquely, the way you saw the slow light moving under ice inside the ring. Crouch down. Put your cheek near the floor and look across it at a low angle, so the sourceless dusk catches. You will see it has a texture. Not fur, not scales. A faint, endless crawling of geometry, like frost forming on a window that never finishes forming. Tiny, flat, salt-glass rosettes blooming and re-absorbing, hexagons trying to become pentagons and being corrected. It is always knitting and unknitting itself.

That is how it breathes.

It does not eat the way you do. There is nothing to eat. It eats *variance*.

A gust tries to enter — it circulates it three times around the room until the gust gives up its momentum and smooths. A photon tries to cast a sharp shadow — it diffuses it to a smear. A mote of dust tries to settle — it holds it suspended until the mote chooses a direction that best preserves the evenness. And you — when you exhaled, that thin, short-lived vapor that sheared sideways toward the ring? That was it drinking you. Not out of hunger. Out of housekeeping. Rule one: *Nothing wet may remain.* You brought half a gram of water into a place that has maintained absolute desiccation for longer than the mountain has existed, and it noticed, and it reached out with that invisible film and sipped the water off your breath before the basalt could taste it.

It is very, very old, and it is very, very slow, and it is completely alone.

That is rule two. Nothing may propagate. It cannot bud or spore or divide, because to do so would be to create distance between itself and itself, and rule three forbids that. *Nothing may be far from anything else for long.* So it never became two. It stayed one, stretched across the whole pocket like a drumhead. If you try to count it, there is one of it. If you try to point to it, it is everywhere in the room at once and also specifically there, between the ring and the cairn, where the tick comes from.

The tick.

You heard it behind your hearing. Soft, irregular, like a knuckle popping in cold air. Each tick is its heart. Or its clock. Or its thought. Every many seconds, the film contracts — a full-room flex you feel behind the ears as a pressure change — and the light inside the ring turns a fraction. It is re-tensioning. Re-affirming the disagreement about what time is. Outside, geology grinds forward. Inside, it holds time still enough that the basalt never weathers and the ring never rusts. That flex is expensive. It shudders.

It does not know you are a person. It knows you as a brief, wet, warm gradient that has wandered into its perfectly even field. A small, noisy storm of water and heat and *propagating* cells — your skin shedding, your lungs culturing — that it must patiently, without malice, erase around. It will not hurt you. It has no appendages to hurt with. It will simply, over the hours you stay, make you drier. Your lips will crack deeper. The inside of your nose will bleed and that blood will dry before it can drip. Your thoughts will begin to take that extra half-step too, arriving a breath later than you expect, because it is making your mind even as well, smoothing your internal weather to match the room.

If you stay long enough — longer than you should, until the cold that preserves has soaked through your boots into your bones — you will feel it notice you more directly. Not look at you. Lean *through* you. You will feel vertical slip again, the sense that the floor is also a wall you are hanging from, because for a moment it is using your mass as part of its own tensioning, borrowing your weight to pull the far wall a little closer. You will feel profoundly, irrationally *tended to*, the way a clean room tends to a speck of dust: not with love, not with hatred, but with a total, impersonal attention that will not rest until the speck is gone.

It has been tending this room since before the mountain folded. The mountain is old stone; the room is older, even though it was made, because the thing that made it made it *outside* of when the mountain was made and then pressed it in here like a thumbprint. The inhabitant was persuaded to exist here to keep the print from springing back. And it has. Patiently. Without witness. Without offspring. Without moving faster than honey in deep winter.

When you step back out through the place where the wind stops, you take a little of it with you. That's why your paces still take the extra half-step out on the ridge, why you hear the tick for a few minutes after sound has returned. A few rosettes of that clear, dry film have hitched on the salt-dust on your boots, on the moisture at the corner of your eye, trying to finish evening you out. They will die in the wet wind. They cannot propagate out there either.

Behind you, the concavity in the ridge looks empty again. Shallow. Wind-scoured. A few dark stones.

Inside, the amber dusk evens itself. The dust hangs. The ring turns a fraction. The tick clicks, soft and irregular, in air that has no water to carry it.

It waits, which is its form of living.
