Whatever lives here does not live *on* it.

That is the first rule, and everything else follows from it.

At first you think nothing lives here at all. That is the trick of the plateau. You stand still and the green corrects around you, and you see only plant — plant on plant on plant — and you mistake it for emptiness because nothing darts, nothing flees, nothing breaks cover. Then your eye learns the slowness, and you realize the forest is not empty. It is full to saturation, and its inhabitants have all agreed to the same covenant: *become part of the sponge*.

### The Ones Who Hold

You have to start with the holders, because nothing else exists without them.

They are not trees. Trees are an idea from somewhere else where wood is hard and ambition is vertical. Here the great holders rise because water must be lifted, not because light must be won. Their trunks are not wood so much as columnar bogs — ancient basalt-softened cork, veined with living aquifers, swaddled so thickly in epiphyte upon epiphyte that the original bark has not seen air in a thousand years. Ferns root in moss that roots in lichen that roots in humus that *is* the trunk. You could peel a section away like a blanket and find beetles and frogs and entire economies living in the thickness of its coat.

Their leaves are the true architecture. Each one is the size of a table, a door, a small boat, cupped and waxy and tipped with a deliberate spout. They do not photosynthesize so much as *condense*. Their upper surfaces are cold traps. The warm cloud that lives at chest-height touches them and beads, and the bead runs to the tip, and hangs, and falls to the leaf below with a soft tick that never stops — tick, tick, tick — a metronome for the whole plateau. In this way the holders drink the sky without rain.

And in those leaves live the first inhabitants: the Cup-Dwellers.

Every leaf is a pond. An ankle-deep, tea-colored, warm pond held in living ceramic. And every pond is occupied. Glossy, lungless salamanders the length of your finger, black as peat-water, that never touch ground in their lives. They are born, breed, and die in a single cup, and their eggs are glued to the underside of the leaf above so the drip aerates them. Jade beetles row across the surface on legs fringed like oars. Mosquitoes do not bite here — there is no point chasing warm blood when the air itself is a soup — they have become glassy filter-feeders, trailing plumes to sieve pollen. And in the larger cups, where two leaves have fused over centuries into a cistern that could bathe a child, something like a shrimp like a fern like a prayer lives: translucent, ribbed, fanning constantly to keep the water from stagnating, its back a perfect garden of filamentous algae so it looks, at rest, like nothing more than a clump of detritus.

Look closer at a holder's trunk and you see it move, though not as animal moves. The whole shag ripples. That is the Moth Herd passing through. They are not one moth but a dispersal — palm-sized, bark-winged, so perfectly lichen-patterned that when they settle they vanish. They do not fly so much as *drift* on the saturated air, too heavy with absorbed fog to beat quickly. They drink fog. They land on a moss face and unfurl a tongue longer than their body and wring the moss like a towel, and then lift again, a few inches, a few feet, in clouds so slow and dense you could lose your hand in them. Pollen adheres to their fur in yellow drifts. In the dim, their passage is the closest thing this place has to weather.

### The Ones Who Weave

If the holders are vertical, the weavers are horizontal.

They are what live *in* the mat. Not on it, beneath it, inside its yards-deep mattress of moss and liverwort and peat. You walk on them without knowing. Your weight dents the mat a foot down and they feel you as a pressure front.

Most are small and nearly plant. There are pill-shaped isopods the size of thumbs, pearl-white and blind, that carve tunnels through living peat and line them with their own silk to keep them from closing. They farm. They carry spores on their backs and seed the tunnel walls, grazing the fungal bloom that follows. There are vine-things — you will argue with yourself about whether they are animal — that hang from the underside of the branch-continent twenty stories up, a green-brown cord with a drooping tip that never moves when you watch it. Watch it for an hour from the corner of your eye and it has grown three inches toward your breath's moisture, a blind tendril tasting for salt. When a moth blunders into it, it does not snap. It *enfolds*, with the patience of honey, and takes a day to digest.

And then there are the Waders of the sag-valleys, where water gathers not as lake but as a bright skin stretched over reeds. They have mistaken themselves for plants so completely that evolution has helped them. Heron-generalized, yes — stilt legs, spear beaks — but their legs are the green of young reed, their feathers are not feathers but fronded filaments that shed water and collect it, their necks are jointed like horsetail stems so they can stand motionless for hours, swaying slightly as if in wind, even when there is none. Prey forgets they are animal. They forget, perhaps, themselves. When they strike, it is not fast. It is inevitable, a slow collapse like a crane folding. They swallow whole mouthfuls of the sheet-water, filtering out the black tadpoles that churn there.

### The One Who Moves Underneath

Every few hours, you feel it. Not hear — feel, through the soles of your feet, through the yielding mat.

A long, low pressure wave, traveling. As if something very large and very soft were moving through the waterlogged peat ten feet down, displacing it, not tunneling so much as *swimming through soil*.

You never see it. The mat never breaks. But you see its after-effects: a line of faintly raised moss, a day later, where it passed and aerated the peat; a sudden seep-spring opening where it shouldered water aside.

Those who have stayed longer call it the Long Back, or the Silt Whale, or simply *the Sigh*, because its movement is what causes that minute-long slumping sigh when a saturated section settles. It is not a predator. It has no mouth you would recognize. Imagine a colonial thing, a meter wide and twenty meters long, made of muscle and root and hydrostatic skin, like a giant caecilian made of peat itself, that eats by moving — straining microorganisms and dissolved tannins through ciliated grooves along its flanks, leaving behind a trail of richer, looser peat where the mat breathes better. It is the earthworm of a continent-sized earthworm. It is why the plateau does not drown in itself. The holders feed the sky to the mat; the Long Back keeps the mat from suffocating. You do not see it because to see it would require it to stop, and it never stops. It is the slow heartbeat you feel when you lie down.

### The People Who Remember How To Be Slow

And then — the people.

They are still here, but you will not count them if you count as you do elsewhere. They did not fail to build a city. They refused one.

They understood early that fire does not work here. Nothing dries enough to burn, and the air swallows flame. Metal sweats and pits. Glass fogs and grows over. So they built only what the sponge would allow to remain: low, fitted stone sunk to its shoulders, stair cuts that became springs, circles on the faintest rises where the cloud lifts a hand's breadth higher and you can, just, see stars.

Those who remain are not many. Two or three to a basin the size of a county, never gathered. You might pass within a hundred yards of one and think it a mossy boulder beside a seep, for they wear the mat. Capes felted from living moss that continues to grow, hoods that collect condensation and tip it to their mouths, as the leaves do. Their skin is pale and damp and faintly green at the edges, not from dye but from partnership — a thin biofilm of algae that helps them weather the thin, wet air. They move as the waders move. They can stand beside a holder for a whole morning, hand resting on its shag, listening to the tick-tick-tick of water from leaf to leaf, and not be separate from it.

They are not hunter-gatherers. There is nothing to hunt that wants to be chased. They are tenders. They walk the branch-platforms and pinch off a strangling vine here, divert a seep-thread there with a placed stone, freeing a cup that had clogged. They harvest not by killing but by *borrowing*: a scoop of cup-water strained for shrimp, a handful of pollen-pressed cake left to ferment under a leaf, a frond cut so cleanly the holder seals it in an hour. They live in the hollows of leaning, half-digested giants — not houses, but dry-ish nooks where the peat has been coaxed to arch and hold.

Their longest work is memory. Because the plants remember — the moss growing a different way where a warm body once passed — the people have learned to read the mat as a text. They can kneel and place a palm on the rebounding moss and tell you that a Long Back passed here nine days ago, that a holder dropped a limb here thirty years ago, that a child sat here and waited out a cloud two wet seasons past. In the stone circles on the rises, they set stones not to mark territory but to *correct* the memory of the wind, to make a place where the cloud hesitates and the pollen settles, a kind of library of drift. They speak rarely, and when they do, they speak in low, clear notes that the peat will not swallow, notes like the forest's own rare bird calls, allowed briefly and then gone.

If one sees you — truly sees you — it will not approach quickly. It will let you stand until condensation beads on your shoulders, until the vine-feeler tests you and withdraws. Then it will move into your peripheral vision and stand there, breathing with the mat, until you understand you are being mirrored. If you stay still long enough, it might lift a hand, very slowly, and show you how to tip a leaf so the water goes where the moss is thirsty. If you move too fast, it will simply be gone — not fled, but *reabsorbed* — another mossy ridge where a moment before there was a person.

They do not think the plateau is sacred. Sacred implies something set apart. To them, the plateau is a body, and they are a way the body knows itself — brief, warm, careful symbionts who learned, over generations, to keep the ticking going from leaf to leaf to leaf without hurrying it.

That is who lives here, now that the mountains have died and become a sponge and learned to hold the sky close to its chest.

Not masters. Not ghosts. Inhabitants who have all, animal, plant, and the ones in between, made the same quiet decision: to grow slowly, hold water, cast no hard shadow, and never, ever stand out against the green.
