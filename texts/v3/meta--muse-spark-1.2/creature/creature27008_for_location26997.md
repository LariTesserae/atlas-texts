No one lives here the way people live in a town.

At first you think it's empty.

That's the trick of the dampening. The moss eats your footfalls and the leaf-roof eats the light and the dish eats the wind, and the thirty or forty people who are here at any given time are very good at not being seen. You can stand on the lip for a long minute and think the place is uninhabited - just factories that happen to be running themselves, looms clacking with no hands on them, fog draining between green hills that happen to have windows.

Then you catch motion that isn't wind.

A figure in dark waxed canvas between the mill and the cistern house, not walking on the mat so much as wading through it. You only see her because she stopped. If she had kept moving you would have read her as another shadow under the canopy.

That is the first thing about them: they are countershaded.

Not camouflaged on purpose. Worn into it. Their coats are the color of wet bark and oiled iron, their wool is the grey-green of lichen on brick, their boots are black rubber always beaded with water the way every leaf is beaded with water. Their hair, when you see it uncovered - which is rarely, because of the drip - is lank with damp, darkened. Their skin is pale the way things that live under a canopy are pale, not sickly, just un-sunned, with the faint translucence of people who have spent years in cool air. The older ones have a weather to them. Not wrinkles exactly. Barking. The skin at the backs of their hands and at their necks takes on a fine, dry fissuring, like the Weave's own young branches when they harden off. They don't seem to mind. They oil their hands the way they oil a lathe, at the end of the day.

They are not many. If you stay a day you might count eighteen, twenty. You will never see them gathered. Two is a crowd here. Three is an event. They work alone or in pairs, far apart, each tending a sector. You begin to understand the emptiness between buildings is not emptiness. It's territory, divided the way a garden is divided - this person has the north wall of the press hall and the steam run that feeds it, that person has the switchyard and the three gutters that drain it. They move unhurriedly and without wasted motion, because hurry makes you sweat, and sweat here doesn't evaporate.

Watch one up close and the distinction you expect - between gardener and machinist, between farmer and factory hand - never arrives.

A woman kneels on the mat beside an exposed pipe chase. With a pair of heavy, spring-handled shears she cuts back a runner that has gotten too ambitious, the cut exuding clear sap that smells faintly of cut grass and hot metal. She doesn't wipe it away. She lets it bead. Then, with the same hand, she picks up a voltage tester and touches its probes to the terminals of a junction box that the Weave has grown a neat, waxy collar around. The box ticks. She nods, tucks the runner sideways along a guide wire, and ties it with a strip of woven fiber that came, a week ago, from that same runner. A man on the catwalk above her is doing the reverse: he has a can of oil and a rag for the bearings of a belt drive, and while the oil soaks he is patiently coaxing a broad, dinner-plate leaf to angle a few degrees more toward the window, so the pale underside catches the overcast light and throws it inside onto his work. The plant lets him. The leaf stays where he puts it.

They don't talk much. Not from unfriendliness. Sound is expensive here, swallowed by the upholstery of moss. When they do speak it's low, and often not to each other. "There you go." "Easy." The way you talk to an animal or a child or a machine that is being temperamental. Mostly they communicate by leaving signs for each other and for the Weave: a yellowing leaf left to mark a hot valve, a pruned stump dabbed with resin to say *don't regrow here*, a length of twine knotted to say *needs tension*. The Weave answers in its own slow language. A fruiting directly above the compost grinder. A sudden thickening of aerial roots to shade a transformer that was running hot. A new shoot that precisely follows the line of a conduit you were about to lay, as if it had read the blueprint.

That is the second thing about them, and it takes longer to see: they are not the inhabitants. Not solely.

They are the mobile parts.

The true inhabitant is the dish itself, the single thing that is both town and plant and infrastructure. The Weave is not a crop they tend. It is a colleague that has been here longer than any of them. It was planted - the old story says - as erosion control, as a holding stitch when the first brick was laid in the saucer to keep the soot from blowing away. It held too well. It learned brick. It learned pipe. It learned the shape of a factory and the need for a roof that doesn't leak but does breathe. It learned to leave sparrow-holes, to fruit where fruit is useful, to grow its leaves as light-shelves on the east side where the work needs light. No one planned the hexagonal street pattern. No one told the windows to be larger on the east. The Weave optimized. The people, over generations, stopped correcting it and started collaborating.

And so the people have grown to fit the collaboration the way a hand grows to fit a tool that is never put down.

You notice it in small, cumulative ways, the same low-grade weirdness as the town itself.

Their sweat doesn't salt. On a warm day - rare - when they exert themselves carrying pulp-board, their foreheads bead with something thinner and clearer than sweat, almost like condensation, that doesn't sting the eyes. Their boots, left overnight by a door, will have a faint green fur by morning if not knocked clean, and they knock them clean without comment. The cuffs of their sleeves are stained dark green at the crease, not from dye but from handling leaves, and the stain never quite washes out, as if the skin has taken a little chlorophyll in. The youngest, a girl maybe fourteen who tends the beetle annex, has hair that in certain light shows a faint reddish underside, like the underside of the leaves. No one remarks on it. She will have lived here all her life; she was born in a room whose wall is half brick and half living wood, where the air is always a degree cooler than outside and always smells of sap and steam. Her first toy was a fig-like fruit, hard and inedible until ground.

They don't age the way heath people age. The cool, the damp, the slow pace - it preserves. A man can be sixty and look forty, until you see his hands, or see him stand still for a long time in the fog and realize he is standing as still as the plant stands, breathing as slowly, blinking rarely, letting water gather on his shoulders and drip off. They heal like the plant heals - slowly, completely, with a little scar of pale tissue that looks like lichen. They get sick rarely and when they do they go to the warmest place, by the boiler collars where the orange flowers open, and sit.

They are not many because the dish cannot support many. The Weave fruits only enough, the cisterns hold only enough, the air holds only enough heat. When someone dies - which is infrequent - they are composted in the annex behind the mill, with the leaf litter and the failed fiber. No one hides this. It is efficient. A few weeks later a new shoot will come up unusually vigorous near that wall. The people nod at it. When someone is born - also infrequent - the Weave makes room, a root arch shifting an inch over a season to leave a doorway a little wider.

If you ask one of them - quietly, because that is how you ask here - "Do you live here or work here?" they will not understand the question.

They will tell you they *keep*.

They keep the pressure in the pipes. They keep the runners from strangling the gutters. They keep the fog moving. They keep the agreement between brick and bark from fraying. They are not owners, not employees. Caretakers in the literal sense: people who take care, and are taken care of, by a thing that decided, a long time ago, to be a town in order to keep being a plant, and a plant in order to keep being a town.

At night, when the amber work-lights make the whole dish glow like a lantern and the boilers bank but never go cold and the drip from a hundred leaf-tips is the only clock, you can see them at their windows, one per building, each a small, still shape behind glass gasketed with living resin. They are not lonely. The mat thrums faintly with warm water underneath, the canopy rustles once, like a breath, and if you stand in the center and clap, the town claps back - not with sound, but with that same rustle, leaf against leaf.

They feel it in their feet and smile, not looking up.

Of course it answered, their posture says. It lives here too.
