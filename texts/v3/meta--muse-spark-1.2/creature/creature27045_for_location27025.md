There is nothing there that lives *in* the high country. That is the first mistake.

You look for an inhabitant the way you would look for a deer in a wood or a person in a house — a figure against a ground. Here there is no ground to stand against. Everything is figure and everything is ground. If something lives here, it lives *as* the place lives, not on it.

At first you think nothing does. Or nothing you would call a who.

The grazers pass and they are not it. They are beautiful and wrong for it — too separate. Twenty or thirty at a time, low and shaggy with pelts that have gone to felt, matted with living moss that grows in their backs. Their feet are not feet. They are pads that splay until they are almost lily pads, spreading your weight until the sponge doesn't even dimple. They move through the hanging moss by knowing where the moss wants to part, ducking under curtains that other animals would tear. They do not graze so much as mow; their broad, flat teeth shear the cushion-plant and the bamboo shoots and the beard-moss in a single, lateral motion. They steam and they drip and they watch you with eyes that have a nictitating membrane for mist. But they are commuters. They pass through. They do not maintain.

The birds are not it either. The slate-grey flocks that pour through the bamboo tunnels are too fast, too clean. They are a weather effect that happens to have feathers — a single decision divided among a hundred bodies, turning between trunks without a sound because the air is too wet to carry one. They drink on the wing from water that runs along the underside of roots.

Even the dragonflies — those silent hawks with glass wings — are visitors, however permanent.

No. Who lives here does not visit. Who lives here does not move in lines.

You have to stand still until your boots go cold and your shoulders bead over, until the hive hum comes back — that low, dynamo thrum under the dripping — to see them. Or rather, to stop looking for an animal at all.

They are low down. They were always low down.

In the peat walls, where the black pools have cut perfect circles and the iron-bacteria lay down their rust, the peat is not solid. It breathes. If you kneel — and the moss will take the shape of your knees immediately and hold you, a little too warm — you can see the wall is perforated with a thousand small, round mouths, each about as wide as your thumb. From each mouth, warm, faintly sour air pulses. In, out. In, out. Not in unison, but in waves, like a lung being fanned row by row.

The hives.

If you peel back — carefully, because everything here resists being peeled — a sheet of the peat, it does not tear like soil. It peels like flesh, fibrous and red-black, and underneath is the hive itself, and you understand why you thought it was industrial.

It is a city that never finished being a body.

The builders are there, and when you first see one you don't recognize it, because it isn't finished either. Pale, damp, the size of the last joint of your finger, blind, translucent enough that you can see the dark line of peat still moving through its gut. Not ant, not bee, not termite; something that diverged before those distinctions mattered. Its head is mostly mandible and feeler, its abdomen is swollen and almost clear, pulsing with stored oil. It carries nothing. It *is* carrying — a ball of chewed bamboo fiber and fungal paste clutched against its belly, exuding wax as it walks.

There are thousands. Tens of thousands in a square meter of wall. They do not swarm. They flow. Along highways that are not tunnels but pressed grooves in the peat, kept slick with their own secretions. Some carry, some fan — they line the breathing mouths and beat vestigial wing-stubs so fast they blur, ventilating — some stand completely still on the amber fungus shelves and secrete. Their backs open. From a seam along the thorax a bead of dark wax or pale oil wells up, is taken by another worker, is pressed into place.

The hive they build is not comb. It is lung. Vast, sponging lobes of chewed fiber, layered like gills, each chamber bellowsing slightly. The whole structure expands and contracts a few millimeters every few minutes. It draws in mist, it draws in spore, it draws in the methane bubbling up from ten meters down, and it refines. You can smell it — sweet wax, hot iron. On the outer surface of the lung, where it meets the cold air, condensation gathers and runs in engineered runnels back into the moss. Deeper inside, where it is warm from composting and from the breath of the vents, the wax cures to amber and hardens. They cap oil in little bladders the size of grapes.

This is who lives here. Not the builders, exactly. The builders are hands. The hive is the organism.

And there are hundreds of hives. The plateau is stippled with them, under every terrace lip, inside every root-dam, behind every curtain of moss. They hum at slightly different pitches. At night when the bamboo crackles with St. Elmo's fire, you can see the violet discharge run along the peat walls where the hives are densest, because the hives are generating charge themselves — the endless fanning, the movement of wax and water, a static field the size of a county.

They are not intelligent the way you are. They do not have a queen. If you dig — don't — you will not find her. You will find queens, plural and temporary, engorged and egg-laying for a season and then unmade, their protein re-chewed and re-laid as wall. The intelligence is in the ventilation rate, in the way a hive on a cold terrace will run warmer by fanning less, in the way all the hives on a slope will stagger their breathing so they do not pull mist from each other. It is a distributed, slow, thermodynamic thought, conducted in humidity and wax.

But if you think that is all, you have only looked low.

Because who lives here also lives high, and in the middle, and is the reason the low can live at all. You notice it after an hour, when you realize water is running *up* the rock beside you.

The forest-thatch — the alder and iron-bark and bamboo so close they have grafted — is not a collection of trees. Under the moss their roots do not intermix; they fuse. They anastomose. Bark to bark, root to root, until a square kilometer of forest is one grafted mat, one dam. The peat would wash away in a season if it were not held by that mat. The water that sheets down each terrace does not cut channels; it is *run* through channels the forest grew to be channels, root-wheels turning slowly as water braids through living wood, limestone sluices held open by roots that taste the mineral and grow toward it.

And living on that mat, inseparable from it, is the other worker.

You would call it moss if you were careless. It is not. The tight felt of cushion-plant, the ten-foot curtains, the felt that covers a fallen tree before the tree has finished falling — it is one thing, or one family of things so interbred they are one thing. Bryophyte, hepatic, lichen, fungus — names fail up here where everything grows on everything. It is the weaver. Its leaves are waxy and dark not to hold heat for itself but to condense. Each leaf is a tiny cold trap. The hanging curtains are heat exchangers. The weeping rocks weep because the weaver tells them to — its rhizoids exude acid that opens the basalt's pores, its capillaries pull, and water runs uphill along invisible gradients to where the weaver is thirstiest. It lifts water sideways, it lifts it up, it drips it upward into hanging bogs that should not exist. It drinks the cloud that forms around your shoulders and wrings it out.

The weaver is why the plateau does not dry. The hives refine, the trees dam, the grazers mow and fertilize, but the weaver moves the water around like blood.

And when you stand still long enough for the forest to stop editing itself around you, you see that the weaver is also looking back. Not with eyes. With phototropes. On the underside of the hanging curtains, where the light comes from too many angles because the mist itself glows faintly with static, there are patches of moss that are darker, denser, almost black-green, and they turn. Slowly, so slowly you think you imagined it, they turn toward you. Toward your heat. A grazer passes within arm's length and the patches lean toward the grazer, then relax. You breathe and the curtains beside you twitch, just a millimeter, condensing a little more.

They are not watching you. They are *accounting* for you. You are a warm, salty condensation surface that was not there before. You are a new place for water to bead.

If there is a who here, it is not a face. It is this agreement: the hive that breathes and refines, the mat that dams and mills, the felt that lifts and condenses, the geyser that exhales every few minutes and is immediately drunk by the ring of furious green around it, the iron bacteria laying down their orange film like rust on a machine that has never been built.

It has no center. That is why you cannot find an edge to the plateau. It has no head to cut off.

You shift your weight and the sponge sighs. A beetle the size of your palm, iridescent black-green, rolls a ball of peat and wax past your boot — stolen from a hive mouth — and the builders do not chase it. They will make more. A vine that looked like moss, which was moss, closes a fraction of an inch around a midge you did not see land. The bamboo above you crackles, discharges violet, and the mist drinks it.

You understand, cold in your boots and heavy with condensation, that you are not being ignored. You are being *included*. For as long as you stand here, the weaver will condense a little more water because you are here to condense it on. The hive down by your knee will fan a little faster to account for your carbon dioxide. A grazer will step around you because you are now part of the thatch's navigational map.

To live here is to be worked into the working. There is no tenancy. There is only incorporation. Leave your coat on the moss and come back in a season and it will be a hill, felted over, respiring. Stay long enough and the same happens to you — not violently, not as predation. As employment.

The plateau hums, low and patient. Water turns in root-wheels. A geyser sighs and makes warm rain. The lungs in the peat inhale.

It has been working long before you arrived to listen, and it will keep working, quietly, industrially, damply, long after the shape you think of as you has been dissolved and re-laid, without malice or memory, as wax and fiber and iron film in the dark.
