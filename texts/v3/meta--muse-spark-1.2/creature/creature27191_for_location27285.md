They do not look, at first, like inhabitants at all.

If you stand on the grating and let your eyes adjust to the dim, you think the town is empty. You see only the town itself — the scaffolding shivering faintly as the wind goes over, the amber bulbs swinging, a forge breathing orange behind a curtain of sheet metal. You hear the moths more than you see the people. And then something moves where you thought there was only shadow, and you realize you have been looking directly at them for thirty seconds without seeing them.

They are small.

Not small the way a child is small. Small the way something made to fit inside a pocket is small. When one straightens up on a catwalk you see the top of its head comes barely to your sternum - 110, 120 centimeters at most - and it is not stocky, it is attenuated, stretched thin by altitude and scarcity. Everything about them has been pared down to what fits and what lasts.

You don't settle on what they are right away. In the half-light they could be mistaken for the other dry-fauna - another kind of swift, another kind of jointed keeper. They wear the town. Their clothing is not clothing so much as lamination: layers of soot-grey canvas and soft, worn leather and felted moth-silk, all patched and re-patched until the seams have seams, all the exact color of basalt dust and iron oxide. Hoods, wraps, sleeves that end in fingerless gloves black with grease. Masks - not gas masks, simpler - a fold of damp cloth over nose and mouth to catch the breath before the air can steal it. Only their eyes and hands give them away.

Then one turns and catches the forge-light and you see the eyes.

Large, but not owlish. Deep-set, lidded against dust and glare, the iris a washed-out grey or amber-brown, with a faint, bluish sheen when the light hits it - a nictitating film they can draw across, thin as contact glass. They have lived for generations in this dim and they have learned to see in it; the hard bar of sun that cuts the pocket at dawn makes them squint and turn away, they work best in the reflected gloom and the low bulb-light. Their sclera are never white, always faintly bloodshot from the dryness. They blink too often, deliberately.

And the hands. If you see nothing else, you see the hands.

Disproportionate. Pale, but stained at the knuckles and nails with ingrained metal dust that never washes out because there is no water to wash with. Long fingers, the skin thickened into pads that are more like hide than skin - cut, burned, healed, cut again until the fingertips are smooth and nerveless. They can pick up a still-hot rivet and hold it. They can thread a wire through a hole you can barely see. They are never still. Even when the body is resting, perched on a pipe or braced against a bulkhead to save space, the hands are doing something - turning a nut, carding silk from a frame of moth web, rolling a flake of chitin between thumb and forefinger until it is powder.

Their chests give them away as natives. You hear it before you see it - the breathing. In air that thin, at that cold, every breath is audible, a soft, double-drawn *hih-hah*. Their ribcages are barrelled, exaggerated under the layers, like pigeons. When a mask slips you see the lips are always cracked, always bleeding at the corners, and the nostrils flared wide. The children - and there are children, though you won't know them as such at first because they are the same size as the adults and work the same shifts - have veins visible through translucent skin at the temples, their blood working too hard. They are hairless in patches where the dry has taken it, or they shave it. Hair is a place for moths to lay.

They are not a species that belongs anywhere else. Whether they climbed up here generations ago - religious exiles, war refugees, a guild of artificers who wanted a place too inconvenient to be taxed or conscripted - and stayed until the mountain changed them, or whether they are something else that learned the shape of hands through convergent need, no longer matters. They fit the pocket as exactly as the scaffolding does.

You understand them by how they move.

They never hurry, and they never stop. The whole town is a negotiation of volume, and to move fast is to waste air and bump something and make noise. And noise is waste here. They speak rarely and when they do it is low, a dry murmur in a language that has been thinned by the thin air into consonants and breath. Much of what they say is done with taps - a knuckle on pipe, two clicks means *hold*, three means *lift*, a palm flat on grating means *clear*. A cough is an apology. The loudest sound any of them makes voluntarily is the *tink* of a hammer on basalt brick, and even that is muffled with a wrap of hide.

Watch one for an hour and you see the cycle:

She - though *she* and *he* are hard to tell apart in the wraps, except that the women tend to wear their tool belts lower - emerges from a hatch the size of a cupboard where she slept curled around a warm pipe. She does not stretch; there is no room. She unwraps her sleeping mask, folds it, tucks it into a sealed bladder at her hip. Inside that bladder is yesterday's water - not water you would recognize. It is maybe a mouthful, a few ounces captured from her own breath overnight on a cold coil above her hammock, plus what the keepers render from salt crust. She measures it, sips half, wipes the rest across her lips and eyes.

Then work. No commute. Work is two steps away in any direction because you live inside it. She kneels at a wire-drawer the size of a sewing machine and feeds it filament. Or she tends the moth looms - great frames strung between struts where the soot-moths have laid their basalt-colored silk, which she harvests with a card, leaving enough that the moths stay. Or she harvests the keepers, coaxing a long aluminum-bright centipede from a crack with a salt-lick, snapping it behind the head so quick you don't see it, stripping its chitin plates while it still twitches. Everything is harvest and return. Nothing leaves the pocket except heat.

They eat dry. You will not see them cook with water. Food is moth larvae toasted on a hot plate, rust-rat jerky cured in anhydrite salt until it is like leather, keeper-chitin ground and baked into flat, tasteless wafers that are mostly protein and mineral, and a kind of fungus that grows nowhere but on oiled rags in the dark - pale, salty buttons they call *stonebread*. They eat with their masks lifted for seconds, chew quickly, seal again. To spill a crumb is to feed something else, and something else will come - a swift, a rat, a neighbor's hand.

They build the way the moths build: not from a plan but from adjacency. No architect could draw this town because it redraws itself every day. A shard falls from the vent wall - *tick, skitter* - and before it has stopped sliding, someone has decided whether it is rubble to be cleared, brick to be cut, or foundation for a new platform. A motor burns out and its casing becomes a nesting box before it is cool. If you leave a flat surface empty, by evening it will have a box on it, or a sleeping roll, or a brood of moths. They do not resent this. Crowding is not a burden to them; it is a proof of efficiency. To have unused space is obscene.

They are old while young. The dry and the forge smoke age the skin early, etch fine cracks around the eyes at twenty. But they live long for such a hard place, because nothing rots here and infection is rare. When they die - of a fall, of a lung that finally gives out, of the slow accumulation of metal in the blood - they are not buried. There is no soil. They are taken to the kilns on the lowest level, where the same fire that makes brick renders them to ash and a small, clean ingot of salts, which is sealed and set into the wall. The town is mortared, in part, with its own dead.

And they are listening, always, to the mountain.

Every hour or so the stone pings. A deep, almost inaudible *tok* as basalt relieves stress. The whole scaffold hums sympathetically. Every inhabitant pauses, even if only for half a breath, hand flat on the nearest strut, feeling for the direction of the shift. The mountain is still being born, a millimeter a year. The vent widens by a hair. The floor tilts. Cracks open. They feel it in their teeth before they hear it. And then they adjust - a bolt loosened here, a shim added there, a catwalk re-anchored two centimeters to the left. They do not fight the growth. They grow with it, inwards and upwards, like crystals in a cooling vesicle.

From the outside, at sunset, if you were somehow able to cling to that black glass tooth in the gale, you would see only a faint stain of soot and a tremble of heat above the lip, and hear nothing. Inside, in the cool, tooth-aching air, under amber bulbs and the orange pulse of forges, thousands of small, quiet hands are moving in a space no larger than a church, each motion precise and dry and necessary, each breath carefully caught and kept, while around their ankles the moths rise and settle, rise and settle, like ash that has learned to fly.
