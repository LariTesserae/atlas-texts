They live here.

Not on the mountain, *in* it — in the one place the mountain allows life to happen.

At first you don't see anyone. That's the trick of the bowl. In the indigo gloom, with your eyes still starved from the climb, the floor looks like floor. Pale stone. Uneven, textured stone. And then the texture inhales.

And you understand that you are not looking at ground at all.

They are a carpet. A living mat three and four deep that has become the floor. Thousands of them, shoulder to shoulder, back over belly, limb over limb, so densely packed that where one ends and the next begins is a guess. The rock underneath — that young, black, pitted basalt — only shows through in the places they've left open on purpose: the little cupped reservoirs, the black mirror of the central pool.

If you kneel — slowly, because the cold forces you to be slow here too — and let your eyes adjust, one of them resolves.

It is not large. The length of your forearm from elbow to fingertip. Its body is humped, like a woodlouse magnified to an impossible scale, but softened by what looks at first like fur. It's not fur. It's a dense, woolen down, off-white in the half-light, almost luminous against the dark stone. Each filament beaded with condensed vapor. Under that down, when it shifts, you catch a glimpse of something darker — a segmented, amber-grey cuticle — and the folded, damp-paper translucence of wings that will never open here. The air is too thin to fly in, the room too crowded to risk it.

It has six limbs, but you rarely see all six at once. They are kept folded tight to the body, hooked feet dug into the basalt or into the down of a neighbor for warmth and purchase. Its head is small for its body and almost all of it is mouth — a soft, vertical slit fringed with palps — and antennae. The antennae are the most alive part of it: two extravagant, feathery plumes, twice as long as its body, constantly moving, tasting the air for the one thing that matters here. Water.

They are pale because there has never been a reason to be any other color. The sun never reaches this bowl. No pigment is needed for camouflage or warning or display. Paleness is just what happens when you live on lichen and condensation and shared heat for ten thousand generations in permanent eclipse. Some are whiter, some are a yellowed ivory, the very largest ones a faint, dirty grey where their down has collected mineral dust. Age, perhaps.

You watch one drink.

It has been waiting in line for a long time. The line is not a line as we understand it, but a slow flow — bodies inching head-to-tail along a trickle that weeps down a vertical striation in the wall. It can take an hour to move a meter. Everything here is budgeted. Urgency would be death; a quick motion burns heat and water they cannot afford. So they flow.

When it reaches the trickle, it lowers its head and presses its mouth to the stone and does not move for a very long time. Its antennae go still. Its whole body seems to pulse once, faintly. Beside it, another uses a tool.

The tools are what unsettle you after the strangeness of the bodies wears off. Because they are not made. There is no manufacture here, no nests, no hives, no woven anything. The mountain is too young and too cold for building. But scattered through the carpet, held for a while and then set down for another to find, are things that have been *kept*.

A flake of volcanic glass, edges knapped to cruel sharpness by the mountain itself, now held between two forelimbs like a chisel. The individual wielding it — slightly larger, down worn thin on its hump — scrapes at the black-green crust of lichen that etches the wettest part of the wall. Not eating it directly. Scraping it into a fine paste and passing it back, limb to limb, to smaller, paler individuals clustered behind it who receive it into their mouths.

A hollow bone, bird-thin and yellowed, used as a siphon to reach the bottom of a stone pocket too narrow for a head.

A flat shard of basalt being dragged, with infinite patience, a centimeter at a time by two of them working together, to cap a reservoir and keep the night air from stealing it back.

No one owns them. You watch the glass flake get set down on the stone when the scraper tires, and after a long pause another comes and picks it up and resumes the same exact motion. A culture without property, without progress. Only maintenance. A tradition of hands.

They don't eat the other green things. That's the other oddness. In the most sheltered crevices where the condensation is constant are five or six cushions of something that is almost moss but harder — tiny, tight rosettes of needle-filaments that hold a bead of water on every tip like a lens. The pale ones tend them. You see one pause over a cushion, brush it delicately with its feathered antennae, flick away a grain of grit, clear a fallen flake of down that would smother it, and then move on without taking a bite. They farm without harvesting. Or perhaps they harvest so slowly you would have to watch for days to see it — a single filament taken here, a spore there. Robustness is more valuable than a meal.

And then the whole bowl breathes.

You feel it before you hear it. A slow dimming of the rustling, a communal stillness, and then a draw — twenty seconds long — as thousands of spiracles along thousands of humped sides open at once. The downy bodies swell together. The mat rises a fraction of an inch. Then the exhale.

Sound becomes physical. Thousands of tiny whistles, thousands of claws readjusting on sharp stone with a sound exactly like wind through dry leaves, and underneath it all that low hum you felt in your chest. That's the wings. None of them open, but all of them shiver at once, vibrating without ever extending, turning muscle work into heat. In the hard, fresh, echoing bowl that hum becomes a tone, and the tone becomes a pressure. Frost shivers on the rim of the central pool. Your own breath is lost in it.

They ring that pool, by the way. It is no wider than a doorway, rimed with lace-ice that never quite closes, black and so still it reflects the star-thick indigo above even though there are no stars directly overhead. That is their center. Not a temple, not a home. Just the most reliable water. They crowd around it in a tight corona, heads in, antennae almost touching the surface, drinking and waiting and warming each other. The young — smaller, downier, almost translucent — are kept in the middle of the mat, away from the stone and the void, where the heat is best conserved. The old cling to the vertical walls like shingles, hooked feet dug into the striations, enduring the most cold.

They know you are there in the way a tide knows a rock. Antennae near you will turn, taste your exhaled moisture, your alien warmth, and then turn back. If you don't move, they will slowly flow around your boots and incorporate you as terrain. If you stay long enough, one might use the edge of your sole to scrape lichen.

This is not a colony that will spread. There is nowhere to spread to. The mountain is a knife and the bowl is its only pocket. This may be all of them in the world — a single village that is also a single organism, living on the edge of what is possible, keeping a handful of found tools in circulation, keeping the moss alive, breathing together so loudly in the dark that the young mountain itself seems to breathe with them, waiting out winters that last for years for those few weeks when the sun finally tips over the rim and the ice above gives them a little more water.

Slowly. Endlessly. Together.
