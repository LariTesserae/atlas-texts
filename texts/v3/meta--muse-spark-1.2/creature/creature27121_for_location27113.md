They live in the green, so at first you don't see them at all.

You think the town is empty. That the vines took it. That the weight of the air and the drone has made it uninhabitable even though the houses are clearly tended — the dust swept into careful piles in the corners of lanes, the dew-nets strung taut and trembling, the salt-pans white and waiting. It looks like a place that should have footsteps in it, and doesn't.

Then one of the walls moves.

Not the stone. The mat on the wall. A rosette shivers and a hand comes out from under it — or you thought it was a shadow until the fingers flex. The leaves there had been arranged, not just grown. Pulled back to let a shutter open. Someone inside leans out to check the sky, and you realize you've been looking at people the whole time without resolving them, because they are the same colors as everything else.

Who lives here has learned how not to stand out from the stone and the leaf.

You hear them before you properly see them, because you have to. The buzz is at a pitch that swallows ordinary speech. When one of them calls across a courtyard — *“Net’s heavy!”* or *“Mind that vine!”* — they don't shout so much as project, from the chest, a voice made to cut through insect noise. It is loud, and flat, and good-humored. Everyone speaks that way. Even the children bellow at each other when they play, their little voices startlingly carrying.

When you do start to pick them out, they seem at first all one person, repeated. Then the differences resolve, slowly, the way the plateau does everything.

They are short. Not dramatically — you wouldn't call them dwarves, they'd be offended if they had a word for that — but compact, with a sturdiness that comes from a life of carrying water that is never quite water. Broad chests, necks thick, legs a little bowed from walking always on soft ground or on crust that might give. Nothing about them is thin or wasted. In a place with so little, they are not starved. The mat feeds them too well for that, if they tend it correctly.

Their skin tells you how long they've been here. A new child is startlingly pink underneath, like the inside of the stone when it is freshly cut. Within a year that pink is gone. The dust does it. The fine, pinkish-grey flour gets into everything — you breathe it, you eat it no matter how you try not to, it settles in the creases of your elbows and the lines of your palms and never washes out because there is no water to wash with. The people are dust-colored. Not dirty, exactly; dyed. A warm, ashen grey-pink that makes them look carved from the plateau itself when they stand still. Their hands and feet are palest where the skin has thickened and cracked. Lips always chapped, always tasting of salt and iron because they lick them without thinking. Hair, when they keep it, is lank and very fine, bleached to a dusty white-yellow by the dim sun, cut short with those precious copper knives so it doesn't catch in the nets. Many keep their heads shaved and wrapped in lichen-fiber cloth the color of rust.

Their eyes are the adaptation you notice last and remember longest.

The light here never gets bright. The sun is a coin behind gauze, the world seen through amber. Down below, that would make pupils small to protect. Up here, eyes have gone the other way. Large, long-lashed against the dust, irises a washed-out grey-green or amber-brown, pupils that stay wide even at noon. They don't squint. They gaze. And they have a second lid — not quite a lid, a translucence you see only when the wind picks up and the powder hangs — that slides sideways, nictitating, to keep the flour out without blinding them. Children have to be taught not to rub when it moves. Elders' eyes are clouded at the edges with a faint, salty film, but they see better in the dimming than anyone.

They move the way the vines grow. Slowly, deliberately, never in a hurry. Not because they are tired — though the air is thin enough that you feel it if you try to do anything quickly and they will laugh, loudly, when they see you panting — but because haste is waste here. A quick step kicks up a cloud that hangs for minutes and gets in the nets. A careless elbow tears a tendril that took a decade to reach that wall, and the mat will take months to seal the wound. So they turn sideways in the lanes, they place their feet on the stone crust where it will hold, they lift the curtain of black hair-lichen with the back of a wrist before ducking under it. Every motion is economical. You can watch an old woman wring a dew-net and understand a whole philosophy: twist, pause, let the brine run down the forearms into the jar, twist again. No drop shaken loose to be lost to the air.

Because everything is brine.

There is no fresh water, and they do not expect it. Their bodies have changed to not expect it. What they drink is what the black curtains and the salt-hairs catch in the cold hours before the dim dawn — a bitter, mineral-heavy liquor that beads along the woven webs and is wrung by hand into clay jars. It is never clear. It tastes the way the soil tastes, salt and iron and faintly of the rosette leaves themselves. They sip it all day from gourds hung at the waist. Their kidneys, over generations, have learned to want it. A child offered true fresh water, if you could bring it, would find it strange, empty. Their sweat, when they sweat in the trapped warmth, dries almost instantly into a fine white crust at the temples and upper lip that they wipe away with a thumb and then absently touch to the tongue.

They eat the mat.

Not all of it. That is the first law made flesh in them. They harvest with a reverence that looks, to an outsider, like gardening, but is closer to shearing an animal they live inside of. The broad, waxy rosettes are never pulled whole. An adult will crouch — their knees pop softly, dust puffing — and peel only the outermost, oldest leaves, the ones already yellowing and curling, leaving the tight, pale center to keep growing. Those leaves are beaten, leached in brine overnight to pull out some salt, then baked on the communal stones into a flat, grey-green bread that is chewy and faintly sweet and leaves your teeth filmed. The corky vines give them pods when they flower, once or twice a year: hard, salty capsules with three or four oily seeds inside that are roasted until they pop. And then there is the protein that the air provides. The moth clouds, the beetles. At dusk boys and girls stand on the flat roofs with fine nets of hair-lichen on long poles and sweep them through the air in slow, practiced arcs, laughing loudly over the rising buzz, catching handfuls that are fried quickly with leaf-fat. The lizards are not eaten — they eat the beetles that would eat too much of the mat — but their eggs, found tucked under shingles, sometimes are.

Nothing is thrown away. You will see a man carefully collect his own family's dried waste from a screened alcove behind a house, pound it to powder with a basalt pestle, and carry it in a shallow pan to scatter at the base of a wall where the vines are thin. The vines thicken within weeks. They do not find this shameful. It is manners.

They live very close together and do not seem to mind. A courtyard that you thought belonged to one house belongs to four. Grandmothers mind all the children. The stonework that looked medieval from a distance is, up close, intimate — every wall has handholds rubbed smooth by centuries of palms, every doorway is exactly wide enough for two people with jars on their hips to pass if one turns. They have no chief, no mayor. When something needs deciding — whether to re-string the great net between the oven house and the cistern house, whether a new family can build a lean-to against the south wall — the elders gather, which means anyone who has lived long enough that their eyes have filmed, and they talk loudly over the buzz until they agree. Which they generally do, because the plateau does not allow for sustained disagreement. You need your neighbor's wall to anchor your vine. You need their hands to help wring at dawn before the sun takes the dew back.

Their children learn stone before they learn stories. Before they can properly speak loudly enough to be heard, they are taught to rub stone on stone — the soft plateau basalt — to make a wall true. They are taught to listen to the mat: to put an ear to a rosette and hear the faint, almost inaudible tick of its salt-hairs opening, which means the dew is coming. They play in the powder, building tiny houses and letting the black curtains sway over them, and they are scolded — gently, but always — if they play too close to the edge.

No one lives at the edge. You understand why once you see an adult go there to check the seed-bank. They don't walk right up. They stop three or four meters back, crouch, and look. The lip is a low, wind-rounded stone rim where the mat grows thickest and oldest, a shag of white salt-hairs so dense it looks like frost even in the warmth, trembling without wind. That is the mother growth, they will tell you if you ask, not in a religious way but in a practical one: all the other rosettes came from there, carried inward by the slow creep of centuries. To step on it would be like burning a library because you were cold. The wind there is strange, too — a constant, soft out-breath over the rim, as if the plateau were exhaling. It tugs at your wrap. It makes the gliders shy away. The people keep low walls there, unmortared, rebuilt every generation, not to keep people in, but to remind them where the town ends and the world ends.

Do they know there is a world below? Yes, in the way you know a story about the sea if you've never seen it. They have found things in the dust-lens — a feather from a bird that never lived up here, a seed with a hard coat that could not have grown on rock — fallen from below and carried on the high veil. The very old say, when the light is at its deepest dimming and the buzz softens, that once there was a way up, that the town's first people climbed when the sides were not so sheer. But no one tries to look over. The haze is absolute. There is no below. There is only the flat, the green-furred houses, the nets, the dust.

They are not lonely. That is the thing that takes you longest to understand, because you arrived feeling the isolation like a weight. They are saturated with company — with each other, with the lizards that sleep in warm cracks beside them, with the four-winged gliders that nest in their eaves and scold them with clicks, with the mat itself which they speak to out loud while pruning: *“There, there, that's enough, old fat one.”* It is a loud, slow, contented life, repeating itself not out of stagnation but out of having reached an end point that works. A house will be rebuilt once every five generations, exactly the same, stone rubbed to stone. A vine will put out a single new leaf this month. A child will take her turn wringing the brine at dawn, her small hands white with salt, shouting to her cousin over the drone and being shouted back at, laughing.

If you stay until the dimming, when the pale coin of the sun thins and the buzz rises to a felt vibration in your molars, you will see them on their roofs. Not looking out at the haze. Looking in, at each other, at the green that glows faintly even without sun, at the little amber lights they set in shallow stone bowls — not fire, but a cool chemiluminescence cultivated from a fungus that lives in the rosettes — that make each courtyard a small, warm constellation. They talk. They mend. The powder settles. The dust-lens holds the warmth in.

They belong here the way the rosettes belong here. Not as visitors who adapted, but as an expression the plateau finally, after three billion years of patience, decided to try: a way for the stone to tend its own garden, to wring its own air for drink, to make a noise to answer the buzzing.

You leave — or you would, if there were a way down — with salt still on your lips, and the sense that the town did not notice you very much, and did not need to. It will be there, loud and lush and dry, doing its slow work, whether anyone watches or not.
