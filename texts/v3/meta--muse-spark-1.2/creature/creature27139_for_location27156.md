Nothing lives here the way you expect something to live.

At first you think nobody does.

That is the scale trick of the plateau. From any vantage the valleys fall away bench after bench into darkness, and you can see whole weather systems not happening, and your eye keeps looking for a headcount — a herd, a flock, a forest that moves. It doesn't find one. The black garden looks still. The peat looks empty. The air is too thin to carry a call.

Then your eyes dark-adapt, and your ears stop looking for loudness, and the place resolves into what it actually is: not empty, but *spaced*.

Everything that lives here lives far apart on purpose. Abundance without density. That is the first rule of altitude.

### The ones who catch

The foundation isn't animal at all. It's the catchers.

Up close those door-sized leaves aren't leaves. They're traps and lungs and wombs. The black-purple fronds — waxy on top, bearded white underneath with a felt of hollow hairs — never photosynthesize the way you were taught. There is too little light for that to be a living. Instead they drink the air itself.

Every evening the frost comes down like wool, feathery crystals an inch thick on every upward cup. Every morning it doesn't melt. It *vanishes*, sublimates straight from ice to vapor in the cold sunless air, and the hairs underneath comb it. Each hair is a cold finger. Water beads on it, runs down the channel in its center, and drops. *Plip.* One leaf, one drop at a time, into the peat. Enough. Over a hundred square meters of that forest, it's a steady rain that never fell from a cloud.

They are warm to the touch. Not metaphorically. Put your hand under a frond and the felt is 10 degrees warmer than the air, trapping the geothermal sigh coming up through the basalt. At night they close slightly, like hands around a candle.

And if you don't look at them directly — this takes a while to believe — they glow. Not bright. A deep, chlorophyllic green-black, the color you see behind your eyelids. Peripheral light, there and gone when you turn. They make a little of what they need when the sky won't give it.

Between them stand the pillars. You thought they were trunks. They're moss, but moss that forgot how to be small. Columns three, four, six feet thick, soft as stacked felt, solid enough to lean your whole weight against. They are colonies, thousands of generations pressed together, their dead making peat at the base and their living breathing out that knee-high mist that never lifts. If you press your ear to one you hear water moving inside it the way you hear blood in your own ear — slow, pressurized — pumping seep uphill against gravity.

In the sheltered hollows the catchers pool what they can't hold. The eyes.

Each eye is no wider than a table, perfectly still, rimmed with that orange bacterial velvet. The water is black glass until you kneel, and then you see it's clear and the black is basalt, and the orange is alive — a mat of thermophiles respiring the iron and sulfur from below. The water is warm as a mouth. It never freezes. Nothing drinks from it with a mouth. The life around it drinks with roots and hyphae and osmotic skin, wicking heat as much as water.

### The ones who smelt

You hear the beetles before you see one.

Not a swarm. Never a swarm. One furnace every few hundred yards, working alone in the vastness, so the chuff of one fumarole and the click of one worker have a mile of silence between them. That's how you know how big the place is.

They are the size of seals, low and broad, matte black armor pitted like cast iron, moving with a slowness that isn't sluggishness — it's weight. Six legs splayed, each foot a wide, frost-cracked pad that doesn't break the moss. The head is small, tucked, almost blind. The abdomen is huge, domed, banded with seams that pulse amber when they breathe.

They are not eating the leaves, exactly. They are *smelting*.

They chew basalt. They chew fallen fronds. They grind iron-rich rock and mineral-saturated peat in a gut that runs hotter than boiling water — you can see the cherry-red working through the plates of their thorax at night, a dull furnace glow — and what comes out behind them is not dung but manufacture. A cooling braid of black glass, slag, still ticking as it contracts, laid down on the peat in a perfect rope half an inch thick. It steams, then frosts over instantly. Over weeks that braid weathers and leaches, and the orange bacteria bloom along it, and the pillar-mosses send rhizoids toward it for the iron.

Their spiracles sigh. The steam halos for a second and then snows. Every forty seconds on the wind you hear another one, a mile off, exhale: *chuff.*

They do not meet. They do not need to. When two do cross paths the amber in their backs brightens, they pause, exchange a puff of hot, metallic breath that hangs between them like a word, and continue. They are solitary blast furnaces wandering an endless field that is also their mine.

### The ones who weave

The hillside itself is alive and you mistake it for ground until it breathes.

Acres at a time, the interlocked fungal mats — not lichen, not quite mushroom, something older that made a decision to become a continent — tighten. Then release. When they do, a warm wind rolls downhill that smells of ozone and hot stone.

Kneel and you see the sparks. Faint blue lines crawling along the mat's edge, splitting, rejoining, like St. Elmo's fire trapped under skin. In this air, so thin and dry, static builds monstrously with every thermal differential between the cold above and the heat below. The mats harvest it. They are electroactive, piezoelectric, alive with differential. They crackle. Insects die when they land on them. Slime molds avoid them unless they have to cross.

Nothing eats the mats. Things feed *off* their byproduct — the faint rain of charged particles, the warmth they bleed — the way you might live off the waste heat of a power station.

### The ones who drift and the ones who walk

Because the ground does the work, the animals don't have to be many. But what is here is large, and quiet, and built for distance.

High above, when the bruised-blue stain that is this place's day comes, you see them moving against the too-close stars. Moths that are also birds, or birds that decided to be moths. Wingspans longer than a man is tall, wings translucent as rice paper, veined with amber furnace-light that pulses in time with whatever heart they have inside that papery chest. They don't flap so much as row the thin air, each stroke slow and huge, and they never call — there isn't enough air to waste on song. They are filterers, straining spores and ice crystals and the tiny glassy midges that spawn over the warm eyes. Their bellies glow as they digest. At night you see a V of them pass, silent, lit from within like lanterns, and then the darkness is absolute again.

On the terraces below, the herd moves single file. From a mile away they look like musk ox, like sheep, like stones. Closer they are none of those. Low, woolly backs that drag the mist, legs too short, heads too broad, with a double coat: coarse black guard hair over a dense, greasy underwool that holds the warmth of their own bodies and the earth they sleep on. They move through the black-green fronds without breaking a stem, along paths that aren't paths but memories — the same line walked by the same line for a thousand years, pressed an inch into the peat. They eat by tearing whole fronds with a sideways pull, and their droppings are hot when they fall, steaming for a long time. They don't hurry. Predation here is not a chase.

The slime knows the herd's schedule better than you do. By day you see nothing. After dark, blankets of it — plasmodial, veined, the size of quilts and low tables — creep out from under the pillars on a hundred tiny feet, leaving a phosphorescent slime-trail that maps where the ground stayed warm longest. They are one cell, technically, but a cell the size of an animal. They flow over dead moth-birds, over slag braids, over shit, and dissolve them without ceremony. By morning they are gone, withdrawn into the peat, and the trails fade.

### The one you don't see

You know it by its absence in the pattern.

The prints are pressed into soft moss between the pools, already filling with lace frost: three toes, splayed, each toe as long as your forearm, with a dew-claw drag behind. The stride is enormous. It steps from one terrace to the next as if the U-valley were stairs.

You never see it. That's part of its adaptation. In a place where air is a pane and sound carries for miles and there is nowhere to hide, the hunter hides by not being where you look. It moves when you blink. Its coat — if it has a coat — is the same black-purple as the fronds, the same heat-furred white underneath. It breathes slowly enough to not make steam. The moth-birds go higher when it is near; the herd bunches without running; the mats stop crackling for a minute.

It doesn't need to hunt often. In this cold, everything keeps. One kill feeds a long time, and nothing is wasted anyway — the slime finishes what it leaves, the bacteria finish the slime, the beetles chew the enriched rock. The predator is not cruel. It is a regulator, spaced as deliberately as everything else, ensuring nothing ever gets crowded.

You feel watched, but not stalked. More like noted.

---

All of them are, in a way, the same thing: ways for the plateau to process itself. To catch the thin moisture, to smelt its own basalt, to bleed off its own heat, to turn stone into glass into bacteria into wool into slime into stone again. No leader, no builder, no villages. No straight lines because no one needs a straight line where every journey is measured in miles of peat and every neighbor is a quarter mile off.

If you stay here long enough your breath starts to hang too long in the air, holding the shape of the sigh for a full minute, and the stones you dislodge fall a little too slowly, and you realize the plateau is not hosting life on top of itself.

It *is* the life. The warmth underneath, the old cold on top, the factory that grew itself without ever needing a maker. The beetles and the mats and the moths and the herd and the three-toed absence are just its organs, working quietly in the dark that lives even at noon — patient, lush, metallurgical, and completely uninterested in whether you ever came at all.
