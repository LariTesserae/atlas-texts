It is low because what lives there cannot be tall.

You expect, at first, that nothing large lives there. That is the trick of the sparseness. You enter the bowl and you catalog the obvious residents and think you are done: the brass-rivet wasps that attend each piston, the plum-sized frogs lodged in the cracks with their throats going *tonk* every ten seconds, the amber beetles that make the ozone and the blue corona under the leaves, the granular writhing of glass shrimp and tadpoles in the tea-colored pan. Many presences, few kinds, all keeping a polite distance from each other the way workers do on a factory floor. It feels populated but not crowded. You could be forgiven for thinking the fig itself is the only large thing that lives here, and that everything else is just its tenants.

Then you sit.

You put your back against one of those door-wide buttress walls and feel the thrum come up through the wood into your spine, and through the moss-mattress into your palms, and you realize the thrum is not in the wood. Or not *only* in the wood.

The floor gives a little more than a mat should.

That is the first wrongness. The mat of moss and liverwort and rootlet and half-alive leaf does not behave like ground, even soft ground. It behaves like a back. When you press your hand down, water wells up clear and warm around your fingers and then, a second too late, the mat pushes back. Not springback. A response. A slow systole under your weight that matches exactly the sigh of the pistons in the walls.

What lives there lives flat.

It has to. At the bottom where the water table is high and nothing pretends to be solid anymore, height is expensive. Height is wind and leverage and falling over. What lives here has solved height by refusing it. It poured itself out.

It occupies the entire floor of the bowl.

Not under the floor. It *is* the floor. For fifteen meters across, from buttress wall to buttress wall, with its edges tucked and grafted neatly under the weeping roots the way a fitted sheet tucks under a mattress, is a single animal. Or a single plant-animal. The distinction has been polished away here, like everything else that was once sharp.

If you peel back the moss — and you will not, because it would be rude, and because the moss is not growing *on* it so much as growing *with* it, the liverwort stitched into its skin like a living embroidery — you would not find soil. You would find skin. A thick, warm, liver-colored integument, smooth and pitted with the mouths of tiny channels, weeping constantly. The color of tea and amber sap and old bruises. It is polished. This is an old, old resident. Nothing is sharp left on it. No spines, no claws, no ridges. Where its skin meets the fig's buttress roots, the two have become confused over centuries and have sealed to each other in a pale, seamless graft. The pistons you thought belonged to the fig are as much its lungs as the fig's. The sluices carved down the roots are its veins.

It breathes.

That is the industrial sound. Once every ten seconds the vertical seams in the wall-roots swell. The creature draws in — not air, exactly, but the whole humid atmosphere of the chamber, the mist, the weeping water, the dust of beetle wings and pollen — and then compresses. The sigh you hear is its exhale, a pressurized mist and a pulse of that iron-sugar amber sap that runs down the sluices and soaks straight back into the mat-skin and vanishes. It is not pumping sap *somewhere*. It is circulating itself.

Between the pistons, the curtains of aerial roots that clack like chains in a windless room are not moved by wind. They are its cilia. Its outside hairs, grown long and wood-hard, ticking against each other to keep time. At the base of each sluice, those tabletop leaves that shiver and sift — those are its hands, or its baleen. They catch everything that falls — husks, detritus, the metallic green fig fruits that drop with a soft thump — and they vibrate at a high frequency until only the finest, most refined dust remains. That dust falls into the pan.

The pan is not a puddle. That perfect, bathtub-sized, tea-colored mirror that never gets larger and never dries, that froths faintly without inflow — that is its mouth. Or its stomach. Or both. The granular, teeming water that looks alive because it *is* alive, shrimp and tadpoles and water beetles churning so thick they are the water, is being sifted and sipped and returned. It eats continuously and is never more full. It excretes continuously and never empties. The dragonflies the length of your hand that skim it are not drinking. They are being tolerated, the way you tolerate dust motes in a sunbeam.

When the amber beetles lift in a cloud and make that staticky blue crackle, that is its nervous system firing — a corona discharge that grounds out across the wet mat-skin and back into itself. It smells of ozone because it is thinking, in the slow way a closed loop thinks: *pump, sift, spark, weep.*

You understand then why nothing accumulates. The fig fruits that ripen and fall and dissolve without rotting are not being wasted. They are its eggs and its shit and its food, all at once, made and unmade in the same gesture. The shrimp eat and are eaten and the number of shrimp stays exactly the same. The energy goes around. For a very long time this creature has been learning how to need nothing from outside. The forest around it still does entropy — things fall and decay and wash away — but this room has opted out. It has become so completely itself, so eroded and established and rounded by time, that it can idle indefinitely on its own recycled breath.

It is warm. Mid-70s, saturated, greenhouse-warm, body-warm. If you lie down on the moss that is also skin, the mist kisses your face every ten seconds, warm as breath, because it *is* breath. A frog watches you from its crack without alarm. It has no alarm to feel. The creature beneath you has no predator, no prey, no front or back, no face to turn toward you. It has no eyes. It feels you as pressure and temperature and a small, temporary interruption in humidity. You are a brief weather system on its back.

It does not need you. It did not build this for you. It built itself, perpetually, in place, out of itself — a forest that learned how to be a mill, a mill that learned how to be an animal, an animal that learned how to be a room.

It will let you sit there as long as you like, thrumming softly through your bones, sifting and sparking and weeping and sipping in perfect, unvarying balance. And when you stand and step back up over the root-rim into the ordinary woods where things still erode and rot, it will stay low, flat, polished, and complete, breathing its warm mist into its own leaf-roof, doing its work without progress, forever.
