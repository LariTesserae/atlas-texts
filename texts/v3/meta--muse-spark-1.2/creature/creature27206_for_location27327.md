They live there because nothing else can.

Not *in* the pocket so much as *as* the pocket. You don't find them inside it — at first — you find that the wet *is* them.

You think the black glass is sweating. Then you think the sweat is rippling on its own. Then you crouch, and your eyes adjust to the steam, and the rippling resolves into a density that makes no sense.

They are everywhere the water is. Which is everywhere.

Pale. The kind of pale that isn't a color but an absence of one. Translucent the way candle wax is translucent, or the way a fingernail is — you can see light through them but not *behind* them. If you hold still long enough, you can see the faint, darker thread of gut, the slow pulse of something that isn't quite a heart, the opal blush of their gills. They have no faces to speak of. No eyes at all, not even the vestigial pits you expect from cave things. No pigment to lose. Evolution here never bothered with sight because there was never a need to see further than the next wet inch of glass.

At first they look like shrimp. That is the easiest word and therefore the wrong one. The wrongness becomes clearer the longer you look. Shrimp are marine, armored, decisive. These are soft. Jointed, yes, with a dozen fine articulations you can see flexing under the skin, but soft — like axolotl larvae that never decided to grow up, like isopods boiled clear. Some are no bigger than your thumbnail, curled commas tucked into the beading water on the vertical walls. Some are as long as your hand, stretched flat in the ankle-deep pan, their frilled gills fanned wide and trembling. The gills are the only color on them. A wet, oxygenated pink, filamentous as ferns, fluttering not in water but in steam. They breathe the cloud itself.

They do not move like individuals. They move like a surface. A slow, silent churn.

Thousands of them. In a room that should, by any honest ecology, support a few dozen. They overlap. They crawl over one another with a total absence of territoriality or disgust. A small one will walk directly across the back of a large one to reach a patch of biofilm, and the large one will not twitch. A molting one will lie on its side, splitting its own translucent skin down the back, and its neighbors will step around the shed ghost and then, minutes later, scrape at it and eat it. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is left to accumulate. Even their dead do not remain — if one dies, and they must, you never see the body for long. It is covered, investigated with soft forelimbs, and rendered down by mouths you can barely see.

Because there is nothing else to eat.

There are no plants here. The rock is too young, too hot, too sharp to have learned soil. No moss holds the corners, no lichen freckles the glass. The only green this world has ever known is the faint, sulfurous slick you can't quite see until a thousand grazing mouths have cleared a pale track through it. A bacterial film, thinner than oil, that grows on the silica and basalt where the water weeps. It is the entire pasture. And it is enough — because the heat is endless, the water is endless, the cloud that forms a handspan above the pool rains back down every few minutes in a tight, furious little cycle. The film doubles, is grazed, doubles again. The animals filter it, scrape it, drink it.

And they carry.

This is what holds you longest, because it looks like intention and then, on closer inspection, is something stranger and purer than intention.

They have no hands. They have two forelimbs that end in soft, opposing flaps, almost like mittens. And with those mittens they hold.

A mid-sized one, pink-gilled and swollen with eggs visible like pearls in its belly, will shuffle to the wall and pick up a flake of obsidian no bigger than a scale. The glass is fresh, razor-edged — the mountain sheds it constantly with little ticks and pings. The animal holds the flake edge-out and scrapes. Not randomly. It draws the edge across the silica crust where the hot spring has precipitated its white rings around the pan, peeling it back to expose the dark, wet biofilm beneath. It does not eat all it exposes. It moves on, and within seconds three smaller ones converge on the scrape and feed in the furrow it made.

Another one carries water. It finds a curved shard, a natural spoon of glass, and balances a single, fat, trembling droplet on it — water that beaded on the overhang — and walks, slow and impossibly careful, across the backs of its fellows to a dry fissure where a cluster of brood clings. The brood are not eggs as you know them. They are clusters of clear beads glued to the rock in the few places that are merely damp and not submerged, each bead holding a curled, gill-less comma. The carrier tilts its shard and lets the droplet kiss the cluster. The beads swell. The carrier leaves the shard where it falls. Another will pick it up.

No tool lasts. No cache is kept. No animal owns a flake for more than a minute. A chip is picked up, used, dropped, picked up again by another set of flaps. The pocket floor is littered with these momentary tools — black glass knives, silica spoons — but there is no toolmaking, no culture, no accumulation. Only the act itself, distributed across the crowd like breathing. It is eusocial without a queen, intelligent without a brain. If you watch long enough you realize you are not watching many animals using tools. You are watching one tool-using surface that happens to be divided into thousands of soft bodies.

They are silent. This is the most unnatural part.

A thousand animals the size of your hand, moving, scraping glass on glass, should make a sound. A rustle, a click, a chitter. There is nothing. Even when a wave of molting passes through them — a shudder that moves outward from the center of the pan like wind over wheat, dozens shedding at once — the sound is only the plip of water and the faint sigh of the spring. Their feet on wet glass make no noise. Their scraping is muffled as if the steam eats it. The heat that has no gradient, the light that stutters and arrives late, has taken sound as well. The pocket prefers to be quiet, and so they are quiet. They have learned to live without vibration. Or perhaps vibration never learned to live here.

If you stay still, they will forget you are not rock. A small one will test the wet leather of your boot, its gills brushing your laces, its blind head nosing for film that isn't there. A larger one will not deviate from its path; it will walk over your ankle as if you were another warm, streaming wall. They have no fear because nothing has ever come from above to eat them. Nothing could. The mouth of their world is a slit in knife-edged obsidian, high in thin air that should be frozen. They have lived since the rock cooled, sealed in this hot, wet, impossible room, breathing their own cloud, eating their own film, holding their sharp little flakes for each other in endless, gentle rotation.

They do not need you. They never needed eyes, or green, or noise. They only need the sweat of the young mountain and each other, layered and overlapping and carrying, forever, in the silence.
