Nothing lives here.

That's your first thought. That's the point of it.

You crouch on that sharp lip and look down into the steam and the shriek and the thunder, and your animal brain does the inventory it always does — movement, shadow, eyes looking back — and comes up empty. No tracks in the lime except your own, and they fill in before you're done making them. No nest, no burrow, no cobweb in the pump house doorway. Nothing has tried to make this place *home* the way an animal understands home. It's too hot for hiding. Too loud for listening. Too wet for keeping anything dry. The third rule holds: whatever lives and moves will not stay.

And so for a long time you think the only resident is the machine.

It *does* live here, after all, if you are loose with the word. The pump in the cinderblock house has a pulse. The fat pipe has a vein, sweating and ticking as it carries the same hot blood up and out and down again. The transformer hums in your teeth like a throat. The vapor jet never inhales. Together they make something that is almost a body — an old, stupid, faithful body that doesn't know it was abandoned and just keeps pumping, keeps shrieking, keeps sweating, keeps circling the same water through the same loop because no one ever told it to stop. You could be forgiven for thinking the pocket *is* the pump, that the stone room was built to house it.

Then your eyes adjust to the white.

You thought the floor was just water and mineral. That mustard-white and rust-pink crust that laces the whole pan, those lipped terraces with their razor rims, that thin ice that crunches — you thought that was just chemistry. Evaporation. Precipitation. The place building itself.

Crouch lower. The steam parts for a second.

It isn't crust. Or it isn't *just* crust.

There is a skin on the water. Not *on* the water — *as* the water. A film that holds the water, tints it, thickens it. In the shallowest pools where the water is only a breath deep, the bottom is not white at all. It's olive. It's olive-green gone so dark it looks black until the sun hits it straight on — and the sun always hits it straight on here — and then it flashes, suddenly, terribly green.

That's the first one. The first citizen.

It doesn't live *in* the pocket so much as it *is* the floor of the pocket. It has no front or back, no head, no legs to leave with. If you scoop a handful of that warm bathwater, you hold nothing. If you scrape a fingernail across the travertine rim, you come away with a smear the thickness of paint, gritty with fresh lime, that stings your skin. Put it under a lens — if you had brought one — and the smear would resolve from grit into city.

She is not a single thing. She is a sheet. A city that forgot to build up and instead built out.

A million, a billion small bodies, each too simple to have a name you'd know, holding hands. Some of them are green the way the two ferns try to be green — they eat the terrible noon sun that has nowhere to hide here and make sugar out of it, even though the light scalds. Some of them don't bother with the sun at all; they eat the heat itself, they drink the hot iron you taste when you breathe, they breathe the sulfur that hisses off the pipes and exhale the very mineral that makes the crust. The mustard-white is their skeleton. The rust-pink is their rust.

You thought the place was building a hot spring. It is. But the builders are not men. The travertine isn't being *left behind* by the water; it's being *laid down*, cell by cell, rim by rim, millimeter by patient millimeter, by this living mat. Each season a new lip, a little higher, a little sharper. The water braids down the basalt weeping-walls and the mat catches it. The sun pounds down and the mat holds it. The vapor jet scalds and the mat furthers itself right up to the edge of the scald, bleaching only at the very margin where even it has to pull back.

That is why nothing else can stay. Not because the place is dead, but because it is *too* alive in a way that leaves no room.

There is no room for an insect to skim here because the surface is not a surface — it is a field. A field being eaten and excreted every second. There is no quiet for a bird because the pocket is already full of sound, and the pocket's resident doesn't hear sound at all; it feels vibration through water. You have to raise your voice to think. It doesn't think. It spreads.

Watch it long enough and you see that it's not sparse the way the grass is sparse. The grass — those three or four bleached clumps in the concrete cracks — and the smear of algae on the north wall and the two furred ferns by the jet are just vagrants. Tourists who will be dead by August, or gone to seed and waiting. The real resident is continuous. Under the inch of warm water. Over the concrete. Up the damp feet of the catwalks where the steel is slick with lime. Even onto the sweating insulation of the big pipe, where the beads never dry: a darker, more leathery patch holds there, learning to love the shiver of the machine.

It likes the machine. The closed circle suits it. The pump lifts the same ancient water from deep in the hot rock, runs it past its back, spills it over its face to evaporate, to be caught again. Forever. The engine thunders and the mat does not flinch — the tremor stirs the water and brings more iron. The vapor jet screams and the mat inches closer, because where the water is hottest, competitors cannot follow.

If you put your hand flat just above the floor, not touching, you feel its breath. Hot iron and wet stone. That's not the rock. That's the mat exhaling.

It doesn't move fast. That is its power. It has traded movement for endurance. No legs, no leaving, no need to leave, because the three rules are promises to it:

*Water will come out of the rock forever* — so it will never be dry.
*The sun will always be overhead* — so the eaters-of-light will never be in shadow.
*Nothing that lives and moves will stay* — so nothing will ever come to graze it down to nothing.

You came and your footprints filled instantly behind you. That's how it wants it. The water smooths. The pump hammers. The stone weeps. And under it all, the floor — which you thought was floor — tightens its hold by another fraction of a grain, building its white, pink, razor-lipped house higher one cell at a time.

If you sit long enough your sweat stops feeling like your own, and starts feeling like the pocket's. Beading, swelling, running in braided threads down hot skin, dripping off any edge it can find. You are, for a minute, doing what it does. And it notices you not at all, the way a plain doesn't notice a boot. Then the heat gets to you, the shriek gets to you, and you have to leave.

It doesn't.
