They live low.

Not low like something that crouches. Low like something that was *pressed*.

If you stand still long enough in one of the rib-wide alleys and let your eyes adjust to the industrial dusk, you start to see them properly — not as people moving through a city, but as parts of the city that have learned to move.

### The Hands First

You notice the hands before you notice the people.

Because the light is always from below — kiln-mouth orange, oil-lamp amber, that milk-vein fungus-light in the mortar — everything above the wrist is in shadow, but the hands are lit. And they are all the same hands, even on different bodies.

Broad. Short-fingered. The palm is a pad of callus so thick the creases have been filled in with stone dust. Nails are cut to the quick and black underneath, not from dirt but from basalt fines hammered into the nail bed over years. The knuckles are swollen, not with age but with use. Even the children's hands look like this after a few years — pink at first, then grey, then the same color as the flagstones.

They never stop. That's the first rule of the body here: if a hand is still, something is wrong.

A woman squats and strikes a flint to a peat trough with one hand while the other steadies a pot of render. A boy of maybe six drags a sled with a block half his weight, rope burned into his shoulder through damp wool, and his hands are already white at the knuckles from gripping. Two old men on a scaffold pass bricks hand to hand to hand without looking, a rhythm so old it has worn grooves in their shoulders. You hear the hands before you see them — that arrhythmic *tink-tink-TAK, tink-tink-TAK* — and when the hammering stops for a breath, the absence is so total it feels like a pressure drop.

They have learned to hammer in their sleep. Literally. You will see them doze in shifts against a warm kiln wall, and their fingers will still twitch, tap, test a joint for looseness.

### The Bodies That Fit The Made Ground

They are short. Not dramatically — no fairy-tale dwarf — but consistently, generation after generation of living in a place where a ceiling is someone else's floor and an alley is as wide as your shoulders. The tallest among them would have to duck under most of the lintels in the old basalt quarters. Their legs are short and bowed slightly outward, good for bracing on slick flags. Their chests are deep and barrelled — not from strength alone, but from breathing cool, thin, soot-laced air that never quite fills the lungs. When they cough, which they do often and without notice, it is a dry, stone cough that they swallow.

Their skin tells you there has never been sun here. It is not pale like someone who stays indoors. It is pale like something that was *fired* pale — the color of unfired clay, of mushroom flesh, of the underside of a flagstone. In the kiln-light it looks almost translucent at the temples and wrists, and you can see the blue of veins close to the surface. Soot never washes off entirely; it lives in the pores of the forearms, the creases of the neck, the rim of the ear. Even babies, born in the warmest rooms behind the kilns, have that faint grey undertone within a week, as if the air itself dyes them.

Eyes are large, but not in a beautiful way. Large like a cave fish — lids heavy, iris darkened to an amber-brown that catches lamp light. They do not have good distance vision. They don't need it. Nothing is far away. What they have instead is exquisite close vision and hearing that borders on echolocation. They tilt their head to listen to a wall, to hear if a drain is starting to clog three streets over, if a scaffold pole has developed a hairline split. They speak quietly, because the stone carries. A shout two alleys over sounds like it's behind your ear. So their language is clipped, low, consonant-heavy, built for echo. They don't call to each other so much as *tap* — a hammer code on stone, on pipe, on a brick — that says *bring more lime / prop this side / water coming*.

Hair is kept short or shaved, because fungus will grow in it if you let it. Beards are rare for the same reason. What hair they have is lank with condensation, the same damp that beads on the walls. Clothes never dry. Everyone wears the same thing with variations: inner layers of felted wool made from that white hairy fungus-fiber — it never quite dries, it smells faintly of mushrooms and smoke, but it stays warm even soaked — and outer layers of oiled leather, aprons, knee-pads, caps with a brim to keep drips out of the eyes. Every pocket is full: wedges, a trowel, a bit of twine, a damp rag. No one here is ever unarmed with a tool.

You can't tell age easily. Without sun, skin doesn't wrinkle the same way; it goes crepey and then stays. A child of eight and an elder of sixty have the same stooped posture from looking down at work, the same soot under the nails. The elders are only recognizable because they move slower and because they are the ones who know the sayings: *"Don't lay square in the third hour after rain. Lay thirsty."* *"A room left un-lit will be larger when you return."*

### How They Live Together — Because There Is No Apart

There is no privacy here. There cannot be. A house is not a house. It's a knot.

You will not find a free-standing building. Every dwelling is three walls borrowed from neighbors, a roof that is also a street, a floor that is also someone else's ceiling. Families do not live in houses; they live in *thicknesses*. A grandmother tends a trough of fermenting fungus in what is technically the neighbor's hallway. Children sleep in shifts in niches carved into kiln walls for warmth, curled like cats, breathing in unison. You learn to sleep through hammering, through feet shuffling six inches above your head, through the constant drip.

They are not a crowd. They are a lattice.

Work is not a job. It is metabolism. The settlement — the basin, the hive, the reef — will begin to unmake itself if they stop, and they know it in their bones. It is not a belief; it is an observation repeated a thousand times. Leave a flagstone unwalked for a week and it tilts upright, as if trying to stand. Leave a room dark and empty and when you come back the far wall is an arm's length further away. Leave a rope hanging and it slowly corkscrews against gravity. So they keep the place *remembered* by touching it.

This is their religion, though they would not call it that. There are no temples, because every kiln is an altar. No priests, only masons who have memorized how the stones behave when the air goes heavy after rain. They do not pray. They *maintain*. A boy sweeping condensation from a wall with a fungus-mop is performing the same act as an old woman re-laying a leaning wall — they are telling the stone: *you are still here, you are still flat, you are still ours.*

Children are born rarely and are immediately useful. A baby is carried in a sling of fungus-cloth while its mother works a loom or sorts cinders, and by the time it can walk it is carrying water in small buckets from cistern to kiln, learning the sound of a true strike versus a false one. There are no schools. There is apprenticeship from the moment you can hold. You learn by echo. You learn that some alleys you do not linger in after it rains because the acoustics turn wrong and the air feels thicker there. You learn not to stare too long at the empty kilns that are warm but empty, as if something else is being fired inside them that you cannot see.

They sing, but not like you think. With so many hammers, they have to. The work songs are not melodies but *timings* — low, humming chants that fall into the gaps between strikes, that let a team of six dragging a block keep the same pull without seeing each other around a corner. The sound is mournful and practical at once. If you stand on a scaffold tower and listen, the whole basin hums, a low, arrhythmic, human-made drone stitched together from a thousand throats and hammers, never ceasing, even at night, because night never comes. Half the basin sleeps while the other half hammers, and then they swap.

And they die low too. There is no burial ground — there is no ground that wasn't made. The dead are not wasted. A body, cleaned, is laid with lime in a new foundation, under a new flag, inside a new wall. Not from cruelty, but from practicality and reverence: to become part of the Made Ground is to finally, truly, not be forgotten by it. The oldest quarters are quite literally ancestors. If you put your ear to certain basalt walls in the deep old city, the masons swear the stone is warmer there.

### The Other Residents

They are not alone, but they are the only ones who build.

In the drains and the condensation channels live the pale, forearm-long things — not worms, not woodlice, but something in between — with segmented backs that farm the white fungus. The people do not eat them. They *tend* them, the way you would tend a bee. They harvest the fungal hair carefully, leaving enough for the drain-things to eat. Sometimes a child will crouch for an hour watching one groom mineral salts from a flagstone with feathery mouthparts, utterly still, which is the only stillness this place allows.

Higher up, on the swaying scaffolds, are the long-legged blind crawlers, almost invisible against pale wood, tasting soot with antennae. The people brush them aside gently. They are bad luck to kill — not from superstition, but because they eat the mold that would otherwise rot the ropes.

And then there is the bird. Every basin has one, perhaps more, but you never see two at once. Rat-grey, with eyes that reflect kiln-light too brightly, it lives on nothing. There is nothing here for a bird to eat — no seeds, no insects in number — and yet it watches the work, head cocked, as if checking it. The people leave it alone. The old mason will tell his apprentice, *"Don't look at it directly, it will remember your face wrong."* What that means, no one can explain.

But the truest inhabitant is not any single body. It is the *habit* itself. The basin is alive the way a reef is alive — not as one creature, but as the accretion of a million tiny, relentless lives, each performing the same small task with primitive tools, until the task itself becomes a kind of organism. The people are its polyps. Their hands are its cilia. Their hammering is its heartbeat, echoing off close stone in the cool, damp, soot-orange dusk, saying over and over, stone by stone, *stay, stay, stay.*

If you stay long enough, your own hands will start to twitch in your sleep.
