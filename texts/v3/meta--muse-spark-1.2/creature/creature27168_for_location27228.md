You would think at first that nothing lives here. That is the trick of it.

You stand in one of those narrow streets - which is not a street but a negotiation between two trunks - and the city seems empty. Abandoned. The amber walls glow around you and the air is still and the soft moss under your feet takes your weight without a sound, and there is no smoke, no voice, no footfall. The doorways are open but dark. You could be forgiven for thinking the builders left, or died, or never existed.

Then you wait. And the place teaches you how to look.

The first to show themselves are not the people. They are the small keepers, and you were already seeing them without knowing they were alive.

That pale fruit on the rib of the column beside your shoulder - perfectly still, dusted white, six legs folded tight under a domed shell - twitches a feathery antenna the length of your finger. It tastes the air. It scrapes. It moves a millimeter and stops, as if moving too far would waste something irreplaceable. There are dozens on this one column alone. Hundreds in the grove. Hand-sized, wax-backed, blind or nearly so, they spend their whole lives on a single vertical acre, licking the floury bloom off the succulent's skin and polishing it again with their own wax. When your shadow falls across them they do not run. They close. They become seamless pearls, perfect spheres tucked into the grooves of the bark, and you understand: they keep what they have by becoming indistinguishable from the thing that keeps them.

Above you, what you took for a dead frond lets go.

It doesn't fall. It unfolds. A mantis of dry membrane, amber as the wall it hung from, so thin you can see light through its limbs, so still you realize it has been breathing once every few minutes while you stared right at it. It has been there for hours. It will be there for hours more. It does not hunt by chasing. It waits for the bloom-scrapers to forget.

And down at your feet, the ground itself moves.

Not the moss - what lives on it. A herd, if you can call twenty creatures moving as one body a herd, of low, pill-shaped grazers. Each no longer than your thumb, each translucent as pale honey, each with a dark green smear visible through its back where the rubbery liverwort is being slowly processed inside it. They advance like a single tide, soft mouths grinding, leaving behind a faint, slightly shinier trail where they have mown the moss close. They do not scatter when you shift your weight. They tighten. The whole herd pauses, holds, then resumes. Their water - if you could call it water - was made inside them, from the breaking of the moss itself. They will never drink. They will never piss. What they take, they keep.

This is the abundant life of the bowl, and it is abundant precisely because it has learned to be invisible, inaudible, and slow. But it is not the town.

The town is for the others. The ones the rooms grew around.

You have to duck into a doorway to meet them, because they do not come out while the sun is high.

Inside the column the air is ten degrees cooler and smells faintly sweet, like warm resin and clean oil. The light is not white here but filtered amber-green through living walls a handspan thick. The floor gives slightly under you - it is alive, springy pith. And in that dim, the inhabitants are finally visible, because they are the same color and translucence as the walls, and because they do not move when you enter.

They are not tall. Nothing tall could afford to live here.

At first you think there are two of them curled in the back chamber, but there are three - the third is a child, or what will be a child, tucked against the larger one's side. They are low and broad, not quite waist-high to you if they stood, which they rarely do. Their bodies are barrelled and soft, without the hard angles of mammals or lizards, and they have no neck to speak of. Their skin is not skin. It is the same waxy, bloom-dusted rind as the columns, bone-pale and jade-veined, thick as leather to the touch and faintly warm. You can see light through the edges of their limbs. You can see, when one shifts, the slow swirl of clear, golden oil inside its trunk and thighs - its blood, its water, its food all at once, sealed in.

They have no hair. Instead their crowns and shoulders are felted with the same microscopic down that covers the fronds outside - a dense, silvery fur that is not for warmth but for catching. At night, when the rock gives back its heat and the air inside the bowl briefly holds a little more vapor than it does by day, they will climb, slow as sap, to the roofs and crowns and stand there with those felts erect, combing the dark for damp that never becomes dew. What the felt catches goes directly inward. Nothing is lost to a droplet.

Their faces - if you keep watching - resolve. They have eyes, but they are lidded with a transparent wax film, lidless in the way of a succulent, always open, always glistening but never weeping. They do not blink away moisture because they cannot afford to make tears. Their mouths are small, lipless, usually closed. They feed rarely, by scraping bloom and soft pith and the oil-rich bodies of the smallest grazers, and they chew for a long time. Six limbs, you realize. Or four limbs and two others that are something between arm and root - the lower pair thick and foot-like, for bracing against the springy floor, the upper pairs more delicate, tipped not with fingers but with flat, spatulate pads that can adhere to the living wall and taste it.

They do not look at you. Not right away. Sound is expensive here - the air inside is so still that a voice would carry from one end of the bowl to the other, and to make a voice you must exhale wet air. So they do not call.

They speak with light and touch.

When the larger one finally acknowledges you, it does so by leaning, very slowly, and placing one of those pads against the wall of the room, and the wall - and then its own flank - flushes. A faint, amber blush travels under its translucent skin, then fades. Another blush answers from the second adult, on its back. The child pulses, weakly. It is not bioluminescence as you know it from the sea; it is the oil inside them, stirred and brought closer to the surface, catching the filtered sun. In the dim they signal each other through their own bodies, and through the body of the house, which conducts the glow from chamber to chamber the way the columns conduct sunlight at dusk.

If you stay very still, one will come to you. Not out of curiosity as you understand it, but out of maintenance. You are warm, you are exhaling water, you are a temporary irregularity in a system that keeps what it has. It will reach out and brush your forearm with that pad, not to feel you but to taste the salt and oil on your skin, and then it will wipe its pad carefully on its own flank, reclaiming even that faint smear.

They are not many, but they are dense in the way the grove is dense. Five or six to a column, twenty columns grafted together into a block, blocks braided into the whole bowl. They do not own rooms. Rooms happen. A pair will sleep for years in a chamber while the plant slowly grows a membrane across the back of it, dividing it. Then there are two chambers. A child will be budded - not born, not laid - as a swelling on the parent's side that slowly pinches off over months, already waxy, already filled with its own small reservoir of oil, already knowing not to cry. An elder will grow stiller and stiller until its bloom thickens and its joints stiffen, and then it will not move again, and the family will not move it. They will let the column grow around it, incorporate it. Over years the wall will thicken where it lay. The dead do not rot here; there is no wet rot. They dry and bleach and become architecture. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is buried.

They tend, more than they build. With small, patient scrapes of a keratinous plate on their underside they prune a rib that is growing too fast and closing a street. They guide a vine to fuse two crowns so a bridge forms. They carry the waxy, lantern-like flowers - when the rare humid night comes and all the crowns bloom at once - from column to column themselves, crawling with pollen stuck to their felts, because there is no wind inside the rim to do it for them.

If you stay until the light slants, you will see them emerge.

Late afternoon, when the basalt outside has soaked its kiln-heat and the sharp rim throws its perfect shadow across half the bowl, the whole city lights up from within. Every wall becomes a lamp. And in that divided world - half amber, half jade - the inhabitants come out onto the streets and onto the roofs. Dozens. Perhaps a hundred in this bowl that is only a few hundred meters across. They do not hurry. They stand on the high fronds and lift their felted backs to the cooling air. The floaters - those bladdered drifters you thought were seeds - descend among them, trailing filaments, and the inhabitants raise their arms and let the filaments brush them, harvesting the tiny gnats the floaters have caught, sharing this slow commerce without a sound.

They know the edge. Every one of them has walked to the scree where the glassy plates still fit together and touched the sharp lip where the world stops. They know there is nothing for them beyond it but thin air and thirst. So they stay. They keep.

And if you sit long enough on that warm, living floor, breathing too loudly in the quietest lush place you have ever been, one of those small, barrelled people will pulse its light at you again - not as welcome or warning, but as an offering of information, the only kind that matters here: *I am still keeping. You are still keeping. We are keeping together.*
