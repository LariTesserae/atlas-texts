They are not easy to see at first.

That is the first adaptation. In a place this big and this pale and this quiet, anything that moves a lot does not live long. You stand on the boulevard looking up at the trunk-towns and you think at first the place is empty - a factory left on after the shift, lights on, machines purring for no one. Then your eye catches a flicker on a balcony three hundred meters up, or a dust plume that doesn't settle the way processor wind should, and you realize you have been looking straight at them.

You thought they would be people in the Columns. They are more like people *of* the Columns.

From a distance they look like part of the cork.

They are tall and narrow and still, the way things are narrow when there has never been any reason to be broad. In the open boulevard they do not hurry. They walk with a long, economical stride that barely lifts their boots off the pressed mineral floor, as if to keep from stirring the dust that already hangs forever. Their clothing is the same color as the walls they live in - not by camouflage but by economy. Coats and wraps and hoods pressed from the same fibrous cork you see peeling off the Columns in sheets, felted and beaten until it is soft as paper-cloth, bone-white and oatmeal and undyed. Over that, aprons of oiled canvas, dark with the amber plant-oil that never washes out. Where the light hits them from the sun-shafts they are almost luminous.

Closer, the correction.

They are human, or were. The plan of them is human - two arms, two legs, a head that turns to look when you step too loud on the iron plates. But they have been edited by aridity the way a river stone is edited by water, only in reverse. Everything about them is sealed.

Their skin is not skin as you know it. It is pale to the point of translucence, then waxed. You can see it on the backs of their hands where the sleeve pulls up: a faint sheen, like the bloom on a leaf, not sweat, not oil, but a secretion they rub in daily from rendered Column wax. It closes them. They do not perspire - they can't afford to. Their faces are narrow, lips thin and kept closed, nostrils small and valved. Most wear a fine veil or a wrap of cork-gauze over mouth and nose when they are outside the trunks, not for worship but to keep the hydrophobic dust out and the trace moisture in. Their eyes are large and dark and perpetually a little narrowed, lidded with a second, clear membrane that clicks across when the wind picks up. They blink rarely.

They do not drink. Not in the way you think.

You watch an older woman on a low terrace outside Column Two. She tends a blister on the trunk wall, a taut dome of living cork the size of a bathtub, amber with stored oil inside. She taps it with a long, hollow needle and the hydrocarbon sap runs slow as honey into a sealed tin. When she is done she wipes the needle on her apron and then, with a practiced tilt, takes a small sip from a separate, smaller flask at her belt - not water, not oil, but brine. A sharp, clear salt-liquor drawn from the deep halite, the trace water locked in crystal for millions of years and now rendered. That is their water - a few milliliters a day, taken like medicine, conserved in bodies that have learned to do chemistry without it. Their urine, if they produce any, is a dry white powder. Their shit is pressed pellets they return to the root-felt. Nothing is flushed. Nothing can be wasted. You understand then why the air is so dry and stays so dry - they are dry themselves. They taste of camphor and salt if you stood close enough to smell them.

And they are quiet because the place demands quiet. Sound here dies in cork. A voice raised is a waste of energy and also somehow rude, like shouting in a library that is also a body. They speak low, in short sentences, often with hands. On the cableways and scaffolds you see them signaling more than shouting - a raised palm, a flick of oiled fingers. Their language has a lot of words for textures of dryness and stages of peeling and the sound a Column makes when it is ready to be hollowed. They do not have a word for damp. Or they had one once and let it fall away.

Work is not labor to them. It is tending.

Because the Columns are alive and the city is alive inside them, there is no distinction between maintenance and husbandry. The young - and there are not many, the density is sparse by design - learn to read the root-mat the way other children learn to read faces. You see a boy, perhaps eight, perhaps eighty, it is hard to tell because they all look cured rather than aged, lying on his stomach on an iron balcony, watching a root tip. White as a cable, as thick as your wrist, it noses along the basalt, secreting a bead of acid that fizzes faintly. He does not touch it. He watches for the tremor that says it has found purchase. Next to him an older man sprays a fine mist of hormones from a copper canister onto a bulge in the cork wall. The mist smells sharp, like iodine. Where it lands the living tissue swells almost imperceptibly, softening, beginning to extrude a new lip that by next season will harden into a floor, a room, a home for someone not yet born. They are not building. They are coaxing. The Column will give them a balcony if they ask correctly.

Other guilds move differently. The harvesters wait. They do not cut. They stand under the vast dead sheets of outer cork - each one a peeling as big as a house - and listen for the subsonic crackle that means the layer has delaminated. Then they step back and let it fall. It comes down silent and enormous, a *whumph* of displaced air that lifts dust in a slow ring. They will drag it to the mills at the base where it will be pressed into board and paper and fuel, the dead skin of their house becoming their house.

The refinery workers are stained amber to the elbow, their gauze veils yellowed. They move among vats where the Column-oil is clarified, burning in low lamps that never smoke. At night - though it is always late morning here - you can see their windows from the boulevard, each trunk a vertical street of warm lamp-light, each window a workshop where someone is mending a coat, or scraping an isopod shell for its chitin, or simply sitting very still, conserving.

Because they conserve everything. Movement, moisture, heat, noise, even offspring. Families are small, spaced far apart like the Columns themselves. An elder when they die does not rot. You understand this when you pass a small niche cut into the root-felt wall on the way to Column Three. Inside, wrapped in cork shroud, is a figure, desiccated intact, features preserved like paper, hands folded over a chest that still holds its wax. It will lie there for years, slowly bleaching, until the isopods are allowed to mill it or until the family decides to press it into the wall itself. There is no burial in ground that is iron plate. There is only curing.

And they are not alone. Not even counting the moths and the stilt-browsers and the dog-sized isopods that leave their pale trails on the root-felt, the air between Columns is full of the other residents - the drones. At first you thought they were machines. Closer, you see they are grown. Many-jointed legs of chitin and actuator cable, backs of pressed cork, eyes that are both lens and photophore. They pick their way along the trunk walls on no one's command, pruning a dead aerial root, dabbing resin on a crack, herding a drifting moth back toward the canopy. The people do not command them; they pass each other with the indifference of coworkers who have shared a shift for decades. Some of the drones have names scratched in iron on their sides. Some of the people have grafts that look uncomfortably like drone joints at the wrist.

If you stand long enough in the center of the boulevard, between Two and Three, with the sun-shaft throwing its hard white column down through dust that never seems to land, one of them will notice you. Not with surprise - surprise takes water.

A woman stops on her way from the processors, a tin of salt-liquor at her hip, her veil down so you can see her mouth, thin and sealed. She looks at you the way you looked at the root tip - to see if you have found purchase, if you are secreting the right acid to anchor.

Then she nods, once, economical. And keeps walking. Her boots do not echo long. The dust she raised hangs in the air behind her, lit gold by the Fresnel sun, for a very long time before it decides to settle.
