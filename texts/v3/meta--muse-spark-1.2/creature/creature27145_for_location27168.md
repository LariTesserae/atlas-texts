What lives here is not *in* here.

That's the first wrong idea you have to let go of. You look for an animal the way you look for a figure against a ground - a deer in a forest, a fish in a pool. Here there is no figure. There is only ground that has learned to move.

If you crouch and stare at what you thought was moss, it will stare back, but not with eyes.

Closer. Stay there. Let it resolve.

What lives here lives by the four rules, because it *is* the four rules made flesh.

### 1. It must be close.

Nothing here is discrete. Nothing is allowed to be an individual in the clean, counted sense.

At first you think you are seeing separate creatures - a beetle on a leaf, a newt in the pan, a snail on the bark. Then you stay still long enough for the rain that isn't rain to bead on your neck, and you see that the beetle's feet never quite leave the leaf. Its pads exude the same waxy film the leaf exudes. The newt in the pan is not swimming; it is pressed belly to belly with three other newts, and their undersides have gone translucent and adhered. The snails on the trunk are not crowded, they are fused at the lip of their shells by a living mortar of algae and slime that is itself alive.

What lives here is flat. Even when it is tall, it is flat. Pressed.

The true body plan of this basin is the mat and the sheet and the curtain. Lungs, if you can call them that, are not inside. They are outside. The amphibian things that chorus - and there are millions of them per acre, you understand now that the *water* was amphibians - have no real lungs at all. Their skin *is* their lung, broad, frilled, kept wet by the weeping rock and the air that sits on you like a damp hand. They lie in the ankle-deep tea-colored broth with their chins touching, throat sacs inflating not to call to a mate - there are no mates, there is no need to travel - but to pump air across that skin. The thrum you feel in your molars is breathing. The whole plain breathing.

The insects are the same. The dragonflies as long as your forearm are not hunters ranging wide. They hover and they orbit a single hummock, a single column of light between two interlocking crowns, their whole lives within ten cubic feet of air, because to go farther would be to violate closeness. Their wings are not clear; they are veined like leaves because they carry zooxanthellae, or something older, green in the blood. Their backs are beaded with mites that are not parasites but continuations, little orange dots that are eyes for the dragonfly and wings for the mite.

If there is a larger animal here - and there is, you will see it if you stop looking for shape - it is colonial. A crab the size of a dog, climbing a soft-wooded herbaceous trunk, is not one crab. Its carapace is a garden of liverworts and egg clutches and the white, blind isopods that pick at them. When it molts, it doesn't climb out of its shell; the shell softens and is eaten from the outside while the new shell hardens underneath, so there is never a moment where it is exposed. To be exposed would be to be far from something.

Even you, if you stood here long enough, would begin to feel the rule tugging at your edges. Your boots fill with warm water and you don't mind. A leech as thick as your thumb finds your ankle and you don't remove it, because removal would be a kind of distance. Leaf-cutter ants, except they are not ants, begin to lay a trail across your instep. The felt of moss under your palm, which you thought was plant, is warm to the touch. It pulses.

### 2. It must be spread thin.

Nothing here gathers. That is why there are no rivers, no mouths, no stomachs as you know them.

Look at how anything here eats or drinks. Nothing has a throat that leads to a deep interior. Everything is a surface. The broad-leafed herbs with table-sized leaves do not have roots that go down; they have roots that hang down like curtains into the air to drink the condensation directly. The amphibians do not drink; they sit and let water climb them by capillarity. Their bellies are pale, almost transparent, and you can see the tea-colored water moving through them not in a gut but as a stain spreading along a paper towel.

The dominant life - if you must give it a name, call it the Sheet - is not species but habit: to be two-dimensional. Flatworms as wide as dinner plates glide under the floating rafts, so thin you can see the shadow of leaves through them. Slugs as long as your arm, color of wet ash, plow through the moss-mattress leaving a furrow that fills with water instantly and is already being plaited over by the moss behind them. They have no front and back. Either end can be the head.

Even reproduction is spread thin. There is no spawning aggregation, no point. The warm pans shiver continuously with eggs being released and fertilized in place, a fine, invisible snow in the water that never clouds it, because as many eggs dissolve as are laid. You will never see a nest. You will never see a parent guarding. The young are not born, they are exfoliated - a bud of skin on a newt's flank swells, darkens, detaches and is already pulsing with its own throat sac before it has drifted an inch away. Nothing travels to meet.

### 3. It must share broken light.

Eyes here are useless or they are everywhere.

The light never arrives straight, so nothing looks straight ahead. The true trees - those fast, hollow green-barked things that make the roof - have no eyes at all, but their bark photosynthesizes even in this dim, and the animals that live on them borrow that. If you turn over one of the fist-sized snails, its shell interior is green. Not from algae *on* it, but *in* its mantle. If you hold one of the small warm-blooded things - not a bird, not a bat, the furry gliders that launch in clouds - up to the thin light, its wing membrane glows. It is translucent and veined with chlorophyll. Everything is a little bit plant, because to be only animal here would be to demand too much direct light.

So vision is not for hunting at distance. The dragonflies have compound eyes the size of peas, but they are milky, tuned only to movement within a foot. The crabs have eyestalks but they are encrusted with epiphytes and mostly retracted. What matters is touch and taste and the sensing of vibration through water and stem. The whole plain is an eardrum. The insect saw, the amphibian thrum, the plop of condensation from leaf to leaf - those are not songs. They are the way the Sheet feels itself. When one amphibian inflates, the water around it trembles, and ten thousand others adjust their inflation by a fraction. You can watch this ripple run across a pan like wind, though there is no wind.

If something like a person had been born here, not come here, it would have no eyebrows, no lashes - they would collect water and rot. Its eyes would be small, deep-set, and filmed over, milky as the dragonfly's. It would not look at the horizon, because there is no horizon, only next leaf. It would navigate by smell of iron and crushed green, by how loudly its own skin hums against the chorus. It would be low and wide, not for crawling but because height is distance from the wet. Its chest would be broad and its skin damp and permeable, its ribs visible through it, pulsing. It would not be alone. It could not be alone. To be alone here is to die, and death is not allowed to accumulate.

### 4. It must replace itself exactly.

This is the quiet weirdness you felt. Nothing dies more than it is replaced, instantly.

There are no corpses here. Or, there are, but they never become corpses. A leaf falls - and you will watch this if you watch long enough - and before it hits the water it is already covered. Not by scavengers that arrive, but by scavengers that were already there, on it, in it. Beetles that lived inside the leaf's veins continue eating as it detaches. Fungi that were waiting as spores in its waxy cup germinate on the way down and have already sent threads into it by the time it lands. An amphibian beneath it opens its broad, lidless mouth not to catch prey but to receive the leaf's fall.

Death is a thinning, not an event. An old newt does not go still. Its skin goes more and more translucent until one day you can see right through it to the water below, and then the water is there and the newt is not, but there are two smaller newts sitting exactly where its left and right sides were, already pulsing. A dragonfly's wings fray, and as they fray, the mites on its back eat the fray, and the bacteria on the mites eat the mites' waste, and there is no remainder.

Because of this, nothing learns. Nothing evolves. The place has not finished being made, and it will never finish, because finishing would require succession, would require that something give way. Here everything gives way and does not give way at the same instant. The plain is embryonic forever because its children refuse to be born as anything other than the embryo.

So what lives here?

A single, country-sized, shallow, warm, breathing skin that has decided to be many small bodies the way a sea decides to be many waves.

If you put your hand flat on the moss that is also beetles, and keep it there until the sweat of the ground beads between your fingers, you will feel it - not a bite, not a crawl, but a faint, polite pressure as the life beneath you adjusts to include the new warm surface you have offered. A snail will change its course by a millimeter to touch your little finger. An amphibian in the pan by your knee will time its throat to yours without knowing it is doing so. The roof above you will drip a fat, warm drop onto your shoulder, and the drop will contain a larva, and the larva will already be looking for a place to be close to.

It does not want anything from you. It does not need you. It has been holding its breath, perfectly, before you arrived, and it will keep holding it after you leave, if you can persuade your feet to unstick from the hissing, springy, living felt long enough to go.

It lives as the basin lives: low, wet, dim, touching itself everywhere at once, teeming so absolutely that teeming becomes stillness.
