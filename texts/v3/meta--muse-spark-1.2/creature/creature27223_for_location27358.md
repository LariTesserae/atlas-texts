What lives here has learned how to live in air that should not be there.

At first you will think nothing does. Not really. Because the emptiness tricks you.

You stand between two of the Titans — trunks like broken cliffs sixty feet through, their first branches a hundred feet over your head — and you look across a hundred yards of open, moss-soft ground dappled with clean sun, then another hundred yards to the next trunk, and another, and another until haze eats them, and your brain says *savanna* or *parkland* or *abandoned orchard*. A place thinned out. A place waiting for something to fill it.

Then the air moves.

And you realize the emptiness is not empty at all. It is full the way the sea is full. You were just looking at the wrong scale.

#### The Throng

The first nation here is too small and too many to count, and it owns the ground.

Not the soil — because there is no soil as you understand it — but the living sponge. That peat-deep carpet of moss and felt-lichen and interwoven root-mat, a yard thick in places and thousands of years deep, never dry, never quite wet. If you kneel and put your hand in it, it gives like a mattress and sighs. If you put your ear to it, it clicks and chirrs.

Everything lives in that. Mites the color of rust moving in herds through the moss forests. Springtails detonating like fleas. Beetles the size of your fist, black and bottle-green, their backs so hard they tick when they hit bark, plowing slow furrows. Jewel wasps and stingless bees, solitary and colonial both, in such radiant, improbable colors — opal, copper, aniline blue — that you think at first they are flowers that have learned to fly. Ants. Above all, ants. Not in hills but in rivers, braided highways a foot wide where leaf-cutters carrying green sails march for miles between Titans, disappearing into root-wall tunnels, reappearing half a mile away. They farm fungus in the damp heart of the mat, and the fungus breaks the moss, and the moss holds the water, and the water feeds everything.

And when the heavy night condensation falls — that endless, quiet second rain dripping from every leaf — the carpet wakes up a second time. Flatworms glide. Tiny black millipedes coil. Tree-frogs no bigger than your thumbnail, gold and moss-green, boil out of the cistern-pools hidden in the moss-basins and call, and call, and call until the air itself seems to vibrate.

You don’t walk *on* this place. You walk *over* a city.

#### The Air Itself

Because between the Titans there are no shrubs, no understory, no brush to get in the way. Any lowlander animal would have hidden there. Here nothing hides. The great evolutionary pressure of the plateau was to master the nave.

So almost everything flies, or glides, or falls with style.

The daytime sound you hear from miles away — that wall that makes you shout to be heard — is wings. Clouds of white moths turning shafts of light opaque. Beetles droning past like freight. Dragonflies with two-foot wingspans that hawk straight down the colonnades, transparent as glass. And the birds.

There are so many birds you stop trying to individuate them. Small swifts the color of ash that never land, sleeping on the wing, drinking from the cupped leaves of the air-plants. Parrot-things, heavy and loud, green flecked with red, that crack the barrel-bromeliad fruits and drop the rind to the moss where the ants claim it in minutes. Crow-kin, black and clever, that have learned to follow people to water pockets. And the gliders, which are not birds at all — mammals, maybe, or something older — with skin membranes stretched from elongated forelimb to ankle, faces like foxes, eyes huge, launching from the hanging gardens three hundred feet up, flaring, coasting the whole open gap to the next Titan in a silent, shallow glide, sometimes a mother with two young clinging to her belly, the air suddenly full of them at dusk.

It works because the air is reliable. The plateau never storms destructively. The thermals are gentle. The distances are far but not *too* far. A creature that can cross a hundred yards without touching ground can live its entire life without ever descending to the moss. Many never do.

Higher, slower, there are the browsers. You will not see one for a day, then you will stare at a horizontal limb the thickness of a house and realize it *is* an animal. The sloth-giants. Five tons, shaggy with epiphytes growing in their own fur, moving so slowly that swifts nest on them. They live up there for generations, hook-armed, stripping the door-sized ferns that curtain from the Titan crowns, never coming down. Their shit falls three hundred feet in fibrous bolts and vanishes into the moss before you can reach it. Where they feed they prune, where they prune the light changes, and where the light changes, a thousand insects explode.

And there are hawks. Not many. They don't need to be. With nowhere to hide and nowhere not visible for miles, a predator here is not an ambush hunter but an interceptor — falcon-things with translucent flight skins, not feathers, that stoop through the empty nave at terrifying speed. You hear the sudden slice in the chorus, a pocket of silence that opens and closes again, and a swirl of white moths where there was a swirl a second before.

Everything is diurnal until it isn't. By midday the birds thin and the cicadas — each as long as your hand, bark-colored, invisible until they start — take over the sound, sawing from every trunk until the Titans themselves seem to hum. At night it never gets quiet. The frogs do that.

#### The Thin People

And then there are the people. Fewer than the birds by a factor of thousands. Fewer than the beetles by a factor of millions.

They are not an exception to the ecology. They are part of the same answer to the same question: *how do you live where water lives only in cups, and the ground is alive, and the sky is a room?*

You don't find a village. You find a line. A trail worn into the moss, not down to dirt — there is no dirt — but compressed, a darker green that springs back if not walked for a season. You follow it and find a house stitched between two buttress roots, off the moss entirely, floor of woven living fern that still grows and must be trimmed. Bark-slab walls, vine cordage, a roof thatched with leaves that root and flower. Empty in the day; its people are up.

Their bodies haven't changed much. They are a little leaner, a little louder. Children here learn to shout not out of rudeness but because the day chorus is a physical thing; to be heard at twenty feet you must use your chest. At night they go whisper-soft and listen, because the night chorus carries meaning. Old people are hoarse.

Their technology is all tending. They do not cut the Titans — an adze of bloomery iron could work for a year and not get through that bark — they coax. A vine is trained over three years to thicken into a handrail. A bromeliad is hollowed with a hot stone so it stays alive, holding a gallon of tea-colored rain in its rosette without poisoning, and its axil is learned as a well as reliable as any lowland spring. A limb-garden is pruned to make a bridge that will bear a man’s weight in eight years. They wear bark cloth beaten soft and capes of stitched insect silk, light as air, dyed with lichen. They carry gourds not as canteens but as dippers for the cisterns. They read the Titans the way lowlanders read sky: the deep spirals carved at chest height are not art but ledger — this cistern fails in dry months, this crown fruits every ninth year, this way to the escarpment where the mists pour.

They live in bands of three to seven shelters who share a water-tree. No chief, no surplus to support one. When a Titan flowers — once a decade, the entire crown going crimson overnight — everyone within a day's glide comes, because with the flowers come the moths, and with the moths come the birds, and with the birds come meat and eggs and feathers, and the epiphytes on that Titan will all fruit at once, as if someone gave an order.

They know about the order.

#### The One That Contains Them

Stay long enough and you notice the pulse.

It isn’t the sound. It is *through* the sound. At first the din is chaotic, every bird and bug and frog for itself. Then, if you stand still between the pillars with your eyes closed around noon, you hear a wave pass: a ripple of calling that starts at the distant bone-white wall of the escarpment and sweeps inward across miles of open nave, bird to bird to cicada to frog, like wind through grass but made of voices, and then it passes you and goes on, and a few minutes later it comes again from the other side, having circumnavigated the whole table.

The vines curl when the mist comes. All of them, at once.

The flowering. The fruiting. The way none of the living carpet ever slides, ever erodes, despite being a sponge perched a mile in the sky.

It lives here, too. Not a who. Not a what with eyes. The plateau itself has had so long to settle — erosion that stopped eroding, heat that learned to hold itself — that the plant has become a single thing. Not a forest. A meadow that became a continent that became an organism with Titans for bones and moss for skin and hanging gardens for lungs, breathing damp in and out every night, holding water in a million cupped hands so its children do not die of thirst.

The insects are its blood. The birds and gliders are its breath moving between ribs. The sloth-giants are slow thoughts passing along its arms. The people are... what? Lately, perhaps, it has grown clever enough to be tended. They are not separate from it; they are the part of it that learned to make ladders and remember where the water is.

If you stay until evening, when the light slants forever under the crowns and turns the whole colonnade gold, you will see them up there, little figures moving on the great horizontal limbs among the curtains of fern, checking the bromeliad cisterns, harvesting, singing loudly over the sawing of the cicadas a song that, if you listen, is timed just slightly to that passing wave.

They sing not to be heard, but to be in time.
