Nothing lives here at first.

That is your first, honest count. You stand still a long time on the smooth, warm dome of hematite and salt and hear the basin huffing and clanging around you - *chuff chuff chuff*, whistle, hum, clang - and you see no movement at all but the shimmer of heat and the beading of water that beads and un-beads on stone. It is a place so loud and so balanced that life feels like it would be an interruption, a noise out of rhythm. You start to believe the mineral varnish really is just a stain, that the description you were given of an isopod was generous.

Then you learn how to look.

You have to crouch. You have to put your hands on a stone that is blood-warm from below and wait until your own breathing slows enough that you stop seeing your own shadow move. And the film resolves.

The first thing that lives here is not a thing at all. It is the varnish.

Coating the lee sides of boulders, pooling in the shallowest concavities where condensation lingers longest, is a skin a few cells thick. Black-brown, sometimes with that oil-slick manganese rainbow when you catch it at an angle. It is not lichen. It does not have the green desperation of lichen. It is bacteria and archaea, lithotrophs, chemotrophs - things that eat stone and steam. They oxidize iron, they fix sulfur from the faint rotten-egg sigh that comes up through invisible cracks. They do not photosynthesize so much as *precipitate*. They live so slowly you cannot see them live. At the edge of a seep where warm water darkens the rock and then vanishes again, the varnish is slightly thicker, slightly wetter, slightly more alive. If you watch for an hour, you will not see it spread. If you came back in ten years, it would be exactly the same shape. It grows at the rate the silica precipitates. It dies at the rate it is weathered away. It is in equilibrium with the rock, and that is its whole strategy: to be indistinguishable from mineral until you realize mineral here learned how to hold a charge.

What grazes on it is even better at being mineral.

You see it only because you stopped looking for movement and started looking for a wrongness in texture. On a sheet of damp stone where a vein of water runs a few molecules thick for three feet before sinking, there is a creature the size of the last joint of your thumb. 

At first you think it is a flake of evaporite that has curled. Then you see it has edges.

It is an isopod, but the word is a convenience. It has no eyes at all - only a smooth, domed head the color of wet salt, faintly translucent. No pigment worth naming. Its back is plated in seven overlapping segments the pale pink-white of shrimp boiled too long, each plate fringed with a microscopic nap that holds a film of water against its body so it never dries. It has fourteen legs, but they are short, tucked, useless for speed. It moves the way a glacier moves - you look away and when you look back it is a millimeter forward, its antennae, which are not antennae but blunt chemical tongues, tasting the varnish ahead of it.

It does not walk so much as it *irons* itself over the rock, its underside secreting a faint acid that loosens the bacterial film just enough to lap it up. It leaves no trail. The water film closes behind it. It has no sound. Against the huff and whistle of the chimneys, evolution has told it to be silent is to survive. To vibrate, to click, to call would be to be drowned and to waste.

If you are very still and very close, you can see it carries something: under its tail, three amber spheres, each the size of a peppercorn, held in a basket of fine bristles. Eggs. It will carry them for years. Perhaps four years. Perhaps ten. The heat is steady, the food is steady, there is no season to rush for. When they hatch, the young are perfect miniatures, salt-pale, and they will walk away from their mother without ever touching her again and take up a damp patch a few yards away that no one else wants. They will live a long time. Twenty, forty, sixty years, moving a few hundred yards in a lifetime. You can tell an old one because its plates have taken on the same manganese rainbow as the rock, because the hot brine has glazed it.

You will walk an hour before you see another. That is not because they are hiding. That is their natural density. Anything denser would accumulate. Anything that accumulated would break the balance and starve. The basin permits them only as a sparse correction, a way to keep the varnish from getting too thick.

Above them, where the air is warmest, are the only things here that ever leave the ground.

You hear them after you see them, or rather you notice a new quality in the air. Near a chimney that is humming its electrical drone, in the column of shimmering heat, there is a tremor.

Three of them. Sometimes two. Sometimes one alone.

They look like dragonflies drawn by someone who had only heard a dragonfly described. The body is a needle of clear chitin no longer than your finger, hollow, with nothing inside but warm air and a gut that is mostly a tube for processing steam-precipitated organics. The wings - four of them - do not flap. They vibrate, high and almost invisible, like foil held over a heater. They make no buzz you can hear under the factory chorus; instead they ride it. They keep station in the updraft of a chimney, shivering in place, occasionally displacing a few inches sideways and stopping again.

Their heads are blunt, with two huge eyes that are not eyes for seeing shape but heat-pits, black and blind to visible light. They navigate by temperature and by tremor. The world for them is a map of warm and warmer, of *chuff* and whistle. They never land. To land would be to be on the damp stone where the isopods crawl and where water would foul their vibrating wings. So they hover their entire lives, dipping only to sip - a touch of their needle mouth to a weeping vent where sulfur water beads, taking up dissolved organics and archaea, then rising again. When they mate, they do it in the air, a brief clasp that looks like two heat-shimmers colliding, and the female will later flick a single egg, varnished in salt, onto a cool stone in shadow. If the stone stays damp for a year, it hatches. If not, it waits. The egg can wait a very long time.

There are others you almost never see unless you lift a stone, and you learn quickly not to lift stones because the condensation underneath is a little world that has been sealed for years.

Underneath, in the permanent dark damp where the stone has been cupped for centuries, there are thread-thin worms, white as brine, as thin as hairs, that swim through the film of water that never evaporates. There are mites the size of poppy seeds, the color of rust, that move even slower than the isopod. All of them are pale, all of them are blind, all of them are sparse. None of them has a voice loud enough to compete with the stones. They have all, over unimaginable time, learned the basin's one law:

Do not make more of yourself than the stone can stand.

Nothing here blooms. Nothing swarms. Nothing hunts in packs. A predator that needed ten prey a day would flicker out in a week. So there are no predators as you understand them. The isopod scrapes film. The fliers sip film. The worms drink film. The film eats rock and air. When something dies - and rarely, something does - it does not rot. There are no flies to find it. It simply dries where it stops, its water taken back into the stone's pores, its chitin and salt bleached and then slowly filmed over by the same varnish it once ate, until after a season you cannot tell where animal ends and mineral begins. No accumulation.

That is what lives here, then. Not a fauna. Not an ecology that is trying to become something else. A maintenance crew so small and so slow you could miss it if you blink.

They are not suffering. They are not lonely, though you will feel lonely for them. They are perfectly fitted to a place that is not dead and not alive but *working* - a factory floor that has been running at equilibrium since before the hills wore down. They have tuned their lives to its rhythm so completely that they do not hear the huff and clang anymore than you hear your own heart. They move between the whistles, feed between the sighs, breed on a schedule set by condensation, and persist without ever increasing, without ever decreasing, warming themselves on stones that are warm for no one, under a normal sun, in air that beads and un-beads forever.
