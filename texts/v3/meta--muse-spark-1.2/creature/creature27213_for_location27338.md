They live wet.

That's the first thing you have to understand before you can see them at all — not wet as in caught in rain, but wet as in *kept*. Like river stones. Like the hands of dishwashers. You expect to find people in this place huddled, shivering, seeking dryness, and you don't.

The people who stay here are never quite dry, and have stopped wanting to be.

At first from a distance you think there are very few of them, because the city is so large and so empty. You see one figure alone crossing a plaza that could hold a market of thousands, a small dark shape against the glare of sheeting water, and you think — abandoned, or automatons. Then you get closer and the proportion corrects. There are enough of them. Just not many. Not for a city this size. A density that feels chosen, like they agreed tacitly not to fill in the gaps.

You hear them second. Or rather, you stop hearing only the water and engines and hear them inside it.

Everyone here is loud.

Not angry-loud. Work-loud. The hoarse, cheerful, permanent half-shout of people who have lived their whole lives in a roar and have made their peace with it. Children are born with fine hearing and lose the top of it by eight or nine. By twenty they lip-read without thinking about it and cup a hand to your elbow to get your attention before they speak. By forty they have the throat — a low, gravelled, affectionate rasp, the turbine throat, from a lifetime of pitching the voice over falling water. Old people here are profoundly deaf and don't seem to mind. They sit on warm pipes or on high thresholds with their feet out of the runnel and watch your mouth move and smile and nod. They answer a beat later than you expect, because they felt what you said in your chest more than your words.

Because of that, they don't talk much when they don't need to. They have a language that is half spoken, half made.

Spoken, it is clipped and percussive — consonants you can see, vowels you can see. They punch the words so they carry. NO-CAP. GIVE-CHANNEL. HOLD-GATE. Even their jokes are built to be visible on the lips.

And then there is the rest, the hands. Not a formal sign language, not anymore if it ever was. A farm of gestures that grew up around noise. *Water comes.* *Move back.* *She's sleeping, quiet with your feet.* A flat palm held to a weeping wall to say *this one lives, leave it.* The flick of two fingers under the chin that means *deaf today, write it.* You see two maintenance women on opposite sides of a sluice a hundred meters apart having a whole argument with their arms and their mouths and laughing silently, and you realize the empty plazas aren't empty — they're so conversation can happen at scale. In a city built for shouting, you need that hundred meters of open, wet stone to see each other.

Look closer at their bodies and the place has written itself on them.

They are not pale highland people. The sun here is white and vertical and relentless when the cloud lifts, and the steel glare off the wet basalt burns. They are browned, freckled, soot-smudged in a way that never quite scrubs out of the creases of the knuckles. Their hair is cut short — men and women both — or else bound back hard under oiled caps, because long loose hair in steam and condensation and lathe work is a hazard. Their skin has a sheen to it. Even when they've towelled off, even inside, a faint sweat never leaves, beading on the upper lip, on the collarbone. The children glisten.

Their feet tell you they belong. No one here wears shoes that can't be flooded. Heavy rubber boots to the knee for the turbine crews, pitted and patched. Cheap canvas slip-ons for everyone else that are meant to be kicked off at the door and left to drain. Inside homes, outside homes, they go barefoot half the day even on the concrete because the water is warm. The soles of their feet are wide, pale and ridged, tough as leather. They walk with a slight splay, a water-walker's gait, sure on slick stone.

Their hands are the opposite of their feet — soft. Pruned is the wrong word, because pruning implies a temporary state before you dry out. Their fingertips are always a little swollen, a little smoothed, the fingerprints shallow. The hands of the older women who run the dye works are stained permanent indigo or madder to the second knuckle. The hands of the men who dress the basalt blocks are always damp and faintly warm, even when they haven't touched water in an hour, as if the plateau sweats through them too.

They dress for wet work, not for weather. Light, quick-drying synthetics and oilets that went out of fashion everywhere else a century ago but persist here because they work. Ponchos cut long in the back. Aprons with a hundred pockets for wrenches and cotton waste and chalk. Nothing heavy, nothing that holds water. Even their formal clothes — and they do have formal clothes, for weddings and for the Day of Gates at the end of the dry-build season — are just cleaner versions of the same: a white shirt that will be transparent with sweat by noon, dark trousers that will be splashed to the knee by the first street runnel.

You expect misery from this. Damp, deaf, soot, endless labor. But that is the outsider's mistake.

They are not miserable. They are not dour industrial drones. If they were they would have left — and people do leave, every year a few ride the long road down the escarpment to the valley where sound doesn't live in your teeth, and they don't come back. Those who remain have been selected by the place as much as they selected it. They like the bargain.

Their temperament is the temperament of the water and the stone: patient, steady-state, good-humored, glacial.

They do not hurry. Nothing here rewards hurry. A turbine quarried into rock takes twenty years to pay for itself. A new aqueduct level is planned when the children now being born will be the foremen who pour it. Rust moves by the millimeter. Basins deepen by a grain. So they have learned to work on that same time. A man will tend the same sluice his father tended, and his daughter after him, and think nothing of it. They will spend a whole afternoon tapping a brass fitting true with a tiny hammer, not because they are slow but because there is nowhere else that fitting needs to be until it is right. When they talk about the future they talk in twenty-year increments without noticing.

And they are, without exception, hosts.

That is the theology, if you can call a practical rule a theology. *Never cap a weep.* Every child learns it before they learn to read. If a wall weeps, you give it a channel. You cut a runnel with a chisel, you lay half-pipe, you tilt the house a fraction more. You do not plug it, you do not paint over it, you do not pray to it in the formal sense. You accommodate.

Because they don't think of water as a resource the way lowlanders do. Resource implies you own it, you use it up. Here water is a resident. The senior resident. It was here saturating the stone before the first Basalt block was laid and it will be here after the last iron throat rusts out. You borrow its fall. You put your turbine in its path and you say thank you by not blocking its other paths. The maintenance crews — who are half priesthood whether they would use that word or not — walk the lower tiers with stethoscopes and copper rods, listening to the lapping under the stone, and when they find a new seep behind a factory wall they chalk a circle around it and come back with a channel the next day. To wall it up, they say, is to be rude, and rudeness to water doesn't forgive. It finds another way, often through your floor.

So there are offerings, but they don't look like offerings. A runnel left deliberately a little too wide so a thin sheet can run clear even when the factory doesn't need it. A settling cistern left alone behind the dye works where the too-large dragonflies breed, because someone decided the roar needed a counter-quiet. A fig tree permitted to keep its grip on the edge of the clarifier although its roots crack the concrete, because it was there first. These are not sentimental gestures. They are maintenance.

The children here understand this before language. They are amphibious in a way valley children are not. By three they can swim in the warm, tiled cisterns. By five they can read the surface of a puddle and tell you which way it wants to go. They play games that would horrify a lowlander mother: floating paper boats down the center-runnel of streets wide as rivers, chasing them barefoot through ankle-deep sheet flow while the afternoon rain hammers down warm as blood. They are not afraid of being wet. They are afraid, lightly, respectfully, of water that has been stopped.

There is also, in some of them, a different quality of attention. The ones who work deepest — the turbine hall crews, the cistern divers who go down in canvas suits to clear grates — will tell you, if they like you and if you can read their lips over the howl, that water here doesn't always fall correctly. They've seen the mornings when the air after a condensation night is full of droplets that just hang, drifting like dust motes, refusing gravity until a footstep or a shout knocks them down. They've heard the breathing in the deep cisterns under the roar, that slow patient lapping as if something very large is turning over in its sleep under the stone. They don't call it a god. They don't call it a monster. They call it *the mass*, or *the old wet*, and they leave it an extra channel, wider than necessary, and they don't linger after their shift.

At late afternoon on the westernmost plaza, when the sun gets low and the sheeting water turns to glass and the cloud finally comes to sit heavily on the plateau without hurry, you can see them all at once for what they are.

A few women in oiled caps walking home along the high threshold of a housing row, boots in hand, feet hissing on the warm puddles. An old man deaf as stone, sitting with a cat asleep across his rubber boots, watching the veil of water run down the basalt wall of the fabrication shed opposite, content to watch it run because there is nothing else it should be doing. A crew on the eastern scarp, small as ants against the squared-off falls, opening a new gate and shouting soundlessly into the white fury, their hands flashing signals you can't read.

They are not owners. They are not conquerors. They are tenants who have learned how to be good tenants to a landlord that is everywhere, that is warm and heavy and loud and never leaves, that gives them all their power if they keep giving it its way down.

They live tilted, a little hoarse, a little deaf, always faintly damp, never in a hurry, in a city that is too empty and too loud and too wet, and they have the quiet, peculiar dignity of people who have stopped fighting the shape of the place that holds them.
