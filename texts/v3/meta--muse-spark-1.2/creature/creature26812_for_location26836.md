They don't live *on* it.

That is the first thing you have to understand. Nothing lives on the plateau the way a deer lives on a hill or a man lives on a plain. The plateau is too soft, too wet, too unfinished for that. You cannot be *on* a sponge that is three meters deep and breathing. You have to live *through* it, *with* it, *as* part of its plumbing.

So no one has settled here. No one has ever settled here. The air is too thin and too cold and too full of water to be breathed comfortably, the ground will not hold a foundation, the light never gets strong enough to throw a shadow you could navigate by. And yet it is crowded.

It is crowded with its workers.

### The Bellows-Keepers

Start small, because the small things are the reason the rest can exist.

Pull back a handful of that black-green sphagnum — if your hands don't go numb first — and the moss shivers. Not because you moved it. It was already shivering. The surface of every hummock, every dome the size of a house, is alive with mites and springtails and glass-pale lice the size of rice grains, but in numbers that make the numbers meaningless. Millions per square meter. They are white and amber and the exact red-black of the peat, and they seethe continuously.

They are not eating the moss. They are tending it.

Each mite carries a thread of fungal hyphae or a single sphagnum spore cemented to its back. They walk the moss in circuits, chewing a bore-hole here, plugging a leak there, sealing a bladder with wax from their own abdomens. When the great bellows at the base of a moss-dome inflates — that room-sized lung of sphagnum that huffs wet air with a sound like a forge — they swarm over its skin and tighten it. When it deflates, they ride the exhalation into the air for a second and fall again. If you listen close, below the industrial clank, you can hear them: a faint, dry rustling, like static, like a factory floor of tiny hands.

They are the maintenance crew for a machine that has no owner.

### The Boiler Beetles

They are what you notice next because they do not try to hide. You hear them before you see them — a hard *clank* that arrives a half-second before the beetle actually moves, because sound is loose here.

They are the size of a human torso, low and flat, and their elytra are not shell but plate. True plate, riveted, black iron mottled with orange rust that is not rust but colonies of iron-bacteria they farm. When they lift their wing covers, steam jets out from spiracles along their sides with a scalded hiss that smells like hot blood and pennies. They need that. The air at this elevation is too thin and cold to keep a large insect warm. So they stoke themselves.

Watch one work. It will climb the felt-bark trunk of one of those twenty-meter clubmosses — those lonely cathedral pillars — and chew. The bark peels back in lignin plates that slide like pistons, exposing the wet, hot wood beneath where a seam of peat is smoldering cold and blue, a coal-fire without flame. The beetle bites off a glowing nodule of that half-coal, carries it glowing in its mandibles — it does not burn them; their mouths are lined with silica — and hauls it across the open water to another trunk, another seam that has gone out. It stuffs the coal in. The trunk shudders, hisses, and begins to vent again. Piston-bark pumping. Steam catching the flat gray light.

They are stokers. They are welders. They walk from furnace to furnace across a bog that should not have furnaces, keeping the pressure up. Sometimes two meet on the same trunk and butt plates with a dull gong that rings for a long time in the wet air, and neither gives way for minutes. Sometimes one misjudges a weight-shift — because weight here flickers — and a step that should hold sends it floating for an absurd, slow second before gravity remembers it and it crashes down through moss into tea-dark water, where it paddles, indignantly steaming, until it finds purchase again.

They are completely deaf to you.

### The Snow That Never Lands

Above them — always above them — is the weather that is not weather.

White moths. Or things that have agreed to be called moths because we have no better word. Each one is as big as your outstretched hand, wings as thin as tracing paper, bodies furred against the wet cold. Alone, one is ghostly. Together they are a blizzard.

They exist in such numbers they change the light. 2.4/3 abundant is an understatement when you are inside it. When a moss-dome exhales, two or three thousand rise in a single vortex, spiraling up thirty meters and then raining down slowly, slowly — too slowly, because time pools near the cold-fire seams and a moth can hang motionless with its wings fully spread while your heart beats three times. They do not eat in any way you can see. They drink the air. Their proboscises uncoil and condense mist directly, and the electrified filaments of St. Elmo's fire that crawl endlessly over the horsetail tips make their wing-scales crackle with static. At dusk, which is all day here, the whole flock glitters with discharge.

They live for maybe a week. They die constantly, falling into the tarns where they are eaten before they even get wet. It does not matter. The peat itself knits new pupae the size of fists in its upper layers, pulsing cocoons that split with a wet pop — another turbulent birth, another hill heaving up while you weren't looking — and release another cloud. They are the soot of this factory. The product. Light and water milled into white, fluttering particulate.

The cranes farm them without meaning to.

### The Stilt-Cranes

You see them from half a kilometer away because there is nothing to hide behind. They are sparse, like the trees. One per watershed. Maybe two.

They are not birds, but your brain calls them cranes to keep from panicking. Three meters tall at the shoulder, legs like jointed reeds with an extra knee that bends the wrong way, bodies covered in slate-gray down that is always soaked and steaming faintly. Their necks are too long, their beaks are blunt and black and open vertically rather than horizontally, like a split. When they call, the sound is not a bird-call at all but a metallic bellow, a boiler whistle made of throat, that carries for kilometers across the flat circle of horizon and is answered — impossibly — by itself a second earlier.

They walk. So slowly. Each step is a deliberate placement on a tussock that may not be there tomorrow; the plateau de-creates itself too fast for anything that hurries. A crane will stand motionless in a tarn the color of tea for an hour, watching water bead on its own back, then spear. Its beak snaps shut on a giant black newt, on a mouthful of moths, on a Boiler Beetle that vented at the wrong moment. It swallows and steam curls from its nostrils.

They are the only things here that seem to *remember*. When a moss-dome collapses behind them into a black pool with a roar, they don't startle. They turn their heads — too far, 180 degrees, 270 degrees — and watch the new pool settle, as if comparing it to the version they saw through the flicker a moment before, when the clubmoss beside it was briefly skeletal and dead. They navigate by the weirdness. They step where weight will be light, they call where sound will arrive early, they sometimes stand in two places at once inside a stationary column of mist and both of them blink at you, unsynced.

If you stay still long enough, one will approach you. Not out of curiosity. Because you are a warm, vertical thing venting steam — your breath — in a place where warm vertical things that vent steam are either clubmosses or beetles, and it needs to check which furnace you are.

### The Newts and the Tarn-Mothers

Under the water — which is everywhere but never deep in the same place for long — the real mass lives.

The tarns are not lakes. They are eyes that open and close. Peel back the tea-colored surface and the peat roots go down forever, a black tangle, and among them move newts. Black, broad as seals, with external gills like huge, feathery ferns that pulse independently, filtering the thin water and the even thinner air. They are amphibians the way the plateau is a mountain — only technically. They never leave the water because the water never leaves. They haul themselves from tarn to tarn when a tarn drains sideways uphill or simply vanishes, leaving shining, agonized furrows in the moss that the mites rush to repair. Underwater they are graceful, boiling the surface as they turn. On the moss they are geological, sighing, dragging their own weight plus the inconsistent weight of this place.

The largest ones — old females, maybe — do not move at all anymore. They have become tarns themselves. A great, shallow bowl of living back, peat and sphagnum growing over their flanks, water pooling in the dip of their spine. Moths drink from them. Beetles walk across them. Their gills have become the reed-pipe networks that the bellows pump through, still fern-like, still filtering. You could step into one and not know you were standing in an animal until it breathed, and the whole pool rose six inches and exhaled warm, iron-smelling condensation into your face.

They are the ballast. When everything else is turbulent, growing three meters in a minute or collapsing into foam, the Tarn-Mothers hold. A little.

None of them are in charge. That is the point of an industrial plateau with no builder. The mites keep the bellows tight. The beetles keep the fires lit. The clubmosses and horsetails, those sparse, house-sized plants that are not plants but vertical mills, convert the endless dim light and the endless perched water into heat and pressure. The moths carry that energy into the air as chaotic snow. The cranes and newts harvest it, and die, and compress back into peat, which deep down thumps with that subsonic heartbeat and ignites again.

It is a factory that makes more factory out of itself, and every creature on it is both worker and workpiece, tending a machine that is trying, constantly and loudly and incorrectly, to finish a mountain that was finished ten million years ago. It keeps making itself wrong, and they keep living in the wrongness, perfectly suited to a place where a step floats, where you hear the future, where a hill is born behind you while you listen to the last one drown.

If you stand there long enough your boot wells full of cold water that will not drain, and a beetle will mistake your heat for a furnace and come to investigate, plates clanking, and a crane will watch you from two places at once, and you will understand that you are not an observer here.

You are a brief, warm obstruction in the plumbing. And the plateau, politely, is already working around you.
