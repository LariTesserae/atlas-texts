Whatever lives here doesn't live here the way the roach does.

At first you'll think you've already taken the census. The moss in the sump grout. The line of fern. The pale fungus in its perfect circle. The roach. The lizard. The bloodworms waving in the warm water. That's the list. That's what can stand the heat and the damp and the dark at such a perfect, narrow intersection. And for the first ten minutes, maybe twenty, you will believe the room is empty except for that.

Then you start to notice the room is tended.

Not maintained. Tended, the way an animal tends its own body. The handrail to get down here is polished to a dark sheen, but not where a man coming and going would hold it. Lower. Where something dragging itself up the last few steps would pull. The valve wheels that are worn blank have recent polish on their rims, a faint, damp shine in the amber dark, as if handled by something that leaves moisture behind. The square sump in the floor - the white tiles tea-stained brown - has its water at the same perfect two-inch depth no matter how long you watch, even though the perimeter gutter weeps into it and the hot air should be taking it away. Something drinks.

You have to learn to see on its time. This is not a place for eyes that dart. The pump turns at two revolutions per minute. The relay clicks every few minutes. The fern grows a frond in a week. Whatever lives here moves at that speed, and if you look for movement you will miss it. You have to look for stillness that is too still.

It lives in the slots. Not on the floor - the floor is too exposed, too hot, too often crossed by your boots. It lives in the negative space left over between things, in the hot, narrow gaps between the pipe canopy and the angle-iron frames, between the electrical trunking and the vault. It is a creature of interval and clearance. It is flattened by necessity, the way the lizard is flattened. You could turn sideways to pass between the two central stanchions. It never has to.

When you finally see it, you will think at first it is part of the lagging.

High up, where the thickest pipes braid across the barrel ceiling and the heat pools heaviest, there is a bundle that is too thick. Asbestos cloth and pitted iron and damp, frayed insulation bandaging. Except the bandaging glistens. The room is full of condensation that beads and never falls, that elongates into trembling tears and slides sideways - that wrongness - and the same thing is happening on it. Its skin - if it's skin - carries the same film. Beads swell on it, grow heavy, hold. When one finally does move, it doesn't drop. It crawls sideways across a shoulder, a back, a limb, to be reabsorbed at a seam.

It is pale in the way everything down here has lost its pigment. Not white. The deep, mottled grey-brown of the cured concrete, the tea-stain of the sump tiles, the dark crust of the stable rust. It has taken the final color of the room. When it doesn't move, its surface has the same pocked texture where aggregate shows through. You could have stared right at it for minutes and called it a pipe. It has no front or back that you can determine at first. Only when your eyes adjust to the pilot-flame orange and the gauge-needle green do you see the slow heave of it.

It breathes once every few minutes. A long, shallow inflation that lifts the whole mass a fraction and settles again, with a soft *tick* of contracting metal that isn't metal. It is warm. Not warmer than the air - that is the other wrongness. The heat in here has no gradient because the heat *is* it. It radiates at exactly room temperature, which is why you can't feel where it is until you are very close and the air takes on the faint, mineral smell of the sump water.

Closer, it resolves. Not one bundle, but a body fitted to the pipes it lies among. Long, low, without a proper neck or waist, it has learned the shape of being squeezed. Its limbs - four, maybe six, the number is hard because they fold so tightly - are jointed too many times, each joint thickened and scarred where it has rested for years against hot iron, the skin there calloused into something like lagging itself. The underside, where it presses against the pipe, is paler, soft, constantly damp. You can see the faint tracery of what might be veins, or might be mineral scale shining through translucent skin. Its head, if you can call it that, is mostly mouth and mostly recessed. No eyes that reflect the amber bulbs. Instead, two shallow pits on either side where condensation collects and never falls, that sense vibration and heat. The pilot flame on the far wall paints them orange when it heaves.

It does not hunt. Nothing here can afford to chase. It waits, and it tends.

It drinks the water that refuses to fall. You will see it do it if you sit on the warm concrete lip of the sump long enough and let your own breathing join the *thuk... thuk... thuk* of the slow pump. A bead on the pipe above it swells, trembles. A slow, white, almost translucent tongue or lip or fold of skin extends - so slowly you doubt it - and meets the tear of water. The bead doesn't break. It is drawn sideways, along skin, into a groove that runs the length of its body, a man-made gutter like the one on the perimeter floor, half-clogged with its own pale scale. It shivers when it drinks.

It feeds otherwise on what the room provides at the room's pace. The pale fungus in its perfect circle - it crops that circle with a careful, circular mouth, never taking it all. It will press itself flat over the moss for an hour and leave a paler patch. The roach that moves with absolute deliberation will walk directly over it without fear, and sometimes it won't walk off again. The lizard watches you, but it watches *that* more closely. The bloodworms in the sump are its most reliable crop; it lowers a limb as thin as a finger-pipe into the warm, oily water and they gather to it.

And it tends the boiler. That is the oldest part. The squat, riveted house-sized bulk at the north end that idles but never fires - the brick surround still radiating - has a dozen small responsibilities. A weeping joint that needs a catch-tray emptied one drop every twenty minutes. A gauge whose needle quivers a millimeter. A cherry-red heating element in an open panel that someone left open decades ago. It moves between these on a circuit that takes hours, dragging its damp weight along the catwalks you thought were only holding up more pipes, fitting itself through gaps you had to turn sideways to pass. Where it passes, brass brightens a little. Scale is licked clean. A valve eases a fraction. It doesn't understand the building that used to be above, with its T's and U-bends that make no thermodynamic sense. It just keeps the idling from ever stopping or ever starting.

It has noticed you. Not quickly. Light is an insult here; your flashlight would be a violence if you used it. Your heat is the wrong kind - you radiate too hot, you sweat and it evaporates, you breathe too fast. It felt your steps as a new tick in the metal long before you saw it. Now one of those damp pits is angled very slightly in your direction. It does not approach. It has no need to close distance. It can wait longer than you can stay. In here, a year to seize a valve is fast. A month to evaporate a puddle is nothing. If you stay until the fern puts out a new frond, it may decide you are part of the room's weather and come to taste the salt on the handrail where you held it.

This is what lives below. Not a visitor that got lost and adapted. A native of the made world. Something that was not put here but emerged because everything that *was* put here made a perfect, hot, damp, dark, quiet interval exactly its size. It was not born. It settled, like the sag in the barrel vault, into its final form. It looks like it has always looked like this and will for a long time.

If you leave - and you will, because you cannot breathe so slowly - it will still be there, pressed to its pipes in the peach dark, drinking water that never falls, keeping the heat at exactly itself, listening to the relay click and cool and click again.
