Who lives here has never been to the surface.

They were not born elsewhere and adapted to this. They were made by this, pressed by this, thinned and widened and slowed until they fit exactly into the space the water allows.

At first you think there is nothing living here at all. That is how well they fit. For a long time you will mistake them for the plain itself.

Then you learn to see the way this place sees — not by light, but by interruption.

### The General Shape of Life Here

Nothing here is tall. Nothing is round and hollow, nothing holds air inside itself, nothing tries to stand up against the weight. Everything that lives here has learned the first rule: pressure makes shape irrelevant.

So they are flat.

Not flattened like something stepped on — flat like something that never thought to be otherwise. Bodies like cold pancakes, like lids, like leaves pressed between pages. Tissue the consistency of jelly and wet leather, water for water, so the kilometers of ocean above pass straight through them. No swim bladders. No lungs. No cavities to collapse. If you pick one up — and you would have to move very slowly to catch it — it would feel less like an animal and more like a thickened piece of the water itself, cool and giving, with no center.

They have no hard edges. Evolution here has sanded everything down just as the water sanded the basalt. Spines are short and soft, if they exist at all. Shells are thin, flexible, or internal. Even the crabs are not armored the way a shallow crab is armored; their carapaces give a little under your thumb, like cold wax.

Most are pale to the point of not having a color. Translucent white, ash grey, silt brown, a pink so diluted it is almost the pink of water. There is no advantage to pigment where no light reaches. Some are so clear you can see the dark thread of their gut, and the few grains of silt still inside it.

And they are small — not because the place is small, but because food is small. The largest thing you will meet in an ordinary day would fit in your two hands. To be larger would be to require more than this thin rain of snow can give. Giants exist, but only as rumors and only briefly: a wandering angler-fish as long as your forearm, a sleeper shark moving like a blimp through smoke, there and then gone for a year.

### How They Sense

They do not look. Eyes, if they have them at all, are atrophied to smooth white beads, or gone entirely. What would they look at? The water is never clear for more than a few meters. Light is a language only a few speak, and only in short, blue-green sentences.

Instead they feel. And they taste.

Their whole body is a tongue and an ear. Long antennae - sometimes twice their body length, jointed, feathered with fine hairs - sweep constantly ahead of them, stirring the slurry and reading what falls into it. The shrimp drags his before him like a blind man’s cane. The isopod, low and many-legged, shivers its filaments and knows from the quiver whether the current ten meters away is turning. The holothurian - the sea cucumber the color of wet ash - has no front at all; its skin is covered in tiny tube feet that all taste the mud at once.

Sound comes through bone and jelly. The groan of a nodule rolling half a centimeter per decade, the sigh of a turbidity flow starting a valley away, the endless tick-tick-tick of marine snow landing — they feel these as pressure on their skin long before anything arrives.

And smell is everything. In water this crowded, this still, a dead whale a kilometer away is a lighthouse made of grease. A bacterial mat is a warm stove. A mate — rare, impossibly rare — is a faint chemical trail that may be days old and still fresh, because cold preserves and water does not let go of what it holds. They will follow that trail for weeks, moving a meter an hour, never hurrying, because hurry is heat and heat is death.

### How They Live

They do not live *in* the water so much as they live *with* the silt.

Because the water is not empty, they cannot swim the way a surface fish swims. To dart would be to throw up a cloud that blinds them and chokes them. So they do not dart. They creep, they glide, they hover just millimeters above the clay, fanning the snow toward their mouths with careful appendages.

There are, broadly, three ways to make a life here:

**1. The Clingers.**

They do not move at all, or so slowly you would call it growth. The anemones, fist-tight and fringed, anchored to a manganese nodule that has not moved in a hundred thousand years. The bacterial mats, dark and felt-like, spreading a fraction of a millimeter a year across black stone, eating the stone itself, eating dissolved methane. The pale filament forests that wave from cracks where a little chemistry seeps warm. They endure. When the turbidity flow comes — that slow avalanche of liquid earth moving at walking speed — they close, they flatten, they are buried. And they wait under the mud. Months later, perhaps years, a current will uncover a corner of them again and they will open.

**2. The Sifters.**

These are the wanderers. The blind shrimp, white as milk, picking grain by grain with hands too delicate to see. The giant isopods, flat as dinner plates, plowing the silt with their broad heads, leaving behind a neat, pale furrow that fills in within an hour. The holothurians vacuuming the plain, ingesting mud and digesting out the one percent that is organic — the snow, the bacteria, the flecks of bone — and excreting perfect coils of cleaned clay behind them. They are never in groups. If you see two in the same ten meters, one will turn away. There is not enough food to share a spot. Their territories are measured not in boundaries but in time: *I was here an hour ago, so you should not be here now.*

They leave almost no trace, except that behind each of them the water is briefly clear, a tunnel of filtered water that hangs for seconds before the blizzard fills it again.

**3. The Light Bearers.**

Rare. Solitary. The ones who have kept a little fire. An angler with a lure the size of a grain of rice, dangling blue-green in the black, not to illuminate but to lie. A comb jelly that pulses once, a traveling constellation, then goes dark for a night. A dragonfish with photophores along its flat jaw like a line of dying stars. They do not use light to see. They use it to speak the only lies that work in total darkness: *I am not here. I am something you want. I am something you fear.* The light never shows the plain. It only shows itself, and then the dark is larger afterward.

All three live on the same economy: the endless, silent rain.

Marine snow falls always. Dead things from the white ceiling kilometers above — fragments of algae, fecal pellets, scales, whole corpses that become, by the time they reach here, a soft descending confetti — fall through the crowded water and settle on everything. The silt itself is food, because it is half organic mud by now. Life here does not hunt so much as it waits for the sky to feed it, and then it strains, and sifts, and licks the stones clean.

Because cold preserves, a single meal can last a very long time. A whale falls somewhere in the bowl — a rare, cataclysmic event — and for a hundred years, that one hill of bone and blubber becomes a city. The mats thicken into shag. The shrimp come from kilometers away, following the grease on the current. The bone-worms bloom. And then it is gone, grain by grain, and the plain is empty again for another century.

### Their Mind, If You Can Call It That

There is intelligence here, but it is not the kind that builds.

You see it when the pale octopus finds two halves of a dead clam, both already half-buried, and pulls them together around itself, not because it understands shelter as an idea, but because the feeling of being uncovered is wrong and the feeling of being covered is right. It will sit there for days, until hunger moves it, then abandon the shells without a glance.

Or the crab that carries a sponge on its back. Not decoration. Camouflage tasted with chemical sense: *if I smell like the plain, the plain will not eat me.*

No teaching. No accumulation. Each animal figures this out alone, slowly, over a life that may last decades because everything is slow. They have time. Their metabolism is a low flame. Their heart — if they have a heart — beats once, waits, beats again. They can go months without eating, motionless, buried to their eyes in clay, listening to the water shudder.

They do not know loneliness the way you do. Loneliness requires the expectation of company. Here, to meet another is an event, a weather. Most will mate once, or twice, in a whole life. They will find each other by a trail days old, touch antennae, exchange a packet of eggs or sperm in the dark, and separate without lingering. The young, if they live, drift for a little while in the smoke before settling somewhere a few hundred meters away, where they will live and die without ever meeting their parents.

If you lived here, you would be flat, and pale, and patient beyond any human patience. You would have no name for yourself. You would move only when you must, eat only the dust that falls on you, and listen with your whole skin to the slow churning of water heavier than stone. You would make no light unless you needed to lie. You would carry your home only as long as you needed it.

And you would be perfectly, completely at home in the dark, in the cold, in the packed and ancient soup of a world that never wanted to be anything else — and so, finally, became exactly itself.
