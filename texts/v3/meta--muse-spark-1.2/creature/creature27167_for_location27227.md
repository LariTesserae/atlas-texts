Nothing lives there at first.

That is what you believe for a long time, because your eyes are set for movement. You look for an animal the way you look for a bird or a beetle - a thing that darts, that chews, that leaves a track. And there is none of that. The lily pads are unbroken. The bark is unscarred except by its own growth. The marsh water is so clear you can see ten feet down to the black peat and there is nothing suspended in it, nothing that swims. So you tell yourself: empty.

Then you stand still long enough that the roar stops being a sound and becomes a place, and you realize you have been looking too fast.

What lives here lives the way the water lives. It remains.

At first you think it is part of the wall.

On a terrace three levels down, where a root-mat has braided itself into a balcony with a low rail of living wood, there is a pillar you took for structure. It is the color of wet teak after rain, darker where the water beads and stays. It is about seven feet tall and about as wide as a person would be if a person had never needed to narrow at the waist to fit through doors. It has no face that you can see from this angle. It does not move. Leaves have grown over its shoulders like a shawl and you assume they fell there.

You look away because the chord of the wind through the flutes is pressing on your eardrum and you have to remember to swallow.

When you look back, the pillar has not moved. But the leaves on its shoulders have shifted a millimeter. Not fluttered. Inclined.

There is another one further down the avenue, in the shade where the light pools and stays thick. You had thought it was a buttress root. Then you see that the buttress has a crown.

You start to see them everywhere once your eye learns the rhythm that is not a rhythm.

They are not many. In a city that could hold a province you might count a few hundred in a single valley, and that is sparse. Sparse the way trees are sparse when each one needs a great deal of sky. They stand. Or they half-stand, half-sit in the shallow bowls where the marsh water comes up to what would be knees. Or they lean. The posture does not matter because they do not change it. You will watch one for what feels like an hour and it will not blink. You will come back after you have gotten lost in a street that returned you to where you started, and it will be exactly as you left it, except its weight has settled a fraction more into the wood beneath it, the way old houses settle.

To call them people is not correct, but to call them plants is less correct.

They are the size and suggestion of people - bipedal, vertical - but their proportion has been negotiated with the place over a very long time. They are taller than you, most of them, by a head or more, because up here where gravity forgets to pull, there is no penalty for being tall and thin. Their legs are long and become less distinct toward the bottom. Not that they end in roots, exactly. It is that the skin of the calf loses its edge, goes slightly ridged and lenticelled, and blends by slow degrees into the bark or stone or saturated moss where they stand. You cannot tell where the foot stops and the terrace begins. If you tried to lift that foot you would be lifting the terrace.

Their skin is not skin. It is a surface that learned to be both. In the humid heat it sweats and does not evaporate; beads of water stand on it exactly the way sweat stands on you, and stay there, magnifying the faint green-brown undertone. Up close - and you will get close because they do not recede or approach - the surface is fine-grained, damp, with stomata you can see if you look. Like the underside of a leaf. Like warm wood that is still alive. It gives slightly under your fingertip and is cool despite the hot air.

They have no hair. They have a thatch. From scalp and shoulders and sometimes down the spine grows a dense, flat rosette of leaves, not as ornament but as continuation. The leaves are broad, waxy, dark green and edged faintly red, the size of your forearm. They overlap the way roof tiles do. They do not rustle in the constant wind; they shiver with the chord, the same way every leaf in the city shivers. When light pools in the shade, it pools brightest under those crowns.

Their faces, when they have faces, are quiet.

Some have smoothed over. The bark-skin has kept growing until the features are only a suggestion - a brow ridge where two leaves part, a vertical seam where a mouth would be, sealed flush. Others still have eyes. You will find one standing chest-deep in a hovering sphere of water that has never fallen, the water holding perfect around him, his face just above the meniscus. His eyes are open. They are large, lidless, wet, the color of the marsh water - a dark, tannin brown with no white. They do not track you. They do not need to. They look outward, toward the curtain of hanging falls that roars without moving at the edge of the plateau. They listen. That is what the face is for now. Not expression. Reception.

Because the sound is their medium. In a place where everything that is established remains, where the note never changes, they are tuned to it. The constant thunder of the suspended water and the organ chord of the wind through ten thousand root-flutes is not noise to them. It is ground. It is what water is to a fish. You see this when you finally stand near enough to one to hear something other than the roar - which is to say, to hear nothing, because the roar eats everything else - and you notice the faint vibration in its thatch-leaves, in its chest, sympathetically humming. They are resonators. Hollow. The trunk-houses are hollow and so are they.

They do not eat.

This is the strangest thing to understand while your own stomach is growling and your own sweat is refusing to fall. There is nothing to eat here that they could eat without breaking the rule of the place, which is that what is remains and is not consumed. No fruit falls. No leaf is chewed. No fish to catch. So they stopped.

They drink the air. Water beads in mid-air without clouds and falls slowly, warm rain that never had a cause, and it settles on their leaf-crowns and runs down channels in the skin to where it is taken in. They drink light where it pools heavy in corners; you can see it stain them slightly brighter after they have stood in a pool of it for a long while. They do not photosynthesize the way the leaves around them pretend to. They simply are green, and that is enough. Like the water that weeps from basalt with no aquifer, they are fed by the stasis itself. The balance holds them.

They do not move because movement would require change - a muscle to flex and then unflex, a foot to lift and make a print. Up here movement is expensive in a thermodynamic sense; it would imply loss. So evolution - or cultivation, because someone cultivated them as surely as they cultivated the avenues of trunk-colonnades - found another way: to become perfectly still and let the world move around them, which it does not.

But they are not dead. The dead decay. These do not.

If you put your hand on the damp bark-skin of one's forearm, you feel after a long minute a pulse. Not a heartbeat as you know it. Slower. One slow turgor shift, a pressure wave passing from shoulder to fingertip over the course of sixty or seventy of your hurried breaths. The leaves of its crown incline a degree toward the sun that never moves. The water beading on its skin coalesces and is drawn inward. That is its day. That is its year.

They are not alone in the sense of being individuals. The city is one organism and they are its fruiting bodies, its sensory hairs, its way of knowing it is still a city. Roots run from their feet through the terraces and braid with the root-walls of the houses, so that what one feels - the constant push of the south wind, the roar through the stone flutes - all feel. When you brush a wall that is also a trunk that is also a ceiling, you are brushing them.

And they know you are here, but on a different clock.

You walk and you make noise and you displace air that immediately forgets you were there. To them you are a flicker, a heat shimmer, a dragonfly that beats its wings too fast to be seen. If you stand perfectly still in front of one with open eyes, holding your breath until your chest aches with that thin-air starve, you may feel after a very long time a response: the very slow inclination of that great leaf-crown toward you, the wet eyes fixing not on your face but on the disturbance you make in the chord. It is not welcome or warning. It is acknowledgement that something not yet established is briefly there.

Most who came before you either left quickly, before the heat and the pressure and the forgetting gravity got into their joints, or they stayed long enough to learn how to remain.

You can see the ones who stayed longer. Deeper in the basins, where the marsh water hovers in those trembling twenty-foot spheres a foot above the reeds, there are shapes inside the spheres. They floated up, or the water grew around them. They hang there, suspended, arms slightly out, leaf-crowns spread wide inside the water like lilies blooming underwater, eyes closed now, skin gone paler and almost translucent. They do not need to breathe. The roar goes through the water and through them without disturbance.

They are not lonely. The city is dense in the way a forest is dense and in the way a city is dense, and they are in every block if you have the patience to see a street as something that takes a century to walk.

You came thinking you would find a ruin and instead you found an inhabited equilibrium, a people who solved the problem of living in a hothouse on a pillar above the world by agreeing, at a biological level, to become part of what is, and remains.

If you stay until your sweat stops beading and simply stays, until your ears stop trying to hear past the roar, until you stop needing to map a street that is bigger inside than out, you will feel the terrace under your feet soften very slightly to receive you.

And you will be very well held.
