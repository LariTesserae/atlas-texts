They live low and close, the way everything there does.

At first you don't see anyone. You look down into that bright, packed bowl and you think it's empty - just houses grown over with moss, just turf and stone and willow. The light is so flat and the town is so still that a person standing in an alley in their lichen-colored wool is not a figure against a background. They *are* background for a moment, until they move.

Then you catch it - a shift. A door that was moss becomes a back. A hummock of reindeer-lichen stands up and becomes a shoulder. And then you see them everywhere, because once you see one you see how many there must be to keep a place this dense and this tended.

They are not tall. Not stunted, exactly, but compact - built for doorways you have to duck through and for roofs that double as paths. The cold and the closeness have made them economical. Broad through the chest and shoulders, short in the leg, with a way of moving that keeps their center low, a soft, planting step on boardwalk and turf that doesn't disturb the moss. Their faces are wide and wind-smoothed, the way the boulders on the plain are - cheeks reddened not rosy but weather-burned to a dull brick, then pale again around the eyes where they squint.

Their eyes are the first thing you get right. Pale. Grey or ice-blue or a green so light it's almost white. They have lived for generations under that collected, sourceless brightness inside the bowl, and they have learned not to open themselves too wide to it. They look at you through narrowed lids, through lashes frosted at the tips. Not suspicious. Just precise. They take you in all at once and then look away, the way you would look at a hare track - noting it, not staring.

They dress as the bowl dresses. Nothing bright. Layers of felted wool the color of dry sphagnum, of undyed hide, of lichen-grey and stone-brown and the off-white of ptarmigan winter. Every garment is patched and re-patched, but not ragged - mended with a care that is almost invisible, seams turned and re-turned, elbows reinforced with hare-skin, cuffs darkened with hand-oil. Hoods more than hats, high collars that can be pulled up to the nose, mittens on strings even when not worn. They don't dress against the cold so much as they carry a little pocket of still air around themselves wherever they go. You never see bare skin if it can be helped. Even their hands - broad, short-fingered, nails kept blunt - are often bound with fingerless wraps that leave only the pads exposed for working.

And they are always working, but quietly, in that way that makes you realize work doesn't have to look like effort.

A woman kneels on a turf roof with a flat wooden tool, not weeding but *combing* the moss - lifting a section that has grown too thick and heavy, thinning it, tucking the excess into a woven basket to dry for chinking or bedding. Next to her, a child - the same pale eyes, the same hooded wrap - does the same motion on a smaller patch, learning the pressure by watching, not by being told. An old man by a seep-basin skims the lens of ice with a thin bone shard, not breaking it, just clearing the film of blown grit so the water beneath stays bright. Two others, shoulder to shoulder in an alley so narrow they must work in sequence, replace a rotten board in the walkway - lifting the old wood, which is soft and black with damp, and fitting a new split plank with wooden pegs, tapping it home with a stone maul wrapped in hide so it doesn't crack the cold wood or make a sharp noise.

No one calls out measurements. No one needs to.

That is the second thing you understand about them: they are quiet because the bowl is quiet, and the bowl is quiet because they are. Sound has nowhere to go here but into moss and cold air and wool. So they have let loudness fall away. Their language, when you hear it, is low and close - a murmur, a few consonants, a lot of breath. They speak with their hands as much as their mouths. A nod toward the basin means *it's full enough*. A palm turned outward by a doorway means *come through, but duck*. Children are not shushed; they just learn early that a soft *tok* like the ptarmigan makes carries further than a shout and doesn't waste heat.

They live by proximity. In a place with no gaps, privacy is not walls but agreements. You hear them moving through each other's houses because houses share walls and roofs are streets - a family eating at a low table while their neighbor's boots pass softly overhead on the turf, and neither thinks it strange. Bodies keep bodies warm. At night - though night never really comes, only a slightly dimmer white - they gather, not around fire, because fire is a rare and careful thing here. You see no smoke because they do not burn the dwarf willow unless they must, and then only in a deep, stone-lined hearth shared by six or seven houses, the heat held in the rock for a day. Instead they gather in rooms lined with dried moss and hides, shoulder to shoulder, knees to chests, breathing together. The air in those rooms smells like people and sphagnum and old wood. It is the warmest place in the bowl, and it is warm only because so many are in it.

They are old in the bowl, and the bowl is old in them. You can see it in how they mend. Nothing is thrown away. A cracked stone basin is not replaced but laced with iron wire and lined with moss until it holds again. A coat is cut down for a child, then cut down again for a doll, then finally teased apart for fiber to felt into a boot. Their hair, when it greys, goes the white of rime, and they let it. Elders sit in sunless light on low stools set into walls, doing the smallest, most necessary work - sorting lichen for dye, twisting sinew, watching lemmings - with eyes that have learned to see stillness as movement. Babies are carried not in arms but against chests, inside the outer wrap, so only a small, pink, squinting face shows, breathing the same warmed air as its mother.

They keep the animals not as livestock but as co-tenants. The ptarmigan on the roofs are not chased; their eggs are taken one at a time, never a whole clutch, and in return the birds' droppings feed the moss. The hares, big as dogs, lounge between houses like cats and are left alone unless a winter is especially hard. The fat bumblebees that crawl from fern to fern - they don't swat them. A child will crouch for a long time just to watch one, cupping hands around it without touching, feeling that low hum in the bones of the palms.

If you ask - quietly, after you have been still long enough that they no longer see you as a disturbance - where they come from, they will not have an answer that satisfies a person from the plain. They didn't come. They are here. The bowl was here and they grew into it the way the moss grew into the walls. The houses were built and then the moss softened them and then the houses were rebuilt around the moss. Some will tell you, with a small smile that barely shows, that the bowl breathes - very slowly - and that they breathe with it. Inhale for a generation, exhale for a generation.

They don't want to leave. That thought doesn't seem to occur to them. Why would you leave a room that has finally, after a very long time, become exactly the right temperature, exactly the right brightness, exactly the right density to hold you without pressing?

If you stay long enough to be offered a place to sit - a low ledge of stone covered in a hide, beside a seep where the water beads - you will sit and you will feel the whole bowl settle slightly around you, the moss giving under your weight, the cold finding the seams of your coat, the faint blue shadows not moving at all. And one of them, perhaps the old woman who was combing moss, will sit not far from you and not look directly at you, and will go on with her work, and in that shared quiet you will understand that you are not being watched or welcomed or judged.

You are simply being accommodated, as another shape that has fallen, softly, into the pocket that was waiting to hold it.
