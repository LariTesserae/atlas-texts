You don't see them at first.

That's the trick of a place that is both total and empty. You stand in the half-kilometer of cold air between two pillars and you think: nothing lives here. It is too high, too thin, too dark. The wind comes down the empty avenue and takes your breath and you have to turn your face from it. The drip falls and falls through nothing. The felt sighs under your boots and rebounds too slowly, as if the mountain were breathing shallowly under it.

Then you stay still long enough for the cold to get into your lamp and make it gutter, and they unhide.

Not all at once. The forest doesn't give up a single image. It gives up a texture, and the texture thickens until it is a life.

### The Felt Itself

Start low, because low is where everything starts.

What you called moss isn't moss. Not only moss. It's a mat, a country, a lung. The tight black-green and rust-red felt is alive in a way soil never is. If you lie on it and put your cheek to it - and the people who live here do, often - you feel it faintly warm. Not from the sun. From itself.

It is composting and growing at the same time, always. Under the springy surface, white threads run like nerves. Tubers swell down there the size of your forearm, pale and sweet and full of stored water that steams when you cut them open and then flash-freezes into glass. Mites the size of rice grains turn dead leaf into felt. Worms thick as your wrist - blind, pink-white, slow - churn through the compressed layers, leaving behind tunnels just wide enough for condensation to run like veins. You hear them sometimes at night, a soft peristaltic rustle just under the surface, as if the ground were shifting in its sleep.

And it never stops making water. Cut it with a basalt flake and it doesn't bleed sap. It weeps. Clear, warm water wells up and spills and smokes for three heartbeats before the air takes it. That's what everything drinks.

### The Air Between

Nothing that lives here is in a hurry to fall.

Gravity feels lighter at 4,000 meters, but it isn't - the air is just thinner, and everything has learned to wear it like a coat.

First, the moths. You thought there was one, then you realize there are thousands. Pale, furred things the size of your two hands spread, with wings that don't beat so much as row. They hang in the empty air between pillars, almost stationary, drinking the canopy's endless drizzle mid-flight. Each heavy drop falls straight for three hundred meters and a moth will intercept it, curl its tongue around it, ride it down a meter before letting go. At dusk they cloud so thick your lamp catches only moth, moth, moth, a snow of white fur that doesn't melt, utterly silent except for the faint dust of their wings. Their caterpillars live in the canopy, chewing waxy sail-leaves from the inside, glowing faintly green with the foxfire bacteria they farm.

Where the moths go, the gliders go.

You hear them before you see them. *Kee-kee-kee.* A metallic scream that starts on one pillar and ends half a kilometer away on another, with no sound in the middle because the animal is in the middle, gliding. They live *in* the pillars - not on them. The pillar-trunks are not wood, they are braided fiber, and they are hollowed by use into apartments. Bands of them - you would call them lemurs if you needed a lowland word, but they are more like squirrels crossed with flying foxes, with flat black eyes twice too large for their heads and a membrane from wrist to ankle that catches the thin air like a kite. They launch themselves into the void and go. And go. The emptiness that would kill you is their road. They scream the whole way to keep their family spaced in the dark, until the avenue between pillars rings with overlapping calls and you can't tell near from far.

They have long, comb-like fingers for scooping moths out of the air, and they fold their wings around themselves to sleep in the lee of a pillar while the wind howls ten meters away.

And something listens for when they stop screaming.

You've only seen its prints, pressed deep into the felt where it landed heavy after a glide-intercept: four toes, a pad wide as a dinner plate, claws that left little drag marks. At night there's a cough - not a roar, a low *whuff* that seems to come from everywhere because the cold air bends sound. When it makes it, every glider goes silent at once. That's how you know it is real. The felt goes quiet too, the moths vanish, and even the drip seems to pause. The people call it the hush-cat, or the long-owl, depending on the family. It's cat-pale, cat-heavy, with a face flattened like an owl and ears that swivel independently under ruffs of white fur. It doesn't run. It waits on a pillar ledge, watches a glide-path for hours, and then throws itself into the air and *steers*. It can turn in mid-air, just slightly, by twisting that big ruff and tail. Enough. It hits a glider mid-void and they both fall, tumbling slowly, impossibly slowly in that thin, cold air, until they hit the felt with a soft thump you feel through your boots a kilometer away.

There are other things that own the void: the sail-leaves themselves, when they fall, will hang on an updraft for an hour, turning, and on their backs ride colonies of tiny black beetles that never touch ground in their whole lives.

### The Water Inside The World

The tarns are not ponds. They are eyes.

The felt sags between pillars and the drip collects. A basin forms, lined with compacted fiber so watertight that the water sits high, black and still, reflecting the false sky of the canopy so perfectly you get vertigo looking into it. Some are a boot-print wide, just a trembling meniscus under a skin of ice that sings when it contracts at night - a high, clear *ping* that carries for kilometers. Some are lakes.

If you kneel and hold your lamp over one, you see down. The water is so cold it burns, so clear it looks like air. At the bottom, pale shrimp the length of your finger paddle upside down, grazing on fallen foxfire. Fish - or not-fish - hang motionless: long, translucent things with huge, lidless eyes and fins that look like moth wings, beating so slowly you can count. They never see sun. They see the faint glow of the bacteria on the ice above and the occasional drowned moth, which they take whole.

Where the felt has been cut by a young scarp, you see the real rivers: not on the surface, but *in* it. Tunnels the diameter of your body, lined with white root-hair that pulses every twenty seconds, carrying water sideways through the plant-mass itself. Put your ear to a pillar and you hear it - *thud... thud... thud...* - the hydraulic heartbeat that lifts water half a kilometer straight up to leaves that never knew rain.

The deer drink from this system. They look like deer until you get close. Six legs, not four. A barrel chest that has to be that big to get enough thin air, a pelt the exact color of wet bark and old felt, hooves splayed into snowshoes that spread their weight so they don't punch through. They move in small, silent lines, cracking the tarn ice with a foreknife on their nose, drinking without ever lifting their heads to look around - because they listen. Their ears are enormous, cupped, and they twitch independently toward each distant *kee* and *whuff*. Their fawns are born the color of frost-moss and lie flat while the wind salts them with condensing snow.

### The Knots

And then there are the people, who are not many, who have learned density is a kindness.

You won't see a village. You will see that the wind has changed slightly, and realize a shape 100 meters away is not a distortion in the felt but a hut.

They are built the way the forest is built: they don't clear, they knot. A family - three, five, sometimes eight - will choose a dry rise between two tarns where the felt is thickest, cut felt-bricks with long basalt knives and stack them into a low dome, then coax a living canopy root down - one of those cable-thick aerial roots that dangles from the ceiling half a kilometer up - and stake it over the dome. In a season it greens. In a year the roof is alive, waxy leaves hissing in the wind, dripping its condensation into gutters braided from root fiber that run straight into a cistern of moss. In winter that living roof gives off a faint heat you can feel with your palm.

They are high-altitude people through and through. You see it when one stands: chest deep as a barrel, neck short, cheeks red even in the dark from a lifetime of thin air. Eyes large, black-brown, with a reflective sheen at the back like a deer or a lemur - they navigate as much by sound and texture as by sight. Their hair is cut short or shaved, because moths will lay in it. They wear the moths themselves: parkas and trousers of white moth-fur, smoked and softened, impossibly warm, that shed water and never wet through. Knives of chipped black glass and boiled deer-bone at their belts. Lamps of rendered moth-fat that burn low and yellow and throw light only as far as your hands, which is sufficient. Far is not for seeing here.

They do not shout. Sound carries too well and too strangely between the pillars; a shout will fold over itself and come back from the wrong direction and confuse the gliders and the hush-cats alike. They speak low, or they sing. A mother will sing a single, flat lullaby for an hour while weaving fiber, not because the child needs it but because the song tells the gliders: *this food is guarded*. Travelers sing as they walk between tarns, a low repetitive thrum that lets their family know where they are without having to see them. The cairns they pile - the only stone you will see brought up through the felt - are not markers so much as drums; tapped with a knuckle they ring differently when the felt is waterlogged and unsafe.

They cut the felt constantly, but reverently. They know the pulse. No one cuts a pillar. You press your ear to one at night and you feel *thud... thud... thud...* through your skull, hydraulic, inexorable, and you understand the old woman who told you the pillars are the forest's legs and the canopy is its head and the tarns are its mouths. When someone dies, they are not buried in stone that is days of cutting down. They are laid in a shallow hollow in the felt during a windless day. In a week the felt has stitched over. In a month you can't tell. The forest eats and keeps.

Children learn to walk on the rebound. They learn to read ice-song - that *ping* and *crack* - to know if a tarn will hold. They learn to catch condensation in their mouths by standing still under the canopy avenues with their faces up in the dark, waiting for the heavy drop to fall exactly onto their tongue. They learn to be quiet when the hush-cat coughs.

They are not lost here. They are not surviving against the forest. They are one more way the mountain decided to be a forest.

If you stay with them for three days and walk their line between two smoke plumes, you begin to do it too: you walk without looking far, you listen to the drip and know by its tempo how high the canopy is above you, you feel the wind come down the pillar avenue before it hits, you know which felt will sing under your boots and which will swallow your foot with a sigh. You stop expecting ground, and accept that you live *in* the plant, as the fish live in the tarn and the moths live in the falling water.

Above you, the interlocked leaves turn their sails slightly down, breathing. Below you, the young rock waits, sharp and patient, holding it all up. Between, everything that lives has learned the same rule:

be vast and be small at the same time.
