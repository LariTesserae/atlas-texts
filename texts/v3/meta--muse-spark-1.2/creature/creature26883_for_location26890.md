It does not *live* here the way something lives in a forest.

Nothing here was born to this place. Everything here *arrived* at it, and then stopped arriving. And then stopped leaving.

At first you think nothing lives here at all. The shivering on the walls, the pattern on the lamps - that could be mineral, that could be trick of the amber light. This place is so dry, so loud, so evenly warm that your brain wants to file it under *sterile*. An industrial clean-room. A salt tomb.

Then you blink, and the pattern resolves a little.

It is crowded.

Not teeming. Teeming implies motion, overflow, water. This is crowded the way a subway car at rush hour is crowded when no one is allowed to get off. Shoulder to shoulder. Edge to edge. Saturated.

### The Grazers

You see them first because they are the only thing that moves at a speed you recognize.

They are pale. Everything is pale. They have to be — there is no selection pressure for color when there is no sun, no camouflage, no display. Pigment costs water.

The isopods are the most numerous. Not the pill-bugs of the surface, soft and damp. These are dry isopods, desert isopods, cave isopods taken to an extreme. Each as long as your thumb, segmented plates like overlapping fingernails, a translucent off-white through which you can see a faint dark line of gut. They do not scuttle. They *pick*. Slow, deliberate, their seven pairs of legs moving with the careful economy of something that cannot afford to waste heat. Their antennae are absurdly long, twice their body length, feathered, constantly combing the air.

They are not looking for food. They are tasting the air for it. In this stillness, scent doesn't plume. It hangs in invisible strata. A brush of bacteria two meters away leaves a faint, salty-sweet exhalation that will sit there for weeks until an isopod walks through it.

Behind them come the shovel-crickets. You thought they were crickets until you see their hind legs. Not springs. Spades. Wide, flat, edged with chitinous teeth. They don't jump — there's nowhere to jump to and nothing to jump away from. They dig. Or rather, they *pry*. They wedge those legs into the hairline seams between salt plates and lever, and pop up perfect six-sided crystals to get at the film underneath. The biofilm is invisible to you, but to them it is pasture: a rust-red-black smear of halophilic bacteria and salt-fungus that lives on the faint condensation that never actually condenses, on the waste heat that leaks from the conduit.

They eat without hurry. They are eaten without hurry. You will see an isopod step calmly over a shovel-cricket's back, antennae brushing. Neither flinches. There is no chase here. Predation, when it happens, is a negotiation. A larger, flatter thing — like a woodlouse the size of a dinner plate, so pale it is almost clear — will simply settle itself over a cluster of fungus-brushes and lower its mouth. The brushes don't withdraw. The isopods don't flee. The energy budget is too tight for panic.

The sparse vegetation is not vegetation. It is those brushes. Every few meters, in the warmest seams — where a lamp housing bleeds a degree or two above ambient, where the transformer thrums — a tuft pushes up. A centimeter, two centimeters tall, stiff as wire, the color of dried blood and rust. Salt-tolerant fungus and chemolithotrophic bacteria braided together into a single body. They do not photosynthesize. They eat heat and salt and the faint iron exhaled by the machines. They do not spread. They cannot. A spore released here does not drift. It hangs in the gel-air until something brushes it. So each brush holds its territory for what might be decades, centuries, growing a fraction of a millimeter a year, never wilting, never seeding beyond arms reach.

### The Shingled Ones

You look up because the light is amber and the hum is loudest up there.

The lamps are wearing coats.

Blind moths. Hundreds per lamp. Thousands in the chamber. Each the size of your palm, wings so thin you can see the filament of the bulb through them, the color of salt paper and old parchment. They do not fly. Flight is for places with wind and distance. Here, flying would mean crossing three meters of dead, still air and then having to find the way back to the only heat source. So they cling.

They overlap like shingles, like scales, heads all pointed toward the warmth, wings overlapped so precisely that from a distance it looks like the lamp was wrapped in textured insulation. Only at the edge of the cluster can you see individual heads, featureless, eyeless — or rather, eyes still there but filmed over with the same salt dust that films the control booth windows, useless. They breathe through spiracles, they drink heat through their bellies. And they vibrate.

Not flutter. Vibrate. A high, almost inaudible tremor that harmonizes with the sixty-cycle hum. If you put your hand close you feel it more than hear it — the air itself buzzing. They are not trying to make sound. They are letting the sound make *them*. The lamp's transformer sings, the moth's wing muscles sing back, until you can't tell where the machine ends and the animal begins. When one dies — which must be rare — it does not fall. Its neighbors simply shift, close the gap. It remains there, incorruptible in the dry air, becoming part of the shingle.

Beneath the lamps, on the warm steel housings, the other heat-drinkers cluster. Things like crickets, like roaches, but bleached and soft, with abdomens pressed flat to the metal. They are not there for food. They are there to bask.

### The Rooted Ones

It takes you longest to understand these, because you keep wanting to see them as *on* the machines, and they are *of* them.

Along the black conduit, where the wiring runs vein-like across the salt, the steel has swollen.

Translucent sacs, melon-sized, clustered like grapes or like lymph nodes. Their skin is not skin — it is a chitinous membrane so thin you can see through it to the pulse inside. Each sac beats, slowly. Not a heartbeat. Slower. One pulse every four or five seconds, perfectly in time with the tick of the relays in the control booth. Inside each, suspended in clear fluid, is a filament. Copper-bright. It glints when the LED bar catches it. It is not *like* a wire. It *is* a wire, or a nerve, or both. You can trace where the membrane has grown around the conduit, where the filament has spliced itself directly into the insulation, tapping the current.

These are the electrovores.

They have no mouths. They have no need for the bacterial film. They feed on the circulation itself. The transformer hums, the sac fills a little tighter, its filament glows a little warmer. They are rooted. They cannot move. They do not need to. Energy is not spent here, it is circulated — the third rule of the chamber — and they have inserted themselves into the circle.

Nothing preys on them. Nothing needs to. Occasionally an isopod will walk across a sac's skin, antennae tapping, and the sac will not react. Reacting costs ion exchange. Why bother? The isopod cannot eat through the membrane. The sac cannot eat the isopod. They have reached equilibrium.

If you stand very still and watch one for a long time — and time behaves strangely here, as you noticed, dust hanging unmoving — you will see it grow. Not visibly. But the filament inside gets a hair longer each year, spiraling. The sac on the end of the gantry is larger than its sisters, because the gantry was left energized but unfinished, a dead-end of current with nowhere to go except into whatever would take it. Something took it.

They are the only things here that could be called *new*. The moths and isopods you could imagine evolving in a salt cave over a million years and being sealed in by accident. The sacs could not have existed before the wiring.

They are post-industrial animals. Children of the hum.

### The Invisible Majority

And then there is what you don't see until you realize the dust hasn't settled.

The air is not empty. In any other cave the dust would fall in minutes. Here it hangs where you kicked it, each mote fixed as if pinned. If you look closely, some of those motes... twitch. Against the light.

Salt-mites. Air-mites. Smaller than a grain of halite, almost transparent, each with feathery legs splayed wide to catch the stillness. They live *in* the suspended dust cloud you made. They live in the permanent haze that has hung in the chamber since the last time someone opened the shaft — years ago, decades. They graze on the skin flakes and fungal spores that also never fall. They mate in mid-air without falling. They die in mid-air without falling, becoming new motes.

They are why the air feels like gel. It's not just humidity and pressure. It's occupation. The atmosphere itself is saturated.

---

Nothing here is young. Nothing is old. Nothing is in a hurry to become something else.

If you watch long enough you will see that no one is reproducing quickly, no one is dying quickly. A moth shingle shifts. An isopod molts and leaves a perfect, dry husk that will sit beside it for years, un-decayed. A sac pulses. The sound never changes. The light never flickers. The salt spears grow a micron longer.

Whatever lives here has learned the only lesson this room teaches: *holding on is more efficient than reaching*. So it holds. Densely, palely, patiently.

It does not need to notice you. You are a draft that will soon be gone. You kick up dust that will become someone's entire world for the next century, and then you leave, and the chamber remains what it is — a warm, amber, humming equilibrium, crowded to its exact edges, sharp as the day it was cut, waiting for nothing, needing nothing, keeping everything it has, forever at 40 percent.
