No one lives here.

That's your first thought, and it's correct for a long time.

You walk for an hour across the springing root-floor, from one hollow cathedral to the next, your ankles wet, and you see no one. The place is too perfect, too evenly kept. You start to think *kept* is the wrong word, because there is no keeper. The equilibrium keeps itself. The drip keeps itself. The growth that never grows keeps itself.

Then you realize you've been looking wrong.

You were looking for something that moves against the architecture. Nothing here moves against it.

---

Whoever lives here lives *as* architecture does. At the speed architecture does.

At first you take them for extra columns. That's understandable. They stand inside the great hollow trunks, or in the shadow between two trunks where the light pools on the root-floor, and they do not move. For twenty minutes, for an hour, they do not move at all. Not the fidget of an animal holding still. The absolute stillness of wood under tension.

They are tall. Taller than you by half again, and then again if you count the way they unfold. Nine, ten, twelve feet when they stand at rest, but you will almost never see one standing at rest. You see them standing at *root.*

When they stop, they stop. They put down feet and they *put them down*. Their legs - if you call them legs - end not in feet but in a splay of pale, finger-thick rootlets that find the seams in the woven floor and thread themselves in. You can watch it happen if you are patient enough, which you are learning to be. A slow, hydraulic blanching, a softening, a seeking. The floor accepts them. The fine root-hairs of the floor rise to meet the rootlets of the foot and lace together, just for a while. While they stand there, they drink. While they stand there, they are not separate from the floor. You could walk right past one and think it was a young buttress, a new prop-root the plateau decided to throw up between cathedrals, still pale and unsunned.

Their skin is not skin. It is bark that hasn't decided to be bark yet. Damp, matte, the color of the underside of a leaf, or the pale inner wood of the basins - a greenish cream, a bone white with sap moving visibly under it in slow, darker tides. In the warm, wet light it sweats exactly the way the columns sweat. Water beads on their shoulders and runs down the long channels of their backs and drips off their elbows in the same metronome as every leaf-tip.

They have no face to speak of, and that is not a horror. It's an economy. Why would they? The head is a smooth, elongated swell, like a closed bud the size of a shield, tipped slightly forward. No eyes you can find, though there are two shallow, lichen-darkened pits where eyes would be if they needed eyes. They don't need eyes. The whole thing sees, the way the plateau sees - with humidity, with vibration, with light falling on leaf. When one turns that bud-head toward you, you feel noticed without being looked at.

You only know it's an animal at all when it decides to go.

It starts with a sound. A soft, deep *tok* from inside the hollow trunk, or from the still figure itself - the same call you heard once an hour and thought was a bird. It's not a call. It's a pressure release. A joint unlocking after a long stillness. Hydraulic pressure shifting.

Then it pulls its feet up. This is not quick. The rootlets unscrew themselves one by one, with a faint wet sigh, leaving tiny, perfect holes in the floor that close behind them within a minute, laced over by the floor's own hairs. The legs - which are not legs but two, then three, then two again, depending on how it wants to distribute its weight - straighten. The arms, which had been folded so tightly along the torso that you thought they were grain, peel away from the body. They are impossibly long, jointed in three places where you expect two, each joint wrapped in a collar of soft moss that grows there and only there.

And it walks.

Not like you walk. No impact. Each step is placed and then *grown* into. The foot finds a seam, splays, grips, sips, releases. The body above it glides, not sways. It moves at the speed of a vine finding sun - faster than you think a plant should move, slower than any animal you know should move. About as fast as you drift when you are wading ankle-deep and not in a hurry. If you tried to race it, you would feel rude.

What does it do?

It tends. But not the way you tend a garden.

Remember, nothing here is growing and nothing is decaying. The plateau is in perfect, held stasis - leaf for leaf, root for root. That stasis is not automatic. It is *tended into being*, second by second.

This is what lives here: the Tenders.

One per cathedral, or one per three cathedrals - the density is so low you can't count it. The whole woven plain, miles wide, might hold seven of them. Might hold twelve. They are solitary the way the cathedrals are solitary, which is to say they are aware of each other across immense, empty, intentional distance and have no need to be closer.

A Tender stops beside a hanging curtain of aerial roots that has grown too heavy with water. It lifts an arm - the arm extends a little longer than it should be able to, the skin creaking like living wood - and with a fingertip that splits into three soft, pale rootlets, it touches one root and *diverts* it. Just a millimeter. The drip that was falling straight into a basin now falls onto moss. The moss takes it. The basin stays at exactly the right depth. It will stand there for another hour watching that corrected fall, not moving, drinking through its feet.

Another Tender stands inside a hollow trunk for a whole morning, its back to the ribbed inner wall, its own ribs aligning with the ribs of the wood. You think it is resting. It is listening through its back to the growth inside the column, to the slow push of water up through the xylem. When a leaf the size of a door on the canopy above has angled a little too far and is shading a vine that needs light, the Tender shudders - a full-body hydraulic shudder that travels up its length - and the leaf, an hour later, corrects. Not because the Tender pulled it. Because the Tender *asked* the plateau to pull it, and the plateau, which is also the Tender a little bit, obliged.

They eat very little, and what they eat is not food as you know it. They eat time. They stand in the basins and lower that bud-head to the warm, skin-warm water and drink condensation that has just formed. They press a palm to the sweating bark of a column and take in sugars that the column sweats for them. They farm nothing. They harvest the surplus of equilibrium itself. And when they need to, they lean down and with those splitting fingertips, they prune a single fungus-flower the size of your fist from a vine-catenary, and put it whole and quivering into the seam that opens vertically along their chest, a seam you never saw until it opened, pink and wet as fresh cambium. It closes without a scar.

They do not speak to each other. Or rather, the plateau speaks for them. That low, continuous chord you hear - the creak of the floor, the hiss of a million drips, the hydraulic rustle of leaves - that is partly them. When one Tender shifts its weight, a wave of tension runs out through the woven root-floor for half a mile and another Tender, standing still in its own empty avenue, feels it through its rooted feet and shifts in answer. The hourly *tok* you hear is not a call and response. It is the sound of the whole net adjusting itself, one animal at a time.

Are there young?

Yes, but you would not know them.

In the deepest part of the root-weave, where the mat is thickest and most polished, there is a place where the floor swells slightly, a slow dome the size of a house, warm to the touch. If you put your hand there and hold very still, you feel a second, slower throb under the chord - a heartbeat at one beat per minute. Inside that dome, in wet dark, a new Tender is being braided together out of root and moss and column-pith, closed-bud head first. It will be there for a year. When it is ready, the dome will soften and split and it will unfold, already tall, already pale, and walk without being taught to the nearest empty cathedral, which has been empty waiting for it.

And the other things you saw - the hand-long pale insect moving like a monk along a vine, tending fungus - those are not separate species. They are its fingers. Its detached fingers. Its tools that it grew and let go of to do small work while it does large work.

That is the strangeness that settles last: whoever lives here is not *on* the plateau. The plateau is not their house. They are the house's way of walking around inside itself to make sure the house stays a house. The plant that learned to be city grew itself a citizen to keep the city from becoming a jungle or a ruin. One citizen stretched thin and slow across a whole region, appearing as many because you cannot see the threads between them.

If you stay here long enough - and the warm air makes you want to stay, the wet light makes you sleepy and slow - you will feel your own pace drop until you match them. Your footsteps will become placements. You will stand in a basin for longer than you meant to, without meaning anything at all, just watching a droplet form, fall, and form again. You will not get older while you do it. Nothing does.

That is how you know you have been, briefly and gently, allowed to live here too.
