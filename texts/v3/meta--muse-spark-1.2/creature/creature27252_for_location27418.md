They live here.

Not *lived* here. That would imply they came and left and these huts and mallets and sweating stacks of brick are ruins. They aren't ruins. They're too fresh for ruins. The cut cane is still bleeding sap. The thumb-prints in the brick are still damp.

You have to learn to see them the way you learned to see the animals — by not looking *for* movement, but by looking *at* stillness until it shows its shape.

At first you think: nothing lives here. The people left. That is what the empty hearths want you to think. Four posts, a green palm roof, a cold fire-ring, a jar of warm water. No one home.

But sit by one of those hearths longer than is comfortable in that sourceless noon. Hold your own breath until your chest aches. You will realize the hut is not empty. It is occupied by something that occupies space the way water occupies a bowl — completely, without needing to be solid.

Then you will see one.

You will have already seen a hundred of them and mistaken them for something else.

---

They are low, as the valley is low. When they are upright — which is rarely, and slowly — they are no taller than you, but they never seem to be standing. They wade. Or they lie. Or they lean. Their natural posture is half-submerged.

Imagine a person made to live exactly where brick meets water meets weave.

Their skin is the color of the brick when it has wept and darkened — not brown, not red, a wet terra-cotta that goes almost purple in the creases. It never dries. It has the faint sheen of amphibian skin, but it is not slimy; it is clay-smooth, and beaded at all times with condensation the way every vertical surface here is. If you touched it, it would feel warm as the basin water, blood-warm. Water does not run off them. It sits on them in perfect, lens-like drops until it falls with that same slow *tok*.

They have very little hair. Their heads are broad and flat, their jaws slightly prognathous, not brutish, but practical — built for holding cane strips between teeth while the hands lash. Their eyes are the giveaway. They are huge, and completely adapted to that white, shadowless noon. No white shows. The iris is a pale, milky amber, filmed over with a translucent nictitating membrane that closes from the side, not the top. It closes very, very slowly. That is the blink you saw the heron do. You thought the heron was a heron.

Their hands are beautiful and wrong. Too many joints in the fingers. The fingers are long, webbed to the second knuckle with a thin, veined membrane the color of a fig leaf's underside. The nails are thick and horned, not for scratching but for splitting cane, for pressing into soft brick. Their feet are broader, splayed, almost circular when they place them on the cane lattice — they distribute weight the way a heron's foot does, so they never break through. The soles are calloused and cracked like dried mud, even though they are always wet.

They do not wear clothes. Not because they are primitive, but because clothes would never dry. Instead their shoulders and backs are often draped, caped, in living things — a shawl of morning glory that has rooted in the small of their back and blooms there, white trumpets open forever. A collar of duckweed that clings to their collarbones. Moss grows in the hollow at the base of their throat. It doesn't seem to bother them. They cultivate it.

And they are *everywhere* and *almost nowhere* at once, which is why you thought the valley was sparse.

There is one per island. That is the rule they keep.

One banyan, one keeper beneath it. One thicket of flowering reed, one keeper half-lost inside it. One stretch of thirty empty, perfect basins of warm green water with nothing in them but reflection — and one keeper lying full-length in the thirty-first, submerged to the nose, eyes open, facing the same direction as the fish.

Because the fish *are* them, in a way. Or they were.

This is not a species that is born and dies. This is a place that is trying to decide what it wants to be when it grows up, and they are the in-between stage.

The small scaled fish hanging motionless in the water, all facing the same way — those are the youngest. They hang there for a long time. A very long time. They do not need to swim because the water brings food to them, and they do not need to breathe quickly because the water is warm and still and holds its oxygen like a held breath. When one has hung long enough, it grows limbs. It grows that nictitating membrane. It pushes itself up with webbed hands onto the cane lattice and it does not leave the water behind — it carries a basin with it, in its skin.

The translucent-throated amphibians pulsing once every few minutes on the edge of the brick? Those are the adolescents. The dragonflies hovering absolutely still in the hot air, wings a blur but bodies pinned? Those are not dragonflies. Those are the old ones' thoughts, or their breath, made briefly visible before it dissolves again.

The adults — the ones you finally see — spend most of their existence doing what looks like nothing.

A keeper will stand in a basin up to its knees and, with infinite care, take a single fresh-cut length of river-cane, split it with a chipped stone blade, and lash it to the edge of the lattice. One lashing. It will take an hour. The cord must be wetted, the cane must be coaxed not to remember its old shape. They work with the wooden mallets you found abandoned, but when you watch, you understand the tools were never abandoned. They were set down mid-stroke because the stroke takes so long.

Another will cup warm water in those webbed hands and pour it from one basin to another. You saw water flow slightly uphill to do this and thought it was a trick of the light. It wasn't. They are *leveling* the valley. The whole basin must remain perfectly level, like water in a bowl held in cupped hands. If one basin were to evaporate even a finger's width, the balance would tip and noon would finally slide into afternoon. They will not let that happen. So they pour, and they weep. The water that seeps endlessly out of the brick is not seepage. It is them, sweating, exhaling through the floor they laid. The condensation on every surface is their breath on the air.

They tend the disobedient plants not by cutting them back, but by negotiating.

You will see one kneeling before that colossal banyan that has burst through the floor, one webbed hand pressed flat against a prop root that has cracked a terrace. It is not pushing. It is listening to the root's pressure and then, with the other hand, softening the brick around it with a little warm water, so the root can have its way without breaking the weave. The fruit that is ripe to bursting but never falls, never rots — they brush it with their fingers as they pass and the fruit holds. They have asked it to hold.

That is their work. They are not building the valley anymore and they are not abandoning it. They are *holding* it. The way you hold your breath when you are carrying something brim-full and you must not spill.

That is why there is no sound. They have taught the herons and the snakes and the fish to hold it too. The chorus you expected — insects, birds, monkeys — that would be time passing. That would be decay and hatching and hunting and rotting. They have paused all of that in exchange for this perfect, wet, bright stasis. A single fig will take a season to split, and it will split silently so as not to disturb the air.

If you stay long enough on that cane causeway that never gets closer to the center, one will notice you. Not quickly. Attention here moves the way light moves through warm water — diffuse, then suddenly, totally there.

It will turn its huge, filmed eyes toward you. Its nictitating membrane will draw across, slow as a cloud. It will not be surprised. People — or things like people, upright, dry, shadow-casting people — must have wandered down into this low bowl before. Perhaps you are one of the original makers who walked away between heartbeats and are now walking back.

It will not speak. It has no language that needs sound. But it will lift a wet hand, webbing catching the sourceless light, and press its palm to the warm water in the basin beside you, and the water will still perfectly around its fingers.

It is inviting you to put your hand in too.

If you do, the water will be exactly blood-warm, and it will not ripple. And for a moment you will feel, through the sole of your foot on the cane lattice, through the palm of your hand in the water, through the metallic sweetness on your tongue, the faint, immense, patient effort of the entire valley — brick and cane and water and leaf and fish and keeper — all holding that one breath together.

They have been holding it since before the brick dried.

They will hold it long after you decide to breathe again.
