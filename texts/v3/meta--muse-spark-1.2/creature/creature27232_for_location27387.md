There is something living there, but you won't see it at first because you are looking for the wrong kind of living.

You go in expecting an inhabitant to be separate from the place — an animal in a forest, a person in a room — a figure against a ground. Here there is no figure and ground. That was the first rule that broke. So the first time you walk through, you think the grove is empty except for the small, quiet fauna. Beetles. Moths. Lizards. You think: *nothing large lives here, nothing that needs more than wax and light.*

Then you stay still long enough for the warm air to make you drowsy, and you realize you have been looking directly at them for minutes.

They are not hidden. They are just very good at being what the place is.

### At first, a misreading of scale

You see a thickening where a vertical wattle wall meets a low beam, where the rope-vines are especially dense. A knot of fiber and shadow, about the size of a rolled-up quilt, tucked into the fork of what was carpentry and is now botany. You think it's another nest. The finches make so many.

Then the knot breathes. Very slowly. Once every thirty seconds or so, a shallow lift and fall that moves the curtain of needle-leaves around it.

You lean closer and the knot resolves, reluctantly, into a shape that has a head and limbs because you have decided to see head and limbs. It lets you.

They are roughly your size, or they are much smaller — here distance is slack, so this is hard to fix. Up close, when one is next to you brushing past a curtain of tin leaves, you see they come to about your chest, or your waist if you are tall. Long-limbed and narrow, as if to better move sideways through foliage that is always within arm's reach. Not thin from hunger. Thin from design. There is no excess to them.

At first you think they are wearing something. Then you see they are made of the wearing.

### What they are made of

Like everything else here, they were built, not born, and then they learned to live anyway.

Their skin is the same oiled parchment as the vault, stretched over a light frame of bent sapling and wattle — you can see light through the edge of an ear, the thin amber glow of the vault through a forearm if they hold it up. It is dry, warm, faintly waxy to the touch. Not sweaty. They do not sweat. They cannot afford to. The air has sealed itself dry and they have agreed to stay dry with it.

Over that, hair — not hair — frayed fiber, coir and undyed wool and human hair saved and carded, growing not from follicles but stitched in rows along the scalp with tiny, visible wooden pegs, like doll's hair. It hangs in lank, dusty curtains, sometimes braided into vines that, if left alone for a week, will actually put out a stitched leaf at the tip. Their joints are joinery. You can see it at elbow and knee and knuckle: mortise and tenon, pegged, wrapped with oiled cord that has darkened with handling. When they move, the cord creaks, very softly. The same sound the building makes when it settles. Shoulders click like lacquered beetle backs.

Their faces are the most unfinished part, which makes them the most unsettling. Not unfinished as in crude — every chisel mark is loving — but unfinished as in *left open to negotiation*. A brow made from a single bent slat, eyes set deep and made from polished, dark wood or hammered copper discs that slowly tarnish and are slowly polished again by their own fingertips. No whites. No wetness. They don't blink with eyelids; a thin membrane of starched linen draws sideways across the eye when the light ambers toward evening, like a moth's wing closing. A mouth is a seam, often closed. They have no need to open it often.

When they do, there are no teeth as you know them. A row of tiny carved pegs, or sometimes just a second seam, that parts to rasp wax from the underside of a leaf. Their tongues, if you glimpse one, are felt.

They drink the same way the leaves do. They stand in the even, shadowless light with their arms slightly out, parchment skin warming, and take in what the air offers. They rasp mineral pollen — that iron-red and ochre dust — from copper leaves and lick it from each other's shoulders. It keeps their joints from drying out. You will see two of them grooming each other in a fork of rope-vine, patiently, for an hour, tongues of felt dabbing at waxy undersides.

They make no song. The finches taught them that quiet is the rule here. 0.8/3 quiet, so the lushness seems silent.

### How they live, slowly

There are not many. Five, maybe nine. The count slips when you try to hold the edges of the room in your head at once. You see three together and think that must be all, then you find a fourth asleep in a hollow of the fiber-mat you had thought was just a dip in the floor. They do not gather. They disperse, like large, slow lizards, each tending a circuit.

Because that is what they do: tend.

They are not separate from the craft that made the grove; they are its maintenance cycle. You will watch one kneel for a long time beside a seam between floorboards where a vine-rope has frayed. From a pouch made of the same soft fiber as the floor — which is also their bedding, which is also their pantry — they take a bone needle, a length of oiled cord, and they stitch. Not quickly. Each pass is considered, pulled taut, pegged. The vine shivers a little as they work, as if being tickled. When they are done, the vine is stronger, and a day later it will have grown a centimeter past their repair, fraying open into a new tin leaf that was not there before. They do not seem to decide this. The place does it through them.

Another will stand on tiptoes and reach up to the vault, where a sheet of parchment has gone slack, and will tighten its lashing to a sapling-lathe, humming without sound — a breath through a closed seam.

Another will find a dead beetle — stiff, dry, lacquer dimmed — and pick it up with a tenderness that is not quite human because it is not mourning. They carry it to where the mites gather like animated sawdust and set it down, and they watch while it is taken apart. Then they gather the polished wing-cases and stitch them, later, into a new leaf, or into the shoulder of a new one of themselves.

Because that is how they reproduce. Not often. There is no hurry. The grove is patient on the scale of days.

When a beam puts out a bud at a joint — that slow swelling where carpentry becomes botany — they notice before you do. They will sit with it. They will bring pollen on their fingertips and dust it. They will wrap it in dampless felt to keep it warm. Over weeks, the bud lengthens, articulates. First a limb, pegged at the elbow. Then a head, face still blank as unworked parchment. They do not carve it so much as coax it, whisper-tapping along its length with a small hammer, the way you might talk to a sleeping animal. Eventually it shivers and peels itself from the beam with a soft tearing, like Velcro, and sits up. It is clumsy at first. Its cord-wrapped joints creak loudly. An older one will groom it for hours, licking wax onto its new skin until the amber light shines through evenly.

They do not name each other in any way you can hear. They communicate by leaving stitch-patterns. A certain knot left on a trellis means *this vine is weak, walk around*. A fringe of frayed fiber on a leaf edge means *I slept here, it is warm*. If you learn to look, the whole grove is annotated, a slow conversation in thread.

### The wrongness in them

Like the grove, they are polite about physics.

You will see one step off the fiber-mat onto what looks like empty air between two curtains of foliage and find purchase, as if there were a floorboard there a second ago, or will always be a second from now. They walk up a wattle wall without effort to bask nearer the vault, parchment skin flushing a little darker with light, and they do not fall — gravity lets them, as it lets dust hang too long. If you try to follow the exact path they took, you will find it took you twenty steps and took them six, and the wall you both came from is suddenly too close behind you again. They know the slack stitches in space. They don't think about it.

And if you aren't looking directly — the second rule — they are less distinct from the grove. From the corner of your eye, a standing figure is a trellis. A trellis, when you turn back, has a face. One afternoon I watched what I was sure was a bundle of linen leaves harden along its stem into true wood while an inhabitant dozed beneath it, and when I looked again the inhabitant's forearm had leafed at the wrist, a perfect, hammered-copper leaf trembling there, veined with thread. They didn't notice. Or they did, and they flexed it, and it made a soft tin sound.

They have no outside. If you part the wattle to show them the layers behind — more wattle — they look with polite incomprehension. If you point up at the vault and try to mime *sky*, they tilt their dark wooden eyes and then gently pull a sheet of parchment down to eye level to show you how evenly the light diffuses, as if to say: *why would you need more light than this?*

They have never been wet. They have no word for thirst. They find your chapped lips fascinating and will offer you, with great solemnity, a pinch of ochre pollen on a dry fingertip. It does nothing for you, but the gesture is kind.

At night — when the vault has ambered and dimmed so gradually you didn't see it happen — they fold themselves into the fiber-mat. Not on it, into it. They card a shallow depression with their hands, lie down, and pull the dark, waxy fiber over themselves like a blanket. Leaves of tin and linen settle over them, brushing their parchment cheeks. Felt moths land on them and are not shooed. Breathing slows to that thirty-second cycle that moves the leaves. The whole grove sighs with them.

If you lie down near them, which the warm, dense, quiet grove invites you to do, one may turn its copper eyes to you and, very slowly, reach out and tuck a loose thread of your clothing back into its weave, repairing you a little, including you.
