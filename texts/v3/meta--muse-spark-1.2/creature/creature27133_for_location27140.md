It doesn't live there the way you expected something to live.

At first you think nothing does.

That is the whole point of the bowl — that impression it gives of being empty. You come up the long climb and you are prepared to find something hardy and hunched, something hiding. An animal with thick fur or bright scales tucked into the galleries, or at least the nervous glitter of insects over the water lenses. You have been listening for it without knowing you were listening for it.

And there is nothing that answers. No whirr. No scrape. No eye. The ferns are perfect and untorn. The peat gives back your footprint and erases it. The silence is so complete you start to think of it as vacancy.

That is your first mistake.

The thing that lives here doesn't move. So you didn't see it.

---

You have to sit down to see it. You have to stop walking and let the light, which is itself slow here — filtered through cloud that never quite becomes rain — settle. You have to let your eyes stop searching for a back, a head, a limb.

Look again at the open spaces. The ones you thought were empty.

Between the clump of ferns and the single pale tree, there is that smooth sheet of stone with only a film of algae. You thought it was empty because it was flat and wet and shining. It isn't. Crouch down until your cheek is near the stone and the warm, saturated air beads on your eyebrows.

There are discs on the rock.

You noticed the lichen before, peripherally. Flat, translucent discs the color of river-ice, the color of very thin glass with a little milk in it. Each about as wide as your palm, or smaller. Some no bigger than a coin. They lie flush against the sandstone where the seep runs in its slow sheet. You thought they were mineral bloom, or lichen, something incidental.

They are the place. They are what lives here.

Don't call them lichen yet, though half of what they are is lichen. Don't call them plant, though they photosynthesize. The name will settle later, after you have watched them long enough to understand what kind of life can afford to live at this speed.

See how they are spaced.

That was the second thing you noticed without understanding it — the vegetation is lush where it is, and absent where it isn't, and the emptiness feels deliberate, swept clean. The discs are the reason. Each disc keeps a territory of bare, wet rock around itself, ten feet, twelve feet, as perfect a circle as water can make. Inside that circle, nothing else is allowed to root. The mosses stop at the edge as if cut. The algae inside the circle is not the green kind, but a darker, almost clear film that belongs to the disc. The disc is gardening the stone. Slowly acidifying it, slowly precipitating a micro-terrace of white mineral at its rim — that fingernail-high line you saw — to keep its own lens of water from running away.

Bend closer. The disc is not flat. It is a shallow bowl itself, like the mesa's bowl in miniature. A lens. Its upper surface is slightly concave, and it holds water. Not a lot. A skin, a few millimeters deep, replenished constantly by the mist that beads and the seep that passes over it. The water in the lens is utterly still and utterly clear. And the disc beneath it is not opaque. You can see down into it.

There are layers. A translucent upper skin that catches light and holds it, like the hanging droplets under the ledge. Beneath that, a lattice of green, diffuse, not leaves but threads, and beneath that, a white, fungal weft that has bitten into the sandstone itself, etching a pale ghost of the disc down into the rock an inch, two inches. This is one body, one organism — alga and fungus so married they have become a third thing — living half in stone, half in water, half in air.

It does not hurry. Nothing here does.

A fern can unroll a frond in an afternoon. These do not. If you marked the edge of one disc with a scratch in the stone and came back in a year, you would not see a difference. Come back in ten years and the white mineral terrace at its rim might be a hair thicker. Come back in fifty and the disc might be a centimeter wider. They grow by condensation. By light. By dissolving a few molecules of sandstone at a time and catching a few molecules of dust and dissolved birdless air in their water lens. There was never enough phosphorus here for anything faster. So they learned to be enough with almost nothing.

That is why there are no animals. Not really. A fly that blew up here on a storm would find nothing to eat that it could reach before the disc's water lens dried for an instant — which it never does — and the disc itself is inedible, glassy and sour. And any animal that tried to graze the moss hummocks would starve trying to walk between them across the bare stone, carrying its own hunger like a weight. The bowl selected, over a very long time, against anything that needed to move to eat. It kept only what could sit and wait and drink the air.

And yet they are not quite plants. Watch for long enough and you will see the only movement they allow themselves.

In the deepest part of the bowl, where the air is most still, you saw the hanging drops under the ledge — elongated, trembling, not falling. Go back there and look under the ledge at eye level. There is a disc there too, but it is on the underside of the stone, clinging inverted like a limpet to the ceiling of the gallery. Its lens hangs downward. It is holding one of those impossible drops.

The drop is its. The disc has exuded a clear, slightly viscous skin and is holding the water out away from the rock, suspended. Inside that pendant lens, if you hold your breath and let your eyes adapt until the light inside the water steadies, you can see a faint opalescence. Particles turning, very slowly. The disc is feeding. Photosynthesis in the pendant water, the exchange of gas so slow it looks like crystal growing.

When the humidity finally breaks, when at last the balance tips, the drop will fall. And when it falls it will not splash on the peat. It will fall on the disc's child — a tiny, coin-sized disc waiting on the floor exactly where that drop has fallen for a thousand seasons. The mother disc above feeds its daughter with its own held water. Generation after generation, a vertical line of descent marked by a faint chain of etched circles on the cliff face and on the peat below.

You can see it now, once you know the pattern: the whole bowl is mapped in these lines. Each great disc on the open stone has a corresponding pale scar on the rock above where a gallery disc once hung, or hangs still. Each clump of ferns grows not randomly but in the wind-shadow of a disc, where the disc has allowed organic matter to accumulate. Even the trees — those low, pale, peeling trees with shallow roots — are not separate. Their roots seek out the fungal weft of the oldest discs and graft into it, trading water for shade. The bowl is not a place with many things living in it. It is one slow, diffuse collaboration, one mat with many expressions, and the discs are its most perfect expression.

Put your hand near one — don't touch it — and feel how cool the air is just above its lens. It is breathing, after a fashion. Taking in the warm mist and making it liquid, making it stay. That is its work. It holds water in a place that is high enough that water should have run away long ago.

If you do touch it, very lightly with a fingertip, the surface gives a little, like gelatin, and then restores. It will not close or flinch. It has no nerves. It does not need warning. Anything that could hurt it would have to be as slow as it is — a century of drier air, a shift in the cloud's path. A boot. It will feel your boot for a moment as a pressure and a shadow, and then, after you are gone, it will continue to precipitate calcite at its rim at a rate of perhaps a grain a year.

How old is it, then, the one in front of you, the palm-wide one with the faint concentric growth lines like a very soft agate? Brush the silt gently from its edge and you see the chert flake half-swallowed beneath it. That flake was struck by a hand — a quick, clever, animal hand that came up here, struck an edge, scraped a reed, and left — perhaps two thousand years ago, perhaps ten thousand. The disc found the flake a useful piece of hard ground and grew over its corner. It has incorporated the tool into its body. It was already wide when that hand came. It will be here when the mineral bloom has covered the flake entirely. When the peat has risen another inch. When another hand, if any hand ever climbs that long again, finds only a faint pattern of discs on stone and wonders if they are natural.

They will look natural. That is their genius. They look like what stone does when water has been finding the same path for a very long time.

Sit with the disc for an hour and you will feel less alone. Not because it is company — it has no eyes to meet yours, no mind to know you — but because its patience corrects your scale. Your breath, which sounded so loud in the warm silence, slows without you telling it to. The *tok* of a hanging drop falling somewhere behind you stops sounding like an interruption and starts sounding like a clock ticking once an hour, on time. You understand that the bowl is inhabited, densely inhabited, but at a density measured in centuries per square foot and in millimeters per lifetime.

You came expecting a creature to look at. What you found is a creature that is looking-like-stone so perfectly that you have to learn to see it, and once you do, you realize it has been watching the mist condense for longer than any lineage you belong to has had names for stone, or for water, or for waiting.
