Nothing lives here the way you were taught living should work.

At first you think nothing can. Not really. Not *stay*. The air is too wet to breathe and too dark to see and too loud to think, and the ground is not ground. Anything that tried to live here like an animal lives in a field or a forest or a city would drown, or rot, or go mad from the press of the cloud. And you are right. Nothing like that lives here.

What lives here lives like the marsh lives — spread thin, saturated, half-dissolved into everything else.

### The Occupants

If you stand long enough to let the lights sort themselves — fauna from machine from rot-light — you start to see that the marsh has three kinds of inhabitant, and they all bleed into each other.

**1. The Small Multitude**

They are the true natives. They were here before the terraces were farmed and they will be here after the last condenser rusts through.

They do not live *on* the marsh. They *are* the density of it. Every cubic meter holds them, the way seawater holds plankton.

The broad moths are the most obvious, because they are the loudest. They are not moths the way you know moths. They are the size of spread hands, or dinner plates, with wings like oiled paper that don't make a sound individually — the sound comes from the air being moved, from thousands of them moving wet air at once. Their bodies run hot, almost fevered, and they swarm the heat of anything warmer than the ambient bathwater: your face, a vent, a pump station's sighing flank. Their larvae do not eat leaves. They eat the mist itself, filtering condensation through combs on their legs. When they die — which they do constantly, falling like wet ash — they are dissolved by the moss before they hit it.

Below them, in the black mirrors of every basin and boot-print pool, the amphibians. You thought there was one kind. There are dozens, but you will never learn to tell them apart because you identify them by their light, not their shape. They farm light inside themselves. Colonies of bacteria in translucent dorsal sacs, in their throats, on their eggs. Each pool has its own chord of light — cold white, amber, arterial red, a sullen green that pulses with the animal's heart. They sit shoulder to shoulder, throat-singing. Some never leave the water; some have legs and walk the peat on nights when the cloud is low, dragging their lit backs through the ferns like lanterns. They call with the wood-knock sound. They breed explosively, then eat their young, then breed again. The water never stays empty.

Between them, in the peat and under the fungal shelves and along the hanging roots, the sliders. They are not snakes. They have no eyes that you can find. They are warm, muscle-ropes as thick as your wrist or as thin as a finger, covered in a slick that carries its own faint, fading bioluminescence. When one passes over moss it leaves a glowing ribbon that persists for seven or eight seconds and then dies. You see their trails more than you see them. There are thousands at any moment moving under your feet. They hunt by feeling the thrum of the moths through the peat. They hunt each other.

And under and through all of them, the mat itself — which you keep mistaking for ground cover. The moss, the peat, the black ferns, the laminar fungi, the aerial root-tangles. It is not a backdrop. It is an animal in the way a reef is an animal. It grows over anything that stops moving. It tastes with fine root-hairs. It sweats. It heaves. If you put your ear to it you can hear water moving through it like blood through a limb.

None of these things have territories. They have wetness gradients. They crowd where it is wettest and thin where it is only damp.

**2. The Tenders**

People live here. Not many. Never many. The marsh cannot hold many.

You will not find a village. You will not find a town. A town would sink, or rot, or be swallowed to the roof by a single season of ferns. The people who live here do not *inhabit* the marsh. They *tend* it, the way you would tend a bedridden god.

They are born elsewhere, mostly — down below the cloud deck, where there is still sky — and rotated up for tours that last months or years. A few are born here now, in the dry rooms inside the machines, and you can tell them because they are different, even as infants.

They are all pale in a way that has nothing to do with sun. Their skin is soft, pruned, almost amphibian, perpetually damp. They have given up trying to stay dry. Their hair is kept shaved or in tight, oiled braids because anything loose greens with algae in days. Their eyes are large and dark-adapted, shot through with visible capillaries from staring into low light; many of them have a faint nictitating film they can draw across the eye when the mist is thickest, and they blink too slowly. Their lungs are strong but they breathe shallow, wet breaths, because the air is so heavy. They speak quietly — not from reverence, but because sound carries forever and a loud voice will bring everything listening.

You will rarely see one standing on the peat. They live *on* the industry, because only the industry stays above the growth.

They ride the crawlers — those wide, sighing harvester-barges that move at walking speed, stripping fungal shelves and laying them bare. They sleep in hammocks strung in the warm, thrumming engine bays where the air is drier and vibrates against their bones. They shelter in the condenser towers, those black iron lungs, because inside the baffles there is a metal catwalk and a maintenance shrine with a kettle and a light that never goes out. They string their own amber work-lights between towers and navigate by them, because to get lost between lights here is to walk in circles until the moss takes your boots.

Their culture is a sound-culture and a light-culture. They do not shout across basins. They use the amphibians' trick — they have taken to wearing strips of cultured bioluminescence on their cuffs and hoods, bacteria-fed patches that they can dim or brighten with a squeeze, a hand-signal in the dark. Their language above ground is clicks and low hums that carry under the drone of the moths without being lost. They mark time not by days — there are none — but by harvest cycles, by when a fungal shelf has regrown enough to be grazed again, by when a hovering lake finally collapses.

They are not colonists. They are maintenance lineage. They know the peat will take any tool left on the ground within an hour, so they tether everything. They know not to stand still, so they sway when they wait. They know the water that sweats from the basalt is warm and full of dissolved iron and sulfur, good to drink if you let it settle, but that the water that collects on the ferns will give you a rash that lights up faintly in the dark. They do not try to drain the marsh. They wring it, gently, endlessly. They pull cloud through cold baffles and pump warm mineral water from stone to pan, and skim the growth, and keep the wires sparking. If they stopped, the marsh would not die. It would simply close over them.

The ones born here — the second and third generation Tenders — are stranger still. Their chests are broader, their hearing sharper. They can hear a slider coming through peat from ten meters away. Their skin holds a faint, symbiotic sheen of the same bacteria the amphibians use, so in absolute dark their hands glow faintly when they are agitated or warm. They do not remember sky. If you show them a picture of stars they think it is a kind of foam.

They are kind, in the way people who have to listen constantly are kind. They will find you by the heat of your face. They will not ask why you came.

**3. The Machines That Are Also Animals**

And then there are the others, which you kept counting as buildings.

The condenser towers, the pump stations, the harvesters, the high wires. They are not inhabited the way a house is inhabited. They *inhabit* on their own. No one pilots the harvester-barge when it grazes at 3 a.m., its running lights sweeping through the ferns. It goes because it knows the circuit. The pump station breathes because it was told to breathe a long time ago and was never told to stop.

They have become fauna. The moths cloud around their heat exchangers as if they were huge, hot flowers. The moss grows thick on their north sides and is scraped off by Tend-ers and grows back by the next watch. The amphibians have learned that the overflow pools beneath their legs are the warmest, safest places to lay eggs, so every pump hums over a nursery of light. When a crawler passes, the sliders scatter from its vibration the way they scatter from a predator's footfall.

They have territories and migrations. A condenser that has stood for twenty years develops a particular thrum that the insects recognize. If it goes silent, they leave. The high wires spark and spit and the aerial roots lean toward them, tasting the ozone.

The Tenders do not worship them, but they talk about them as if they were old, stupid, reliable pack animals. They pat the iron. They listen for a change in the breathing.

---

All three live together because they have learned the one rule the marsh teaches: do not try to be separate.

No one here is dry. No one here is alone in the dark. No one here is quiet. The moth needs the tower's heat. The tower needs the Tender's hand to clear its moss. The Tender needs the amphibian's light to walk and the slider's absence to sleep and the fern's water to drink. The fern needs the rot of everything that dies.

If you stay long enough, you will feel the marsh begin to live on you, too. The film will form on your skin. The moss will find the seams of your boots. Your breath will join the drone. And from a pool nearby, something lit from within will answer.
