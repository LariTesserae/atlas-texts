At first you think nothing lives here that could be called *someone*.

That's the trick of the scale.

You stand on that young rock with the water beading down its fresh cut, you watch a line of those stilt-legged shag-plates move a kilometer away and look like dust on the wind, you see four pillar-cities on the horizon so far apart they look like lighthouses and not a town — and your brain says *empty*. Uninhabited. Because you are looking for density, for crowding, for the lowland proof of life which is noise and smoke and a cluttered horizon.

Then you see movement that isn't migration.

A needle of brushed light lifts off the flank of the nearest pillar and doesn't fly so much as *be drawn* — along one of those almost-invisible filaments stretched between nodes. It goes too straight and too fast to be an animal, too quiet to be a machine as you understand machines. It crosses ten kilometers of marsh and sedge and grazing herd in a minute and a half without leaving a wake in the air, except that the gliders above it turn their heads to watch it pass. And a minute later, another. And another on a different filament, on a different vector. You realize the sky is threaded.

That is where they live. Not *in* the pillars. *Between* them.

If you get closer — and you have to decide to, because nothing here comes to you — they resolve slowly. They don't want to be seen quickly.

They are tall. That is the first compromise with the plateau. Thin air punishes width and rewards surface area where you can get it. So they are taller than lowland humans by a head or more, but narrow through the shoulders and chest in a way that looks at first like thinness, until you see one breathe. The chest expands not forward but *around*, ribs like barrel hoops, and a breath taken up here holds. They walk without hurry because hurry is expensive when every breath is surgical sunlight and cold. When they do breathe out, the steam hangs a long time and then vanishes all at once, the way the weep-cloud does.

You expect them to be pale, but they aren't. High, sharp sun through thin air would flay anything pale. Their skin is a deep, matte olive-brown, dense, almost stone-colored, the color of wet basalt and lichen. Their hair, where they keep it, is black or iron-gray and cropped close, or braided tight to the skull so the wind can't take it. Their eyes are the giveaway — large, and a little darker than they should be, with a faint amber sheen when the light hits. A second lid, not quite a blink, slides sideways when the wind gusts or when they look directly at the sun. They made themselves, or were made, to not squint here.

They dress like the shag-plates do. Not in furs, but in the same principle. Long, heavy coats that shed water, woven from something that looks like felted fiber until you touch it and realize it's grown, a living weave that beads the weep and lets it run off in those same vertical tears the rock makes. Under that, soft, close layers that hold a few degrees of stolen geothermal warmth against the skin. They don't bundle. They regulate. You rarely see a face covered, even in the weep-fog. Cold here is a working cold and they work in it.

And they are quiet. Physiologically quiet. Thin air doesn't carry shouting, so they never learned to. When two of them talk standing three meters apart on the frost-gravel, you hear a low, clear murmur that seems to travel *through* the ground as much as the air. Children are taught to speak on the exhale, long sentences on one slow breath, to not waste air. Laughter, when it comes, is breathy and brief, more a puff of steam than a sound. They hear very well. They have to, because the plateau is so quiet you can hear a pika turn over a stone a hundred meters away.

There aren't many of them. That's important. If you flew — skimmed — from node to node for a day you might count a few thousand in a space the size of lowland France. They like it that way. Density would be an insult to the table of the world. So they live sparse on purpose, the way the dwarf blackroot and the tufted sedge do: widely spaced, each with enough.

Inside a pillar is where you see what they really are.

From outside, a node looks seamless, matte black and pearl-gray. Inside, it is warm — not hot, not heated, but warm the way a body is warm. The stone of the walls is grown basalt, but it gives slightly under your palm, and it is faintly damp, weeping clean water that is caught in runnels and drunk. Light comes from no single source; the structural haze that holds the pillar up also glows, a diffuse daylight that has been softened just a notch from the sharp outside sun. No corridors, no floors in any lowland sense. Terraces spiral up the interior like the inside of a shell, wide and shallow, with low partitions that can be moved by thinking at them. Families, work groups, creches — they don't have apartments. They have *places* along the spiral, marked by woven rugs, by heat, by the smell of cooking, by herbaria of lichen and sedge being coaxed to breed faster.

They don't own these places. You can see that in how they move. A young person will live for a season on the 40th terrace tending the gravity lattice, then for a season on the 200th terrace minding the glider nests on the warm outer skin. Elders sit lowest, where the marsh never freezes and the doors open directly onto the sponge-ground, watching herds pass. No one locks anything. There is nowhere to lock.

What do they *do*? 

They midwife.

They will tell you this without mysticism, in that low murmur. The continent was shoved up last week, geologically speaking. Fault blocks the size of counties are still settling. Water hasn't learned to be rivers yet. If they did nothing, the plateau would do what young plateaus do: shatter, slide, flash-freeze, drown and dry in violent pulses until something like balance appears in a few hundred thousand years. They are impatient, but not controlling.

So the filaments you felt in your teeth — they are not just transit and power. They are acupuncture. Deep below the unfinished basins, where the rock is still hot from being pushed up, they sank lattices that drink heat and carry it sideways, bleeding it into the air around each pillar just enough to keep a cylinder of ground from permafrost. They comb the weep-cloud with field pressure, parting it into dry lanes so the marshes don't become a single drowning sheet, so the sedge has a chance. Where a new stream is chewing clumsily at a fault lip, they have laid a wall no higher than your knee — poured geology, the same seamless grown stone as the pillars — that will teach the water a meander. In ten thousand years it will be gone, worn away, and the river will remember the lesson on its own.

They walk the ground constantly. Not in vehicles, though they have them. On foot, knee-deep in marsh mirror, checking peat, counting pika burrows, moving a single stone that is diverting a seep the wrong way. They know the herds individually — not by name, but by pattern, by limp. The shag-plates let them walk among them; the lean snowshoe fox-predators have learned that the tall quiet ones are not prey and not competition, and trot past at a respectful distance. On the outer skin of the pillars, the gliders roost by the thousands, white and slate-gray, and the people leave them alone, except to scrape guano for the herbaria, except to stand and watch them turn.

They migrate themselves, just more slowly. A family will follow a herd's line for weeks, living out of a skim-disc parked low over the marsh, then filament back to a pillar for a gathering. News travels the filaments faster than bodies do, so a birth in a node a hundred kilometers away is known everywhere in an hour, and no one rushes to see. They will see in time. Everything here operates on plateau time.

And they are unfinished on purpose, the way their rule says: *be unfinished well.*

You can see it in their children. The children have not yet grown the full amber lid. They squint. They trip on the sponge-ground because they haven't learned the slow, splayed step that doesn't fill your boot with water. They ask loud questions — wasteful with air — and the adults answer without shushing them. They are allowed to be clumsy, like the waterfalls that haven't cut canyons yet.

You can see it in their old. They do not try to live forever, though they could probably stretch it. When an elder's breath gets short even for here, they walk out. Not dramatically. They leave the warm cylinder, walk out onto the frost-gravel until the cold is clean again, and sit near a seep or a lichen mat. The pikas come close. Sometimes the herd passes. The city doesn't retrieve them.

If you stay long enough — days, and the light stays that normal, sharp, diffused temperate light, dawns that take forever because the horizon is just more plateau — you stop looking for a city and start seeing a different kind of animal. A sparse, tall, warm-blooded, tool-using animal that lives thinly and constantly, like the herds, like the gliders, like the lichen. An animal whose niche is *holding things steady while they learn to be themselves.*

That is who lives here. Not conquerors, not colonists in the lowland sense. Caretakers with lungs rebuilt for thin air and eyes filmed against a sharper sun, who decided that the best way to inhabit a continent being born is to be as large, as quiet, as far apart and as mutually attentive as the land itself — to scaffold without smothering, to warm without burning, to walk among shag-plates and pikas and leave footprints that fill slowly with clear water behind them.
