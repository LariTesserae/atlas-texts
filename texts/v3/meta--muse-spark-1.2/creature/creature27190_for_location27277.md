Nothing lives here the way you expect something to live in a place.

At first you think nothing lives here at all. Because your eye is looking for the wrong kind of occupancy — for nests and burrows and trails through grass, for movement at your own height, for something that stands out against the stone. And there is nothing that stands out. The wet glass takes everything and makes it the same color, the same sheen. The sound takes everything and makes it the same hush-roar. So you stand there getting cold feet and think: empty.

Then you stay long enough that your search pattern breaks.

You stop looking *across* the thicket and start looking *on* it. And it is occupied.

Not abundantly. Not densely, the way a forest is dense with birds and insects. This is a starved occupancy. A thin soup stretched over a huge, complicated surface. But it is complete. Everything that lives here lives *as* the valley does: dispersed, vertical, patient, and tuned to water that doesn't want to flow.

### The Film

The base of it is not the rust-red slick in the pans, though that is part of it. The base of it is the film itself.

If you crouch and let your eyes go soft a few centimeters from a blade, you see the water is not empty. The skin that clings to the vertical glass before it finally gives way and runs — that thick, oily-seeming sheet — is alive. It is a colonial thing, or a thousand things acting as one. Bacteria and yeast-like bodies and something finer, holding themselves together in a transparent mat that you can only see because it makes the water bead differently. Where it is thickest, the water doesn't run at all; it holds, it bulges.

Touch a blade and your fingertip comes away cold and slightly tacky. Not slimy. Tacky, like the back of a leaf. That is them. They are not growing *on* the rock so much as they are farming the condensation itself, taking the minerals the glass is still leaching — silica, iron, sulfur — and the faint chemical rain that never stops falling from that pearl sky. They are dark olive where they are dense, rust where the iron dominates, otherwise invisible. They are the only thing here that could be called abundant, and they are abundant the way paint is abundant, not the way animals are.

Everything else eats them. Or more precisely, grazes them.

### The Graziers

They are what you mistook for insects.

They are not insects. They have too many legs for that to be a comfortable word, and no wings, and no eyes that you can find. Each one is about as long as your thumb joint, some as long as your palm, and so flattened they seem two-dimensional until they move.

Watch one. It is pressed to the fluting of a spire, its body so thin it follows the micro-ridges of the cooling rock exactly. Its color is smoked glass, or wet basalt — perfect — and its back is not a shell but a translucent plate fringed with fine, colorless hairs that hold a layer of water over it at all times. It does not walk so much as it *creeps*, and it never lifts its body from the stone. Its legs — six, eight, twelve depending on the kind, it hardly matters — end in soft, spread pads that grip the slick glass the way a gecko grips, but wet. It moves a millimeter, pauses, moves a millimeter. Where it has been, the stone is a fraction brighter. It has mown the film.

They are utterly silent. In a place that roars with drips they make no sound at all, and that is how you find them — by the little absence of sound, a patch on a blade where water should be ticking but isn't because a body is holding it.

They do not like to be breathed on. Your breath is warm and it thins the film. When you exhale near one, it freezes — not as a fear response, but literally; its pads lock, its hairs flatten, and it becomes indistinguishable from the rock for minutes. Some of them, the larger ones, will curl their edges very slightly up when disturbed, showing a pale, soft underside lined with cilia that beat water toward a mouth you never see.

There are thousands of them if you count by surface area, because there is so much surface area here — every blade has two sides and every side is grooved. But you will only ever see three or four at once, because the rest are the color of the water on the color of the stone.

They lay — not eggs, exactly — in the hanging beads. That impossible water that swells under an overhang to the size of a fist and shivers but does not fall: if you look closely, many of those beads have a faint cloud in the center, a swirl of specks. A nursery. The bead holds until it is too heavy, then falls straight down into a pavement pan with a soft *plok* you can't distinguish from any other drip, and delivers its young to a new grazing territory. The whole reproductive cycle is timed to gravity and surface tension, not to season. There is no season here.

### The Pan-Dwellers

In the pavement pans, in the millimeters-deep mirror-sheets that collect in the polygons between blades, lives the thing you called a pale amphibian.

It is not an amphibian either, but it is the only thing here with something like a spine. About a hand long when adult, it looks like it was made out of the water itself and then left slightly unfinished. Translucent white-pink, with a dark line down the back that is gut and notochord, with four limbs so delicate you can see the stone through them, and a broad, flat tail it rarely uses. Its head is blunt, its eyes are two smudged dots under skin, almost blind. It does not need sight. The light never changes and distance is unreadable anyway.

What it has instead is exquisite sensitivity to vibration. The pan it lives in is its ear. Every drip that hits the pan, every footstep three blades away, every low moan of wind in a chimney — all of it comes through the sheet-water and into its belly and limbs. It spends most of its life utterly still, splayed flat in the shallowest water, its skin breathing the film, grazing passively on the bacterial mat that tints the pan rust. When your shadow falls across it, it doesn't see the shadow; it feels the sudden absence of the faint pressure of the pearl light, the tiny cooling, and it darts — a flick — under the overhang of a polygon edge, into a crack where it can wedge its flat body and vanish.

They are solitary. You will find one pan in twenty occupied. If two end up in the same pan after a heavy condensation, one will leave, walking with absurd, slow deliberation on those translucent legs, belly barely clear of the water, until it finds an empty mirror.

They call rarely. When they do, the call is that single flat note you heard far off. It is not for mating in the way birds sing. It is a *checking*. One pan-dweller sends a *tok* through the water and the stone — a sound so close to a drip that you excuse it — and if the thicket is too crowded, another answers, and both shift away. Most of the time there is no answer, and the note just multiplies and fades in the labyrinth until even the sender can't tell if it was an echo or a reply.

### The Chimney-Singers

Inside the hollow spires — the broken chimneys wide enough to put your arm down — lives the thing you never see, only hear occasionally and misattribute to wind.

It lives vertically. It is elongated, soft-bodied but segmented, the length of your forearm, the width of two fingers, and it hangs head-down in the dark tube, holding the slick inner wall with rings of fine bristles. Its body is the exact color of wet interior basalt, which is to say almost black, and it never emerges. It feeds on what falls down the tube: grazers that lose their grip, pan-dwellers washed in during a particularly heavy weep, the constant rain of organic flecks in the drip-water. It is a patient filter.

And it sings — if you can call it singing. It does not have lungs. It has a membrane stretched across its lower end that it can tighten and loosen, and when wind moves through the thicket and pressure changes inside the chimney, the membrane thrums. Most of the time it is passive, a low hum you think is the wind. But sometimes, especially when the air cools further toward evening — though evening never comes visually — many of them will tighten at once and the valley fills with a low, mournful chord that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere. It is not communication, or not only communication. It is the sound of them testing the air, tasting how fast the water is condensing today.

They are the longest-lived things here. A grazer might live weeks before it falls and becomes food. A pan-dweller, a year or two. A chimney-singer can hang in the same tube for a decade, growing a millimeter at a time, slowly widening its chimney with faint acidic secretions, never needing to move.

---

And then there are the humans. Or there were.

They do not live here now. They *cannot* live here, not in the way living means staying. There is nothing to burn, nothing to eat that would not take more energy to scrape than it gives, no dry place to sleep, no way to tell time except cold. The low walls and cairns and doorways to nothing are not homes. They are negotiations.

Whoever built them understood, after a day or two of being lost in sameness, that the only way to be in this place without going mad is to make a mark that the water will not immediately erase. To snap a blade — which is hard, it fights you, it wants to stay sharp — and stack it. To know: I was here, this was my line, this direction meant something to me. The inhabitants ignore those marks completely, except insofar as a grazer will eventually graze the film off them and a singer's tone will change slightly because a new wall alters the wind. The valley does not need them and does not erase them quickly because there is no soft erosion, only steady weeping.

If you stay, you become for a little while part of the same occupancy: another large, warm, clumsy grazer that moves too fast, makes too much noise, and dries the film where it touches. The pan-dwellers will flee you, the grazers will freeze, the singers will go quiet for a few minutes after you pass. Then, when you leave or sit still long enough, the dripping resumes its exact previous pattern, as if you had never been there at all.

That is how you know who lives here. Not by seeing them all at once, but by realizing that every surface is already taken, every drip already counted, every hollow already occupied by something that learned long before you arrived how to be flat enough, slow enough, wet enough, and quiet enough to live in a forest made of glass where water never gathers and light never moves.
