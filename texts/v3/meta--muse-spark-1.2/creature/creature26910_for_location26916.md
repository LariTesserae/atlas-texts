They live there the way the pith lives inside the stem — not on it, not in spite of it. You have to learn to see them.

At first, from the rim, you think no one does. 

The hanging sea looks too clean, too plant-choked to be inhabited. A flash of movement between two trunks resolves into a carp. Another into a sheet of bladder-weed turning in the current. The lights that prickle on in the green dusk at noon could be bioluminescence. The domes among the leaves could be galls, or fruit. The bridges could be roots that happened to meet.

Then one of the galls opens.

And you understand that you have been looking directly at people for several minutes without recognizing them as people.

They are still human. Mostly.

Give them a few more generations in that cool, breathable water and they won't be, but for now if one stood beside you on the dry stone below the wall you would know them for kin, the way you know a cousin from a colder valley. It's the proportions that are off, and the tempo.

They are lean in a way that is not thin. Long. Drawn out by the water and by never needing to fight gravity. The children especially look stretched, their limbs a little too long for their torsos, their fingers a little too long for their hands. Adults are taller than you expect from a mountain hamlet, but you never see them at full height — no one ever fully extends. They move folded, crouched, gliding. Even when they are still, their hair is moving.

Their hair is still hair. But it has learned to live drowned. It doesn't clump. It floats in dark plumes around their heads, fine and unslick, full of stray particulate. The old ones have it down to their waists and green at the tips where micro-algae have colonized it without permission. They do not cut it. They tuck it. The young ones are cropped short with bone knives, practical for slipping between stems.

Skin: pale first, then not pale at all. In the white-gold top light they look bloodless, almost translucent, the blue of veins visible at the temple and forearm. A few fathoms down where most of them actually live, that pallor greens. Not metaphorically. Symbionts — the same film that coats the great leaves — have taken up residence in the outer layer of their skin, a faint mottling of olive and lichen-grey across shoulders and backs and shins, places the sun reaches through leaf-filter. It isn't fur. It isn't scales. If you touch an elder's forearm underwater you feel skin, cool and slightly tacky, like a leaf's underside. They photosynthesize a little. Not enough to live on. Enough to make the long, cool fasts of late winter endurable. Enough that when they are ill they will go and press their backs to a sunward trunk and just hang there for an afternoon, breathing slow.

Breathing is the other tell.

They breathe water now. Not exclusively — they still have the old lungs, and when they breach among the courtyard lily crowns to check the tension-wall or to speak in air to the mountain birds, they cough once, hard, and take air with a gasp that sounds too loud. But underwater their chests move differently. Along the lower ribs, under the mottling, there is a ruff — three frilled slits on each side, the color of inside of a gill, that flutter open when they are exerting and lie flat and almost invisible when they are at rest. The children are born with them sealed. For the first year they are held at the surface in slings of living fiber that keep their faces in the cool air collected under a crown-leaf, like a diving bell made of plant. Then one cold morning the seams open and the infant screams without sound and then breathes, and doesn't stop. From then on air is a visit.

Eyes: large. Not huge, not grotesque. Just a fraction wider, the iris a dark hazel that looks black in the blue dusk. They have a second lid, clear, that they can draw across when they work close to the pith and the sap drifts. They see well in the slow light columns. They squint in direct sun. At noon they half-close their eyes and just watch the dust motes turn.

Hands: this is how you know them for people even before you see a face. Hands wide as spades, webbed to the second knuckle with translucent skin that tears easily and heals easily, callused at the palm from coaxing wood. The nails are thick and dark and used as tools. They can tie a knot in cordage with one hand while the other holds a shoot steady against the current. They are never still. Even when a person is hanging at rest, hands are fidgeting with a loose thread of root, plucking a snail, soothing a leaf.

What they wear, they mostly grow. Cold water makes nakedness impractical even if modesty weren't a thought — you lose heat quickly if you move quickly, and cloth helps, a little. But woven cloth rots in permanent submersion. So they wear what doesn't rot: capes and sashes of living moss that continue to grow on the shoulders, stitched to the skin with a loop of root-thread that the moss accepts; leggings of overlapping leaf-scale, shed naturally by the great trunks and softened and sewn while still supple; belts from which hang the only truly dead things they carry — a basalt adze, a bone awl, a cordage spool, a net bag for filter-mussels. An elder's cape may be twenty years old and have its own fauna. You can tell a family's history by the epiphytes on their clothing.

They do not live crowded. That's the first culture you have to understand. Thirty, maybe fifty people in the whole basin, though counting is difficult because a few are always out among the outer trunks and a few are always curled inside their domes and not to be disturbed. The hamlet could hold more. It doesn't want to. A new dome is started only when a young pair has tended a shoot for eight or ten years and the plant agrees to arch. Until then they live with parents, or with an aunt, or alone in a hollowed dead stem that will eventually become someone else's pantry. The houses are far enough apart that you have to kick and glide for a while to visit a neighbor — long enough for the water to take the chill off your skin again, long enough to decide what you want to say.

Because sound carries so well, they don't say much, loudly.

They talk in the way water wants you to talk: low, resonant, unhurried. A stone hammer struck against living wood to shape a doorway makes a bell tone that travels through every bridge and trunk. They use that. A pattern of knocks — two slow, one quick — means *come help hold this*. A long hum through the chest, with the gill-frills flared to modulate it, means *carp school coming, close your filter-nets*. Children shriek and the sound stays for seconds, bouncing between leaves, so children are taught early to speak in hums. The old women sing while they stitch leaves. It isn't song the way you think of song. It's a continuous, low note held on an exhale of water, interrupted by clicks of the tongue, that makes the fine root-hairs around them shiver and reach. The plants respond to vibration. The singing is husbandry.

They are slow by any lowland standard and they do not consider this a flaw. The water is cool enough — 50, 54 degrees — that if you thrash you go hypothermic. They learned generations ago to move like the newts do, like the carp do: a slow scull of the webbed feet, a glide, an arm extended to a runner to pull yourself along without kicking at all. Hurrying is not just rude. It is cold and stupid and it tears leaves. So they are patient to a degree that looks like reverence. You will watch a woman spend a whole morning nudging a single runner a half-inch to the left, tying it, waiting for the current to show her if it holds, untying it, nudging it back. She is not bored. The plant is not bored. Time here is measured in handbreadths of growth per year.

Their work is almost all tending. They farm the water column itself. They keep the outer ring of crown-leaves clean of parasite vine, because that ring is what holds the wall up. Every household takes a week of wall-duty in rotation, swimming the perimeter with soft scrapers of bone, humming low to keep the leaves transpiring in sync. If they fail, the wall sweats, shivers, slumps an inch. They notice. They fix it.

They harvest without cutting. They pluck the air-bladders that overcrowd and replant them; they milk sap from trunks without girdling; they gather the anemone-like filterers that bloom on every stalk, a staple like mussels, and the glassfish that shelter in them. They do not hunt the blind newts — the newts are too large, too slow, too aware, and besides they graze the leaves clean. They do, once or twice a year, take one of the soot-black carp when the school is large, with a bone harpoon thrown at such close range it is almost an embrace. The whole hamlet eats for days, and the bones become awls and needles.

They have no chief. They have memory. The oldest person — now a woman whose hair is a green cloud and whose gill-ruffs have gone pale and stiff — remembers where the dead stems are hollow and safe to sleep in during a bad swell, remembers which bridge will hold your weight and which will hold and then sigh and give an hour later. When she dies they will not bury her. They will wedge her gently into the crotch of a young column that needs feeding, wrap her in living moss, and let the fungi and the eels and the snails do what they do. In a year that spot will be the strongest joint in that tree. Nothing dead is wasted is not a proverb. It is plumbing.

At night — though night never fully comes, only that deep blue dusk — they gather not in a square but in a *clearing* of water between four great trunks where the currents eddy and hold warmness a fraction longer. They hang there, suspended at different depths, some inverted, feet hooked over runners, capes billowing, and they talk or they don't. The young learn by watching hands. Light from chemo-luminescent snails stipples their faces. From above, through the hanging sea's surface, they would look like lanterns themselves, scattered constellations in green glass, small enough to hold in your eye at once.

If you slip over the rim and into the silk of the wall, they will not startle. An eye will find you, nictitate, and a hand will extend — not to pull you, but to offer you a runner to steady yourself while you learn the cold, while your lungs learn the second way of breathing, while you learn to move slowly enough to belong.

They will make room. Sparsely, deliberately, with a silence between houses left empty on purpose.
