They are hard to see at first.

That is the first thing about them. You stand on a roof and look out over the terraces going on until the air itself blurs them, and you think at first that the city is empty. That it is an empty honeycomb that water moves through. Then your eye learns the scale, and the mottling resolves.

A figure moving two terraces down. Another, still, standing on a wall. A third carrying something white across a courtyard. They wear the color of the stone they cut, and they move at the speed the stone allows, so they do not draw the eye the way motion should.

When you do see them closer, you understand the camouflage is not intentional. It is just what living here makes.

They are short. Not dwarfed, not stunted - compact. A low center of gravity. The men and the women are nearly the same height; you tell them apart at distance more by the cut of their wool than by stature. Broad through the chest and the back, with a depth to the ribs that looks almost barrelled under their layered sweaters. It comes from the air. Children born up here have to learn to breathe a thinner draft, and their bodies answer by making more room for it. Even at rest you can see it - the slow, deep rise and fall, the faint flare of the nostrils in cold morning air, breath pluming and gone. They do not pant. They do not hurry uphill. If they need to go faster they just lean forward a little more and keep the same rhythm.

Their skin is the color of weathered limestone where it is covered, and darker where it isn't - hands, faces, necks burned and chapped by the bright, cool sun that you can stare too long at without feeling heat. Their hands give them away more than anything else. The hands are large. Too large for their frames, you think at first, until you watch one work. Broad palms, thick fingers, nails cut short and always with a black crescent of stone dust under them that never really washes out because the water is so soft it doesn't take it. Callus on callus. The pads of the fingers are smooth as river stones. A child of eight has the grip of someone who has spent her life holding things that do not want to be held - wet basalt blocks, slick with seep.

Their faces are broad and flat, the cheekbones wide, the eyes set a little deeper than you'd expect, and pale. Every eye is some variant of grey - grey-blue, grey-green, lichen-grey - iris colors that don't compete with the light. They have developed the habit, or the reflex, of squinting not against glare but to measure. They look at a wall the way you or I might look at a face, taking in its whole cast and then focusing to a single joint, a single stone that has settled a finger's width lower than its neighbors. Their hair is dark, almost black when wet, which it often is, and kept cut short or bound tight under felt caps. It straightens and coarsens in this climate. Beards, where kept, are short and patched with grey early.

They are not loud people. You expect density to make noise, but their language has been thinned by the same wind that thins the air. It is low and consonantal, with a lot of breath and tongue-against-teeth sounds that carry between stone walls without echoing. They speak rarely above the volume of the trickle nearest them. A mother calls a child not with a shout but with a particular two-note whistle that carries better than a word and stops exactly when it is answered. When two people talk on a terrace they stand closer than you would, shoulders almost touching, so they don't have to raise their voices over the wind. Much of what they say is not said at all. A tap on a stone means *hold*. A flat palm means *water here*. An open hand tipped over a cistern means *it's full, come bail*.

They live in families that are not families the way lowlanders mean it. A house is a courtyard cut down into the plateau and the rooms around it, and the people who belong to that courtyard are whoever was born in it, married into it, was taken in when their own courtyard grew too crowded, or decided to stay after helping mend a wall through a winter. Five to twelve, usually, of all ages. The old are not set apart. You will see a very old person - seventy, eighty, a hundred, it's hard to tell because the wind ages everyone early and then stops aging them - sitting on a low stool in the sunniest corner of the courtyard with a lap full of wool or a pile of chipped slate flakes, watching. Always watching. Their eyes are the palest. They do not move much because moving costs air, but when they do move, everyone makes way. Not out of deference exactly. Out of physics. An old body knows where water will go next spring by where it went for the last sixty springs.

Children are everywhere underfoot and yet oddly careful. They are born quiet. They don't wail the way lowland infants do, or not for long - the air punishes that. They learn fast that crying empties you. So they fuss, and then they breathe. By the time they can walk they are already tasked - not cruelly, just continuously - with small, necessary things: carrying a gourd of seep water from the eye in the courtyard floor to the cistern, picking the cotton-grass heads before they seed into the drains, fitting small flat stones into the gaps in a low wall where an adult's hand won't fit. They play too, but their play is the work mimeticked. They build tiny terraces in marshy corners and flood them and watch how the water finds its way. They stack slate until it falls and stack it again.

There is no one in charge of the whole. The city is too big and too level for that. There are only the keepers of portions. The Water-Keepers are not priests, though strangers think they are - they are the people with the best memory for slope. They walk their quarter after every rain with a notched stick, measuring how deep the runnels are, where a cistern is silting, where a marsh common needs its sedge cut so it doesn't dam and flood a lower tier. When they speak, others listen because they have been right before about where the water will decide to go. The Stone-Keepers are similar - masons whose hands can feel a block's grain without looking. They settle disputes not by law but by fit: if two families quarrel over a wall line, the Stone-Keeper comes and looks at the bedding plane and says *the stone wants to break here*, and that is where the wall will go.

Their faith, if you can call it that, is entirely material. They don't pray to sky or storm, because they live inside the weather. They venerate patience. A well-cut block that has sat level for three generations is beautiful to them the way a song is beautiful to others. They leave offerings not of food but of attention: when a weep spring that has watered a courtyard for a lifetime slows, they will sit with it, clear its throat of moss with their fingers, wait. When a roof moss pillow grows thick enough to hold water like a second cistern, they do not scrape it clean; they trim a channel through it and let it stay.

They eat what the plateau can spare, which is not much and is enough because they don't need much. A sour, flat bread made from a hardy, low grain that grows in the drained marsh strips. Cress and bitter sedge shoots from the runnels. The milk and occasionally the meat of those long-legged, alpaca-like grazers that they keep in small, moving commons - never fenced, just walled low enough the animals could jump if they wanted to, but they don't, because the people move with them. Pikas, trapped with quick hands in the wall joints. Everything is boiled or baked on courtyard hearth stones that hold heat for hours. They eat together, always, from a common pot, with slate spoons, and they eat slowly, because eating fast is breathing fast.

At night they contract. Each courtyard closes its leather door, pulls its felt across the narrow windows, feeds its small fire. The whole vast city, which by day looked like one continuous organism, by night becomes a field of separate, warm cells. You can hear it then if you are up walking - a low polyphony of murmurs, a cough, a child asking a question, a soft laugh that is cut short so as not to waste heat. The light from each fire reflects in the cistern at the courtyard's center, so from above the plateau looks as if it has briefly remembered a different sky.

They are not melancholy. They are not austere as a penance. They are simply fitted. If you came from below and tried to live here, you would find them maddeningly slow, maddeningly quiet, maddeningly uninterested in anything you could make quickly or loudly. You would be out of breath in an hour and shouting to be heard and aching from the cold that they seem not to notice. And after a few weeks, if you stayed and learned to breathe deeper and speak lower and carry a stone with both hands instead of one, you would notice that your own hands had begun to smooth at the pads, your own eyes had begun to lose their lowland color, and that when the afternoon cloud formed directly overhead and the rain came down straight and thorough for its hour, you stepped under a lintel with the others and watched without comment as the whole city steamed, and you felt, for the first time, that you were not waiting out the weather but participating in it.

That is what lives here. Not a different species. A people who have been edited by water and stone until they are the soft, living part of the same system - the part that moves, fits, remembers, and bails.
