They do not live *on* it.

That is the first mistake your eyes make.

From a distance — from the height where the air finally turns blue again — you think the plateau is covered in something. A fur, a moss, a stain of life spilled over young rock. You think: *vegetation, with animals moving through it.*

Then you get low, until the glare hurts, until the cold makes your teeth ache even through a mask, and you realize there is no *through*. There is no vegetation *and* animals. There is only the thing itself, and it is both.

It does not stand up. Nothing here stands up. To stand up is to be scoured by light and wind and to lose heat you cannot afford. So whatever lives here has learned to lie down, to spread, to interlock.

Let it resolve slowly.

First — the stillness that is not still.

You crouch, and what you took for lichen — that black-green, rubbery rosette, tight as a fist — *breathes*. Not the fast breath of a lowland mammal, but a breath you can barely see. Once every thirty or forty seconds the rosette shudders and opens a fraction, a hairline seam appearing down its middle, exhaling a puff of steam that freezes before it rises a foot. Then it closes. Around you, millions of them do it. Not in unison at first. Then, as you listen, you realize they *are* falling into rhythm, like a room full of sleeping people whose breathing slowly synchronizes. *Hhhh... hhhh... hhhh...*

Those are not plants. They are mouths. Or lungs. Or both. Each rosette is an animal the size of your palm, leathery and low, anchored to the rock by hair-fine roots that are not roots but cold-welded feet. They do not photosynthesize so much as they *warm*. Their dark skins are solar panels, black to drink that pitiless, buzzing white light. Underneath, translucent bladders pulse with brine that never freezes, antifreeze blood laced with iron and copper. You can see it when the light hits one at an angle - a deep amber glow inside. They are called — if you need a word — the Shutters. They carpet ninety percent of the plateau and their only work is to make heat and to make soil. They chew the wind-blown dust, the quartz glitter, with acid mouths on their undersides, and turn sawtooth basalt into a few grains of warm black humus a year. Slow work. Glacier work.

And moving *through* them - no, *over* them, *as* them -

The Conveyors.

You didn't see them at first because they are the exact jade-pale of the branching coral-plant. But the coral-plant is not a plant. Watch one branch. It twitches. Then the whole thicket shifts an inch to the left.

They are millipedes if millipedes were grown to the scale of industrial equipment. Six, ten, fifteen feet long, segmented, each segment a dull, frosted plate of chitin that is half mineral, half grown. Their backs are crusted with the very Shutters they farm, a living lichen on a living back. They have no eyes that you can see — light here is too violent for eyes. They have pits that sense heat and vibration. And they have one purpose: they are belts. Endless, slow belts.

A seam cracks somewhere — you hear that deep sigh as geothermal heat finds a weak place in the young, porous rock and boils a spring into existence for an hour. Black water hisses and steams and cuts a fresh gully, milky with rock flour. Instantly, for hundreds of meters around, the plateau erupts. The Conveyors, who seemed to be dozing, all turn. Inch by inch, a migration at the speed of a clock's hour hand, they converge on the wound. They lower their heads — shovel-heads, flat and hot — and drink the mineral water and scoop the fresh silt, the unweathered basalt that has never seen the sun before. Then they turn again and haul it, segment by segment, back out into the dry cold, excreting it in a warm, fine paste behind them as they go. They are laying down new ground. The Shutters close in behind them and root into the paste. It is a piling, a paving. The plateau is being tiled, slowly, by its own animals.

And standing in the paste, shoulder to shoulder until you cannot see ground between them, are the Digesters.

These are what you thought were grazers. They are. But there are no predators here. There is no need to run, and no energy to waste on running.

Each one is low, humped, barrel-chested, about the size of a chest freezer tipped on its side. Four stumpy legs, not for walking so much as for bracing. Their skin is the most unsettling thing — translucent, like thin wax or frosted glass, stretched tight over a body that is mostly stomach. Inside, you can see the light. A dull, chemical amber, shifting and churning. They are full of bacteria that no lowland lab has ever cultured, lithovores that eat rock and exhale sugar. The Digesters stand and ferment. They chew Shutters, they chew Conveyor paste, they chew each other's shed plates. They stand flank to flank, breathing out a shared cloud that hangs only two meters high — a man-made weather system, a fog of steam, spores, and stone dust that never quite clears. It crackles. In this thin, dry air, the fur on their backs — a matted, mineral wool — builds static until the whole herd snaps and pops. You can hear it from a kilometer away, a faint frying.

Every few minutes the charge must go somewhere, so it does. The branching coral — which is not coral, but the nervous system — takes it.

The Towers. They look like brittle jade coral, branching fractal arms reaching up a foot, two feet, no higher. They are the only thing here that *wants* to be tall. Because they are lightning rods. They are dead, glassy, and full of brine, and they wait. Charge builds in the low fog until — *CRACK* — a white bolt jumps from one Tower to another, ten meters, fifty meters, with a whip-snap so loud in the thin air it seems to come from inside your skull. For an instant the whole plain is X-rayed in negative. You smell ozone and hot iron. The Towers shiver and glow faintly for seconds after. This is not weather. This is circulation. The discharge cooks spores, fixes nitrogen, shatters stone dust into nutrients. The plateau electroplates itself.

And the Boilers. The domes you thought were fungi the size of houses.

They are. And they are not. Walk toward one — your boots now sinking into moss that is actually a mat of interwoven Shutter-backs and woolly Digester hair and you realize the ground gives slightly, warmly — and you see it steam. Not gently. Rhythmically. *Chuff... chuff... chuff...* A dome fifteen feet across, pale, fibrous, veined like a lung pulled outside the body. It inhales cold, exhales scalding damp. Around its base the ice has melted into a black moat where orange algae blooms in a violent ring. This is the heart. The Boiler roots down, down through the porous, bone-warm basalt, down to where the planet is still hot from the thrust that lifted this whole continent yesterday in geologic time. It draws that heat up and pumps it out in pulses. Each pulse is a day of spring. Every organism within a hundred meters leans toward it, grows a millimeter, breeds, dies a little faster.

This is the sound you heard from orbit. Not wind. The *chuff* of ten million Boilers, the chewing of a billion Conveyors, the sigh of Shutters opening, the endless paper-rustle of the Moth-Fog — those hand-sized, translucent moths that are not moths but flying gonads, spore carriers, rising and settling, rising and settling in a cloud so dense it is weather — and the whip-crack of the Towers. A factory floor with no foreman, no walls, no product except more factory.

It is slow. 0.8 slow, the speed of lichen and rust. A Conveyor will take a week to cross what you can walk in a minute. A Digester will stand in one place for a season, until the rock beneath it is digested and it must shuffle six inches forward. If one falls — topples like a silo, legs stiff — there is no rot. Within minutes its neighbors have unzipped it. Plates are pried off and worn. The glowing gut is drunk. The wool is pulled away and felted into the mat. Nothing is wasted because waste is heat and heat is life at -40.

And it is weird, in the way deep cold is weird. Light pools under the ice and stays there, glowing faintly at midnight when the sun is gone. Sound arrives too late, as if it had to walk around something invisible. If you stand still long enough — and you will, because the cold makes you stupid and the brightness makes you snow-blind — the Shutters will find the salt on your boot seams. Tiny white grubs, rivet-grubs, featureless as grains of rice but warm to the touch, will test you. Not bite. *Assess.* Are you mineral? Are you digestible? Are you warm enough to be a Boiler?

There is no intelligence here as you understand it. No leader, no pack, no song. The intelligence is the density itself. 2.9 overwhelming — there is no space between bodies where a thought like "I" could take root. The plateau thinks the way a loom thinks, or a liver thinks: by doing its chemistry so completely, so redundantly, that the doing becomes a kind of mind. A mind whose only thought is: *more of this. More surface. More heat. More skin over sharp rock.*

It has been thinking that thought since the rock was thrust up, fresh and shattered and sterile. It will keep thinking it until the rock is worn smooth, which will be a few million years from now. Until then, under a sun too small and too white, on a continent so high the sky is no longer sky but a hard white circle, the carpet thrums and crackles and steams, laying itself down inch by inch, turning mountain into meat.

You are not welcome, but you are not rejected either. You are simply a brief pocket of damp in the dry air, a temporary warm spring. They will use you while you steam, and then they will cover you.
