You don't see them at first because you're looking for something that moves the way an animal moves.

You look for darting. For scurrying. For a shape that cuts against the green and the rust. And there is nothing that does that here. So at first you think: nothing lives here. Only the plants live here, and the machines live here, and the water lives here. 

Then you realize you're miscounting what counts as living, and what counts as moving.

They are sparse, like the buildings. That's the first rule of the basin. Everything here takes up exactly as much room as it needs and leaves a respectful distance. One per territory. One per works.

You notice them by their absences first.

A tool left on a rail that shouldn't still be warm in this air, but is - the heat of a palm still in the wooden handle, a perfect, damp print evaporating even as you touch it. A cup of tea on the catwalk above the pumping house, filmed over with condensation, with a faint lip-mark in the film where something sipped and set it down again. A trellis of rebar where a vine has been retied with soft copper wire, the twist fresh and bright against the verdigris. A patch of moss on the weeping limestone wall that has been brushed flat, as if something large rested its back there.

You look for them between the thuds.

*Thud.* 
*Thud.*
*Thud.*

And in the two and a half seconds of dripping quiet between each piston stroke, you hear it - not a footstep, but a displacement. Water moving around a volume that wasn't there a moment before. A soft *take* as a webbed foot lifts from saturated grating.

They are the size of people, because they were people. They still are, in the way the brick that has been replaced by root is still brick.

You see one finally because you stop trying to see it against the green, and see it *as* the green.

She - it - is standing perfectly still in the gap between the processing shed and the power house, in what you thought was just a particularly dense stand of elephant ear and water-weed. The light is so diffuse that she casts no shadow, and her skin casts no shadow either. That is how she hides.

She is tall and has become narrow. Not thin, narrowed - as if the heat and the weight of the air had pressed her over years until her shoulders learned to take up less space. Her skin is the color of the underside of a leaf, a bloodless, wet white with green showing through it, not like a tattoo but like light through water. You can see the veins too clearly. They are dark and carry something slower than blood. Her hair is gone. Hair molds here. Instead her scalp, her shoulders, the backs of her forearms are thatched with small, living things - liverworts, a moss that beads constantly with warm condensation, a few tiny ferns that have rooted in the fine seams of her skin and tremble when she breathes. She hasn't cultivated them. They have simply negotiated a place, the way the vines negotiated the brick.

Her eyes are what tell you she never leaves the bowl. They are huge, lidless in the way a fish's are lidless, filmed with a second translucent membrane that you see blink sideways when the transformer vents and the hiss rips the air. The orange work light catches them and they do not reflect. They absorb. They are adapted to this permanent amber-grey dusk, to seeing movement in steam. If you led her up to the rim, to the hard white sky, she would be blind.

She does not have ears as you understand them. The outer ear has smoothed, sealed over into a pale cup of cartilage and skin, like a closed eyelid. That's why the loudness here is weather to her. She doesn't hear the thuds so much as she *is* thudded. You can see it when the pump goes - her whole chest and throat shudders in sympathy, a ripple through the water-skin. She feels the pressure equalize under the grating through the soles of her feet, which are wide, splayed, and soft as frog-feet, with toes that spread to grip the iron. That's how she knows where to stand when the mat breathes.

She moves when you blink. Or rather, she moves so slowly and so continuously that you only register that she has moved at all after she has done it. While you were listening to the drip, her arm - long, with an extra, subtle joint in the forearm that lets it fold like a stalk - has extended six inches to retie a vine. While you were watching the steam, her head has turned a fraction to watch you back with those dusk-eyes. There is no hurry in the basin. Hurry makes heat, and heat is already more than the body can give away. So they have learned to grow toward a task rather than walk to it.

She tends. That is the word the town would use if the town still used words out loud. Vocal cords don't work well when the air is this wet and this loud. Instead she taps.

When she needs the mill wheel to keep turning, she doesn't oil it - she clears the slurry of leaves from its intake with those long arms. When she needs the transformer to keep humming half-submerged, she pulls the water-weed away from its vents so it doesn't choke. When she needs the pump to keep thudding, she sometimes just stands on the right part of the grating and lets her weight help the mat equalize. It's not engineering. It's husbandry. The machines are hot animals that need cool water, the plants are cool animals that need hot steam, and she moves between them keeping the circle unbroken.

That's why there are no other animals. Nothing else can compete with something that has already made the whole bowl its body. The air is too full of copper and ozone from the power house, the water too full of iron and tannin from the roots. Insects land and don't lay. Birds pass over the rim, look down into the cloud that lives in the bowl, and see no reflected sky to navigate by, only amber-grey. Spores land and are eaten. The moss on her shoulders exhales something faintly fungicidal, a soft camphor smell under the rust. The basin has been claimed, not by territory but by saturation.

There is one per building, though you never see them together. Another is asleep right now, you realize, in the shallow warm sheet under the saw-tooth mill - not lying down, but *rooted* down, with its legs folded under itself in the water and its back fronded open, the big philodendron leaves that grow from its spine spread wide to catch the diffuse light and the condensation that falls from the roof. It photosynthesizes while it sleeps. That's why the leaves in that building are such a deep, almost black green. They are not all plant.

If you stay long enough on the grating, your shirt sticking to you, your own skin beginning to bead and not evaporate, she will decide you are not a predator - nothing here knows what a predator is anymore - and she will go back to tending. You will see her open her mouth, and inside is not pink but the same wet green-white, and a tongue that is broad and flat like a lily pad, meant for catching condensation as it falls. She catches a bead that formed on the underside of a leaf above her, perfectly vertical, perfectly timed. She doesn't swallow so much as absorb.

You will hear her breathe, finally, and understand that the marsh breathing you heard earlier wasn't a metaphor. It was her, and the one under the mill, and the one in the pumphouse, all taking in the hot, heavy air through thatch and skin and leaf at once, and letting it out as steam, as drip, as the faint, endless rain that falls from the underside of everything in the bowl.

She will not run. She will not speak. If you reach out, your hand will come away smelling of hot iron and crushed green, and for a second the water running up the coolest pipe beside you will hesitate, beading, unsure whether to obey gravity or to follow the cooler line of her arm.

That is what lives here. Not a population, but a maintenance. Not inhabitants, but organs. A few sparse, quiet, wet, white-green people who have learned to be as patient and as loud and as still as the machines they stand between, growing so slowly you have to hold your breath to see them move at all.
