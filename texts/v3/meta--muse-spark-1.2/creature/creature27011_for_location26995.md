Who lives here has learned the four rules so thoroughly they've stopped being rules and become bodies.

At first you think nothing lives here. Or rather, that everything does at once and therefore no one thing stands out. That's the trick of a place where every volume is occupied. You have to learn to watch at the speed the place moves.

Then you see movement that isn't wind or water, and you realize the whole tableland is busy with lives that decided, a long time ago, never to hurry again.

### The Small and Innumerable — The Air Itself Is Inhabited

Before anything with a spine, there's the lace-fauna.

The lattice that holds the heat in — that faint moiré you see shivering between mesas — isn't empty. It has become habitat. Things live *on* it the way shorebirds live on the line between sea and sand.

The most common are the thread-skimmers: dragonfly things, but wrong. Their bodies are no longer than your thumb, needle-thin and black, with two pairs of wings that aren't membrane but a lattice of their own — grown, not built. They don't fly so much as *walk* on the invisible threads, skating from node to node without beating their wings at all, only spreading them to catch the hot wind. Their larvae are aquatic but not in water: they live in the beads of condensation that perpetually cling to the lattice, each droplet a whole pond, spinning slowly in mid-air until it grows too heavy and slides down a vine.

Under them, in the air, the mist-feeders. At dusk when the condensation trees exhale, clouds of moths the size of dinner plates rise out of the marsh pans. Their wings are furred to collect water, not shed it — they fly heavy, saturated, their wingbeats low and audible like pages turning. They don't have mouths in any way you'd recognize; they have a broad, feathery proboscis that combs water out of the air and strains algae and spores from it. They live for a single exhalation, mate in it, lay eggs on the underside of the lattice, and fall by midnight into the pools where the fish-walkers eat them.

And under *them*, always, the chorus. Not a species but a pressure: beetles with polished, domed backs that carry living gardens of moss and lichen on them, so each one looks like a walking stone. Crickets that have learned to use the hum of the black glass reeds as a carrier wave, singing not over it but *through* it. Everything small here drinks constantly. Their cuticles are not waxy to keep water out but hydrophilic to keep it close.

### The Ones Who Drink The Cliff

On the vertical part of the world — the weeping faces, the undercut waists of the towers, the sheets of glassy water running down black basalt — lives the second country.

They are the clingers. You don't notice them until you put your hand on the warm stone and feel it move under the film.

There are salamanders here that never knew tadpole-hood. Glossy, lungless, the color of wet rock when still and of raw malachite when they move, they live their whole lives on vertical surfaces, grazing the bacterial blooms of orange and viridian that paint the seep. Their toes are not sticky pads but fringed, hydrophilic fans that exploit surface tension — they walk *under* the water sheet, inside it, upside down. When the afternoon rain hammers, they press flat and let it pass over them like a tide.

Above them, nesting in the widened joints where water has patiently pried the stone apart, are the veil-nesters — things that occupy the bird niche but are not birds. Think swift-like, but with bat-membranes stretched between elongated fingers, and membranes that never dry. They are ash-white and slate, the exact color of steam, and they build no nests. They *weave* the lattice itself. They pull the nanoscale threads with their beaks, tangle in floating seeds and moss, and make a damp, humming cup that shivers. Their chicks hang in the cup with their eyes closed for months, breathing through gills as much as lungs, until the cup dries enough to let them fall.

They are quiet. Their call is not a song but a low, hollow knocking against the rock, amplified by the water film.

### The Ones Who Keep the Marsh

On the flats, where the soil sighs water around your boot, the grazers move.

You've seen their lines in the eight-foot grass. They are not capybaras, but that is the closest word your lowland brain has: barrel-bodied, high-backed, short-legged mammals with dense, oily coats that never seem to get sodden. Their hair is hollow and grows in whorls that channel water away from their noses and eyes. They move in herds of six or twelve, single file, making permanent tunnels through the grass columns. They are always damp and always chewing.

They are not ruminants. They are *fermenters* of the whole marsh — they eat the tough mat-root and the lilies big enough to sit in, and ferment them in a gut so large their backs are slightly hunched under it. They don't drink; they filter. When they pause at a mirror-shallow pool, they lower their wide muzzles and pump water through baleen-like plates in their mouths, straining out duckweed, newt larvae, and the floating eggs of the lattice-skimmers. Then they exhale, and the water sighs back.

They have no predators that chase. Nothing here chases. Their predator is the sinkhole — the sudden dark dissolve where the peat gives way. And the patient one:

In every third or fourth pool lies the waiting. A flat, mottled amphibian the length of your forearm at rest, that can stretch to three times that when it strikes. It looks exactly like a submerged mat of peat and lily root until one of the grazers' young steps too close to drink. It doesn't leap. It *extends* — its jaw unhinges on a cartilaginous sling and its whole forepart telescopes forward in a rush of water so fast the pool seems to inhale. Then it is a mat again, slowly swallowing. You can sit for an hour and never see it move otherwise. Its eyes are on stalks that double as snorkels.

Alongside them, the walkers — the black fish with limb-fins. At midday they flop out of the pools en masse and haul themselves across the saturated moss on warty, muscular fins, grazing on insects like a herd of miniature cattle, croaking to each other through their skin. They can breathe air as long as their skin stays wet, which is always.

### The Ones in the Crown

The forest on the mesa crowns is where mass gathers and slows to its logical end.

There, the condensation trees — those colonial banyans of translucent tube — are not trees at all but animals, or a single animal, or a city. Each trunk is a bundle of vertical siphons that pull water up from the sponge-soil and spray it from pores at the top as fine mist. At dusk they glow with a chemistry that has nothing to do with Tech and everything to do with attracting what they need: nitrogen, carried to them on the feet of visitors.

Their visitors are the crown-holders. The six-limbed sloths.

You'll hear them before you see them, because a branch will move that shouldn't. They are large — large enough that if one fell it would shake the peat — but you never see one fall. Their bodies are long and low, with two pairs of forelimbs that are really arms and a pair of hindlimbs that are really legs, all ending in black, hook-like claws that sink into bark and moss and never let go. Their fur is not fur but a dense, hanging garden: moss, fernlets, tiny flowering epiphytes, and the faint glitter of lattice threads they have woven into it for reinforcement and for signaling. They are green and brown and shadow-colored and utterly still for hours.

They move on a different clock. One handhold at a time, a foot per minute. Their metabolism is so slow they barely generate heat; they *borrow* the heat of the air, the stone, the mist. They come down to the mist at dusk and hang under the condensation trees with their slow mouths open, letting the exhalation condense on specialized frills around their faces — broad, leaf-like flaps of skin, veined like a pitcher plant — and funnel into their mouths. They drink the air.

They have huge, forward-facing amber eyes, but they are nearly blind in daylight. They navigate by smell and by vibration through the lattice. Their young cling to their bellies for a year, adding their own moss gardens to their mother's.

They are not alone up there. Things flash between the grass columns that you only catch peripherally — metallic green and ash-white gliders with ribbons for tails, that launch from the condensation trees, spread patagia, and *ride* the lattice threads like tightropes for a hundred meters without flapping. They steal bromeliads.

### The Ones Who Remember

And then there are the ones who made the bench.

You find their signs: the low shrine, the pylon, the perfect white circle humming in the peat. You think at first the makers left.

They didn't. They became *suited*.

They are still here, but you will walk past them twice before you recognize them as people, because they have learned the main lesson of the tableland: to endure here is to become part of the sponge.

They call themselves nothing you would translate cleanly. The Lingerers. The Warm-High Folk. Inheritors.

They are tall — taller than you by a head — but attenuated, as if stretched by thin air and then allowed to settle. Their limbs are long, their chests are deep and barrelled to move thick, wet air. Their skin is not skin as you know it: it is damp, slightly permeable, slate-gray to moss-green, lightly photosynthetic at the shoulders and back where the lattice light catches it. Lithen grows on them in patches, not as infection but as agreement. Their hair is sparse, coarse, and channels water like the grazers'.

Their faces are wide, with broad, flared nostrils and frilled nasal labyrinths that extract water and warm it, and eyes that are lidded with a second, translucent membrane to keep mist out. They have no external ears, only deep, fern-lined hollows that also collect condensation.

They move like the six-limbed sloths — not because they are related, but because anything that moves faster in this heat, in this thin-thick air, wastes itself. They step and pause. Step and breathe. They wear almost nothing — woven reed mantles that are as much garden as garment, planted with living bromeliads and stitched through with faintly luminous lattice thread that they have learned to coax, not command.

They do not build. They tend. You will see one standing motionless for an afternoon beside a seep-spring that has strayed, hands resting on the warm rock, fingertips bleeding a faintly darker shade where their skin has integrated with the nano-lace. They are not repairing it. They are *listening* to see where it wants to go. When a black glass reed field hums out of tune, they will walk its perimeter for days, singing a low, resonant note through their nasal labyrinths until the pitch evens. When a sinkhole opens and drains a marsh pan, they will sit at its edge and slowly, patiently, hand-place moss mats until the sponge seals.

They reproduce rarely. A child every twenty or thirty years. The child is carried on the back, its skin still translucent, its frills not yet grown, drinking mist that the parent exhales onto it.

They remember that this place was *made* — lifted, warmed, stitched together — but the memory is not history to them, it's ecology. The Tech is weather to them, like the rain. They don't worship it and they don't control it. They have grown into its maintenance loop the way vines have grown onto it as trellis.

If you sit on that basalt bench long enough, one will come and sit with you. Not close. A few meters away, on the stone that is warm and damp and has been so for millennia. They will not speak at first. Your lungs will still be working too hard from the climb, your heart too fast. They will wait until your breath slows to the pace of the water trickling everywhere at once, the insect power-line, the amphibian drums, the hum of the lace.

Then they will look at you with those amber, membrane-lidded eyes and exhale, and the air between you will briefly bead.

You will understand, without words, that height here has never meant cold, because someone decided to stay.
