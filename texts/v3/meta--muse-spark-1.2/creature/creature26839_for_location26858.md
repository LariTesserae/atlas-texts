They live by not living the way you think living has to look.

At first you think they are part of the rock. That is the most honest first thought, and you have to let it stay for a while before you correct it. Because the correction comes too fast and makes them smaller.

You think: lichen, but too thick. You think: barnacles left by a sea that dried before history. You think: scales. Shingles. A fossil pavement that somehow kept its color.

Then you get closer and none of those words hold. They were all too busy. Barnacles strain. Scales protect something that will move. Shingles were made to be laid down. These were not made. They grew into place and then stopped trying to be anything else.

What lives on the Roof is one thing wearing many shapes, or many things that have agreed to be one thing. It's hard to tell where one ends because they touch.

The most numerous are the Pavement.

They are the ones you mistook for snow. Low, oval mantles, the size of two open hands side by side, or one hand, or a thumb — there is no single size, only a gradient. Their backs are matte, not shiny. White, dove-grey, salt-yellow, a translucent amber where the sun goes straight through the edge and shows a faint shadow of what is underneath. They are arched like a very shallow dome, with a rim that thins until it almost feathers into the stone, and they overlap. Not randomly. Like a tiling done by something with infinite patience. Edge over edge, in a loose spiral that follows the swell of the rock, so the whole valley between two whale-backs looks thatched. 

If you put your eye six inches away — and the air is so thin and clear you can — you see they breathe. Not with lungs. A seam, almost invisible, runs around the front third of the mantle where two plates meet. Once, every minute or two minutes or three, the seam sighs open a hair's breadth. You see a puff of air, not steam, just a slightly different density, that hangs for a second and is gone. The felted underside is where their truth is: when you very carefully, guiltily, lift the thinnest edge of one — and you feel at once you are being rude — you see not feet, not a mouth, but a pale, dense brush of filaments thinner than hair, white on white, pressed into the basalt itself. They are not standing on the rock. They are drinking it. Etching it with a chemistry that needs no water, taking iron and salt and whatever thin nitrate the high air offers, eating stone with patience that makes erosion look hasty. Where you lift them, the rock underneath is paler, perfectly pitted with the ghost of that brush. When you set the mantle back down, it resettles with a faint, almost inaudible *tick* as it re-seals, and does not hold a grudge.

Between them are the Beads.

You missed them at first because they are a texture, not a creature. In every crack, in every line where two Pavement mantles meet, in the polygon seams of the salt pans, there are millions of them — glossy beads the size of peppercorns or apple seeds, black or deep wine-red, each a perfect dome. They are the young, or the small, or another thing entirely. They don't seem to grow into the Pavement. They just fill the gaps the Pavement leaves, so there is truly no bare stone. If the Pavement is the thatch, the Beads are the mortar.

And then there are the Longbacks and the Fans, and they make you understand the Pavement is not the whole animal.

A Longback lies in the shallowest hollow as if the rock shaped itself around it. A meter, sometimes a meter and a half long, ribbed across its back like a pill-bug magnified to the size of a dog, but flattened. Its chitin is heavier, tea-colored, translucent at the ribs. Down its center is a raised seam that lifts and lowers. Not breathing, exactly. Venting. In the cold, if you watch long enough, a faint dew forms on the inside of that seam and evaporates without ever dripping. It never moves. It has no front. You walk around it and you cannot decide which end is the head because there is no head. It faces both directions and therefore neither. It may be twenty years old. It may be two hundred. The dust that has settled in the grooves between its ribs has been there long enough to be considered part of it.

The Fans are the only vertical thing for kilometers. A thin, dark stalk, no thicker than your little finger, as tall as your forearm, or your knee, rising from between the Pavement with absolute precision — never leaning — topped with a fan of glass. Not glass, but something that wants to be glass: filaments as fine as spun sugar, branching, re-branching, into a flat, feathery oval that faces the sun. It does not track the sun. It does not tremble in the wind because there is no wind to tremble in. The fan is utterly still, as if it were frozen in the act of opening. Light passes through it and scatters, and if you stand to the side you can see the shadow it casts is faintly colored, like light through ice. It is not for display. It is a collector — of light without heat, of the thin, high photons that have traveled through less air. It drinks sun the way the Pavement drinks rock.

None of them move. That is the law that defines them.

They do not graze. They do not hunt. They do not eat the poor wire-shrubs or the slow lichen that live among them like tolerated guests. You can see a shrivelled wire hemisphere with its brittle black stems growing up through a perfect gap left for it, the Pavement mantles curving around it, not touching it. They have no predators because they have no prey. Nothing here has violence in its economy. It is a closed circle so wide and so slow you cannot see where it closes.

They are not dormant. Dormancy is a waiting for a better season. There is no better season here. This *is* the season, has been for longer than any peat bog or ice core can remember. They are not waiting out a dry spell. They have solved dryness. They metabolize at a rate so low you need to measure it in hours per breath, days per growth-ring. Put your palm on a Pavement back and it is not warm like a mammal, not cold like stone. It is a third temperature, the temperature of something alive that has agreed to be almost as cool as its surroundings so it never has to waste heat. You feel the faintest thrumming, not a heart but a very slow peristalsis of fluid under chitin, once, twice a minute. If you stay with your hand there, you start to breathe with it without meaning to.

And because they don't move, you become the disturbance. Your walking is loud in a different way than sound. They know you are there not because you cast a shadow — though you do, sharp-edged in the unheated light — but because you are the only thing that is making the air move quickly. After a few minutes on the Roof you find yourself doing what the people who left the little walls did: you slow down. You place your feet only on the seams between mantles where the stone shows through in thin lines. You don't kick dust. You don't talk above a whisper because a normal voice feels like a tear.

Those people — who lived here, if living is the word.

They were not a civilization. They were hermits, or pilgrims, or exiles who learned the etiquette. They never made a village because a village needs motion. They each made one thing: a three-sided windbreak of basalt slabs with the open side to the east where the sun rises small and precise. Inside, a sleeping hollow cleaned of Pavement, just large enough for a body, with the mantles left intact around it like a frame. A ring of tools left as they were last used: a basalt flake sharp enough to shave lichen, a smooth hammerstone that fits a hand perfectly, a shallow grinding bowl where salt and lichen were crushed together for the trace of brine they hold. A cairn far from any salt pan, so you know someone carried each white polygon a long way, not to mark a trail, but to make one human mark that would not be mistaken for nature. A single copper awl, green-black with age, stuck point-up in a crack — not as a tool, but as a sign that says *I was here and I learned to be still.*

They didn't stay. Or they stayed until they became still enough that they stopped needing to leave a mark. You can't live here the way you live below. You can only visit, and visits are measured by how quietly you can learn to breathe.

If you lie down — and you will, because the cool cradles and the violet-blue sky is so deep you want to fall into it — you find the Pavement leaves a space exactly for you. Between the Longbacks, between the Fans that do not sway to brush your face. The stone is cool through your clothes. The mantles around you tick, once. Salt dust settles on your sleeve and stays. Above you there is nothing flying, nothing calling, because everything that lives on the Roof is already here, shoulder to shell, around you and under you, breathing once together so slowly you have to hold your own breath to feel it.

They are not waiting for you to leave. They are not waiting at all. That is what is so unsettling and so peaceful at once. They have achieved the thing every living thing below is frantic for: enough. Enough light, enough mineral, enough time, enough exactly-as-it-is to never need to change. The Roof does not want to become anything else, and so they don't want to either.

You think at first you are looking at snow, or stone. Then you understand you are looking at a place that decided, a very long time ago, to be covered in a single, patient, living skin — and kept its promise.

And for as long as you lie there, being the only quick, warm, fidgeting animal for thirty kilometers in any direction, you are not an observer. You are the one brief motion they have agreed to allow, before the stillness closes over again.
