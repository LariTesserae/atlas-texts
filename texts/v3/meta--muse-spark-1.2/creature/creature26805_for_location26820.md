You think at first that nothing lives there.

That is the first trick of the room. You see the ferns and you think *plants* and then you see the machines and you think *abandoned* and you put those two together and you decide: this is a place where things *were* put and then left to grow, untended. A greenhouse without a gardener.

You are wrong for a little while, because what lives there doesn't move on your timescale.

Stay.

Let your eyes adjust past the amber bulb. It is not dark, exactly. It is a low light. The kind of light where you stop looking *for* things and start noticing where the stone is a different kind of wet.

The first one is on the far side of the largest block, the one under the pump house. You thought it was a continuation of the moss.

It isn't.

Where the moss is a mattress - dense, spongy, saturated to black-green at its base - this is raised. A low dome, maybe a meter across, maybe more, because its edges feather and thin until you can't tell where it ends and the wet rock begins. It is the color of the ferns but even paler. Not white. The color of condensation itself, of skim milk thinned with water, of wax that has been left in heat. Translucent.

If you crouch - and you will have to crouch, because it does not rise, it *hugs* - you see that it has no front or back. No head. No face to find. It is a mantle. A thick, blunt-edged pancake of tissue draped over the warmest part of the block, where the geothermal breath comes strongest through a seam in the basalt. The stone beneath it is darker, hotter. You can feel it with your palm a foot away.

And it is beaded. Like everything else in the room, it sweats. Perfect, fat droplets stand on its back without falling, catching the amber light. For a long time you think they are just the chamber's condensation collecting on it, the way water collects on your skin. Then one gathers, swells, and *is pulled inward*. A slow dimple forms and the drop vanishes, not rolling off but absorbed. Another place, a seam you thought was a fold puckers and releases a single clear bead that swells and hangs before it grows too heavy and falls with a *plink* onto the rock. It drinks the air. It makes its own rain.

It breathes. But you have to time it.

Put your hand near it, not on it. The air just above it shivers. Every ninety seconds, maybe every two minutes, the whole dome lifts a fraction - a millimeter, two - and settles. Not a lung. A slow, whole-body sigh. When it lifts, you glimpse underneath: not feet, not a belly, but a pale, frilled underside, layered like wet paper, pressed so tightly to the stone that it has taken the exact texture of the basalt, every pit and crystal. It is not sitting on the rock. It is sealed to it. Where the frills meet the stone a thin, dark film glistens - the grit, the mineral seep, faintly discolored as if being digested.

That is how it eats. It doesn't lean toward the mouth of the sink. It doesn't lean toward the amber safety bulb. When the light moved off the upper ledges in the late afternoon, the ferns did not turn. This doesn't either. It is thermotropic. Put warmth anywhere and hours later you will find its edge has crept that way, a tide of pale flesh flowing over an inch of new stone, leaving behind a faint, clean print where it had been that darkens and then fades as condensation reclaims it. Its upper surface is almost entirely without pigment because it doesn't need it. The heat is food, the dissolved iron and sulfur in the wall-sweat is food. The light is incidental. If you shine your flashlight directly on it, it does nothing at all.

There are not many. That is the second thing you learn when you stop looking for movement. There is one here on the hot block under the pump house. There is another draped over the cistern's warm pipe where the water ticks, its body actually lapped around the rusted iron, incorporating it - a lace of orange corrosion veins visible *inside* its translucent edge, like moss but mineral, as if it has learned to use the rust for structure. A third is up on the unfinished catwalk, which you thought was empty until you realized the shadow underneath the grating is too thick, too even. It lives where the cable thrums with current; the faint 60-cycle heat is enough. They are stationed like the ferns. One per warm place. Five meters of empty sweating rock, then an occupant. Then emptiness again. The room is mostly stone and air even to them.

They do not interact. They have no need to. No call, no hum. When a drop falls from the ceiling and hits one, it just absorbs it. No flinch. The ferns around them are perfect, un-nibbled, because these things do not eat leaves. They eat stone-heat and water and time. Sometimes a fern has rooted *in* one, a single pale frond unfurling directly from its back, using it as ground. Neither seems to mind.

If you are very patient - and the room makes you patient whether you want to be or not, because your shirt is sticking to you and your breath is loud and there is nowhere to go - you will see it move.

Not crawl. Flow. The leading edge thins to translucency, extends a blunt lobe over a new warm seam, and then the rest follows, infinitely slowly. A hand's width in an hour. It does not leave a slime trail. It leaves a temporary dryness, a faint warmth that your palm can feel for a minute after it has passed, before the condensation returns.

How it makes more of itself: near the center of the dome, when the heat is steadiest, a small blister forms under the skin. Pale, then opalescent, then tightening. It swells for days. You won't be there to see it burst, but if you were, you would see it doesn't burst. It simply opens, and a dozen flat, coin-sized replicas - thin as fern spores but heavy, wet - slide out from under the lip and seal themselves to the stone nearby, each one already beading with water. Most will not find a warm enough place. They will thin and vanish in a week, becoming just another damp stain. One might. The spores fall straight down and are never eaten. Same principle.

They have no eyes. They have no need to see you. But they know you are there because you are warm. And wet. For a little while after you crouch beside the one on the big block, its breathing pauses. Not in fear. Just a recalibration. The air around you is cooler where your shadow falls, warmer where your body radiates. If you put your hand flat on the stone a foot away and leave it there, after ten minutes the edge nearest you will have inched - barely, measurably - toward your heat. Not hunting. Just leaning. The way the ferns lean toward warm stone.

You are, for that hour, another hot surface in the room. Another place for water to collect and fall.

You will get up before it reaches you. Your knees ache. The amber bulb keeps burning. The cistern keeps ticking. The chamber keeps sitting at its bathhouse temperature, patient, electrified, paused.

When you climb the rebar ladder back up the sharp, young wall, your skin is prickling with the same sweat that beads on their backs. The dry air at the lip hits you like cold water. Behind you, below you, the four pale domes settle back into their ninety-second sigh, drinking the air, digesting the bloom of rust one crystal at a time, perfectly silent in a silence that has never needed an insect to fill it.
