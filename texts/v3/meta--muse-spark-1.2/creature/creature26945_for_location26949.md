Nothing lives here.

That is the first true thing you learn, and you learn it correctly. For a long time — hours, days, for as long as you can stand to drift over that endless, fitted floor — you will see no sign that the rule is ever broken.

And then you will see that the rule is broken so gently, so slowly, that you mistook it for the rule itself.

You have to stop swimming.

If you keep moving you will never see them, because they move at the speed the place moves. The forest sways a centimeter while you blink. A block settles a millimeter while you breathe. To see what lives here you have to match its clock, and your clock is too fast. So you stop. You find a place where two of those low, endless wall-spines meet at a shallow angle — a corner that hasn't been a corner for ten thousand years — and you hold on to the stone. The kelp around you goes still again after your passage. The cloud of dark silt you raised hangs around you like snow and then, grain by grain, decides to fall. The cold comes back into your hands.

Wait.

The first thing you notice is not a shape. It is a wrongness in pattern.

In a forest that has had no enemy, everything leans. Everything yields to the same month-long drift. For a kilometer in every direction, the whip-stalks and the broad door-sized fronds are all combed the same way, north to slightly east, with the patience of hair in water. 

But there — twenty meters off, in the lee of that wall-spine — a small patch does not lean. A column of stalks stands upright. Not stiff. Upright the way something stands that is holding itself upright.

You tell yourself it is a rock. A trick of holdfasts catching on a higher block. You stare until your eyes want to make movement out of stillness.

Then one of the stalks moves. Not swayed. *Stepped.*

It detaches.

What lives here spent so long becoming part of here that evolution stopped bothering to give it a boundary.

At a distance it is indistinguishable from the forest. At ten meters it is still indistinguishable, because it is wearing the forest. The thing is tall — taller than you by half, and thin in a way that has nothing to do with starvation and everything to do with pressure. In water this heavy, there is no need to be compact. It can afford to be attenuated, unfolded. Its limbs are long beyond any human proportion, not because it needs reach but because distance is cheap when you never hurry.

Its skin, where you can see it between the drapery of living kelp, is the same color as silted basalt. Not gray, not white — the pale, translucent amber-gray of something that has lost all pigment because pigment was a conversation with light and that conversation ended. You can see the cold through it. faint, dark lines underneath — not veins, but seams — where the flesh has taken on the texture of old stone. Barnacle-like growths speckle its shoulders and back, but they are not parasites. They are the same moss-felt algae that covers the pavement, growing *from* it. Holdfasts have rooted in its skin. A broad frond grows from the nape of its neck like a cowl and sways when it sways.

It has no face as you understand a face. The head is a smooth, elongated knot, featureless except for a shallow, vertical cleft where a mouth might be, and above it, two deep, lidded pits that are not eyes. Eyes would be ornament here. What it has instead are shallow, fringed depressions at the temples, as wide as your palm, open to the water — pressure pits, vibration pits. It does not see the forest. It listens to the weight of it through the stone.

Because it never left the floor.

You watch it correct itself.

It was not standing upright to stand upright. It was kneeling. What you took for a cluster of stalks was one of them kneeling in the channel between pavements, its long, spade-like hands — and they are spades, broad, flat, the fingers fused and broadened into trowels of cartilage and callus — pressed to the silt.

There is work there. A block the size of a dwelling has sagged on one corner, just a few centimeters, over the last few centuries. Silt has pushed up into the joint. A holdfast has wedged itself in and pried, as plants will. The kelp around it has grown tangled, matted, choking itself.

The dweller does not hurry. It scoops. A slow, two-handed motion that takes a full minute. The silt billows but it does not seem to care if it blinds itself, because it isn't using sight. It lifts the dark cloud and lets the drift take it. Then it places both spade-hands under the lip of the mat of algae and peels it back, the way you would peel felt from stone, exposing the joint again. The sound is the softest tearing, the papery rustle you heard before and thought was fronds brushing.

Then it leans its whole weight — which must be immense, though in this water weight is a gentle thing — against the block. Not to lift it. Just to nudge it. To keep it true.

It will be here for days doing this. Perhaps weeks. It does not breathe as you breathe; its sides pulse only once every few minutes, a slow peristalsis that draws water over gills hidden under the kelp-mantle on its back. Its metabolism is the metabolism of the place: cold, chemosynthetic, almost geological. It eats nothing that you would call food. It sieves the detritus that falls forever through the water — the endless snow of shed fronds and spores — and it absorbs the faint chemical heat that seeps from the stone itself, the last warmth of the world. A mouth that barely opens. A gut that is mostly time.

There is not one of them. There are not many.

If you stay still long enough to see one, you will eventually realize how many you have already passed without seeing. That wall-spine that looked a little too regular, a little too vertical for erosion — that was one standing still, letting algae grow over it for a season. That curtain of filaments that seemed to hang without a stalk — that was one, its arms raised, pruning. They are solitary the way stones are solitary. You could swim for a hundred kilometers of causeway and courtyard and sunken reservoir and not meet another. When two do meet, and they must, very rarely, at the crossing of two causeways that takes a year to walk — they do not acknowledge each other. There is no sign. They pass on opposite sides of the channel, trailing their kelp cloaks, each tending its own endless acre.

They do not know they are alive in the way you know. There is no thought left quick enough to be called thought. What they have instead is a retained instruction, smoother and deeper than instinct, laid down when the builders were still builders.

The builders made the floor. Then the water rose — or the world sank, it amounts to the same — and they did not leave. They stayed while light became rumor, while wood became stone, while green became black-brown. They changed the way the kelp changed: by needing less and less, by growing slower and slower, until slowness became a form of preservation. Their tools became their hands. Their language, which must have been about fitting stone without mortar and drilling holes for pegs, became this endless, wordless maintenance. Straighten. Clear. Nudge. Cover again.

They do not reproduce. Or rather, they reproduce the way the forest does. When one finally stops — and they do stop, you can find them if you look — it simply ceases to move. It kneels in a courtyard and does not get up. The kelp on its back thickens, spreads. The silt covers its spade-hands where they rest on the block they were tending. In a century its skin, already half stone, completes the change. The algae felts over it entirely. From a distance it is just another low rise in the pavement, another softened spine. If you brush the silt away you will find the faint outline of too-long limbs under the mat, holding.

Underneath that mat, if you wait another few centuries, a new one will uncurl. Small at first, pale, pushing up like a frond between the joints. Not a child. A cutting.

They are not animals. That was the most normal thing about this place, and it is still true. An animal wants, flees, hunts, breeds quickly. These do none of that. They are the forest's answer to the problem the forest created for itself by having no animals — a grazer that does not graze, a pruner that does not eat, a keeper that keeps nothing for itself. They are what happens when a people decide that the work is more important than the people doing it, and then the people go away but the decision stays, wearing their shape.

If you stay still long enough, one will pass close to you without altering its course. It will not look at you. The fringed pits at its temples will register the pressure of your body as a small, temporary anomaly in the water, like a block slightly out of true. It might pause. It might extend one of those broad, flat hands toward you — not in greeting, not in threat — and brush the silt from your shoulder the same way it brushed silt from the stone, with the same even, patient pressure.

And then it will go on, swaying a centimeter at a time, tending a floor that has no end, in a dark that has no surface, until the stone and the cold and the forest forget the difference between what was made and what grew.
