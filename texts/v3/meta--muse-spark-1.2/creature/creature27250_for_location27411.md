It doesn't live *in* the bowl. It *is* the bowl.

At first you think nothing lives here at all.

That's the trick of it. You come looking for an inhabitant the way you look for an animal in a forest — a shape that moves against the background, a pair of eyes catching the low light. And there is nothing. The leaves don't tremble when you pass except from your own wake in the damp air. The water doesn't ripple except from a drip off a pipe. You stand on the central gantry and you are, for a long time, completely alone in a place that is completely full.

Then your eye adjusts to the scale it actually lives at, and you realize the background was the inhabitant all along.

What lives here has no front or back. No eye, no mouth. It never needed them.

It began as green.

Not green the way a park is green — isolated things in soil. Green as a skin. A continuous felted hide of moss and liverwort and filamentous algae so thick you could peel it off the brick in sheets like wet carpet. This is the first body. It covers everything that doesn't move too fast or burn too hot — the north faces of every tenement, the underside of every walkway, the static flanks of every boiler. It is inches deep, saturated, black-green and luminous in the dim. If you press your palm to it, it doesn't crunch or give like moss should; it compresses like a sponge and then slowly sighs water back into the shape of your hand, holding your print for a full minute before erasing it.

It doesn't grow *on* this place. It is learning the shape of this place by becoming it. Its rhizoids have found every pit in the old concrete, every hairline crack, every sweating mortar joint, and rooted so thoroughly that brick and plant cannot be separated anymore without tearing both. This green watches without eyes. It feels light as a gradient, heat as a boundary. You can see its intelligence at the edges where it stops: a perfect, crisp line a half-inch from a steam vent where the brick is too hot to touch, a neat collar around the rim of a flywheel where vibration would shake it loose. No one trimmed it. It trimmed itself. It knows where it is allowed to be.

And then there is the second body. The iron.

The machines do not run *for* anyone anymore. They run as a body runs — because to stop would be to die. The fifteen-foot flywheel turning once every twenty seconds is a heart, and it is not pumping anything you can see. It turns because the water wheel below it is turning because the steam above it is sighing because the transformers are humming their molar-rattling drone. It is a closed circulation of force. Water lifts and falls. Chain clanks empty overhead and returns empty. Pressure builds and releases. Heat blooms off a housing, and two feet away moss drinks the condensation that heat just made.

That is not work. That is breathing.

Listen to it long enough and you hear it as one animal would: *drip... hiss... hum... groan... drip...* Not a factory floor, a chest. The exhale from a release valve is so slow and so warm it fogs your glasses from twenty feet away, and the next exhale doesn't come for four minutes, as patient as a sleeping whale. You feel the 60-cycle hum not in your ears but in your sternum, in the handrail, in the water itself.

The iron body is also mouthless. It does not take in fuel you can see. It takes in the water that is always welling up from below and always weeping down from above, and the moist heat the green exhales, and it returns them as warmer water, warmer air. It drinks the same thing the moss drinks. It never rusts past a certain point — a thin, stable skin of orange-black magnetite that the algae then colonizes and protects. The green keeps the iron from rotting. The iron keeps the green from drowning. They have negotiated a treaty measured in degrees and millimeters of water pressure and they have kept it for decades.

But neither of those is the organism, alone.

The organism is the negotiation itself.

What actually lives here is the weave.

Under the spongy mat, under the shallow tea-colored marsh-street, under the concrete, there is a third tissue you can't see until you tear something up — and you shouldn't. Roots of the alders and willows, which looked like small separate trees, are not separate at all. They run laterally for yards in the saturated peat, pale and slick, grafting where they touch. They braid with fungal rhizomorphs thick as rope, black and white, which braid in turn around hot water pipes and rebar and old cable sheaths. The pipes sweat, the roots drink. The roots crack the concrete further, the water that wells up follows the crack the root made. The fungus digests fallen leaf that would otherwise choke the intake, turns it into soft peat, peat holds more water, water carries spores.

It is one contiguous mat. A low, flat, basin-wide body with a hundred green lungs on its back and a few iron hearts beating inside it.

It has no center. If you cut a square of moss from a wall, the rest does not bleed. If you stop one conveyor chain, the hum slightly shifts tone to compensate. It senses without eyes the way your skin senses a room — as a field of conditions. A drop in condensation on the east wall, a rise in heat from a transformer, a clogged channel where peat has built too high. It doesn't see the clog. It feels the back-pressure as a dull ache in the slow water, and over weeks it grows a little more aggressively toward the wetness, opens the channel with root-pressure, or allows the water to find a new path and then reinforces that path until it is permanent. It has been doing this since before the cut edges softened. It is very, very good at being exactly here.

It feeds without a mouth. Every surface is a mouth. The moss-leaves drink the air. The algae drink the light that filters down through the lattice. The iron drinks the temperature difference between the warm ground and the cooler cloud. The whole bowl drinks the weeping that never stops. When a waxy leaf falls — perfect, uneaten, because nothing has a mouth to eat it — it lands in the warm water and is not eaten but unmade. Bacteria that live as a film on every submerged surface, invisible but smelling faintly of hot iron and ozone, soften it over months until it is just brown fibers, then peat. The peat becomes the mat. Nothing is wasted because nothing can afford to be carried away. The rim is too high.

It reproduces without seed, without mating. It reproduces by continuing. A spore lands on a newly opened damp brick two inches from its parent — the air is so still the spores fall straight down — and the felt extends. A flake of magnetite pops off a boiler, lands in the marsh, becomes a nucleus for algae to anchor on. A willow sends a sucker up through a crack in a stairwell. The bowl gets a little smaller, a little more filled in, a little more itself. It never needs to send anything over the lip. It has no elsewhere.

If you stand long enough on that central gantry — long enough that your sweat becomes the same temperature as the steam — you will feel it notice you the only way it can. Not as a gaze. There is no one to look.

It will condense on you.

Water will bead on your shoulders, on your hair, on the back of your neck, exactly as it beads on the underside of every pipe. The moss on the nearest wall, which seemed still, will have advanced a fraction of a millimeter toward the extra warmth you are giving off, a negotiation beginning. The hum will seem to include your own blood for a beat. You are not prey, not guest, not intruder. You are briefly a new, warm, vertical surface in a place that grows until it touches something else, then grows around it. You are just another weather condition passing through the bowl.

Stay a day and you will leave damp, with the smell of wet brick and crushed leaf in your clothes for a week.

Stay a lifetime and it would cover you gently, completely, and continue its slow turning.
