They are human.

You expect that to mean something else after a place like that, but it doesn't. They are human the way the stone is still stone. Only more precisely.

You don't see them at first, not because they hide but because they are spaced out correctly. The town is not dense and neither are they. From the rim you might count thirty roofs and think you've counted the town, then realize each roof has a long interval of wet black rock and marsh and empty canal around it, and each of those intervals has a person in it somewhere, bent over, or standing still. They do not cluster.

They are built for this altitude in the way people get built when they have a few hundred years to get used to a place without ever trying to conquer it.

They are lean before they are anything else. Not thin from hunger - there is plenty of taro, plenty of cycad pith if you know how to leech it, plenty of vine fruit - but thin from a life without burden-beasts and without hurry. Everything here is carried by hand. A basalt block is moved with cord and lever and a wooden wedge swelled with water, an inch at a time. A channel is cut with a copper chisel and a hammerstone, one stroke, then another, then another until the line is a forearm wide and a knee deep and true to level for a hundred meters. You cannot be in a hurry and do that work. Their shoulders and forearms are roped, their hands are wide and calloused at the exact points where stone tools press back. Their feet are the most telling part.

Everyone goes barefoot or in flat woven reed sandals that do not last long and are not meant to. The ground weeps constantly. The rock is warm and porous, dark, always a film of water over it. To live here you learn to feel with your soles how much water is just under the skin of the soil, where the spring is going to break before it breaks, where the channel wants to run. Their soles are pale, softened and hardened at once, like river stone. Their toes splay. They walk slowly, even when they are young. It is a wet-rock walk - deliberate placement, no splashing. You hear the footstep itself more than any other sound they make.

They are quiet the way the place is quiet, but it is not shyness. In a place with no insects, no birds, no animal rustle at all, a human voice carries embarrassingly far. A normal speaking voice at midday will travel across two marsh basins and be heard clearly at the next house. A shout would be obscene. So they don't. When they speak it is low, and short, and often while facing the rock or the water rather than each other. Children are taught not to be silent but to be *well-timed* - to say what needs saying when the wind is up so it is curtained, or to wait until you are close enough to not have to project. As a result their language has become dense with specific, necessary words. They have twenty words for the way water moves in cut stone: the tick against a vertical joint, the sheet over moss, the welling darken under a foot, the cold clear upwelling that means a new spring has found a seam. They have very few words for animal things, and those words are old and smooth from disuse, like stones kept on a shelf.

They dress lightly because the air is always a steady late-summer warmth and always damp. No wool, no leather, no feather - there are no animals to give them. Everything they wear is vegetable. Beaten reed fiber, softened cycad bark, long strips of vine that have been retted in the marsh basins until it separates into thread. The cloth is the color of wet rock and green leaf, undyed, and it never quite dries. It hangs on them a little heavily, always a little damp. They do not mind damp. Their skin has a faint sheen to it always, their hair - black or very dark brown, cut short for practicality - clings in damp locks. They look wet even when they have been standing in the sun.

They are gardeners, but not in the way you might picture. Because nothing eats the plants, the plants do not regulate themselves. Taro will choke its own canal if not thinned. Moss will seal over a sluice gate and divert a whole watershed into the wrong terrace if not stripped that week. Fern will mat over a marsh until the water underneath goes black and sour. So their whole work is restraint and redirection. They are pruners, thinners, diverters.

An old woman will spend a whole morning with a copper knife, knee-deep in a marsh she has known for sixty years, cutting back the same stand of broad-leafed taro so that every plant has air, lifting the cut leaves and laying them in the water to rot back into peat exactly where they fell. A young man will walk the length of his family's canal - maybe four hundred meters of shallow, patiently cut runnel - tapping each sluice slab with a wooden mallet, listening for the hollow sound that means water is finding a crack to run under instead of over. They do not farm to expand. There is no new terrace. The plan of the town does not change. They farm to *hold steady*. To keep the water at that same dark stain on that same stone year after year.

That gives them a strange relationship to time.

Nothing much changes here - you understood that at a glance. But to live inside that equilibrium feels different from the outside. It is not stagnation. It is maintenance as a form of devotion. They know, without being told, that the plateau is young and sharp-edged and still learning how to be a place. That if they cut too deep, or damn too much, or take too much cycad pith without replanting the crown, the weeping will change its path and not come back. So they watch. An elder's most important job is not to give orders but to remember. *Last year the spring on the east seam ran cold three days later than usual. The moss on the north wall of house Lira drank heavier in the mornings before the wind shifted.* They hold the memory of water the way other cultures hold genealogies.

Their houses show it too. Low, one or two stories, basalt block and limestone corner, thatch or slab roof. Inside, the floor is stone cut to be slightly dished, so the condensation that forms overnight runs to a central groove and out into the canal that passes by the door. There are no hearths for warmth - it is never cold - only small fire stones for cooking the taro and fermenting the vine fruit. The walls are always a little green at the base where moss has been allowed to stay because it tells you how wet the house is. They re-thatch before the thatch leaks, re-point the lime before the lime crumbles. Stone for stone, exactly as it was.

They are not many. You can feel the intentional emptiness between buildings as a social fact. They could fill the plateau if they wanted - there is empty wet rock for miles beyond the outermost canal - but they don't. A couple will have one child, sometimes two, and the child will be given a interval of marsh and canal to tend that is not new land, but land someone old has stopped tending. The population holds as level as the cisterns. Death is quiet too. They do not have beasts to carry burial, and the porous rock does not take graves well. The dead are laid in the square-mouthed cisterns that have gone brackish or in the deep slot canyons where water runs straight down, covered with stone, left to become peat. Without scavengers, without worms in the number you would expect, a body softens slowly into water and moss.

And because there are no animals, they have no mirror. This is the strangest thing about them if you stay long enough to talk.

They have no dogs to be loyal, no birds to be free, no predators to be feared, no pests to be cursed. Their stories, their jokes, their insults have nowhere to borrow shape. They do not say *stubborn as a mule* or *quick as a hawk*. When a child asks what a fish is, you have to describe *water moving by itself inside water.* Their metaphors are all mineral and vegetable and weather. A difficult person is *a stone that will not take an edge.* A beloved person is *a canal that holds level.* Grief is *a spring gone white* - when the clear water suddenly carries lime and means the seam has shifted.

They look at you, when you arrive, with a calm that is not quite welcome and not quite suspicion. They are listening more than looking. Listening to how your feet fall on their wet rock, how loud your breathing is in air that is thin and warm, whether you know to step around the moss that is measuring the condensation for them. If you stand still long enough on the rim where the warm air rises from the cloud sea and the cold seep circles your ankles, one of them will come and stand near you, not close, and will not ask where you came from. They will perhaps lift a sluice slab a thumb's width to correct a trickle that only they heard was wrong, and in that small, competent, unhurried movement you will understand the whole of them:

Patient, damp, precise, level. A people who have become the human equivalent of a well-cut channel - not imposing their shape on the plateau so much as letting the plateau weep through them, exactly, without spilling.
