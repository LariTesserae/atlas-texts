They live in the thickness of it.

That's the first thing you get wrong. You come up from the lowlands expecting inhabitants the way a house has inhabitants - things that walk on top of a place, sleep in it, leave it empty when they go. Nothing lives *on* the plateau. Everything lives *in* it. The mat is not a floor for them. It is a body.

At first you think: birds. And you are right, but not in the way you mean.

You hear them before you see any one of them clearly, because the light is so low and the sound is so total. Then a shape detaches from the amber gloom at chest height and you realize the wall beside you is moving.

It's weavers. Soot-black highland weavers, but not like any weaver you've seen below. The ones in the lowlands build a neat pendant nest and defend it. These have forgotten how to be solitary. Their black has gone matte with damp, their beaks are pale bone against it, and they are smaller than crows - starling-sized, you realize when one lands two feet from your face to worry at a loose vine. They don't fly so much as commute. Short, whirring flights from hummock to hummock, or more often they run. They run through the thatch on strong, scaly feet, sideways along vertical walls, upside down under arches, disappearing into holes exactly the width of their own bodies. They are everywhere because there are millions of them, and you stop counting after the first minute because counting implies individuals. This is a colony that has become a tissue.

Watch one work. She lands with a fresh strip of green - some sedge hauled up a thousand feet from the valley - and she doesn't look for a place to build. She finds a place where the weave is thin, where light leaks or rain has started a bald spot, and she stitches. Over, under, pull through with the beak, press with the chest, turn around inside the stitch to smooth it with belly feathers oiled with resin. It takes thirty seconds. Then she is gone and another takes her place. No bird owns a nest anymore. They own work. A dome you thought was a nest is just a place where the mat happened to bulge into a warm pocket. Tomorrow three different birds will lay in it, and the day after something else will.

Above them - though above is a relative term when the canopy is only ten feet over your head - are the swifts.

You only see them when a collapse lets a shaft of real sun in. Then the air in the shaft boils. Soot swifts, also black, also highland, but built entirely differently: sickle wings, no legs to speak of, mouths too wide. They never land on the mat. They can't. They cling to the underside of the vine leaves with their saliva. Their nests are not nests - they are hard, glossy cups cemented to the thatch with spit that dries like lacquer, waterproof and white as porcelain. You can see old ones in cross-section in the eroded cuts, thousands of them layered like barnacles, each one repurposed by something else. The living swifts live in the air itself, in a flat torus of screaming flight just under the leaves. They scream constantly, not in alarm but in echolocation, in contact - a high, dry *chit-chit-chit* that is almost lost under the insect roar. They drink the condensation off leaves without landing. They mate on the wing. When they die, they fall into the thatch and in an hour the beetles have taken them apart.

That is the second layer. The third lives between the fibers.

Put your hand flat on the wall of thatch and hold it there. Wait. The wall will move under your palm.

Beetles the size of your thumb joint shoulder through the pulp, their backs polished black or iridescent green, chewing old thatch into new pulp and excreting it as fresh wall. Leafcutters in two endless rivers - one going out empty-jawed, one coming back carrying green sails bigger than themselves - repair any breach within hours, laying down a living thatch of fungus they farm inside the mat. The fungus is not a visitor. It is a builder. Its white threads bind everything, digest the guano into humus, fruit into soft brackets along the darkest tunnels that the weavers eat when seed is scarce. Wild bees - stingless, black and gold - have built paper tubes the width of your fist that run for meters through the thatch, humming so low you feel it more than hear it. Ants. Mites by the billion in every feather. Tiny, pale, blind crickets that live their whole lives in a single dome and whose song makes up part of that deafening wall - you thought cicadas, but most of it is these crickets, shoulder to shoulder, stridulating not to attract mates across distance but to say *I'm here, I'm here, the wall is whole here*.

And where there is a cup that holds water - and there are tens of thousands of them, every sealed bowl, every bromeliad axil, every resin-polished hollow - something lives in the water.

Thumb-sized frogs the color of wet thatch, with skin that looks like lichen. They never touch ground. They are born, breed, and die in a cup the size of a teacup. At dusk - which is to say, always, under the vines - they begin to pulse. One note, then another answers, then another, until the whole plateau throbs. You feel it in the thatch under your back when you lie down. Their tadpoles filter the permanent condensation. Their eggs are glued to the bromeliad leaves above the waterline so they don't drown when a cup overflows in a heavy sweat.

In the deeper dark, where even the weavers don't go often, are the hands.

You were told there were hands. You look for monkeys and don't find them at first because you are looking for something the size of you. They are not.

They are still and then they are suddenly close - the size of a marmoset, or a large rat, with forward-facing eyes huge and amber for this perpetual dusk, with naked, damp hands that look too human to be comfortable. Highland thatch-mice, some call them, though they are not mice; they are not primates either, not quite - a relic lineage that got trapped up here when the mat was still thin and then evolved *into* it. Their fur is the same mottled grey-green-brown as old thatch, shaggy and perpetually damp, full of commensal moths and beetles that clean it. They move slowly, deliberately, on all fours along the tunnels, then sit up to work with both hands.

And they work constantly. They are what the weavers cannot be. Where a beak can pull a strip through, a hand can tie a knot. They take vine tendrils and loop them, they press mud with palms, they pick mites off nestlings and eat them. They live in extended families of six or eight inside a single large bower, sleeping piled together for warmth, all of them nursing each other's young. They don't have a language you would recognize - a soft, low hooting and tooth-chattering that is almost inaudible under the insect wall - but they follow the weavers and the weavers follow them. If a hummock collapses, the weavers alarm and the hands come. If the hands find a cold, dry spot where nothing will germinate, they carry guano to it in cupped palms and seed it.

Nothing here is a predator the way a hawk is a predator. There are snakes, thin as shoelaces and the color of vine, that slip through finch-sized tunnels and take eggs. There are larger parrots - slate-grey mountain parrots with heavy beaks - that crack beetles and sometimes raid a poorly sealed dome, calling in that rolling, guttural phrase that carries for valleys. But mostly, death is not hunting. It's wear. A bird that gets too old or sick sits still, and within minutes the beetles find it, the fungus finds it, the thatch closes over where it sat. Nothing is wasted because there is nowhere to waste it to. The plateau eats its dead the way a body reabsorbs a bruise.

You realize after a few hours that you have not seen a single animal that knows how to be alone. Even the frogs in their separate cups answer each other. Solitary silence means something is wrong - a hole, a leak, a snake. Sound is how the whole thing knows it is still whole. That's why the chorus never stops. It is not background. It is the nervous system.

If you stay long enough to let your eyes fully adjust, you see the oldest inhabitants last: the ones that stopped looking like animals at all. In the thickest places, where the mat is ten meters deep and no light has reached in decades, the thatch has become soil, and the soil has become thatch again so many times that roots and feathers are the same material. There, things live that no longer have names - pale, elongated weavers that have lost their wings and run like moles through pulp, tending fungus; beetles that have become sessile, their backs grown over with moss, filtering condensation; vines that have learned to twitch when touched and close around a tunnel to seal a draft.

They are not species anymore. They are organs.

You lie down at night in an abandoned bower big enough for your body. The inner wall is smooth, waterproofed with saliva and leaf-oil and polished by generations of warm bodies turning in it. It still holds heat. The damp soaks into your back. The dark is complete. The chorus presses on your ears from every side until your own breath disappears into it.

And very slowly, under you, the plateau breathes. Not metaphorically. The warm air under the vine canopy rises, cool air falls through the thatch to replace it, wings fan, a thousand mouths worry at the weave, vines tighten, fungus exhales. In and out. In and out.

You are not sleeping on it. You are sleeping in it. For a night, you are one more warm body in a dome, repairing nothing, laying nothing, just adding your heat and your damp to the place that knows, without ever thinking, exactly what to do with it.
