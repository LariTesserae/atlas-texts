It doesn't live *on* the mountain.

Nothing really does, up there. The summit plain is too exposed, too thin, too dry. The sun bakes the polished limestone until it burns to touch, and the wind has had a million years to polish away anything that tried to hold soil. Up there is for passing over.

It lives *in* the mountain. Or more precisely, in the omission. In the thumbprint.

You won't see it at first. No one does. That is the trick of the bowl.

At first you think the walls are the inhabitants. And they are - the living wallpaper, the noise, the press of bodies. You stand at the silt floor and look up at the tiered, scalloped rings of rust and cream and ash, and the rock appears to move. It is not rock. It is a pelt.

That is colony. That is the obvious life, the life that found this warm, damp, dark room in the sky and decided to fill it to capacity. The soot-bats, the swiftlets, the Martins. They hang shoulder to shoulder in the undercuts, layered three and four deep, a drapery of warm mammalian and avian flesh. The moths lift and resettle like ash. The black beetles river along the sweat-lines. The amber crickets stitch the air with that electric drone that lives in your molars.

You think that is what lives here. And you would be right, but not complete.

Because after twenty minutes, when the ringing in your ears has become a kind of hearing, you start to see the difference between movement and movement.

The bats shift with a leathery shuffle. The birds click and flutter. The beetles run in lines, purposeful, pheromone-bound. 

Something else moves *against* the line.

It detaches not with a glide, but with a reach. A hand over hand.

Stay still long enough — an hour, with your back against the silt and your breathing shallow so you don't inhale the down and the guano dust — and the wall resolves into figures.

They were there the whole time. You mistook them for shadow, for the darker stone between two ledges.

They are small. That's the first correction your brain has to make. In a space that holds forty meters of vertical darkness, your eye wants to make everything large. They are not.

An adult would fit easily in your arms if it would let you, which it never will. Forty to sixty centimeters tall when it stands - which it rarely does - crouched, compact, folded in on itself the way desert animals fold to keep water in. Two to three kilos at most. The size of a vervet juvenile, or a large raven. Sky-island dwarfism. Everything that lives permanently in an island gets smaller except the insects.

Their color is the second correction. You expect eyeshine, pallor, cave-white. They are not pale. They are exactly the color of damp limestone in low light - a mottled, dusty ash-grey, washed with that same rust-red that bands the strata. Thin fur, but not fur the way a mountain mammal is furred. Short, close, almost velvety, sparse on the belly and throat where the skin shows through pink-grey and sweats openly. They need to dump heat, not keep it. At 3,000 meters anything should be shaggy and white. These are naked in places, like swiftlets. The warmth of the bowl has undone the mountain.

Look at the extremities and you understand they were not meant for the plateau, ever.

The feet are huge for the body. Broad, soft, splayed, with a gripping big toe set low and wide — not quite a primate foot, not quite a gecko's pad, but something in between. The pads themselves are ridged and damp, they leave faint prints in the silt that you thought were water drops. They can cling to a vertical runnel that is slick with algae and still hold a flake of chert steady. The hands are the same - long fingers, surprisingly human in proportion, tipped with short, black, curved nails that are more claw than nail. The palms are naked, corrugated, sensitive. When one reaches for a beetle it does not snatch. It *palpates*.

The face is where you stop trying to file it under anything you know.

The muzzle is short, blunt, whiskered heavily - long, white vibrissae that tremble constantly, reading air movement in a room where air hardly moves but sound does. The nose is wet, black. The eyes are disproportionate. Not the blind pink pinpricks of a true troglobite, because there *is* light here, just not sun. They have kept their eyes and enlarged them obscenely. Wide, forward-facing, amber-irised, with a tapetum that catches the faint fungal glow and throws it back as two dull coals when your headlamp passes. They are eyes for seeing phosphorescence and silhouette, for reading the faint green of algae in a fissure and the shape of a hand against stone.

But the ears are larger still. Huge, membranous, almost bat-like, translucent at the edges where blood shows through, cupped and mobile. They swivel independently. Of course they do. In a cathedral that traps and throws every chirp and plink, you cannot live by eyes alone. You live by filtering. The crickets scream at one frequency, the swiftlets click at another, the water drips at a third - and underneath it all they have made a narrow, quiet channel for themselves. When they call to each other you barely hear it, a soft, breathy *hut hut*, subsonic enough to slide under the insect drone. You feel it more than hear it.

And they are social. That is the other thing you realize slowly, as individual motes become a troop.

There are perhaps twenty, perhaps thirty in the bowl. Not a swarm like the bats. A family, loose, dispersed across the tiers but always within that soft contact call. An old female with a bald patch on her crown picks along a seep-line, her infant clinging to her belly with all four limbs, its face still pink and its eyes still shut against even the dimness. Two juveniles chase each other not by jumping but by flowing - hand, foot, hand, foot along the undercut lip of a ledge, never losing contact with stone, their long tails - thin, rat-like, but furred - acting as counterweights, flicking behind them.

They do not fly. They do not need to. The bowl is their entire world. The mountain top is an island in the sky surrounded by a sea of thin, dry, cold air they cannot cross. Their ancestors may have been something that came up from the scrub forest two million years ago when the climate was wetter - a small, generalized rock monkey, a cliff-dwelling corvid ancestor - and got stranded when the world dried and the warm pocket remained. Evolution did the rest in fast-forward: shrink the body, stretch the fingers, enlarge the eyes and ears, thin the coat, learn to drink condensation and eat what the darkness provides.

What does it eat? Everything the bowl overproduces.

You watch the old female stop at the little cache you noticed before - the flat stone beside the meter-wide seep basin. From the side it looked like random debris. Now you see it is tended. A small, neat kit.

She picks up the chert flake - white-grey, carried up from basalt scree a day's climb below, no chert occurs naturally in this limestone - turns it, tests its crescent edge with her thumb pad. With a practiced, almost casual flick she wedges it under the carapace of one of the glossy black beetles and pops it. The beetle, as long as her hand, writhes. She scoops. She does not crush it with teeth. Her teeth are small, peg-like, for soft things. She uses the tool.

A little further up, a juvenile has wedged a twig - stripped, bent while green and then dried into a hook - into a fissure too narrow for even its slender fingers. It fishes, tongue flicking. Out comes a fat, white grub, blind, that lives only by eating guano fungus. The juvenile eats it and then carefully replaces the twig in the crack for next time. No nest. No fire. No art. Just cached, curated extensions of the hand, left where the damp will keep the twig pliant and the stone from rusting.

They also farm without intending to. They move algae. They carry silt. The guano that fuels everything is spread by their feet from ledge to ledge. The wiry succulents that cling so sparsely to the walls are often found growing from a crevice where one of them has wedged a bit of silt to hold a seed that fell from a swiftlet's gut.

They are not loud. In a room that is already deafening, being loud is death. A predator - and there is one, you realize, a pale, narrow-headed cliff viper that comes up the ramp at night to hunt bats, its scales the exact cream of the limestone - hunts by sound. So the troop moves in near silence, their soft *hut* calls lost under the crickets. When a bat colony panics and lifts in a storm of wings, they freeze, splayed flat against the rock, ash on ash, invisible until the storm passes.

At true night, when the oval of sky goes black and the fungus on the guano beds begins its dim, warm glow, is when you finally understand what you are looking at.

Not a relic. Not a monster.

A complete answer to a question the mountain asked: *What would you do with a single room that is always dusk, always warm, always dripping, always full?*

Anything that stayed would have to become quiet, and compact, and clever with its hands. It would have to learn to see by fungus-light and hear through a wall of noise. It would have to shrink until it could live on beetles and moths and the water that the air itself sweats out. It would have to carry a sharp stone up from the dry world below because nothing sharp grows here.

And if you had come ten years ago, you would have seen the same troop, minus one old female, plus two infants. The basin where she cracked beetles would have been three centimeters to the left, because the drip line migrates. The succulent she slept beside would have been shorter. The flake she used would have been a different flake, struck from a different core, but with the same crescent edge, because a crescent is what works to open a beetle here.

They do not know they live in a bowl on top of a mountain. To them, the rim is the horizon. The oval of sky is a lamp that is sometimes blue-white and sometimes black. The world is twenty meters of tiered, weeping stone that holds exactly enough water and exactly enough food if you know where to look and you remember where you left your twig.

You leave before dawn, climbing the collapsed chute back toward the dry wind and the thin sun, and by the time you are halfway up the wall the troop has already vanished again into texture. The wall is just wall again. The crickets saw. The water plinks. The air smells of mineral and living things.

Behind you, a soft *hut hut* passes under the noise, and a small ash-grey hand replaces a twig in a crack, for tomorrow.
