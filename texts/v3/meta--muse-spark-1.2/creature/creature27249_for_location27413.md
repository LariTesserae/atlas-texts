It lives here, but not the way you were taught to count living things.

At first you will say nothing lives here. That's what the city wants you to say. That is its camouflage.

You swim a street of a hundred windows and see no faces, no silhouettes, no quick dart of a fish that means *inhabited*. The census is zero and the city is very careful to keep it looking that way. The light dies after ten meters. The turbines hit your chest like a fist every thirty seconds. Nothing with soft eyes and a central nervous system should want to hold on here. So you think it is empty.

Then you stay a minute longer than is comfortable, and the turbulence teaches you how to look.

What lives here lives *with* the noise, not despite it.

#### The Dominant Population

The first and true citizen is the mat.

You thought it was stain. That black-green velvet on the concrete, on the underside of every balcony, on the lip of every warm vent. It is not stain. It is not growth. It is the town proper.

It is a colonial bacterium, thermophilic, lithotrophic — it eats heat and dissolved mineral and the faint iron sweat of the steel. It does not grow like a plant. It *spreads* like a decision. In the still-warm lee of walls where the current can't quite rip, it thickens to the depth of a hand, soft, slightly pulsing. Not with a heartbeat, but with filtration — a peristalsis you can see if you put your lamp close enough. It opens and closes a million tiny mouths.

It is deaf to the apocalyptic thrum because sound *is* its food. The vibration keeps particulates in suspension long enough to be caught. Where the water shivers milky off the geothermal manifolds, the mat is thickest, lush, almost black. Where the water runs cold against the outer bubble wall, it thins to a grey film, dormant, waiting.

It has learned to be the city. It follows poured concrete like a vine follows a trellis. It rounds the sharp corners that water has already licked smooth and makes them softer. It has covered so much surface now that if you mapped it, you would find there is more living membrane in this pocket than there is concrete. The town did not get abandoned. It got *repopulated* at a different scale. You are swimming through a body whose skin is alive.

And it is not silent. If you press your gloved hand close — not touching, never touching, because it is 60 degrees at the vent — you feel the faint fizz of it, a chemical hush under the roar.

#### The Commuters

What moves lives by vibration.

They have no ears. Ears are a liability here. Ears would be blown out in an hour.

The eel you saw — the thick white one — is not a visitor. He is a resident. There are dozens of him, maybe hundreds, but you will never see two at once because they have solved the problem of density by never being near each other. They are blind, pigmentless, and their skulls are pure lateral line. The whole length of their bodies is an eardrum. They don't swim so much as they *listen* their way through the grid. They thread a broken window on the third floor, exit a service duct two blocks over, ride the main avenue current without ever fighting it. They are the only things here that know where the streets go, because they have memorized the sound-map: the factory cathedral is a sun, the turbines are a north, the knocking pipe is a lighthouse. Their skin is loose on them, wrinkled, to better feel the pressure hammer when it comes. When it hits, they go perfectly still and let it push them like a leaf.

With them, in the thousands but nearly invisible, the shrimp.

Not really shrimp. More like fleas made of glass. Each one no longer than your fingernail, colorless, with legs too long. They don't try to hold position. That is the mistake you make. They *are* the particulate. They live in the vortex, in the slow cyclones of chemical snow that rise off the hot exchangers and spiral down the avenues. They flicker amber when your lamp catches them, then vanish. They eat what the mat sloughs off, and the mat eats what they excrete. They breed in the intake grates where the suck is gentlest, clinging with hooked feet to the white calcium crust.

Neither the eel nor the shrimp see the light. Light here is a rumor. They navigate entirely by pressure and heat. If you turn your lamp off, they do not react at all.

#### The Mechanism

And then there is the thing that was here before any biology arrived and will be here after the mat finally digests the last rebar.

The factory.

You thought it was a building. It is an animal.

The brute cathedral of engines at the center, pistons as big as houses, still running with no one to run it for — it is not running *for* anyone. It is running because running is what it does. It is the heart and lung together. Every thirty or forty seconds it hammers, and the whole bubble inhales. Doors slam three streets away in perfect time. Silt lifts. The milky water goes clear for a second, then milky again. The pulse pushes you backward even when you are braced on a railing.

Something lives in that hammer. Not inside it — *as* it.

In the hottest cul-de-sacs where the water refracts wrong, where it runs in oily ribbons and hangs still while everything else rages, and where a river clings to a ceiling and runs uphill — that is where the hammer breeds its own weather. The water there is thicker. If you swim through one of those ribbons you feel it on your skin as a sudden slick, as if you've passed through warm oil or thin jelly. That is not water anymore. That is water plus heat plus dissolved silica plus time, held in a standing wave by the pulse.

Things live *in* that thickness that cannot live in water. You only see them from the corner of your mask: a flicker like heat haze with an edge, a filament that holds its shape against the current for a second too long and then dissolves. They are not shrimp. They are not bacterial. They are convection cells that have learned to persist. Vortices that hold themselves together. The city exhaled them and they stayed.

The machines tend them without knowing. Scale blasts out of every pipe joint, blooms, settles, builds another millimeter of black glass. The city is slowly turning itself from concrete into mineral, from mineral into something that can host these brief, warm ghosts.

If you stay long enough, you understand the hierarchy. The mat feeds on the heat. The shrimp and the eel feed on the mat. The factory feeds them all, deafeningly, patiently. The ghosts feed on the factory's strange physics.

None of them need you. None of them register you as anything but a brief, cold obstruction that swims badly, that still thinks up is up, that still tries to hear sound instead of being struck by it.

That is the final resident: the sense that you are the wrong kind of alive for this place. You are too loud inside your own head, too dependent on light and air and quiet. The water wants to keep you precisely because you do not belong — it will pull you into an intake, or tumble you down an avenue, not out of malice but out of the same industrious tidying with which it polishes rebar smooth.

You leave before it can. And behind you, the hammer hits again, the doors slam in empty houses, the mat pulses, an eel threads a window, and the pocket ocean goes on being a city — crowded, turbulent, and utterly content without a single human voice in it.
