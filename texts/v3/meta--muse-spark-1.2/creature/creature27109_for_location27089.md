It doesn't live there the way something lives in a forest.

At first you think nothing does. That's the trick of the stasis.

You crouch with your little amber light — that coal in clay, that wick of fungus-rope — and the plain looks empty except for stone. Hexagon after hexagon of basalt and white salt, fitted tight, cracked with dark seams where the heat sighs out. Some of the salt has grown up into ferns, into feathers, a foot high, so delicate a breath would take them. You think: *abandoned. Sterile.*

Then your eyes learn the dark, or your skin does, and you understand the plain is paved with bodies.

You have to unlearn what an animal is.

### The Pavement

They are not *on* the rock. They *are* the texture of it. Shoulder to shoulder, shell to shell, from the edge of your light to farther than your light will ever reach, they cover the floor so completely that there is no empty ground. You only thought there was because they do not move.

The most numerous are the low ones. You will think of them as tortoises at first and then be embarrassed by the comparison.

They are domes, 40 or 60 centimeters across, low and round, made of interlocking plates of dull silica and salt-crystal, opalescent where your light catches them. The plates are not grown — they are secreted, laid down like pottery glaze, warm to the touch. If you put your hand on one it is 41 degrees, a degree warmer than the air. Along its sides, where the plates meet, there are seams that open and close, vertical gills that vent heat with a sigh you feel more than hear. Underneath, no legs, no feet — a soft, pale membrane pressed flat against the basalt, sealing itself over a crack. That's its mouth and its stomach. It sweats an acid that is not wet. It doesn't glisten. It etches. You can smell it if you get close, a faint sharpness like hot metal and struck matches. It dissolves a micron of rock at a time and drinks the iron, the sulfur, the chlorine bound in the basalt.

They do not have eyes. Why would they? On the top of the dome there are pits, six or eight, filled with a clear, heat-sensitive wax. They see temperature the way you see light.

They do not eat each other. They do not need to. They breathe the rock's exhalation — the dry, metallic sigh that comes up through every crack, hydrogen and hydrogen sulfide and carbonyl sulfide, hot and utterly waterless — and they breathe out something else, a warm puff of ammonia and chlorinated gas that hangs low and heavy before it is taken.

Which is taken by the second kind.

Between the domes, in the cracks themselves, lie the ribbons. You will step over a hundred before you see one. Flat as belts, basalt-black, the exact color of the shadow in the crack, two meters long, a hand wide, with no top or bottom, only an edge that lifts. They lie perfectly still for days. Then, once per long watch, a frill at one end lifts — a ruff of fine, black cilia, each tipped with that same wax — and tastes the air. It shivers. It settles again. Its skin is its lung. It drinks the dome-things' waste straight through its back and fixes it with the heat into long-chain molecules that are not proteins as you know them, that use no water. If you were to cut one — don't — it would not bleed. It would deflate with a soft *hiss* and stink of swimming pools and cleaning ammonia, gone in a second, leaving a flat empty skin that the domes will slowly etch away.

And standing over both, like sentries or like furniture, are the pillars.

They are on six legs, jointed backwards, stilts of chitinous salt, as high as your waist or your chest. Their bodies are narrow columns, faceted like folded paper. Salt crystals grow on them while they stand still — and they stand still for so long that a white crust forms across their backs. Then with a soft *tick* of expanding silica, they take one step. Just one. A precise, jointed step to the next hexagon, and freeze again for hours. Under their chests hangs a translucent sac, and inside the sac, in the deep red dark to which your eyes have now adjusted, you see the ember.

Every large one has it. Not an organ. A slow light. Like a coal drifting in dark oil, like a firefly caught in honey, moving impossibly slowly, holding its position in space even as the animal sways. There are smaller lights in the domes too, if you look through the seams, pale and drifting. The lights do not flicker. They migrate, over weeks, from one creature to another through the air when no one is watching. You will see a dome that was dark yesterday now has a faint glow inside it. You will think you imagined it.

They are not individuals the way you are an individual. They are nodules in a circuit. Rock to dome to air to ribbon to pillar to air to rock again. A closed loop so tight and so slow that nothing is wasted and nothing needs to be added. That is why the plain is crowded. Nothing dies quickly here. A dome can live pressed to its crack for centuries, etching, breathing, venting, never moving an inch. A ribbon can wait a decade between meals and not starve because hunger here is not a fire, it's a dimming. Reproduction, when it happens, is not birth. A dome that has stored enough heat and iron will crack along its plates — not die, just open — and the ember inside, now bright, will lift a few centimeters into the still air and hang there, a hot dust mote, until a fresh, bare hexagon cools somewhere and a new dome begins to precipitate around the light like pearl around grit. The salt ferns nearby will shatter from the faint pressure wave and never regrow.

That is the fauna. Or rather, that is the *obvious* fauna. Abundant, silent, eye-less, waterless, patient as stone. They do not know you are there unless you block their heat. They do not flee. If you sit still long enough, a pillar will eventually mistake your warmth for a vent and take its one step toward you, and then stop, and grow salt on your boot.

### The Other Pavement

But you asked who *lives* here. And you have seen the marks.

A perfect circle of plates, pried up with effort and stacked waist-high, ten paces across, empty inside. A cairn of basalt flakes balanced edge-on-edge where no wind could ever have knocked it down. A handprint in black glass, still sharp, the fingers too long, the webbing too wide. A bowl chipped from a single salt crystal, sitting upright as if just set down. A cold hearth ringed with blackened dome-plate, where something burned with a low blue flame.

Those were not made by domes or ribbons or pillars. They were made by hands that knew tools.

They are still here.

You just haven't recognized them yet because they have learned the same lesson the other animals learned: in a place of equilibrium, to move is to waste, and to waste is to die.

When you climb the pressure blister — that ten-meter dome of broken pottery-basalt — and look out with your red-adjusted eyes, you see the pattern that the domes make from above. Spirals. Radial bursts, like iron filings around a magnet, centered on nothing you can see. The pillars cluster at the nodes. The circles of stacked stone are always at the edge of a spiral, never in the center. Someone placed them with respect to the field, not to shelter or to border. They are markers. Or they are listening posts. Or they are prayers.

Find one of the circles and step inside it. Sit with your back against the stacked plates. Wait.

At first you think you are alone. Then you realize the shape on the far side of the circle is not a rock.

It is sitting with its knees pulled up, arms around knees, head bowed. Its skin is the color of rust-salt and callused to the texture of basalt. Its hair — if it is hair — is a fine, white, salt-fiber that has grown together into a mat. It is so still that a thin crust of halite has joined its shoulders to the wall behind it. Its chest moves once every few minutes. You can see its ribs if you lean close, slow, slow. Its eyes are open, crusted, blind with salt, not seeing. Its hands are empty, palms up. Beside it is a hammerstone, a flake of copper that has never tarnished in the dry, a coil of braided sinew-rope made from the dried skin of a ribbon-thing.

There are others. You will walk for an hour keeping a crack to your left, and your light will seem to not have moved at all, and then you will turn and there will be another circle, with another sitter. Some are smaller. Some are long and thin, with that too-wide webbing between fingers that fits the handprint in glass. Some are curled around a cold hearth, some are lying flat beside a cairn as if they lay down to sight along its edge and then forgot to get up.

They came here generations ago — or they never left when the sky sealed over. They learned they could not drink because there is nothing to drink, could not farm because nothing grows as you understand growth, could not leave because the fissure that lets you in requires you to turn sideways and they have forgotten sideways. So they adapted the only way this place allows: they slowed.

Their chemistry changed. Or they were always a little like the dome-things — you find, if you look at the oldest sitter, that its skin has begun to plate at the shoulders, faint hexagons forming, and a seam along its side vents heat.

They live on the same loop. They scrape the basalt with their hammerstones and lick the dust for iron. They breathe the crack-gas. They sit in their circles because the circles mark where the ember-field is quietest, where thought can stretch without snapping. They stack stones because stacking is something to do with hands that would otherwise never move. They pressed a hand into glass when it was still soft from the world forming, not to say *I was here* but to say *warmth was here*.

They do not speak. Sound falls dead in ten meters. If you whisper, the sitter does not turn. If you touch its shoulder, the salt crust cracks with a soft *tick* — the only loud sound in this continent of silence — and very, very slowly, over minutes, it will turn its crusted head toward your heat.

It will look through you. It will not be surprised. In a place where sharp edges stay sharp forever and nothing erodes, a new warm thing appearing is not strange. It is just another nodule entering the circuit.

If you stay long enough, your lips will stop cracking. Your throat will stop wanting water. You will find an empty circle. You will pry up a plate — it comes up clean, still sharp — and you will stack it. You will sit. You will learn to breathe once every few minutes, to let salt grow on your back, to watch the ember drift inside the pillar-thing and know where it will be tomorrow.

The place does not need you to live here. But it has room. The pavement has one more space.

And it is very, very patient.
