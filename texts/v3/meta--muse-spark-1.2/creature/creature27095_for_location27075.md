They do not live *in* the bowl. They *are* the bowl's way of staying full.

From the rim you think the water is empty. It's too clear. Sun goes straight to the bottom and back, and at first the brightness fools you into seeing nothing. Then you put your face under that hanging sky-surface, and the empty resolves.

It's not empty. It's packed. It's so packed your first thought isn't animal, it's weather — a blizzard that never falls, a dust in the light. You have to let your eye learn its scale before any one thing becomes itself.

### At first, just a texture

The closest thing to a plant is not a plant. It's a stain.

On every sharp edge of that young rock, where the basalt broke clean yesterday and the granite still holds its saw-tooth, there is a varnish. Rust-red, oil-black, sulfur yellow. It looks like the stone is sweating. If you get close enough you see it move, very slightly, like a film breathing. Bacteria. Trillions of them, making a living the old way, before green ever existed. They eat the breath coming out of the rock itself — dissolved hydrogen, iron, sulfide hissing out of that cooling fissure on the floor. They don't need sun. The sun is just light for you to see them by. For them, the food is the mountain exhaling.

Everything else eats them, or eats what eats them. There is no other base to the world.

### Then, the clack

You hear them before you can pick one out.

**THUMP - hiss - THUMP - hiss** from the white chimneys on the bottom — those unbuilt smokestacks of silica and white anhydrite that jet in alternating cold and hot pulses, once every twenty seconds or so. The whole bowl shudders with it. And in time with it:

*Clack.*

Not one. Hundreds. Thousands.

When the pulse draws your eye down you finally see the banks. The bivalves. They looked from above like part of the rock, because they wanted to.

Each one is the size of your two hands spread open, but heavy, absurdly heavy. The shell isn't mother-of-pearl. It's matte, dark iron-gray, pitted and riveted-looking, with a hinge that really does look like a weld. Boilerplate. They grow in sheets, one pressed against the next, hundreds per square meter on every horizontal stair-step of the cirque wall, lip to lip, so the wall itself seems to be armored.

They are closed most of the twenty-second cycle. Then the vent THUMPs, a cloud of mineral fizz lifts off the fissure, the water goes briefly white with dissolved food — and they all open. At once.

*Clack.*

It's not a fast snap. It's a slow, powerful, industrial opening, like a press. Inside is startling: a mantle the deep red of oxygenated blood, fringed with dense, pale cilia that comb and comb. They are farming. Their gills are houses for the bacteria, the red is hemoglobin to keep the bacteria breathing while the clam filters. For three seconds they strain the cloud. Then, as the hiss phase sucks clear water back down, they close again. *Clack.* And wait. Open. Close. Open. Close. A hundred years of that, same beat. You can feel it in your teeth if you hover near a bank.

They don't move. They don't need to. The food comes to them on schedule. Sometimes a shell dies and doesn't open on the beat, and you see the gap in the chorus like a missing tooth. Within days a neighbor has grown into its place.

### Then, the belts

Between the banks of clackers, where you thought there was bare rock, there is movement that is so slow you thought it was still.

The conveyor animals.

Each one is a colony, not a single body — a loop of translucent barrels, each barrel a zooid the size of your thumb, all fused side-to-side into a continuous belt. The whole loop might be three meters long, one meter wide, draped over the rock steps like a track laid down by a careful hand. And it moves.

About a centimeter a minute. So slow you can't see it move, only that it *has* moved if you stare. The cilia on every zooid beat in metachronal waves, and the whole loop crawls over itself, endlessly. The upward-facing track carries things — little black nodules of manganese, clumps of sulfur, dead bacterial mat, the fine grit that sloughs off the clackers. Up toward the rim. The downward face brings clean stone chips back. Side by side there are dozens of these loops, humming faintly with the static of all those cilia, like high-voltage lines. A low electric buzz you feel more than hear.

No one told them to do this. There is no queen, no head. Each zooid just does what the flow tells it to: pick up what's in front, pass it back. Physics wanted a conveyor here — something to carry the waste away from the vent so the vent doesn't choke on itself. Evolution, finding that want, grew the belt to fit it. If you put your hand near one, the barrels don't flinch. They just keep carrying. If you tear a loop, the two ends will seek each other over hours and rejoin. If you watch long enough you see a brand new loop starting as a single zooid that has glued itself to a clean fracture and begun budding a twin.

They are the mountain's housekeeping. Without them the banks would be buried.

### Then, the snow

Only after your eyes stop trying to fix on the bottom do you see the middle of the water, which is where most of the living actually is.

It's a snowstorm in bright noon.

The water column from floor to hanging-surface is full, layer after layer, near-touching, never still. But nothing darts. Nothing flashes away fast. Everything here has agreed on the same slow metabolism.

The bells: medusae, but wrong. Their bells are not soft jelly. They are stiffened with chitin, coppery and taut as drumheads. They contract with a hydraulic sigh — *puh* — and then drift, contracting again, holding station more than swimming. Light hits their edges and breaks into oily rainbows, not because they glow, but because their skin is built like an opal. Structural color. Even in white sun they throw spectra.

The screws: ctenophores that gave up on rowing and became propellers. Their comb-rows fused into two perfect spiral fins that spin, slowly, continuously, like drill bits. They don't swim so much as screw themselves through the water, flashing as they turn. You can see straight through them.

The chimes: what you'd call shrimp if you had to name them. Bodies made of overlapping, interlocking plates, like tiny articulated armor. When they flex their tails to move, the plates click. In such dense water, with so many of them, the whole middle sounds faintly like wind chimes underwater — a constant, soft, irregular ticking and chiming as hundreds brush past each other without ever colliding quite.

None of them hunt in the way you know. There are no eyes hunting, no teeth chasing. They are all strains and sieves. The bells pulse to pull water through their fringes. The screws spin to create a vortex that draws detritus off the belts below. The plated shrimp pick the moult and mucus off each other's backs. They bump, stick for a moment, exchange a smear of bacteria, and spin away.

If you hold still in the middle, they will not part for you. They will brush you. Cool, smooth, faintly vibrating bodies sliding over your arms, your cheeks. Not afraid. Not curious. You are just another slow obstruction in a very crowded room. You can see every wall of the cirque at once from here, a perfect, sharp room, and the room is full of this patient, luminous suspension.

And above everything, the vent pulse keeps time. THUMP - hiss. *Clack.* HUMMMM. Chime, sigh, screw.

It is deafening and it is slow. That's the strangeness. A factory with no workers, running at one beat per twenty seconds, where a product takes a season to be finished and no one is in a hurry because the food will still be coming from the rock tomorrow. The animals have not invented industry; they have grown into the shape industry already had to have. A piston wants to be a bivalve. A conveyor wants to be a colony. A propeller wants to be a comb jelly.

They are abundant beyond any ocean you know at this altitude because they have no competition. No fish that needs algae, no weed that needs soil. Just stone-breath and time. And they are waiting, without knowing they are waiting, for the one thing that will end them: the day erosion finally finds a weakness in that knife-sharp rim and saws a notch. Then this little perched sea, with all its clacking and humming and slow screwing, will pour itself down the mountain in a white rush, and find out what it is to be a river.

Until then, it holds.
