They are not what you expect, at first.

From the lip of the moor, looking straight down a hundred feet onto that lid of thatch and shingle, you don't see people at all. You see motion. The whole floor of the Scar seems to crawl, low and busy and muted, like the inside of a hive seen through smoked glass. A hand appears to haul a rope. A back bends over a gutter. Someone crosses a roof on all fours where the pitch is too steep to walk, then drops out of sight down a fissure that is a street. The scale is wrong. Because there is no street, no square, no distance between things to measure them against, you think at first they must be small. Insect-small. Then a pulley creaks and a pine trunk as long as a house is lowered from the moor, swaying, and three figures steady it on a rooftop and you recalibrate — no, they are our size. It's the city that makes them look small.

Closer, if you find the switchback stair cut into the southern wall and go down - and you do it slowly, because the stone is fresh and your boots slip on the seep - they resolve.

They are people.

Not a different kind of people. The same kind that lives on the moor, but altered as water is altered by the cup that holds it.

They are compact. Not short in the way of a storybook people, but built low to the ground and wide through the shoulders and hips. A life lived where everything is carried - timber lowered by hand-rope, water hauled in buckets from cistern to kitchen, stone chipped one wedge at a time - will do that in a single generation, let alone two. The children look like small adults already, thick-wristed. The old are not stooped so much as bowed, permanently shaped to the height of their low doorways and the tunnels between houses where you must duck under a beam.

Their skin tells you what the light is like down here. It is not the pallor of cave people; the Scar is open, remember, and at noon the sun hits the bottom like a hammer. But the sun only hits it straight for an hour or two. The rest of the day half the city is in the wall's shadow, and the light that does reach them is reflected off limestone. So they are pale, but not white — a cool, even grey-pink, like the underside of an arm, with cheeks that redden quickly in the wind when they do go topside. Their hair, where it isn't shaved for lice, is mostly dark and cut short and straight around, because long hair catches on timber and soot. Their eyes are the most changed. They squint less than moor people do. Moor people squint always against the wind and the huge sky. Scar people have eyes that are good at contrast, at picking out detail in that sudden transition from hard noon glare to deep wall-shade. They don't stare at the horizon; there isn't one. They look up and down.

And they are quiet. That is the first thing you learn if you stand among them.

You expect a packed city to be loud. This one is full — so full you cannot stand anywhere on the floor without touching someone's wall, someone's ladder, someone's bean-frame — and yet the limestone drinks sound and the thatch smothers it and the people themselves have learned not to add to it. They speak low, and not much. A mumbled word to guide a beam, a soft call from roof to alley: *"take," "hold," "clear."* Children do not shriek here; they learn early that a shout bounces once off the walls and is gone and everybody heard that it was you. Even their work is quieted by necessity. The adze is muffled with cloth where it would ring on stone. The loom is set on straw. When the stone-cutters work — two men with iron wedges and a maul, tapping, tapping, listening — the whole near row of houses stops to listen with them, because everyone knows that clean crack like a gunshot means a slab is coming down.

You see how the geometry has shaped their manners.

Because you cannot waste vertical space, they have no idea of privacy as surface people do. A family of five lives in two rooms, one above the other, with a ladder through a hole in the floor. The roof of the lower family is the yard of the upper. You hear your neighbor's cough through your ceiling, you smell their stew, you step over their sleeping child to get to your own cistern. No one apologizes for this; to apologize would imply it could be otherwise. They have instead a complex etiquette of not-noticing — eyes lowered on the crowded roof-walks, a hand raised briefly to ask passage through a fissure-street that is only as wide as a man's shoulders, the way they hang a dyed cloth over a doorway when they want not to be seen, and everyone else simply does not look.

Because you cannot block water, they are fanatical about shared things. The central drainage channel is not a sewer, it is a law. Each morning the channel-keepers — older women mostly, who can no longer climb well — walk its length with a hooked pole, clearing moss and lost shingles and the waste that someone tried to hide. If your house floods after a rain because your gutter was choked, no one helps you bail; if the channel floods because you dammed it with a new foundation, everyone helps you tear the foundation out. Water discipline is the only real government they have. There is no mayor down there, no hall. There are the keepers of the channel, and the listeners who know stone, and otherwise families and work-crews who make their own bargains.

Because the stone is fresh and still settling, they are attentive in a way that looks like nervousness but is really reverence. Every child can tell you by the sound whether a wall is safe to lean against. They pat the limestone as they pass it, not superstitiously — practically, feeling for damp or vibration. Where a family has cut a shallow room into the wall, they leave a pillar of raw rock in the middle of it even though it wastes space, and oil it so it doesn't dry and crack. The old blocks that fell on the first days and were too heavy to move — each one has been incorporated, given a name, become a hearth or a corner shrine or simply left as a reminder that the walls are not finished. They do not build monuments. The Scar itself is monument enough, too new to be ignored.

Their clothes show the same logic. Wool, undyed or dyed with the thin yellows and rusts you can get from lichen and iron seep, layered and patched. Sleeves tight to the wrist so they don't catch. Knees and elbows reinforced with leather because everyone is always kneeling, crouching, climbing. Feet mostly bare indoors — the limestone floor is cool and they know every dip of it — and wrapped in soft hide outside, quiet on the roofs. Everything is mended. Nothing is thrown away that can be retied. Even the air smells mended: woodsmoke and wet stone and boiled barley and the faint, sharp tang of the chert dust that never quite leaves their hands.

Watch them at midday when the sun is a white strip directly overhead and the whole Scar is lit without shadow. That is when they do the sun-work: turning the beans on their frames, laying out hides, spreading barley on flat roofs to dry before the seep returns. Old men sit on the highest roofs with their faces tipped straight up, eyes closed, taking the light like a ration. At morning and evening, when one wall glows amber and the other is deep blue shade, they shift — the whole city migrates a few feet toward the light without anyone telling it to. And at night, when the sky-strip is dark and the stars are only visible as a river overhead, they close their shutters — not for warmth, though it is always cool enough that your breath mists — but to hold the small light of their tallow and oil lamps inside, so the Scar from above would look, if you could see it, like a long, low ember bed, breathing quietly between stone lips.

They do not think of themselves as strange. If you ask where they are from — and you have to ask loudly enough that they frown a little at the noise — they will say they are from here, from the floor. The moor is "up there," a place you go to cut wood or to trade for copper, a windy, bright, exposed place that hurts the eyes. Some of them were born before the Scar opened — you can tell by the way they still flinch at the gunshot crack — and some have only ever known the cool draft that moves north to south along the trench, carrying the smell of the next family's dinner. But all of them now are fit to it. Saturated to it. Packed to every inch the way their houses are packed, shoulder to shoulder, hand to hand, listening to the stone and tending the water that will not stand still.

They are not a mythic people. They are not tall or terrible or luminous. They are what happens when ordinary, practical people are given a recent wound in the world and told to live in it, without plan or waste, under a sky that has become a narrow river.
