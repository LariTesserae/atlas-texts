It doesn't live here the way you were taught to look for life.

At first you think nothing does. That's the trick of the pocket. You crouch with your knee sinking into that cold mattress and your light on, and you see green — and you think *plant*, singular, carpet, decoration. Something that *is* rather than *does*. You have to turn your light off to understand that you're wrong.

Turn it off. Let the dark come back and settle behind your eyes. Wait.

What lives here is not a thing in the place. It *is* the place.

### The Mat

There is only one resident, but it is made of thousands. Don't settle on the word "moss" too quickly — that makes you picture something soft from a forest floor, something that wants sun.

What lives on the floor has forgotten sun.

Up close, with your face inches from it in the fungus-light, it resolves. It is not a carpet. It is a city with no streets. A forest with no canopy. A body with no center.

The bulk of it is cushion moss and liverwort — not the bright green of spring, but a deep, bruised green so saturated it looks black until the faint light catches a bead of water on a leaf tip and makes it flare. Antarctic star-moss, *Syntrichia* strains that were engineered to scrub heavy metals off concrete dust, threadwort liverworts the size of a fingernail, a black-green crustose lichen that has never been named because it was never meant to exist outside a petri dish. They escaped. Or they were spilled. It doesn't matter now.

None of them photosynthesize anymore, not really. There is nothing to photosynthesize *with*. The light down here is their own. They live the way deep-sea things live — on chemistry and difference. They eat the basalt itself, very slowly, leaching iron and silicate with acids so weak you would have to watch for a decade to see the rock pit. They eat the stone-dust that was left when the boring machine chewed this void open. They eat the almost-nothing thermal gradient held open by the pylons — that fraction of a degree between the disc that keeps water liquid and the permafrost that wants to freeze it. That is a feast, if you are patient enough. And they are patient beyond any animal definition of patience.

A single frond grows a millimeter a year. Maybe less. The rosettes are dense, overlapping, leaf over leaf over leaf, each leaf rimed with frost-burn at its edge and swollen and waxy at its center. Each leaf holds one perfect bead of condensation at its tip, and that bead acts as a lens for the only light there is. From a distance it looks like fur. From two inches away you see it is architecture. Hundreds of individuals per square centimeter, each one complete, each one pressed shoulder-to-shoulder with its neighbors so tightly you cannot find the basalt underneath without tearing them.

And they have learned not to compete. In a warm place they would crowd each other out. Here they have braided together.

The lichen is the skin. It forms the outer crust on the raised spine in the middle and along the wall bases, a leathery, black-green sheet that cracks with frost and seals itself again with new growth. The moss is the muscle — the thick, springy mattress twenty centimeters deep that gives under your knee with a wet sigh and then slowly, slowly pushes back, cold soaking through your trousers. The liverwort is the understory, filling every seam between the moss hummocks with translucent, liver-colored plates no bigger than a coin.

And between them, bridging them, are the fungi.

Not mushrooms. Nothing here has the energy to waste on a stalk and a cap that will rot in a week. They are shelves — flat, lacquered, the color of old blood and wet paper, jutting from the mat where the pylons make the air a fraction warmer. They are not bright. You will never see them glow if your headlamp is on. In the dark, after your eyes have given up on looking *for* light and start noticing light that is *there*, you see it: a grey-green suggestion on your retina, a chemistry-light, luciferin without purpose. They are not signaling. They are not attracting. They are just metabolizing, and a byproduct of that metabolism is photons. Enough to see the texture of the moss by. Enough for the moss to see itself.

They are the same organism, really. The mycelium runs under everything, white and thread-fine, through the peat layers below. It doesn't eat the moss so much as it *negotiates* with it — trading water for sugars made from rock, carrying chemical messages a centimeter an hour from one side of the pocket to the other. If you touch one shelf, very gently, the faint light in the shelf two feet away dims for a moment, as if the whole mat held its breath.

Because nothing eats it, it has learned to eat itself.

That is the other thing you notice when you stay still and cold starts to make your hands ache. There is no rot-smell. Rot is warm. This smells like peat and clean iron, like a bog in late November. When a frond dies — and they do, at the edges where frost-burn wins — it doesn't disappear. No mites to shred it, no flies to lay eggs in it, no bacteria warm enough to really putrefy it. It darkens, it compresses, it is pressed down by the living layer above it into the mat. Layer after layer after layer, living green on top of dark brown on top of black, until twenty centimeters down it is no longer plant but a thin, cold coal, a memory of leaves. The mat is growing *downward* and *inward*, making its own soil out of its own dead. If you could cut a core from it you could count the years like tree rings, back to when the seam first cracked and the first spore drifted in on a draft that no longer exists.

It knows the built things and has made a truce with them.

It climbs the knee-high pylons in a perfect collar of moss, tight and attentive, but never covers the dull disc on top. A millimeter of clearance, kept exact. It has learned that the disc is the heart that keeps water liquid. To cover it would be to blind itself. Between the pylons, the fungal shelves use them as anchors, spanning the gaps like tables.

On the ceiling, the thick service conduit that sags in its catenary is furred with something else entirely — a pale, filamentous lichen that follows the faint electromagnetic field inside the cable like a vine follows a trellis. It is white-grey and hair-fine, trembling even when the air is perfectly still. It does not glow. It twitches, once every few hours, when the pylons cycle.

You can feel that cycle if you stay long enough. It is not a sound. It is a pressure in your molars, a subsonic *tick* as the field corrects, and then the whole mat exhales. Not metaphorically. The leaves, holding that fraction of a degree of warmth, adjust, and a wave of settling moves across the floor — a barely audible sigh as ten thousand tiny leaves shift a tenth of a millimeter.

It has no eyes. No mouth. No center where you can point and say *here is the animal*. It does not notice you the way an animal would. You are a sudden, wasteful bloom of heat and noise, sixty kilograms of warmth that does not belong in its accounting. When you knelt, you compressed it, and the water you squeezed out will take hours to bead again. Your breath made a cloud that condensed on the basalt and became one more drop that will fall, *tick*, onto its surface sometime after you have left.

It will not remember you. Not as a person. But for a few days where your knee was, the moss will be slightly darker, slightly more open, as it repairs the bruise you left, leaf by leaf, bead by bead.

You are already too warm to stay. Your shivering is becoming continuous, and the pocket has no use for shivering. When you stand and squeeze sideways back through that scraped seam into the dry, dusty, human-warm tunnel, you will carry a few spores on your boot tread without knowing.

Behind you, in the dark you made again by leaving, a drop will gather on the low ceiling. It will swell, it will catch the grey-green fungus light in its curve, and it will fall.

The mat will be there to catch it.
