Nothing lives here alone.

That is the first mistake your eyes make. You stand in the crack-light for ten minutes while your breath goes still, and you think the pocket is empty except for plant. A room made of moss. Walls that weep, floor that gives like a lung, root-columns gone furry with green-black felt. You think: this is where the town's builders left off, and the plant took over.

Then the plant blinks.

Not all at once. A patch of wall about the size of your hand shivers and resettles, and you realize it was never wall. It was back-to-back-to-back bodies, fitted so tight the moss showed only between them.

What lives here lives as *coverage*. As totality. The town above is sparse by design — houses set far apart with a lot of frozen sky and black water between them, each fence meaning *mine*. Down here the rule is inverted. There is no between. To be is to be touching something else that is also alive.

Let it become more itself while you watch:

At the smallest scale, the peat itself is not ground. It is a mat. Sphagnum and liverwort and cold-thread moss braided so thick they've become structure — pillars, curtains, shelves — but the braid is never just plant. If you kneel and put your eyes close to the floor where the water beads and falls and beads again, you see the mat is being constantly turned. 

Woodlice — pale, translucent, the smallest the size of a fingernail paring, the largest as long as your thumb joint — move through it like slow, blind livestock, chewing, excreting, aerating. They are not *on* the moss, they are *part of* its metabolism. Behind them come mites too small to see as individuals, only as a faint dust that makes the green look like it is breathing. On the underside of every root-column, a second city hangs upside down — springtails and pale crickets, long-antennaed, eyeless or nearly so, that carpet the ceiling in a living felt. When you shift your weight and the peat sighs, a ripple goes across the wall two meters away where none of your shadow fell. The wall rearranges. A thousand legs find a new purchase without ever exposing stone.

They are the Maintainers. They keep the wet from becoming rot. They eat the fallen, but slowly — the cold here has put everything on pause. That is why nothing seems to decay.

A little larger, and the pocket finds its shape.

Spiders have made this place legible. Dozens of kinds, from pinhead black specks to long-jawed, glassy-legged things the size of your palm, have strung the root-columns together with sheets. Not spirals, not traps the way the summer forest does it — horizontal sails, hammocks, veils that collect the condensation that is always falling *tit... tit... tit* from every tip. In the cold shaft from the crack you can see the midges hanging. They don't hum. They don't bite. A cloud of grey motes that holds perfectly still in air barely above freezing, and every so often one touches a sheet and sticks and does not struggle. The spiders don't rush. The sheets are not for hunting so much as plumbing — every web glittering with droplets that run along silk to the nearest moss, which drinks. The spiders tend their cisterns.

Moving through the sheets, armored and indifferent: the beetles. Flat-backed, black and then iridescent when the faint lichen-light hits them at an angle. Each one carries a garden. Moss and liverwort have taken root in the pits of their elytra, tiny, perfect cushions that they prune and carry with them. When a beetle stops, you lose it. It becomes wall again. When it moves, the patch of wall walks.

And above all that — the ones that watch back.

The salamanders.

There are not many, which makes them feel more deliberate. Ash-grey, the color of wet basalt, with a sudden, soft orange underneath. They hang from the roots and from the tarred bottoms of the town's larch pilings — those pilings now totems, furred head to foot in white lichen that gives the only light, a light so faint your eyes had to learn it — and they hang like fruit. They do not skitter. They hold still for so long condensation beads on their heads and falls from their noses. Their toes are wide and pale and they grip the moss without disturbing it. Their throats pulse. One at eye level has been watching you since you entered. Its eyes are lidless and reflect the crack-light as two cold discs. When you breathe, it blinks. Slow. Once.

If the woodlice and springtails are the blood of this place, the salamanders are its memory. They live years in this one room. They know the shape of every shelf before a human builds it. They drop — *plip* — into shallow pools that have formed in old bootprints and never drain, and they wait there for hours for something to twitch.

On the floor, between your boots and the low, careful human things — the fish-platform on cross-beams where a three-year-old salt fish lies freeze-dried and then moss-wrapped, its eye now a beetle's nursery; the ladder that goes nowhere, its top rungs already overgrown; the little shrine of flat stones with its moss-double growing beside it, rehearsing it; the collapsed root-cellar wall turned into an arbor — there is a line.

White ants. Not true ants, but pale, winter ants the color of milk. They move in a single, unhurried column from the base of one tarred piling to another, each carrying a fleck of larch bark or peat fiber too small for you to have seen fall. They are building. Not a hill. Something low, and lipped. Like a tray. Like a shelf.

Like the empty moss shelf on the eastern wall that is exactly waist high and exactly crate-sized, that no one made. The pocket prototypes. The plant makes the negative before the hands make the positive. The column of ants seems to understand this. They will work for days, in silence, muffling their footsteps with the wet felt, and when they are done there will be a form waiting that looks useless until a human comes down and thinks, *that would be useful there.*

That is how the town lives here. Not the people. The *town* — the idea of sparse, primitive, human carpentry — lives here as a rumor the moss keeps retelling. The four structures are not inhabited. They are tended. They are interruptions in a density that is otherwise absolute. The inhabitants have grown around them, over them, through the cracks in them, and learned their shape.

There is no queen, no single mind. The inhabitant is the density itself. The isopod carpet that is also the wall. The beetle that is also a walking garden. The spider sail that is also a gutter. The salamander that is also a stillness. The mold-lichen that glows just enough to see by and no more, because anything brighter will be covered in seconds — not eaten, but tended, smothered with soft moth bodies and midge wings until the forbidden light dies. The pocket wants to stay dark. The moss grows toward the dark.

If you stay long enough and hold very still, it lets you see its real population count: not how many creatures, but how little empty space. You are the only empty space. You are a warm, upright, breathing gap in a room that has otherwise solved packing.

And it is waiting to see if you will become useful. If you will set something down on the shelf it made for you, or stack a stone where its moss-cairn already waits, or spill a little blood-warmth that the woodlice can turn over.

Above you, through the sharp-lipped crack, a door slams in Nizhny-Kol and an axe hits wood very far away. Down here the sound arrives filtered through water and peat, as if from another world. The salamander blinks. The wall ripples. The ants continue their white line across your bootprint, already erasing it.
