They don't live *on* it. No one lives on it. They live *in* it — in the negotiation.

You see them first as movement against the gray, because nothing here is ever still and neither are they. From a distance on one of those whale-backed domes, you might mistake them for part of the machinery — another rust-red stutter between the walkways — until one straightens up.

### The People Who Stayed

There were never many of them. 0.6 scattered, which is to say: enough to keep the pumps breathing, not enough to ever be a town.

They are not mountain people in the way you think of mountain people — lean and wind-burnt and peaked as the rocks. These are plateau people, drowned-plateau people, and the land has worn them smooth the same way it wore the granite.

They are short and broad. At four kilometers the air makes you pay for every tall breath, so the body learns to be compact. Barrel chests, thick necks, calves like poured concrete from a lifetime of walking on steel that never stops moving under you. Their faces are pale in the way that comes from living under cloud-scrape for generations — not sickly, but the pale of skim milk and wet wool — with the high, wind-flushed color permanently on their cheeks and noses. Your breath ghosts even in summer, and so does theirs, in little puffs that vanish into the mist that never actually leaves their skin.

They are always damp.

You can't understand them until you understand that. No one is ever dry here. Their hair is always plastered or frizzed with spray, their eyebrows beaded. The wool they wear — thick, undyed, shaggy sweaters from the feral musk-sheep that graze the moss, felted trousers — never fully dries. Over that, oilskin, rubber, battered canvas that has been re-waxed a hundred times until it shines black. Hip waders patched with tire rubber. Mittens with the trigger finger cut out. They smell like the place does: cold iron, peat smoke, lanolin, diesel, and the faint sourness of a body that has been wet for weeks.

They are loud, because they have to be. A child born here learns to shout before she learns to whisper. You cannot call softly over wind plus water roar plus the thud-whine-clank of a hydromill. So their voices are hoarse and carrying, their language flattened into short, practical sentences that can be heard over a spillway. They sing a lot while they work — not for beauty, but to time the pull of a chain or the push of a pole — low chanties that harmonize without trying to with the thrum of the generators.

What do they *do*?

They tend.

That's the word they use. Not operate, not rule. Tend, like shepherds. One family might tend a single pump house and the kilometer of walkway around it. Another tends a sluice gate where one drowned bowl pours into the next over that perfect, worn stone lip. Their day is a circuit, endless and turbulent as the water itself.

Morning — though morning is just a slightly less gray version of night — they wake in the turbine hall. No one builds a house separate from the work anymore; the old turbine halls are the houses, poured concrete stained black and green with seep, with sleeping lofts hammered together from scaffold plank above the thrumming machines. The heat from the generators is the only reliable heat, so laundry steams on lines strung between pipes. They drink black tea boiled with a pinch of salt and eat char split and smoked the night before, and fried cakes of mashed reed-root.

Then out. Boots on steel. The walkways are half-submerged and slick with algae and newt eggs, so they go carefully, one hand always on the cable rail. A man tests a pressure valve with a wrench the size of his forearm, steam sighing out and scalding the mist for a second. A woman clears reeds from an intake grate with a long rake, the water green and choked and alive around her knees. A teenager in waders poles a flat-bottomed punt through the shallows where the drowned meadow shows clear underneath, pushing aside floating mats of peat and sphagnum so thick you could stand on them — and sometimes the cattle do — to get to the cableway. The ore buckets still creak overhead, most empty now, some carrying peat bricks or fish gut to be rendered.

Every hour something needs unblocking, tightening, bleeding, coaxing. If they stop, the water will leave. That's the theology here. The sea wants to fall off the edge of the world in one smooth, catastrophic sheet. The ancient flat stone wants to lie still and be a bog. The compromise is work, constant, loud, hyperactive work. They don't fight the water. They give it places to go — spill-steps, turbine throats — so it doesn't take the rim all at once.

Children here swim before they walk well. By three they can hold their breath and slip under a walkway to free a snagged line, cheeks puffed, eyes open in the cold. Their hands are pruned from birth. Old people go deaf early from the generators and get a particular stoop from ducking under low pipe runs for fifty years. No one is in a hurry, but no one is ever still. Even at rest they rock slightly, as if the wave-chop is in their marrow.

They are not lonely, though they are scattered. At dusk — when the geese and cranes rise in their thousands off the slate-gray water in a deafening, echoing clatter that bounces off every pump house — the walkways fill with figures moving toward the nearest hall where the light is on. They gather because the dark is total and wet and the machines are warm. They share soup, they mend nets, they trade news about which floating island has drifted where, which new reed bed has choked a channel. They are unafraid of the density of life; midges cloud around their heads and they don't even wave them off anymore.

### The Ones Who Were Here First, And More Successfully

If the humans are the tenders, the true inhabitants — the ones at 2.0 dense, 2.2 lush — don't need tenders at all.

The reeds own the shallows. Not decorative reeds, but a wall of them, shoulder-high, thick as a crowd, stems crowded so tight you have to shoulder through and the boat has to be poled, not rowed. They hiss in the wind. Under them, the water vibrates at night with chorus frogs — thousands of small, dark, cold-water frogs with voices far too large for their bodies, making the stems themselves shake.

On every piling, every rusted strut, cling the newts. Small, black-brown, with orange bellies you see when they flick. They never leave the water line, moving up an inch when the wind pushes a wave, down an inch when it recedes, perfect tide-keepers where there is no tide.

Under the surface, the char and trout are fat beyond reason — cold slows them, plenty feeds them. They hang in the green water, silver flashes when they turn sideways, so numerous that a thrown net comes up writhing. They eat the endless rain of midges and stoneflies that hatch in clouds so thick they show on the air like smoke. The birds eat the fish, and the air eats the birds.

And above, the birds are a weather system themselves. Not a few gulls — geese, cranes, ducks, rails — in numbers that make the sky audible. They nest on the floating peat mats, those drifting sphagnum islands that bob and hold together by root alone, strong enough to hold a grazing sheep. At any hour something is lifting, something is landing, crying that flat, carrying northern cry that confusion makes you think is machinery.

On the domes that break the surface, where the black peaty soil is thickest, the moss is the ground. You don't walk on stone, you walk on a mattress of it, ankle-deep, saturated, springing back. Dwarf willow and birch grow sideways, their roots forever wet, their leaves small and waxy. The feral cattle and musk-sheep — shaggy, barrel-bodied, coats dripping and matted with burrs — move through it slowly, utterly indifferent to people, to rain, to wind. They are as much a part of the hydrology as the pumps, cropping the moss so it doesn't seal over the stone entirely, their shit feeding the peat that feeds the water.

And the vegetation below — forests of cold-water weed, kelp-like but freshwater, fronds broad as your forearm, dark green and swaying just under the chop — holds the whole perched ocean together like hair. Phytoplankton turns the deeper bowls that dark green-black, opaque with life. You can smell it: that lush, slightly sulfurous, fertile stink of a world that is rotting and growing at the same speed.

### The Other Life

And then there are the machines, which you have to count as alive here, because they are as dense and as loud and as necessary as the frogs.

A pump house is not a building. It's a chest. It breathes — diesel cough, electric whine, steam sigh — on a cycle that never stops. Pressure valves pop and weep. Cableways creak even when the buckets are empty, swaying in the wind that funnels up over the plateau rim. Sluice gates groan as water forces past them over those smooth, undercut stone lips. The rust is not decay, it's a skin. Algae grows on the concrete, mussels colonize the submerged struts, newts lay eggs in the overflow tanks. If a generator dies, the men and women converge on it the way white blood cells converge on a wound, because a stopped heart here means the water finds a new way down.

Stand on that low hump — that old mountain worn to a hill — and look out over one valley-sized piece of it all: slate water to the cloud, walkways stitching green hummocks, a turbine hall steaming on the next dome like a kettle left on, a drifting island of peat with a lone sheep on it turning slowly in the current, a flock wheeling, reeds bowing, waves slapping stone. You can hear everything at once and it resolves, after a minute, not into noise but into a single, crowded, working chord.

Nothing pristine. Nothing quiet. Nothing still.

Just a very old, very flat, very high place that should be empty stone and wind, instead stubbornly, damply, loudly, completely full — kept full — by everything that has learned to live with its feet wet and its ear to the pump.
