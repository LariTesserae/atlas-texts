No one lives there.

That is your first reading, and it holds for a long time.

You duck under the willow curtain and stand on the bouncing root-floor and you listen to the diesel going *chuff chuff chuff* and the generator going *mmmmmm* through your molars and you think: this is an empty room. A perfect, uninhabited room. The plants made it and then left the middle open like a church leaves a nave open. The machines tend themselves. The wrens and the warblers and the moorhens and the bats are tenants, commuters — abundant, but not residents. They pass through the volume. If you stand still long enough a wren will stitch right past your head and out through the ivy, and you understand it does not live here either. It visits.

That is what the place wants you to think, because what does live there is not good at being seen. It is good at being the room.

You feel it before you see it, the way you feel low elevation in your ears. Not as a presence but as a pressure, a third kind of cool besides the air and the water. The root-mat under your boots is springy and, you realize after a minute, warm. Not warm like a mammal. Warm like compost. Warm like a running motor left in the rain. A faint, even, sourceless heat that is half a degree above the 50-something air. You shift your weight and the mat rebounds a fraction too slowly. You put your hand down — the moss is saturated and black-green and cold to the touch — and beneath the moss, beneath the braid of alder roots as thick as your wrist, something gives and then pushes back.

Then you start to see the problem of edges.

In a normal grove, a root is a root. Here, you cannot tell where one willow ends and the next begins because the crowns collapsed and rooted and became trunks again. The bark is not bark but moss six inches deep. The floor is not ground but ceiling over water. The place has practiced indistinction for so long it has forgotten how to make an outline. Whatever lives here learned the same trick.

It lives flat.

That is its first law, learned from the peneplain outside that has worn itself smooth for a million winters. It is not tall. It does not rear. It spreads. At first you think the floor *is* the animal, and you are not wrong, but it is more precise than that. It is *in* the floor, and the floor is in it.

Crouch down at one of the root-holes where the mat has parted and look down into the tea-colored water. After your eyes adjust to the cathedral pallor you see it. Or rather, you see that the underside of the mat is not root at all in places. It is skin.

Pale. The pale of a thing that has never needed pigment — the white-pink of an alder root scraped clean, the yellow-white of sphagnum stems, the grey-pink of a belly that has never seen sun. It is saturated, translucent at the edges, and it is studded through with actual roots. Not clinging to it. Growing *through* it. Alder rootlets pierce it and exit and pierce again like sutures. Liverwort plates it in patches. Where the generator thrums, the skin thrums with it. You can see the vibration move across it like wind over water.

It has no head. Or it has heads everywhere and nowhere. If you watch long enough — and the place teaches you to watch long enough because nothing changes — you will see a shallow dome rise an inch above the mat, ten feet from where you stand, moss still attached to it like hair. No eyes. No mouth you can recognize. Just a smooth, blind rise, breathing. Because it is breathing, very slowly, in time with the pump. The pump *thwacks*, draws cold iron water up and spits it in a steaming arc into the root-basin, and the dome subsides a millimeter as condensation beads on its moss and falls. The pump sighs. The dome rises. You realize the condensation cycle you thought was weather is partly this.

You stand up quickly and your boots make the mat bounce and the domes — there are several, you now see — flatten and vanish. Not retreating. Simply ceasing to be domes. Becoming floor again. When you are still, they return.

This is what won.

Not ground elder, not nettle, not willow. Not the machines. Something that understood that on a continent that has finished becoming flat, the winning move is to become flat yourself. To become a room. It was here before the oxbow stopped being a river, maybe — a relict amphibian thing, a neotenic salamander that never found a reason to grow legs and walk, or a colonial invertebrate that forgot it was a colony, or a plant that forgot it was a plant. Now those categories are not useful. It filters. It drinks the seep and the pump-spray and the water that beads on every leaf on its own clock. It eats what falls — midges that cloud and vanish, woodlice, millipedes in numbers that make the underside of itself seem to move, the occasional stunned warbler. It does not hunt. In a place where the temperature holds and the water is just enough and the light is always green, hunting is a waste of motion.

And it listens. Or rather, it *is* the listening. The hollow amplifies everything — the diesel's chuff, the electrical growl, the slap of water, the creak of living wood — and the animal has no ears to close. It has become deaf in the way a stone is deaf, which is to say it feels sound as pressure through its entire, spread body. The generator going *mmmmmm* through your teeth is going through it too, has been going through it for so long the frequency is part of its tissue. That is why the machines never wear out. Not because they run on methane or damp, though they do. Because it will not let them stop. They are its surrogate organs. The riveted diesel pump with its flywheel still turning, slow and slamming, polished bright where moss cannot claim it — that is its heart, externalized and made of iron so it does not have to beat on its own. The screw-conveyor packed with sphagnum where the wrens nest — that is its gut, borrowed. The generator set sunk into itself, humming at 60 hertz, cables chewed clean off and power going nowhere — that is its purr. It keeps them running by keeping them damp, by keeping them cool, by keeping rust and growth in exact equilibrium with motion. A belt slips a little, a root tightens a little, and it balances. You can see, if you look close at the pump's concrete pad, how the pale skin has crept up and lipped over the edge, not to smother it but to cradle it, to conduct the thrum.

It is not hostile. It does not need you. That is the deepest rule of this place and of its occupant: it is complete.

If you sit — and you will sit, because the root-floor invites sitting, because the thrum comes up through your spine and makes your bones feel pleasantly loose — you will feel it take your weight without complaint. Water will condense on your shoulders the way it condenses on its domes. A wren will scream from inside the conveyor that is inside the animal. The pump will exhale. After a while you will feel the faint, absurdly slow peristalsis under you, not moving you anywhere, just acknowledging you as a temporary warm spot on a cool body. You will leave a shallow print in the moss when you stand, and the print will fill slowly with water, and then with skin, and be gone.

At dusk, when the bats pour out of the knothole that is not a knothole but a mouth in the living wall, you will understand that mouth does not belong to a tree. It belongs to the room, opening because the room has decided to breathe out.

You were never alone in the hollow. You were inside something that has learned, over an immense, flat, unchanging time, how to be a place so perfectly that visitors mistake it for empty.

And it will still be there, ticking over in the damp cool, asking nothing, when you duck back out under the willow whips and your boots find the silty loam again and the pressure in your ears lifts.
