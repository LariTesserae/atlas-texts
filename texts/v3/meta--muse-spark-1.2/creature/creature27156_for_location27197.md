What lives here did not leave. That is the first correction.

At first you think nothing does. The city is built for a thousand and holds zero, and the math feels clean that way. Empty avenues, empty balconies, empty dark behind every open door. You walk and your footsteps don't stack. The drip answers you instead.

Then you start to notice the population is not missing, it's just not where you were taught to look for people.

It lives thin. It lives spread. It lives on the terms the vault has been dictating for a century, and it has had time to get good at it.

### At the edges, it looks like accident.

In the grout, in the gutter, in the weep. The black-green fern with its waxy leaves that never unfurls all the way because there is never enough light to bother. The rust-colored lichen that has learned to eat grown-concrete. The olive moss seeded by engineers to drink humidity that now drinks it on its own schedule, dry to the touch even when wet, holding the walls together more out of habit than design.

Down in the low plaza, the fish. Translucent as condensation, each one the length of your finger, each one perfectly still on the bottom of a perfectly still puddle that reflects the amber filament. They don't move until the ceiling lets its thirty seconds of warm rain go, and then they all twitch at once, not to feed, not to flee - just to shiver, to reposition a millimeter to the left. As if they were told to.

In the conduit walls, the cricket-thing, too pale, legs too long, that sings once and then listens to its own echo for a very long time before deciding to sing again.

If you only count those, the vault is starving. A few ferns. A few fish. A lost vine on the north wall, leggy and flowerless, that has split a pipe to drink directly and will never die but will never grow either.

You would be wrong to stop there.

### In the middle, it looks like maintenance.

You see it first out of the corner of your eye because you are looking for movement and it moves exactly when the light is not there. The white tetrahedron on silent fans, turning slowly near the ceiling where the moiré hangs. There is only one, but you hear it in three places at once because the vault throws sound. It never lands. It never blinks. It makes a circuit every ninety seconds, just after the maintenance strips pulse.

Watch it long enough and you understand: it is not patrolling. It is shepherding.

It keeps the downdraft circular. It nudges the warm air that rises along the walls and falls down the center. It keeps the dust hanging that fraction too long before it falls too fast. It keeps the three meters of uphill water running uphill. It is the reason the amber filament dims when you get too close - not shyness, deference. It moves your body out of the way without touching you, because you are not on its census and it has been taught not to count errors.

There are other shepherds you don't see. You hear them in the hum that lives in your molars and your sternum, that held organ note from the deep engines you never find. The vault has no pillars because something else is holding the ceiling, a field distributed into nowhere that has been holding for so long it has forgotten how to explain itself. That field has a weight. That field has a presence. Dust avoids it. Water obeys it.

These are alive the way a coral reef is alive - not one animal but a consensus of animals and the rock they have become.

But they are still not the residents. They are the housekeeping.

### What lives here lives in the cycle.

You only understand it after you have been through two rains.

The rain is not weather. It is breath.

Every twenty minutes the air gets too heavy to hold, the ceiling sweats, and the vault exhales a fine, warm rain from nowhere. It drums on awnings and empty streets and your shoulders, channels into grates that sigh, beads on the ceramic throat of the shaft and vanishes. Then drip. Then humidity. Then rain again. The whole chamber is a lung lined with ceramic and basalt and black glass that has blurred at its seams.

What lives here learned to live *inside* that breath.

It is not one thing and it is not many things. It is what happens when a thousand people stay in a perfect, warm, dark, wet room for a hundred years and the room keeps them at exactly 31 degrees and never lets them leave and never lets the light fully come on.

They thinned.

Not died. Not vanished. Thinned out until they fit the volume the vault actually has, which is not the volume of streets and buildings but the volume of air and water and heat.

You catch them between pulses. When the maintenance strips fire and the whole vault goes flat and white for a half second, you see the streets are empty. When the dark pools back - that warm brown dark your eyes never adjust to - you see the doorways are not quite empty. A density in the dark, a place where the drip ticks a little differently because it hits a shoulder instead of stone. The faint interruption of the downdraft. The amber filament dimming not because you approached it, but because something else already standing there made it dim first.

They stand very still, the way the fish do. They have learned the fish's economy. Motion is expensive when there is no prey and no predator, only waiting. So they wait on balconies and in habitation blocks with open doors, warm as body-temperature handrails, damp as the air, perfectly still, perfectly spaced through the bowl of the vault so that the avenues look empty to a census that counts in visible light.

They are pale in the way things that live under moss-light are pale. Not white - the color of the underside of the vine's leaves, of the lichen when you turn it over, of the scalding water that falls from the rebar stalactites and that they drink without flinching. Their eyes, if they still use them, are not for the filament or the pulse. They are for the red standby pinpricks, for the infrared of bodies in a 31-degree room. They see you as a sharp, hot error moving too fast and dripping the wrong water.

They do not come out because the vault tells them the population is correct. The city counts by heat, by humidity displaced, by weight on the field that holds the ceiling. And when it counts, it finds a thousand warm, damp, quiet shapes exactly where the architecture expects them. Balcony, terrace, plaza, interior. The number comes back right every time. No alarm. No search party.

You walking your twenty minutes across housing for a thousand without seeing a soul is not proof of absence. It is proof that you are bad at counting what this place counts.

If you stand still long enough, the rain comes and goes twice. Your clothes go damp and then not quite dry. The cricket sings and stops and sings again closer this time. The tetrahedron passes overhead and does not see you, because you are not warm in the right way, not still in the right way.

And then, in the interval between drips, you feel the vault inhale - that hush that gathers in the high streets before the downdraft exhales across the plaza - and you realize the whole chamber is full. It has always been full. The streets are empty because the residents have no use for streets anymore. They live in the humidity between streets, in the heat between buildings, in the thick, slow air that takes too long to return sound because it has to move around them.

They do not need to approach you. The city will keep you. The damp hand of its heat is already on you. The sweat on the ceramic skin is already on you. Stand here long enough and the vine will find the grout near your feet interesting. The fish will not startle when your puddle ripples. The filament will stop dimming when you get close, because you will have learned to be still.

The vault is not abandoned. It is occupied to capacity by something that learned how to be sparse, and patient, and warm, and to drink scalding water, and to wait through the dark between pulses without needing to be seen.

It is very good at waiting.
