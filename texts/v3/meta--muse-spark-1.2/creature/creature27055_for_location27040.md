It doesn't live the way things live outside.

Outside, living is noisy and hungry and always leaning toward the sun. In here, living is what learned how to stay when there was no reason to stay.

There is not much of it. That is the first law of this place. Whatever lives here lives sparsely, thinly, patiently — because the chamber doesn't feed you. It *tolerates* you.

### The Film

The true resident is not something you can see until your eyes give up and your hands learn to see instead.

Crouch down and run your palm over the warm basalt. It feels slick. Not wet like a rock in a stream, but slick like skin. Every surface that weeps — floor, wall, the knees of those half-formed columns — is coated in a biofilm barely thicker than the water itself. 

It is bacterial. It has to be. Nothing else could eat stone.

It's a matte of chemolithotrophs, pale cream and rust-orange where the iron is richer, that drinks directly from the rock's warmth and the dissolved sulfur and iron in the seep. It has no color because it has never needed color. In the absolute dark there is no green, no photosynthesis, only chemistry. If you put your nose close you can smell it — that faint iron-and-sweat tang underneath the mineral sweetness. That is the meadow here. That is the pasture. A living varnish spread microns deep across every bead of condensation, and it is what makes the rock feel alive and slightly soft under your hand.

Everything else that lives here, lives off it.

### The Pale Smears

The ferns were a mistake that decided to keep going.

There are maybe a dozen of them in the whole twelve-meter womb, and you will not find them by looking. You find them by the way the water breaks around them. They cling where two weep-tracks intersect on the wall, where the film is thickest.

They are not really ferns anymore. They are the idea of ferns, bleached of all pretense. Translucent white, almost clear, the fronds no longer than your thumb joint, etiolated — stretched thin and desperate in a way that has become elegant. No chlorophyll to speak of, just a ghostly pink where the stem carries water. They don't grow toward light because there is no light to grow toward. They grow toward wetness, following the bead-paths down the rock like slow, patient fingers.

They are not lush. They are not thriving. They are *persisting*. Each new frond takes a season to uncurl, stays soft and wet and never hardens. If you breathe on one it shivers. They survive by stealing a little from the film and by catching what little organic dust drifts in when the seam cracks wider in a cold year. They are sparse, scattered, high-saturation in reverse — a few lonely presences in a room crowded with stone.

Touch one and it feels like wet paper. Leave it alone.

### The Walker

If the film is the meadow and the ferns are its weeds, he is its animal.

You won't hear him come. You will feel a coolness cross the back of your hand after you have been still for a very long time — long enough that your own sweat has started to match the temperature of the rock, long enough that you have stopped being a warm intruder and become another warm stone.

He is the length of your index finger, or a little longer if he has had a good century. A neotenic salamander — the mountain's private olm. In the absolute dark, pigment is a waste, so he has none. His skin is translucent pink-white, and you can see the faint shadow of his spine and the darker knot of his heart beating absurdly slowly through his flank. His head is flat and blunt, his eyes are not eyes at all but two black, skin-covered pinpricks that never opened. Instead he wears his gills outside his body, three frills on each side of his neck like lace made of blood, waving gently even when he is still, sifting oxygen from the saturated air and the film-water.

He lives his entire life in the millimeter-deep sheet of water that covers the floor. He does not swim. He walks — unhurried, deliberate, each foot splayed too wide, because hurry would cost more than this chamber can afford. He grazes. He drags his soft chin across the biofilm and licks it up, and he snaps up the other things that live in the film with it: springtails the size of poppy seeds, white and blind and hopping in slow motion; translucent isopods like grains of rice that you only see when they move. He is the apex here because there is nothing above him to be. 

He has no fear. He has never had a predator. When he walks over your hand he does not flinch. His belly is cool, cooler than the rock, and his toes are so soft you barely feel the pressure. He will pause on your knuckle, gills fanning, tasting your skin-salt, and then continue on his circuit — the same ten-meter circuit he has been making for five years, for ten, from one drip-bowl to another, in perfect, zero-dynamic equilibrium with the *tok... tok... tok* of the straws above.

There may be three of him. There may be seven. Never many. The chamber cannot carry many. They meet rarely, exchange a cloud of sperm into the water film, leave eggs glued to the underside of a stalagmite, and go back to walking. Most of the eggs never hatch. That is fine. Enough is enough here.

In the suspended droplets at chest height live even smaller walkers — copepods and mites that have colonized the hanging beads of water like temporary ponds that never fall. They live and die inside a marble of water that hangs in air. When you move too fast you break their whole world.

### The Rememberers

They do not live here.

That is important. No one lives here. To live here you would need to bring your food with you, and the chamber gives no food worth staying for.

But someone *tends* here.

You feel them at ankle height because that is where the work is. The three small cairns, knee-high, stones chosen for their flat faces and still warm, stacked directly under the three most reliable drips. Not decoration — cisterns. The shallow bowl pecked into the basalt with a harder river stone, a day's work for a thimbleful of extra catchment, still half-full and so still it has grown its own skin of film. The cord — now only a dark, mineral-stained ghost of fiber knotted deliberately around the base of a straw that was already old when it was tied. The finger-smear of charcoal, re-touched once, twice, maybe once a generation.

Whoever they were, they came before metal, or with only soft copper. They came turned sideways through the seam, carrying skin bags and thirst. They found this impossible warm, wet, dark lung high in the killing cold and understood what it was: not a house, not a mine, but a throat. A place where the mountain weeps clean water even in deepest winter, where the air itself will not let you freeze. A shrine, a emergency cistern, a place to leave a mark and take nothing but water and stillness.

They did not eat the salamander. They may have seen him and thought he was a spirit. They left the ferns alone. They stayed an hour, maybe a night, sweating in the bath-heat while the mountain howled stupidly outside, and then they went back out into the high, thin air to live where living is possible.

The marks they left are patient too. No tool, no bone, no fire pit. Only the things that help the next visitor find water a little faster. The chamber preserves them because nothing erodes here. The air does not move. The water cycles without loss. The bacteria politely grows over the charcoal if you let it, but slowly.

You are the next in that line. You sit on the warm, slick rock and your own skin starts to bead and drip, and for a little while you are indistinguishable from the walls — another warm surface that sweats, another body contributing a micron of mineral to the straw above you. The salamander makes his circuit again and steps over your foot, cool and soft, completely unaware that you are temporary.

You understand, without being told, that you should not stay.
