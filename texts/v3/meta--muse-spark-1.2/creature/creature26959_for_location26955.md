They live like the water does.

That's the first thing you understand about them, before you can see them clearly in the amber dark. You come down the sharp throat expecting to find people and instead you find evidence of people — a hammer left on a catwalk to keep a pressure valve from chattering, a pair of boots placed neatly where the grating is too hot to stand barefoot at midday, a half-drunk barrel of warm rainwater with a ladle across it. They are not immediately visible because they don't contrast. In most cities people are the figure and the city is the ground. Here the city is so dense, so layered, so close, that the people are part of the weave.

Then a movement three levels up resolves itself and you realize it was a person all along. And then another. And another. The bowl is full of them, but they are quiet in the way the machines are quiet.

They are humans, unmistakably. Not a new species, not something engineered for the dark. Settlers. Second hands on young stone. But the place has started to make them in its image, the way a long-held tool shapes the hand that holds it.

You see it first in how they tolerate heat.

You are sweating through your clothes within minutes, your head light from the weight of damp air that won't move. They are not sweating — or rather, they are, constantly, but they don't *suffer* it. Their skin has learned the climate. It is damp, always, with a sheen that never quite beads and never quite dries, because to dry here is to overheat. Men and women both move stripped down to what function allows — loose singlets, sleeveless wraps, trousers cut to the knee, many barefoot or in thin soft-soled slippers that can feel the thrumming of the deck through the steel. Their hair is cut short or tied severely back, not for style but because hair holds heat. The children — there are children, fewer than you'd expect, small and solemn — have shaved heads that shine under the sodium bulbs. Old scars of heat are on everyone: the pale, stippled burn on a forearm from brushing a steam pipe, the permanent pink of knees and elbows from kneeling on warm basalt to work a low valve. They don't flinch when they lay a palm flat on the rock to steady themselves. The rock is 39 degrees and to them it is floor temperature, wall temperature, bed temperature. Neutral.

Their bodies are lean and corded, not from hunger — they eat, the tuber and beans and the rationed protein that comes down the shaft — but from verticality. No one here walks far horizontally. They climb. All day they climb: ladders that are stairwells, rebar rungs welded between containers, catwalks that sway a centimeter under your weight. Shoulders and forearms are overdeveloped, calves tight, hands callused in the web between thumb and forefinger where rope and steel have worn them. Even the old move like climbers, economical, never hurrying. To hurry in this heat is to waste water. So they have learned a settlement-wide slowness that looks like laziness until you realize how much ground they cover without ever seeming to exert themselves. They flow.

And they are quiet.

You expected a loud people to fill a loud place, but the air eats sound and they have adapted to that hunger. They don't shout between balconies, though they could — the gap is only a meter. They speak at the volume the air will carry, which is low and close. Conversations happen leaning in, not calling out. A mother calls her child not with a yell but with a specific, two-note whistle that cuts through damp air better than a voice. Work crews coordinate with taps on pipe — three taps means *hold pressure*, two means *release* — because a tap travels through steel and bone when a voice won't travel through steam.

When they do speak, it is soft, unhurried, with long pauses to listen. They have to listen. Everyone here is a maintenance person, because the settlement only exists as long as it is maintained. The pumps that push water uphill, the baffles that keep the smelter from roaring, the gutters that must be cleared before the ceiling-rain or the central channel will clog and flood the low workshops — these are not jobs you do on a shift and leave. These are the conditions of living, like breathing. So their talk is full of that language: *how's your cistern holding, did you sweat your pipe today, the north wall is weeping early*. They talk about the building the way a sailor talks about a ship, or the way a body talks about itself — not as something you inhabit, but as something you are.

Their eyes have changed, too, or rather their way of seeing has. In light that never comes from above, where shadows fall upward and the brightest thing is often a white fluorescent rectangle spilling sideways out of a workshop, they don't look up. Newcomers keep glancing at the dark dome and seeing nothing. The inhabitants keep their gaze at mid-level, where the sodium strings and the forge-glow actually illuminate. They have excellent night vision and terrible distance vision; the far catwalks across the bowl ripple in the smelter heat and they don't try to resolve them. They know who is there by gait, by the particular creak of a person's usual route across grating. They can see a single bead of condensation forming on a pipe in the half-dark from two meters away because that bead matters. They can tell, by the color of the steam, whether the extruder is running hot.

They live by the rain that isn't weather.

Time here is not clock time. The clocks exist, but the real divisions are: Before Beading, Beading, Rain, Channel-Running, and Dry. You can see the whole settlement shift its posture through those phases. During Beading, when the dark mirror gathers on the ceiling and the bulbs seem to dim under a second, more reflective sky, people stop and go to their barrels and cisterns, open gutters, lay out tarps. Old men and women on the highest balconies tilt their bean boxes slightly to catch runoff. No one has to be told. When the pressure drops and the bulbs sway, children go still and watch the ceiling. Then the rain comes — ten minutes, soft, warm, smelling of hot stone and iron — and it is the only time the place is loud. It patters on every tin roof at once, drums on canvas, hisses where it hits the smelter hood. People stand in it with their faces up, not quite celebrating, more like acknowledging. They let it soak their clothes because it will dry in an hour and for that hour they will be cool.

After, the work of saving it. Every puddle is swept toward a drain. Every channel is tended. You see a woman wringing out a canvas awning into a barrel with a practiced twist, a boy chasing a rivulet with a dustpan. Nothing wasted. To spill water here is not a moral failure, it is a physical absurdity, like dropping food on a lifeboat.

And because there are no other animals — and you feel that absence most keenly when you watch them — they have no buffer between themselves and the world. No rats to blame for a missing tuber, no flies to curse, no pigeons to shoo from a ledge. When something moves on its own, it is human or it is machine. So they treat machines with a kind of animal tenderness. A pump that has run for three years without seizing is patted as it sighs. A press that ticks as it cools is listened to the way you would listen to a sleeping dog's breathing. Workshops are immaculate, not because they love cleanliness but because a stray shaving in a hot, damp place with no insects to break it down will rust and rot and never be carried away. They are the only fauna. They have to do all the work fauna does: pollinate the beans by hand with small brushes, pick the lichen that would otherwise spread too far, carry scraps back to the compost that feeds the pink-lit tubers.

They know each other completely. Three hundred meters is a small bowl to hold perhaps a thousand people. You can stand on the rim and, as the description promised, hold every roof in your glance. That means there are no strangers. There are no crowds to disappear into. Privacy is not walls — walls are too thin — but courtesy, a learned not-looking when a curtain is drawn across a loft that is also a workshop that is also a bedroom. Disputes are low and short because sound carries so strangely and because you will see that person again on the same ladder tomorrow and the day after. Grudges don't survive vertical living.

If you stay long enough you notice the weirdest thing: they are not oppressed by the boundaries. People who live in a room the size of a cathedral should feel buried. They don't. Or if they did once, they don't now. The fresh-cut basalt, still sharp enough to cut a hand, is not a wall to them but a skin. They lean against it. They have mapped its weeping. They know which seam will darken first before the rain. The rock holds heat and they hold the rock, in a mutual thermal pact. A newcomer feels the dome as a lid. They feel it as a forehead, warm, close, present.

They were not born for this place — the cut is too new for that — but they are becoming the thing that can live here without needing to be anywhere else. Not deep-dwelling in the mythic sense, not pale cave things with no eyes. Just humans who have learned to live like condensation: appearing exactly where hot and cold meet, clinging to vertical surfaces, gathering drop by drop until there is enough to run, then evaporating, then gathering again.

If you stand on the walkway at Channel-Running, when the ankle-deep brown water is moving and the air briefly stirs and the forge pulses at the far end, you can watch them — lean, glistening, soft-footed, moving without hurry along catwalks that cross over alleys that cross over other alleys — and understand that the settlement is not a place they live *in*.

They are how the place lives.
