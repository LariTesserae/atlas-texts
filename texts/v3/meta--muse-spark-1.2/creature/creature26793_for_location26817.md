Who lives there is not a population. It's an ecology. A negotiation.

### The People

There are fewer of them than there should be for a city this size. You will walk a brick street built for a rush hour that never comes and see one person at the far end, lit amber under a sodium lamp, pushing a cart of coal, and then nobody for a long time. They have become a sparse species on purpose.

You recognize them by their slow. Everything about them is geared down to match the mountain.

They are broad and short even when they are tall, made broader by layers — heavy wool, waxed canvas, felt linings, leather aprons gone soft with oil, respirators hanging loose around their necks like second collars. Their faces are pale in a way that isn't pallor; it's the pallor of people who have never been sunburned, whose skin has only ever known that directionless blue dusk. Their pupils are large, staying large. Children born here have a faint eyeshine when an arc lamp catches them, a tapetum that wasn't in their parents. Old workers have lungs that whistle — not from sickness, exactly, but from breathing for thirty years air that is half cloud, half coal smoke, half basalt dust — and they can hear the rock groan without pressing an ear to it.

They do not hurry. Hurrying is dangerous when gravity wobbles and distance lies. They have the gait of people on a ship: knees slightly bent, always ready for the ground to shift a centimeter under them. They tend machines that were designed to be tended slowly. A crane operator will climb three stories of openwork gantry, settle into a cabin, and then move a single I-beam six inches, wait, move it six inches more, all day. A foundryman will watch a piston the size of a house rise and fall, rise and fall, oiling it on the upstroke with a long-handled swab, every time, for years. A maintenance crew will scrape moss off the rails at 06:00 with long shovels — a foot of growth overnight, wet and steaming — knowing it will be back tomorrow, not frustrated by it. This is liturgy, not labor.

They have learned the folk physics and obey it without needing to understand it.

They never stare directly at a joint. They use peripheral vision to count how many water towers there really are and then agree, among themselves, to use the one that is there when you look straight at it. They leave a plate of ore dust and kitchen scraps at the edge of the foundry floor for the beetles, because the beetles have learned to eat there and not in the gearbox. They tie their balconies with chain and build their doorframes crooked on purpose, and when a street starts to run uphill that used to run downhill, they re-hang the signs and keep using it. The church with the leaning steeple is not a joke to them. It is the most honest building in town. The priest says it will be straight for three perfect years sometime in the next decade, and that will be the holiest time.

They don't remember being sent here. Some of the oldest will tell you they came up from below when the peaks were only hills, when the chasms were only cracks you could step over. Their grandchildren think the mountain has always been there and is still being born, both true at once. No one is trying to finish the city. They are trying to keep it jointed and breathing while it grows. They are not the owners. They are the maintenance fauna of a larger animal.

They love the animals, not sentimentally but as co-workers. They talk about the flocks the way sailors talk about weather.

### The Green

The second city is the one that grows over the first.

It lives on condensation. At dawn, when the cloud lowers and beads on every surface, the moss wakes up. It is not like lowland moss. It is thick, black-green, sphagnum-fat, holding water like a sponge, already a half-inch thick on rock that was bare and glassy six months ago. It softens the knife edges that would otherwise cut your gloves. It hangs from the underside of every suspension bridge in curtains five, ten feet long, dripping steadily onto the walkways below. Ivy with leaves as big as dinner plates — waxy, veined with white — has learned to root in rust. It finds a rivet, finds the iron oxide blooming around it, and digs in. Grass comes up through the cracks in pouring yards, pale and fast, and is mown not by people but by the grazers that come down at night.

The pines shouldn't be there. They are. Black bark, needles that are almost blue, roots like fingers prying. A seed lands in a hairline crack where water has pooled for two days, germinates in a week, and by the end of the year is a sapling already leaning into the wind. Its roots will widen the crack by a millimeter, which is exactly what the mountain is doing anyway. The green is not decoration. It is the erosion that hasn't had time to evolve yet. It is the thing that makes soil out of fresh breakage.

People let it. They clear it where it interferes — tracks, turbines, door seals — and leave it everywhere else, because tearing it all off would be a war they would lose by lunch. In the vast empty quarter-miles between the dense knots of tenements, the green is the true occupant. Meadows that shake with insects you can't see. Stands of pine where the six-legged things bed down.

### The Animals

They are why the city feels crowded despite being empty.

**In the air:** The swifts. That's the word people use though they are not swifts. Hawk-sized, narrow-winged, with skin so thin and pale you can see the light of their organs through it — heart, gut, a second, slower heart near the base of the skull that glows amber. They never fly alone. A hundred, three hundred together, turning in a torus or a helix around a peak, so synchronized it looks like a single organism thinking. They scream — a high, glassy trill that cuts through the thrum of the forges — and they never hit the cranes. The cranes move at 0.25x speed, the birds move at 1.5x, and they have worked it out between them. At deeper blue, near night, their bellies light up fully and they become strings of lanterns, spiraling between the ridges. Children lie on rooftops to watch them.

**On the vertical:** The wall-grazers. Shaggy, barrel-chested, six legs splayed wide, each foot a fleshy pad as wide as a dinner plate that can seal to glassy basalt by suction and static. They walk straight up a fresh cliff face as if it were a meadow, heads down, cropping moss with broad, flat teeth, leaving neat, combed swaths. Their eyes are on stalks and swivel independently. Their coats collect condensation and drip. When gravity has one of its wobbles — when dust falls sideways for a block or two — they don't stumble. They were already walking on the gravity that points into the wall. Sometimes you see one cross the underside of a bridge, inverted, not noticing you at all. The workers give them right of way.

**In the iron:** The rest. The girder-raccoons — lean, gray-brown, with hands like a man's but with glittering compound eyes that catch arc light in a thousand facets — live in colonies of fifty in the warm spaces behind forges, chittering, stealing gloves, raising kits in nests of mineral wool. They understand shift changes. The coal-beetles — cat-sized, then dog-sized near the older manufactories — iridescent black carapaces that clink when they walk on catwalks, antennae twitching for carbon. They came for the coal dust and stayed for the heat. A worker will leave a scoop of slack by a warm pipe and a beetle will come and eat it daintily, mandibles clicking, while the worker eats his lunch a foot away. Neither bothers the other. At night you hear them more than the machines: the beetles drumming on sheet metal to call mates, the raccoons fighting, the grazers lowing like foghorns from the cliffs, the swifts screaming — a jungle chorus laid directly over an industrial one, neither drowning the other.

There are smaller things too, things that live only in the temporary pools: shrimp-like swimmers with too many eyes that appear in a chasm puddle two days after it forms, breed frantically, and die when the rock shifts and drains it overnight, leaving their eggs in the damp crack to wait for the next puddle.

### The Other Residents

And then there are the ones that only live here because here hasn't decided what is allowed yet.

The distance itself lives here. You can feel it as an animal. It stretches when you want to get somewhere and contracts when you're going home. Old couriers who run messages between knots know not to trust how far a lit window looks. They count time, not meters, and carry chalk to mark a bridge they've crossed because it might be longer on the way back.

The wobble lives here — a patch of gravity that has learned to lean. It inhabits certain streets, certain chasms. Dust lives there sideways for a few seconds. A dropped wrench will hang. The wall-grazers go there to rest.

The echo lives here. In the deepest rectilinear chasms, where the bottom sometimes shows not rock but the violet of evening though it is noon above, your breath comes back before you breathe it. You hear the hammer before it falls. People do not shout in those chasms. The beetles avoid them.

The duplicates live here, shy. Three water towers in the corner of your eye. Two cathedrals when you're not looking. They are not illusions; the workers will swear that if you need an extra length of pipe, you can sometimes find it by not looking directly at the pipe rack. They have learned not to count too carefully. To stare is to be rude. To insist on one of a thing is to make the others go away, and sometimes you needed the others.

All of these are inhabitants as much as the people and the moss. The city leaves room for them, too. The emptiness between the dense knots is not empty. It is habitat for physics that hasn't settled.

You stand on that half-finished gantry in the blue, industrial light and the damp that ticks off every edge, and you realize the hierarchy has inverted. The people are not living in a city built on a mountain. The mountain is a live birth, the city is a scaffold built to keep the birth from tearing, the green is a bandage, the animals are the attendants, and the people — sparse, slow, patient, oil-handed, scraping moss at the same hour every morning — are just the most recent symbiont to find that this place, which should be uninhabitable, is in fact furiously, abundantly, and indiscriminately alive.

Nothing has been taken away yet. So everything that arrives gets to stay.
