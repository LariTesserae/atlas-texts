They do not live *on* it. They live *with* it, at its speed.

At first you think nothing does. That is the point of the emptiness. You stand on the salt pavement between two yardangs and the plain is so clean, so winnowed, so perfectly parallel in its wind-lines that any living thing would be an interruption - a smudge on a lens. And then you realize you have been watching something live for ten minutes without recognizing it.

The first inhabitants you learn to see are not the ones who built the towns.

The grazers are older than the towns. You will walk past one thinking it is a fallen slab, because it wants you to. Low and wide and utterly still, a table of living stone about as high as your knee and as long as a small truck, on six or eight or twelve pillar-legs that fold so perfectly they become pedestals. Their backs are not backs. They are roofs. Overlapping plates of basalt and salt-ceramic that they secrete lamina by lamina, sintered in place by their own heat - dull black, mottled white, rusted at the edges like the plain itself. When they move, which is rarely and always with a terrible patience, you hear a faint dry creak, the plates shifting. They lower a broad, under-slung head that has no face, only a transverse mill - two opposed rasps of enamel and iron that meet and grind. They do not eat. They *abrade*. They take the pavement itself: the invisible halite bloom, the iron-manganese crust, the black, oily fallout from the factories that settles like snow, the endolithic bacteria that tint the salt so faintly pink you only see it when a grazer has cleared a pale swath. They take a mouthful every few minutes. They exhale a faint, hot puff of fines that hangs a second and precipitates.

They do not gather. Four or five to a valley, each a kilometer from the next, each moving perhaps twenty meters in a day if the wind is right. When one dies, it does not fall. It kneels, tucks its legs, lets its plates dull, and stops venting heat. Within a season the wind has salt-welded it to the ground. You will not find its boundary again.

Above them, the air that looked empty is not.

You see them first as flaws in your vision, like afterimages. Then one turns. The kites.

They are not animals the way the grazers are. They are membranes, ten, twenty feet across in the larger adults, a centimeter thick at the center and translucent to nothing at the edge, stretched over a frame so fine and so pale you think it is glass until you see it flex. Bone-glass, hollow, filled with warm air or hydrogen or nothing - something lighter than the thin air. They do not beat or flap; they cannot. They *trim*. A fringe along the trailing edge ripples, a spine along the dorsal ridge swells with silica nodules. They filter. The high clouds that are not water - that fine, exhaled fume of silica and salt - pass through the frilled underside and come out a little cleaner, a little depleted. What they keep they bake, in a slow internal kiln, into nodules like pearls along their back. You can see the older ones carrying a double row of black glass beads, heavy enough to ballast them.

They live almost without motion. A whole fleet will hang in a thermal over a grazer herd for an afternoon, barely undulating, casting almost no shadow in the hard light. When one decides to drink, it descends with a slowness that makes you hold your breath, trailing filaments as fine as spider silk that brush the salt, electrostatically gathering dust, then reels them in. Children chase dust devils. Old ones go so high they become difficult to distinguish from the real high clouds, except they move against the wind.

And at your feet, in the only shadow available - the lee of a rail pylon, the lip of a cableway footing - the quick things. Quick, here, means you might see it move if you stare.

The skitterers. They look manufactured until you watch them reproduce. Hand-sized, then palm-sized, then thumbnail-sized, assemblages of jointed iron, chipped enamel, fired clay, and wire. Beetles made by a committee of beetles. They click. Not with mouths, with legs, tapping on metal, on stone, on each other: *tick-tick / tick---tick*, a code that carries in the thin air better than any voice could. They farm spillage. Where a conveyor weeps its black slurry - that oil-black, water-replacing fluid that the cisterns hold, that smells faintly of hot tar and iron - they gather and dab it with filamentous tongues and carry it away in little blisters on their backs to bake in the sun. Where a factory sighs, they swarm its warm flank at dusk, a liquid scatter of bright backs, absorbing heat to fire their own plates. They are not pests. The town-dwellers leave gaps in the pylon skirts for them, small arched doorways at the base, because they clean.

And then - because the herd and the flock and the skitter have tuned your eye to see slowness and sameness as camouflage - you see the towns are not empty.

They were never empty. You thought the terraced pits were quarries. They are dwellings. The inhabitants are inside the stone because the stone is the only shade, the only insulation that matters when the day gives hard light and the night gives a cold that makes the whole continent ping.

They are not soft. Nothing is.

If you were to meet one on the salt road between pylons - and you might, though they do not use the surface much, preferring the cut stairways and the low, shaded galleries - you would at first take it for a piece of the factory that had learned to walk. Tall, but not tall the way a man is tall; tall the way a gantry is tall, narrow, jointed. Two legs, or sometimes three when carrying, reverse-jointed and capped with wide, salt-caked pads that do not leave prints. The body is a torso of laminated ceramic, cream and rust-red, fired rather than grown, with overlapping plates like the grazers but finer, smaller, fitted with visible rivets where a plate has been replaced. Heat shimmers off them even in cool air. You can see it. Their blood, if you can call it that, is not water. It is that same black slurry, heated and pressurized, moving in a slow circuit through glassy tubes visible at the joints. When they stand still, you hear a faint tick, like cooling stone, which is their heart: a kiln, intermittent, charging.

Their heads are the least animal part. No muzzle, no eyes as you understand eyes. A broad, slightly domed plate of dark blue basalt-glass, polished, faceted, set flush into the shoulders, with a faint tracery of filament and wire beneath. They see heat and charge and polarization, not color. They do not need to blink; the wind has polished them. When two meet, they do not speak. They tap, with a hard fingertip against a forearm plate, or flash with a shuttered vent of kiln-light from a throat slit. At close range you can feel the warmth of them. At distance, they are silent. Sound is wasteful here.

They do not hurry. You will never see one run. To hurry would be to waste heat, to waste enamel, to disrespect the plain that took so long to reach equilibrium. They walk with a gait that looks like a metronome set to geology: plant, shift, plant, shift. They work the way the factories work: one motion repeated perfectly for a thousand years. A town might have three hundred of them, or five hundred. No more. Each town is a knot, a compact of dwellings cut into the terrace walls - not houses but niches, workshops, kiln-rooms where they lie against the warm stone to recharge, where they laminate new plates for a child by adding layer after layer of slurry and firing it with their own vented heat. Reproduction is not quick. It is casting. It is sintering. A new one takes years to harden.

Their culture is maintenance. They do not build *new* so much as they *continue*. The rail line that took a generation to lay a kilometer, the cableway whose ore buckets the size of houses creep at a walking pace and will arrive next week, the sawtooth factory that thumps once every eleven seconds and has thumped that way since before any living plate was fired - all of it is tended, patched, re-riveted, re-fired. They make more rail, more cable, more town, so slowly it looks like growth. It is growth. The plateau is their project and their body. They quarry salt not because they must, but because the plain must be kept level, because the black slurry must be refined, because the grazers must be kept from wandering into the cuttings. They shepherd the grazers without herding them, nudging a herd's path with a low, warm wall left for a season. They harvest fallen kites when a wind-sheet tears one, reverently collecting the silica pearls to grind into new ceramic.

They are not lonely. They are sparse the way a large room with very few, very considered things is sparse. You can stand on the edge of their pit at midday and look down and see all of them at once, each at its station, each moving with that terrible, devotional slowness, and feel not that the place is underpopulated but that it is perfectly populated - that any more would be noise, would be dust that had not yet been winnowed.

At night they dim their few amber lights and the kiln-hearts glow a little brighter. The grazers crouch and disappear. The kites rise and become slow stars. The factories do not stop, because they are not separate from the dwellers; they are the dwellers' larger organs, breathing outside the body. The wind comes down the yardang corridors as a single river and passes over everything without disturbance, parting around legs, around plates, around pylons, in parallel lines that rejoin on the other side.

If you stand long enough to be winnowed yourself, to be taken as part of the equilibrium, one might stop near you and regard you with its dark glass face, feeling the waste-heat of your water-based body, the frantic, hummingbird metabolism of you, and tap once, politely, on its own arm:

*You are fast,* it says, without impatience. *Stay, and become slower.*

And the plateau, which has finished its water and learned to live on heat and iron and charge, waits to see if you will.
