They do not live there the way a fish lives on a reef.

At first you don't see anyone lives there at all. You see a cloud. A snow of pale specks hanging in the cold, so dense you think it's silt, or marine snow fallen in through the crack and trapped. Then one speck moves against the convection — not drifting, *correcting* — and then another, and the whole cloud resolves and you realize you are looking at a crowd so thick the water between them has become narrow.

They are white the way things that have never seen light are white. Not paper-white, but the white of muscle under skin, of milk in water, of blind cave flesh that has never needed pigment. And they are all blind in the same way. No eyes, or eyes that have become something else — flat, clouded plates that sense only the glow of their neighbors. There is nothing to look at here except each other.

And they glow. Not bright. If you were hoping for a brilliance you will be disappointed. It is a tired, municipal light, constant and low. A dusting of cold fire across every body. The plumes of the big worms seep it. The antennae of the shrimp bead it. The bellies of the isopods smear it. The eggs do it pinkly. It never flashes, never signals in any urgent way — it just stays on, because in water this still and dark, to stop glowing is to stop existing to anyone else. In that dim blue that has almost forgotten light, they make exactly enough light to see the next body over. A city with its streetlamps always on in a fog.

Closer now — because you have to get closer, the water magnifies nothing and the cold makes you slow — you see they are not one thing but many, and none of them live alone.

The first are the Towers.

They were here first, or first enough. Giant vestimentiferan tube worms, each as thick as your forearm, as long as a man is tall, rooted into the bowl of the floor where the rock is still warmest. Their tubes are not separate. Where larvae settled too close their secretions have fused, wall to wall, into a single chitinous tenement. White, parchment-stiff, faintly translucent. From the mouth of each, a red plume — gills, not flowers — unfurls and retracts, breathing the thin soup of sulfide that seeps from the new basalt. They do not move. They cannot. They are the architecture. Their bodies are houses that grew themselves. Inside, bacteria live inside *them*, turning that seeping chemical heat into sugar, and feeding the worm from within. They are landlords that are also farmed.

On and between them live the Masons.

Amphipods the size of your fist, bone-pale, with legs like jointed wire and jaws that never stop working. They are the ones who make the in-between matter. They gather what the Towers discard — grains of hyaloclastite, popped flakes of glass sand, the cast molts of shrimp — and cement them with their own spit and mucus into endless honeycomb. No single chamber is larger than a thimble. Together they make galleries, balconies spiraled around worm tubes, porous walls that slow the water just enough. You can see them working constantly, adding one grain, tamping it, adding another. If one dies, its body is picked clean in minutes and its half-finished wall is taken over before the silt can settle on it.

Through this, the Commuters.

Pale caridean shrimp in numbers that defeat counting. They are the blood. They never stop. Translucent white, with long, blind antennae that glow at the tips like cigarette ends, they flick and walk and swim the thoroughfares — the gaps between tube-clusters where the slow exchange current runs fastest. They carry everything: white bacterial mat scraped from the warm lower walls, pink egg clusters glued to their swimmerets, parasites picked from worm plumes. You watch one lane for a minute and you will see them all going the same way, then, as the convection cell turns, all going the other. Their legs make the almost-infrasonic clicking you heard, thousands of tiny clicks held inside the basalt like a hand over a mouth.

And the Lattice.

Brittle stars, not graceful here but dense, their arms grown thin and long to lace through the city. They have wedged themselves into every crevice, arms wrapped around tubes, around amphipod galleries, around each other, until they form a living rebar — a net that holds the tenement together when a flake of rock ticks off and falls. They filter. They wait. Occasionally an arm snaps shut around a larva that swam too far un-tethered.

And underneath and over all, the base and the cleaners and the ghosts.

The bacterial veils — white, sulfurous, like lace left in water too long — wave on the warmest stone, and are constantly grazed to a stubble by isopods, slow as pillbugs, blind and broad, that drift and scrape and excrete and feed the veil again. Translucent anemones have claimed the ruins near the back wall where a bulge in the basalt crushed a row of tubes; they sit in the wreckage, open, not moving for hours, then closing on a shrimp in a motion too fast for cold water.

Nothing is wasted there. That is not a virtue. It is physics. In a room this small, this sealed, a shed tube is a building. A molted carapace is mortar. A dead worm never falls — it becomes an apartment complex for ten shrimp, then a nursery, then a lattice anchor, until even its tube is dismantled grain by grain by the Masons.

And nothing floats free. You understand that now. To be untethered in water this still is to be exiled. The larvae do not drift like they do in the open ocean. They jet in short, frantic bursts, each trailing a mucus thread like a kite string, already sticky, already seeking. If they fail to grab — a tube, a gallery wall, an arm — the current eddies them slowly toward the cold center where nothing lives, and they die glowing, alone. The ones who find holdfast go dark for a moment, then light up again as part of the city.

It is not intelligent. There is no mayor in the dome. But it is organized the way cities organize without anyone deciding. The tallest, reddest plumes crowd nearest the crack in the ceiling, where cold ocean water sighs in and brings oxygen. The nursery crowds lowest, where the rock is warmest and the bacterial veils are thickest and the pink eggs pulse faintly like low coals. The quiet cul-de-sacs are where a single large anemone has grown fat and territorial. The ruins are always being taken apart.

If you stay until the cold has seeped through your suit and your breath is as slow as the ticking stone, you will stop trying to count species and you will hear it as one thing. The combined rasp of radulae, the contraction of worm plumes, the mason's tamping, the commuters' leg-clicks — all of it muffled by basalt and viscous water, all of it kept inside. A dense, soft, busy quiet. A boomtown that thinks the dome is the whole world.

It has no idea the ocean is outside. It has never needed to know. For the shrimp born on the second layer of amphipod gallery, the crack in the ceiling is not a door — it is the sky, slow-breathing, leaking weather. The basalt that is still cooling is not a wall — it is the ground, warm and ticking, occasionally shedding. And the pale light of their neighbors is not bioluminescence.

It is just light. The only light that has ever been.
