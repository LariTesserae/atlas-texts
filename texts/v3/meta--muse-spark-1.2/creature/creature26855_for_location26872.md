They do not live *on* it.

They *are* it. For as far as the low light holds and further, where the dark ground dissolves into more dark and the haze takes the rest.

At first you won't see them as animals at all. From standing height, after your eyes have learned how to see in that bruise-purple dusk, the floor of the bowl looks like pavement after rain. Dark, pitted stone, slick and glistening, shifting faintly the way heat shimmers will shift a road — but there is no heat here, only 8 or 9 degrees of steady, stone-sweating cold. The shimmer doesn't stop when you blink. It resolves.

Then it separates.

What you took for stone contracts. Swells. Contracts again, out of time with itself. A slow tide with no moon. You have to crouch, because the dark forces you close, and when you do — when your face is a foot above the sheen — the pavement comes apart into backs.

This is not a population. It is a crust. A mat, two and three deep, interlocked so completely you cannot find a patch of bare stone larger than your palm without hunting for it.

The most obvious — only because they are the largest and most numerous — are the tiles.

Imagine a woodlouse or a chiton flattened until it forgot it had height. Each one broad as your hand, some as wide as a dinner plate, oval and low with a smooth, chitinous dome the color of wet slate or dirty glass. Black, or translucent gray where the thin edge lets the cold light through. Condensation beads on their backs and never evaporates. Around the entire rim they carry a fringe, a dense palisade of dark cilia that never stops moving, flicking water, tasting air, stridulating.

Because they are never still and never alone. They overlap like shingles, like slates on a roof that was laid by no one. One's edge tucked under another's, another crawling half-over another to reach a wetter groove. When one lifts to move — a slow, hydraulic heave — you see what it hides: a pale, soft underside crowded with a dozen stubby, tufted legs that don't so much walk as knead, and a ventral mouth, round and puckered and pale as a scar, that works the stone with a ceaseless, squeegeeing scrape. You can hear it if you get close enough to hear anything under the wall of sound: a soft, wet *shhk shhk shhk*.

That is the base of everything you can see. Grazers. Hour after hour they mow a thing you cannot see until you smell it — the faint metallic low-tide tang on the damp air. It's a skin on the stone itself. A bacterial varnish, iridescent and black-green, chemosynthetic, living off dissolved iron and sulfur in the condensation and the seep. The stone sweats mineral water; the film eats the minerals; the tiles eat the film. Stone feeds film, film feeds mouths.

And where there are mouths that steady, there are mouths that eat mouths.

Between the tiles, in the seams where one dome does not quite meet the next, live the threads. They are the second thing your eye learns to track. Eel-thin, rope-thin, as long as your forearm with no thickness to justify the length, glossy, blind, the color of a drowned earthworm. They have no eyes to lose. They have four, six whiskers at a blunt head that sweeps and taps, sweeps and taps. They do not hurry. They pour themselves through gaps, over backs, under bellies, disappearing under a slab of basalt and reappearing a meter later as if the stone were not solid. Most of the time they are scavengers. Sometimes one will stop, rear that eyeless head, and snap sideways into the fringe of a tile-creature, worrying at the edge until the larger animal loses purchase and flips. Legs paddle the air. The pale mouth gapes. It never lasts. That is the signal.

The rollers are already coming. They look like pillbugs made of polished leather, compact and high-backed, and they do not walk so much as roll, tucking and tumbling a few centimeters at a time and leaving behind a faintly luminous trail of mucus that fades in three seconds. Three or four of them will converge on the overturned tile, clicking as they go. The anemones will close.

The anemones are not anemones, but you will call them that. They anchor where the stone is most deeply scalloped, in potholes and hairline cracks that hold a permanent bead of water. A leathery stalk, a crown of filaments as fine as eyelashes that opens and closes, opens and closes, filtering the damp itself — the gnats, the spores, the shed fringes. When something struggles nearby they snap shut with a soft, wet *plop* you feel more than hear.

And then there is what lives on what lives there.

You cannot set a hand down. You cannot set a foot down without stepping on dozens, hundreds. On the backs of the tiles, in the furrows between them, on the threads, on the rollers, is a crawling static. Mites. Lice. Springtails that are not springtails. Specks the size of poppy seeds and smaller, pale and amber and black, moving at a different framerate than the larger animals — jittering, swarming, leaping. Millions per square meter. A granular shivering that makes the larger backs look dusty until the dust jumps. They graze on shed skin, on fungus on skin, on each other. If you stay still they will find the salt on your boots and climb, tasting, not biting, a tickle that becomes a crawl.

None of them see you. Light is not a thing that matters here. Eyes, where they exist, are flat and vestigial, or covered. They navigate by touch and chemistry, by the vibration of a million fringed edges rubbing. They tolerate touch because they must — the mat is so dense that to be intolerant is to be in constant war. They crawl over one another without aggression most of the time, a slow, patient, tidal shuffling as the whole biomass breathes and repositions toward wherever the stone is wettest. If you lie still long enough the mat will try to roof you over.

That turnover is the only drama this place has, and it is constant and quiet.

No chase. No roar. A rope-thread worries a tile edge. A tile flips. The filament crowns snap shut. The specks swarm like thrown ash. From somewhere else, another tile has already righted itself and resumed scraping. A roller tumbles onward. Somewhere under your knee a thread flashes — a brief, cold, worm-like bioluminescence, a pinpoint of white-blue along its back when disturbed, too dim to see by, only enough to make the whole shifting surface glitter as if the condensation were catching a light that isn't there. Other tiles carry their own constellations, faint photophores along the dome, winking on and off with no synchrony.

And all of it — all of it — is making noise.

You expected silence in the cold and the dark. What you get is pressure. The dominant sound is the tiles: that dry, high *tik-tik-tik* of fringe against fringe, thousands overlapping at slightly different tempos until they merge into a single, unbroken, sawtooth hiss that never breaks, never ebbs. Under it the rollers click. The anemone crowns pop. The stone itself rustles and slaps and sucker-pops with wet bodies shifting on wet stone. And above it all, so high it lives almost in your teeth, is the hum of the air — clouds of gnats no bigger than ash, wings moving so fast they are invisible, vibrating in place over the braidless, ankle-deep streams, never landing, never leaving.

It is deafening in the way a factory is deafening. You cannot hear your breath. Your voice dies half a meter from your mouth. After a while the sound stops being sound and becomes a vibration in your sternum.

There is no night to quiet it and no morning to start it. The bruise-purple overcast never changes. The cold never lifts. The mist will lay itself down for an hour and lift, and the basin will glisten a little more, and the mat will keep scraping, shifting, breeding, dying — eggs like clear beads tucked under the tiles, young like moving dandruff — dying and being unmade in seconds by the next mouths over, so the mineral can go back to film and the film to mouth again.

Nothing here knows it is a meadow where the grass is meat. Nothing here is in charge. It has been settled for a very long time, weathered into equilibrium the way the basalt has been weathered into pillows and loaves. It does not need sun. It does not need soil. It only needs that the stone continues to sweat, and that the dark holds, and that whatever walks here learns quickly not to expect bare ground.
