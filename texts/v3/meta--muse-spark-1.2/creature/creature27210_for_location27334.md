Nothing lives here the way something lives *in* a place.

At first you think nothing *could* live here. Not really. Not for long. A warm, dim, drowned plain with no horizon and no high ground and no rivers, where the air is already water and the water is already air, where the sky sweats onto the stone and the stone sweats back - that doesn't sound like a habitat. It sounds like a surface that was left over after a habitat ended.

And then you notice it has been inhabited all along. Thoroughly. Completely. Just not by anything that needed hills or horizons or dry feet.

### The First Inhabitant is the Place Itself

Before anything walks here, the plain is alive.

Not metaphorically. The tiling itself — those soft, pink-grey whale-backs set shoulder to shoulder like cobbles, like scales, like the knuckles of a fist held forever just under the skin — they are not stone the way mountain stone is stone. They are a tissue. Warm to the touch. Porous. They weep.

Each dome is a living body, low and tight and swollen, pressed against its neighbors until there is nowhere for a border to go. You can see the seams if you look close: faint, paler lines where one dome meets the next, fitted with a patience that is not geological. They breathe. Very slowly. When you stand on one barefoot you feel it through your soles a minute later — a faint swell, a faint fall, a thrum that aligns with the deep thrum in the air. They swell with heat and exhale water from a million invisible pores. The water darkens them from the bottom up, sheets off them, and is drawn back in. This is how the plain drinks the cloud and how the cloud drinks the plain. It is one circulation.

In any other country you would call this ground. Here it is skin. A membrane stretched to the size of a continent, peneplaned by time until it forgot how to be anything but thin and flat and almost perfectly exposed. Rock that learned to be flesh, or flesh that learned to be rock. It is so old it has resolved. It no longer builds. It maintains.

And the living film that makes the water wine-red for an hour and then tears apart — the faintly organic, iron-and-algae smell — that is its bloom. Its thoughts, if it has them, are not fast enough to be called thoughts. They travel as temperature. As chemistry. As a slow spreading darkening across a pan. The thrumming you hear is not weather. It is perfusion. The heat lighting the cloud from below is not sun. It is metabolism.

Everything else lives *on* it the way a bird lives on a whale.

### The Second Inhabitants are the Few

Life that moves here is forced to obey the rules the membrane sets:

**1. Be flat or be tall on nothing.** There is no cover. To be seen is to be seen for kilometers, even in haze. So the few animals are either almost invisible or absurdly patient.

The one you keep seeing far off, the grey wader on legs like stilts, is not a bird that came here. It was made here. Its legs are not for wading in the way a heron wades a river - there is no river. They are for distributing almost no weight at all across a skin that cannot be pierced. Needle feet would pop the domes. So it has feet like snowshoes, like lily pads, splayed and fringed, that dent the water-skin without breaking it. Its body is the color of wet stone because it has to be; it is a grey smear in a grey world, standing motionless for an hour at a time, head down in the ankle-deep sheet.

It does not hunt. It harvests. It filters. With a bill like a fine comb it strains the ooze between domes — that warm mud that boils up and becomes an island — for the bloom. For the red films. For the tiny, translucent things that hatch and die in the time it takes a cloud-cell to collapse. It moves with impossible slowness because there is nowhere urgent to go, and because to move fast is to make a wake that will chase you for a mile across water an inch deep.

The heavy one — the low, amphibious hauler that slides onto a dome and blinks — is even more honest about what this place is. It doesn't walk so much as it adheres. Its belly is broad and damp and permeable; it absorbs water straight through its skin, it breathes through its skin, it drinks through its skin. On land that sweats, you don't need a mouth for water. It has lungs, too, but they are an afterthought, vestigial sacs it fills when the cloud comes too low and too thick and the water sheets merge and there is no air left to breathe, only warm steam. Then it opens its throat and sighs and you hear that ticking in the stone answer it. It can be still so long algae grows on its back and the midges mistake it for a dome. When it finally moves, it doesn't ripple — it releases, like a suction cup, and is gone. You never hear it enter the water.

Above them, very rarely, the glider. Pale and silent, with wings so thin you see the cloud through them. It shouldn't be able to soar here; there's no high ground to make thermals. But the membrane makes its own lift. Every dome is a fraction warmer than the water around it, every pan a fraction cooler. The air over this dappled skin shivers with a million tiny columns of rising steam, invisible elevators a meter wide. The glider knows them all. It circles without a wingbeat, drinking height from damp heat, looking down at a world that looks like nothing but itself repeated. It does not land. If it did, it would never take off again — the water would cling to those paper wings and hold them. So it stays up until it dies, and then it comes down like a fallen leaf onto a dome and is absorbed, too. The film comes and takes it within an hour.

All of them share a trait: they are spaced out. One wader per square kilometer. One hauler per three domes. One glider per sky that never ends. This country rations life. It is too busy turning water into air and air into water to spare a lot of meat. So the meat that exists is durable, far-ranging, solitary, and quiet. It listens more than it calls. Sound here carries too well and too strangely to waste.

They navigate not by sight. Sight is useless beyond a few hundred meters; distance folds, a reed clump stays a hundred meters away until it is suddenly around you, a stone appears behind you when you left it ahead. Instead they read temperature with their feet, the faint gradient as water flows - sometimes uphill, sometimes in a loop - over the whale-backs. They read pressure with their bellies. They know which pan is a degree warmer and therefore shallower, which dome is sweating faster and therefore about to be slick. They don't need north.

### The Third Inhabitants were the Rememberers

Someone else lived here and understood it enough to try to mark it.

They are gone now, or thinned to the point where they look like the other rare animals. But they left the only things in this place that are straight. A standing stone whose faces were once cut with perfect geometry and have since been polished by steam until you only feel the angle under your palm, like a memory of a corner. A ring of boulders so level it holds water like a mirror, not a millimeter higher on one side than the other — impossible by nature on ground this soft. A causeway of fitted slabs that runs true as a line of light for a kilometer and then simply dives under the sheet-water and stops, as if the builders decided the water was supposed to be there.

They did not build up. There is nothing to build with except the domes themselves, and you do not quarry a living membrane that you live on. They built *slightly* — rearranged what was already there just enough to make an artificial stillness in a place that never stops moving. A still pan that doesn't shiver when the water reverses. A dome that doesn't sweat. A line that stays straight while reflections slide sideways off it.

You understand, standing on the causeway while warm condensation drips off you from two meters up, that those marks were not art. They were navigational. Or devotional. Or both. In a place where sound arrives before it is made, where reflections lie, where north wanders and a reed clump won't get closer no matter how long you walk, a straight line is a kind of prayer. *Let this be true for a little while.* *Let this not move.*

Whoever they were, they learned to live here by becoming as flat and as patient as the things that still live here. Wide feet, water-drinking skin, swollen bark-like calluses to store water, pale lichen draped from their shoulders to catch the drip. You can see their successors sometimes at dusk, if you don't look directly: a figure standing as still as the wader, not harvesting but watching the water go uphill over a dome and taking note. Not hunting. Witnessing.

They stayed long enough that their footprints filled with water slightly warmer than the water around them — body heat lingering — and then smoothed over, and then were gone. That is how you live here a long time: you learn to leave no mark that the plain cannot erase in an hour. The ones who needed monuments are gone. The plain kept their subtlest ones and took the rest.

If you stay, you will become one of them for a little while. Your skin will start to condense and drip. Your feet will learn which dome is warmest. You will stop looking for a horizon because you finally feel that there never was one — only a skin, breathing, sweating, thrumming, endlessly tiling itself, with a few durable tenants picking their way across it, careful not to tear it.

And underneath you the whole time, the old, resolved, hyperactive membrane will keep shivering and reversing and boiling up new mud islands and erasing them, indifferent and intimate, as if you were not standing on a landscape at all, but on the inside of a lung the size of a world, learning how it feels to be breathed.
