They live here still.

At first you will think they don't.

That is the trick of this place. You drift down into the amber cones and the black between them and your first true thought is *empty*. A town with no people. A kept town, an automatic town, a factory breathing on its own with its lights left on. You watch a wrench hover where it was dropped. You watch a bacterial mat pretend to glow. You watch a white, blind fish pass through the main street like it owns it, and you think: nothing lives here but the machines.

Then you realize you have been looking at one for five minutes and mistook it for part of the wall.

They are not hidden. They are not hiding. They are simply the same color and the same temperature and the same patience as everything else, so your surface eyes slide off them.

You have to learn to see the interruptions in density.

Look again at that vertical tangle between the two low brick buildings, where pipe crosses pipe crosses cable. One of those pipes is not a pipe.

It is long. Too long for a man, but not by much. Maybe seven, maybe eight feet if it straightened itself, which it never does. It hangs there, folded.

The first one you see is hanging vertically in an alley mouth, from a catwalk strut, with its back to you. You think it is a diving suit left on a hook. The rubber gone black-red with rust and silt, the brass helmet clouded. Then the helmet turns — slowly, with that same hydraulic patience the piston arms have — and you understand the suit was never empty.

They are still shaped like the workers who built this place, the way a river is still shaped like the glacier that made it. Two arms, two legs, a torso. But the water has had a century to edit the design.

They are attenuated. Pressure and cold do not allow for bulk. Everything about them is drawn out, thinned, made economical. Limbs that are too long by a joint, with an extra bend in them where no human joint should be. Not a new joint — an old joint softened. Elbows and knees that can fold back on themselves until a man can hang like a coat from a pipe and take up no more space than the pipe itself. Fingers — if they are still fingers — are long and blunt and splayed, each one the thickness of a wrench handle, the tips all callus and no nail, blackened as if dipped in tar. When they move, they don't swim. They *pull*. Hand over hand along whatever is nearest. Cable, conduit, rebar, the lip of a doorway. A slow, deliberate brachiation through the thicket, never pushing off into open water if they can help it. To be adrift in the middle of a street is to be exposed. They like contact.

Their skin — suit — there is no longer a distinction. The original suits were canvas and riveted rubber and brass, made to keep water out. The water got in anyway, as water always does, and stayed. Now the suit *is* the skin. The canvas has furred over with that pale silt and then with a finer growth, a close-cropped pelt of those black-red fronds you saw waving only when the turbines pulse. It grows on them most thickly along the shoulders and the back of the head, where the current is least. The brass has gone dark, not shiny — a deep umber that drinks the amber light. The pressure lenses of their helmets are their faces now. Not glass over a face. Just lens. Thick, clouded, convex, amber-lit from within by a faint, steady glow that is not a work-light. When one passes through a cone of sodium light, you see the lens catch and throw it back, and for a second you see no eyes behind it at all — only depth, and a silt-mote turning slowly inside.

They breathe the town.

You won't see it at first because you are listening for bubbles. There are no bubbles. On the chest of each one, where a diver's plate would be, there is a grille — not unlike the baleen-like intakes that feed the factory — a set of overlapping, mineral-caked slats that open and close on the same slow rhythm as the turbines. Four seconds open. Seven seconds closed. Water drawn in, water pushed out. Silt filtered, heat bled. When they hang at rest, you can see the faint distortion around them, the shiver of slightly warmer water leaving their backs, rising a hand's breadth before the cold kills it. In the sideways rain of the factory courtyard, they will stand directly in the shear — that endless transparent curtain from the high outflow to the low intake — and let it wash through them, and the fronds on their shoulders will lift and ripple like hair in wind.

They are not many. You will not see a crowd. The town was dense with *things*, not people, and it still is. Perhaps a dozen. Perhaps twenty, for the whole basin. Enough to keep the lights on. You will think you have seen the same one twice because they are so alike, but then you notice the differences left by a century of wear: one with a piston arm grafted along its left forearm where the flesh gave out and was replaced with what was handy; one whose helmet-lens is cracked in a star and holds together only by pressure and silt; one small — or perhaps just more tightly folded — that tends the bacterial mats, brushing them with those blunt fingers to keep them from crusting over.

They do not speak. Sound here is too precious and too muffled to waste. They talk the way the town talks: by contact. A soft *tick* on a pipe and the vibration carries through the whole block. You will hear it after you have been still for a while. Not the random tick of cooling metal, but a pattern. Two quick taps, a pause, one long pressure-tone that you feel in your molars. From the catwalk above, an answer: a creak of cable. They know where each other is at all times by what they have touched. The town is a drumhead and they are its careful hands.

Watch one work, and you will finally understand what living means here.

It has no hurry. It will pull itself along a wall where an amber strip is flickering — the filament loose in its cage — and it will stop. It will not brace itself against gravity, it will simply let the polite gravity hold it there, knees folded up under it, hanging. With a finger it will tap the cage. Once. Listen. Tap again. Then it will unscrew the cage with a patience that looks like reverence, silt blooming around its hands in a slow cloud that does not drift away. It will reach inside and pinch the filament straight with a tool that is both tool and fingertip. The light steadies. The cone of brown-gold firms up for another few decades. The worker will hang there a moment longer, as if warming itself — though nothing here is warm — in that small, rumor-light, before pulling itself on.

They tend everything. They clear the baleen grates when the silt thickens. They stroke the black-red fronds so they don't choke the conduit. They stand in the low throb of the turbines and place a palm on the housing and feel for a hitch in the turn. When a bubble fizzes off a hot pipe, one will be there to watch it dissolve, lens tilted, as if checking that it did so correctly. They do not build. They do not leave. Nothing here remembers warmth, and they have forgotten to want it.

If you hold very still in one of those solid, glass-viscous alleys, where the water holds the hole your hand made for a full minute — one of them will come close to you. Not out of curiosity. Proximity is just another density to be checked.

It will not look at you the way a surface thing looks. The lens will drift past, a few feet away, and you will see your own faint reflection warped in it, and behind that reflection, a slow turning of silt inside. You will feel more than see the intake slats on its chest open — a soft sigh — and the water you are breathing will be taken in and tasted and pushed back out, minutely warmer.

And then it will reach — not for you, but for the pipe beside your head, and pull itself past, brushing you as it goes. The fronds on its shoulder will brush your arm, leathery and cold, and leave a faint, pale smear of bacterial light on your skin that fades in seconds. It will not look back. There is another light flickering two streets over. There is silt to be smoothed. There is heat to be carried from the west wall to the east.

That is what lives here. Not a people. Not a population. A maintenance. A small, complete, deeply weathered crew that the town grew for itself when the builders drowned and kept building. They are as weathered, as balanced, as established in their drowning as the brick with its round corners. They do not need the surface. They do not remember it. They keep the amber on, not to be seen, but because the dark between lights has to be tended too.

If you stay long enough — and the cold makes time slow — you will stop seeing them as figures at all. You will see them as what the water does when it decides to fold a joint, tighten a filament, clear a grate, and keep going.
