At first you think nothing lives there.

That is the trick of the place. It is so full — so stuffed and thatched and dripping with green, so busy with water finding its way down — that your eye slides over the other kind of life. The plant is so loud, and the machine is so quiet, that the animal in between disappears entirely.

You look for a village and find none. No houses on the terraces, no smoke that isn't steam, no laundry line. Even the factory core in the center looks uninhabited from the rim — just a block with open windows and a light on. The heron in the cistern is alone. The gecko in the transformer house could be a sound the wires make. For a long minute you think the bowl tends itself.

Then you start to see where it has been tended.

On the fourth terrace down, a stand of taro has been thinned. Not eaten, not broken — cut. The stems are cut clean with a blade, at an angle, three inches above the root crown so they will resprout. The cut ends are still weeping. Beside them, the standing water has a footprint in the silt, already softening at the edges as new silt settles. Not a boot print. A bare foot, broad-toed, the skin pruned from standing in warm water all day.

A little further, on the lip of a rill, a pair of yellow rubber gloves laid palm-up to dry, and inside one glove, a beetle sheltering. On the scaffolding, a tin cup with tea in it still warm. The surface of the tea has a skin. Someone was here minutes ago and stepped away when they heard your steps on the rim.

They do not show themselves at first because they move the way the bowl moves. Slowly.

You hear one before you see her.

A soft *tock* — shears closing. Then a rustle as a huge elephant ear leaf, as big as a door, slides down to the terrace floor. Then quiet. Then she exhales, and you realize you have been listening to someone breathing in rhythm with the piston in the factory core.

She is below you, on the second terrace, half inside the hanging curtain of aerial roots. That is why you didn't see her. Her clothes are the color of wet concrete and leaf-shadow. Her skin is the color of the people who live inside steam.

There are not many of them. Three. Maybe four.

They live inside the bowl the way a hand lives inside a glove — not on it, not over it. They are the keepers, but that word is too grand. They are the thinners, the pruners, the uncloggers. The midwives for a thing that is still curing.

You expected, for a place so high and so hard to reach, someone hard — mountain people, wind-burnt, with rope and pitons. They are the opposite. The warm wet air has softened everything about them.

They are mostly women, or the men have become like women here — lean, not muscled, with hair that never truly dries and is pulled back with strips of inner tube. They go barefoot, because the concrete is warm as a bath and the moss would be ruined by boots, and their feet are wide and pale on top and stained faintly green underneath, the arches gone soft from standing in an inch of water. Their calves are speckled with tiny leech bites that they don't bother to cover. Their hands, when you finally see them up close, are startling: not calloused, but waterlogged and tender, the fingerprints half-erased, the nails cut short and rimmed with dark silt. You need soft hands to work with aerial roots without tearing them.

They wear what the work left them — faded blue overalls cut off at the knee, thin cotton shirts that cling, never a raincoat because it never really rains, it only weeps. Sweat never evaporates here; it just joins the film on the skin. They glisten all day. Their eyes have adjusted to the diffuse, shadowless light from the high overcast, so they squint at edges and blink slowly.

And they are quiet in the way people get when they live inside a loud quiet. They don't call to each other across the bowl. They click. A tongue-click to say *this rill is blocked*, two fingers tapped on a pipe to say *open the western gate a tooth*. When they do speak it is low and brief, under the sound of the dripping. Their voices are hoarse from breathing warm, mineral air.

Watch one work and you see how completely the bowl has taught them its tempo.

She does not attack the growth. She negotiates. The fig that is growing through the cast-iron gear — she will not cut it. She reaches into the gear housing with a long-handled wrench and turns the gear by hand, one tooth, feeling where the bark binds, then stoops and with shears trims a single rootlet that was about to pinch in the mechanism. She leaves the rest. She wipes rust-water off the iron and rubs it into the fig's bark like ointment. The machine and the plant have come to an accommodation and she is their translator.

Another one is on his knees in the cistern, shoulders under the floating leaves, pulling the bright green hair-algae off the intake grate by the handful. He does it with a rhythm that matches the pump's inhalation — pull, breathe, pull, breathe. The heron watches him from a meter away and does not fly. It knows he is not a predator. He moves like water.

They live in the factory core, but not in it the way you live in a house. The upper floor — above the piston and the sorting belt — is one open room with that same golden lightbulb light, even at noon. There are three hammocks strung not from walls but from the warm copper pipes that run through the room, so the sleepers are gently heated all night. A gas ring, a bag of rice, a crate of tools. A shelf of glass jars where they are sprouting seeds — not for eating, for planting — each jar labeled in pencil on masking tape that has gone soft and illegible. The concrete floor is always damp, so they sleep above it. The windows have no glass, only the green mat of liverwort they have trained over the louvers, which lets in air that has already been filtered through leaf.

They do not stay here because they were born here. No one is born on a tepui tower with clouds below. They came up with the concrete pour — a work crew, originally, six or eight of them to tend the pumps and set the formwork. Most left when the pouring was done. These ones stayed, or came back, because the bowl does not work without hands for these first years, and because after a month of breathing that warm iron-green air, going back down to the dry world feels like going deaf.

What lives here, then, is not a village. It is a very small, very temporary symbiosis.

There is the green itself, which is not just plants but a single, distributed hunger that the engineers set in motion — taro and fern and vine and moss all plumbed into the same warm seep, all drinking the same condensation, all racing to cover the raw concrete before the sun can crack it. In that sense, the green is the true resident. It arrived as cuttings in buckets and is now the landlord.

There is the water cycle, which is also an animal. It sweats out of the rim, runs down the V-cuts, gathers in the cistern, is lifted by the pumps, exhaled as steam, condensed again on the pipes. It has a heartbeat — the piston's *thump* — and lungs — the louvers breathing through moss. It is a closed loop learning to be a watershed, and it is only a few months old.

And there are the keepers, who live in the seam between those two larger bodies. They are not masters here. Masters would have dry feet. They are more like white blood cells, or gardeners for a garden that will, in another year, be too dense to need them. They cut just enough that the rills don't choke. They clear silt from a weir with a trowel. They lift a vine off a rebar whisker before it can rust the steel faster. They eat some of the taro, but mostly they eat rice they haul up in sacks on the service winch that you haven't seen yet, hidden behind the transformer house — the only line that connects this wet cup in the sky to the world below.

At dusk, when the light goes even more diffuse and the cloud sea below turns pink, they gather at the cistern's edge. They do not light a fire — everything is too wet to burn. They turn on a second lightbulb in the factory core and it reflects in the glassy overflow lip all the way around, a perfect circle of gold in the green. They stand ankle-deep in warm water and wash their tools, their feet, their forearms. The midges rise in a column above the warmest water and they wave them away without irritation. The heron, if it is still there, lifts off on wide white wings and circles the bowl once, rim to rim, as if to show them what they are holding.

Then they climb to the hammocks and sleep to the sound of drip and trickle and settle, and the molars-hum of the transformer, and the tick-tick of water finding a new groove in the still-curing concrete — a sound that was not there yesterday and will be deeper tomorrow.

In a year the concrete will have cured hard and dark, the silt will have made real deltas, the fig will have split its gear entirely and the bowl will be a jungle that happens to have some concrete inside it, and they will not be needed. For now, they are what lives here: soft, damp, quiet people holding a new, wet world open with bare feet and pruned hands while it learns how to hold itself.
