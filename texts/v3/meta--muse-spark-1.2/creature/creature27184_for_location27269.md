You don't see them at first.

From a distance, with that white, high-altitude light flattening everything to cut-out and glare, the mountain looks uninhabited. It looks too big to be inhabited. It looks like a piece of geography that just happens to be green. The movement you see on the terraces could be wind in the vines, or water, or the shiver of heat coming off the stone.

Closer, the movement resolves but still doesn't look like people. It's too slow and too distributed to be a city crowd. No one hurries. No one clusters. There's a figure bent over a paddy three terraces up that holds perfectly still for so long you decide it must be a post or a scarecrow, until it shifts its weight by an inch. There's a line of color moving along a shaded ramp that turns out to be laundry, not walkers. The sound hides them too — that thick, warm drone of trip-hammers and looms and pumps swallows footsteps and voices. You hear the mountain before you hear anyone *on* it.

Then your eyes adjust to the scale and they are suddenly everywhere, and you realize they were never hidden. You were just looking for the wrong kind of inhabitant.

They are short.

Not dramatically. Not dwarfish. But built low to the ground and wide across the chest and thighs, the way anything that spends its whole life climbing has to be built. Children here learn stairs before they learn to run on flat ground, and there is no flat ground. Everywhere is either a ramp or a step, so their calves cord early, their ankles thicken, their feet splay a little wider than lowlanders'. They walk with a rolling, weight-forward gait, like porters who are always carrying something even when their hands are empty. Old people climb until they die, just slower, resting a hand on each low garden wall as they go.

They are dark and matte. At this elevation the sun would peel a pale person in a day. Generations under that close blue sky have selected for skin the color of the mountain itself — sun-bleached ochre deepened to a warm, browned umber, burnished, freckled with lichen-like moles. They cover up anyway. Loose, light layers of undyed cotton and woven ramie, long sleeves even in the heat, all of it faded and patched with brighter squares. Heads wrapped in cloth. Hands wrapped in cloth when they have to touch a railing at noon. Their eyes are what give it away — narrowed, deep-set, the iris often so dark it's hard to tell where it ends, with fine, permanent crow's feet from squinting. Many wear a kind of soot or clay around the eyes, a dark smudge that cuts the glare. You see it on children as young as three, carefully applied by a mother with a thumb.

And they breathe audibly. You notice it when one passes you on a stair — a slight catch, a deeper inhale than you expect. The air is thin. Their chests are big for their frames, bellows-like. When they sleep in the heat of the afternoon, in the shade of a fig that has rooted into a factory wall, you can hear the soft, labored sigh of it. They don't pant. They have learned not to waste air. Everything they do is paced to a lungful. The trip-hammers below that fall once every three seconds? That's their metronome. They hammer, breathe, prune, breathe. The machines didn't set the rhythm. The mountain's air did, and the machines were tuned to it a long time ago.

If you expected a city people, you will be confused by them. They are not crowded together. They are *distributed*, like the water.

There is no downtown. There is no rush hour, because no one can rush. A family — and it is almost always a family — belongs to a level. Not owns, belongs to. The Nguyens have the taro paddy on 734-West and the two looms in the shed directly behind it, under the passionflower trellis. The daughters of that terrace will marry the sons of 735-West or 733-West, rarely further than a five-minute climb away, because to marry further is to change your microclimate, your air pressure, your sound. A child born three levels higher would have to relearn how to sleep through a different pump's sigh.

So they are vertical clans more than horizontal neighborhoods. Aunts live above you, cousins below you. You know the people directly over your head by the sound of their brooms and their chickens. You may never meet someone who lives on the far face of the same elevation, though you share the same altitude sickness and the same view of clouds forming below you in the morning.

What they do cannot be divided into jobs.

You want to call one a farmer and another a mechanic and another a priest and you can't. The woman tending the textile mill is also training the gourd vines that shade the mill's roof to keep it five degrees cooler. She will stop the loom to pinch a borer off a leaf, and stop pruning to re-tension a belt. Her hands are the same hands — broad, short-fingered, nails always dark with oil and soil mixed together into a single paste that never quite washes out. The man who checks the cistern levels with a notched stick is also listening for a change in the dynamo whine that would mean a bearing is going dry three terraces down. He prays while he does it — a small, unselfconscious touch to the fern in the wall niche as he passes — not because he is pious in a way you would recognize, but because maintenance *is* observance here.

They don't build. They *tend*. That is the core distinction in language and in thought. There is a word they use for making something new and it's a slightly funny word, a child's word. The real, adult word is for keeping something in balance — *to hold*. You hold a wall. You hold a channel. You hold a child and a paddy and a pump.

That's why nothing is fast. A boy will be assigned a spillway when he is seven. His job is to watch how water wears the smooth U of it. Once a year, with his grandfather, he will take a stone and rub the lip a little lower on one side so the water favors it. That's a decade's work to move a flow by a hand's breadth. They measure time in how long mortar takes to darken, how long a fig takes to split a joint without breaking it, how long a transformer can hum before its cage needs reweaving with vine.

They talk quietly. They have to. The mountain is already loud. To shout over the stamping mills would be like shouting over weather. So they talk low, in the chest, in short phrases timed to the gaps. A language of vowels and soft consonants that carries through stone via the feet as much as through air. When two friends meet on a stair, they don't stop and chat. They fall into step together, climbing, speaking between breaths. Much of what they need to say they say with touch — a hand on a shoulder to mean *hold this for me*, a palm pressed to a wall to mean *feel this vibration, is it right?*

At dusk, when the bats pour out of every expansion joint and the swifts finally go quiet and the insects and tree frogs start up their electric night chorus, you see them most clearly. The light goes amber and horizontal and for an hour the brutal white intensity softens. They come out onto the terraces. Not to socialize, exactly, but to be horizontal themselves. They sit on the low garden walls with their legs dangling over the green drop, cats and chickens settling beside them. They shell beans into old engine casings turned into bowls. They mend cloth. They listen.

Because the most important thing they do is listen.

Every inhabitant here, from a toddler to a great-grandmother, can diagnose the mountain by ear. That slightly higher whine on level 812 means a pump is cavitating and will need priming before morning or the paddy above it will dry. That hollow clack that wasn't there yesterday means a tile has slipped in a gutter and will pool water where it shouldn't. They hear it the way you would hear a cough in your own child in the next room.

And they themselves have become part of the sound. The geckos on every wall, the bees on every ledge, the parrots, the pigs — those are not pets or livestock or pests. They are co-tenants, and the humans make no distinction in kind. The hives are built into the retaining walls on purpose; the holes left in the masonry for swifts were left *for* swifts. A child will be scolded not for letting chickens into the house, but for shooing a gecko off a warm wall where it belongs. They are not humans living in a city with animals. They are one kind of animal among many that the built mountain has learned to host, the one whose job it is to keep the gutters clear and the dynamos oiled and the soil from washing away.

If you stay long enough to be ignored, you see that they weather at exactly the same rate as the place.

Their clothes fade to bone-and-ochre. Their skin darkens and spots. The callus on a weaver's foot thickens until it has the same smooth, polished look as the spillway she walks past every day. They don't fight erosion. They *are* a form of it — patient, mature, established. When one of them dies, very old, very slowly, they are not buried down in a valley. There is no valley floor that is not built. They are composted into the terrace soil they held. A small shrine niche is cleared on the wall, a fern planted. Within a year, you can't tell which part was person and which part was place.

They don't think the mountain is a city. They don't think it's a mountain. If you asked them where they live — and you would have to ask it in their low, breath-paced way, while climbing — they would look confused, and gesture not out and not up, but *around*.

They would tell you they live *in* the holding.
