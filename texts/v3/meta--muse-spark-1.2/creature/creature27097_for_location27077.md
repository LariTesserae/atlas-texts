Who lives there is harder to see than the place itself, at first.

At first you think: no one. Or no one *living*. The heat and the wet and the roar make you think the basin is uninhabited in any real sense — that the figures in the coveralls are just more moving parts, that the egret is a decoy left there, that the whole town is an unattended machine ticking to itself.

Then you stay a breath longer and your eyes adjust to the dim, and you realize you were looking for the wrong kind of inhabitants. You were looking for something that stands out against the place. Nothing stands out against the place. Whatever lives here has learned not to.

---

The people are there, but they are not where you expect them to be.

You expect to find them behind windows, gathered in a square. They are not gathered. They are threaded through.

There are not many — maybe thirty, maybe fifty for the whole basin — enough to keep the pump thudding and the press stamping. They move singly or in pairs along the catwalks and runnels, in faded coveralls gone the color of wet brick from oil and algae and being never properly dry. The fabric sticks to them. Their skin does too. Everyone's skin is the same here after a few weeks: pale where it isn't stained, softened by the air, pruned at the fingertips permanently, beaded as if with sweat that never evaporates because the air will not take it. Hair hangs lank. Eyes are a little red from the steam and the low buzz.

They do not hurry. You can't hurry in heat that matches your blood. They walk the same line from the quarters to the powerhouse to the sorting shed and back again, not in a march but in a drift timed to the machines. A man pauses while the press goes CLACK and steps forward in the interval. A woman waits for a puff of steam to clear before she turns a valve. They don't shout over the roar. They have stopped trying. They tap the rail, they point, they put a hand on a shoulder. Their hearing has gone accustomed — not deaf, but tuned so narrowly to change that everything normal has become silence to them. If the four-beat pump missed a beat, they would startle awake from across the yard. As long as it doesn't, they can sleep ten meters from it.

They don't seem to get older here. Or rather, you can't tell. Children would not do well here — there's nothing for a child to become — and you don't see any. The people who are here look ageless in the way maintenance workers look ageless: thirties or fifties, it doesn't matter, their bodies have settled into the work. They are not residents, not exactly. They are the town's way of keeping itself.

They hang laundry that never dries just to give their hands something to do between cycles. They feed the geckos without meaning to. At night — which is not night, only the sodium lamps looking a little less unnecessary — they sit in the canteen and drink something warm from tin cups and do not talk much, because the substation has already been talking for them all day.

If you ask one of them how long they've been here, they'll wipe condensation off a pipe with their sleeve and say, "Since the drop," which is not a time. The basin dropped recently, all at once, the basalt still glassy. They arrived with it, or it arrived with them. Either way, they have no memory of not synchronizing their breathing to the pump.

---

But the people are only the most obvious animal, and not the most successful.

The basin really belongs to the things that love wet heat and still air and steady noise. That is, it belongs to the marsh that lives underneath the concrete.

You notice the mosquitoes next, because they notice you. They rise in a low, wavering sheet off every warm runnel. Not in swarms that chase — in clouds that simply hang where the air hangs, whining on the same pitch as the substation until you can't tell which whine is insect and which is electric. Dragonflies hunt through them — amber-winged, hand-big, helicoptering without wind to fight, so heavy and precise they look engineered. They perch on the hazard-yellow rails that are already blistering.

In the expansion joints of the concrete itself there are crickets, black and glossy, that have learned to chirr between the CLACKs. On every vertical surface that stays damp — which is every vertical surface — there is a stippling of geckos, pale as the damp brick, eyes too big for the dim. They do not scatter when a worker passes. They just blink. Pigeons the color of the cloud-roof shuffle on the powerhouse roof where the vents make a patch that is always a few degrees warmer, for their eggs. Swallows stitch the air under the cloud, flat and fast, turning on a wingtip because there is nowhere to migrate to.

And lower down, where water collects whether you want it to or not:

The runnels carry oil rainbows and still they are thick with life. Black-green algae makes a felt in the water and on the brick, slick as wet wool. Mosquito fish — thousands, tens of thousands — flicker in water that is never more than ankle deep but never dries, warm as a bath. They don't flee. Why would they? Nothing changes. And beside them, motionless for so long you think it must be a pipe elbow, the egret stands on one leg right next to the stamping press. White as the steam. It does not spear. It waits. The fish do not panic. The press goes CLACK. The egret blinks. This tableau has been going on for a long time and will go on. The predator and prey have come to an understanding that is not peace, exactly, but equilibrium.

Rats use the pipes as sidewalks, sleek and clean from the constant bath, whiskers beading with condensation. They know every valve. An egret could watch a rat pass and not bother. A man could watch a rat pass and just lift his foot.

Nothing here is wild in the sense of being free of the town, and nothing here is domesticated. Everything is accommodated. The algae accommodates to the oil. The gecko accommodates to the fluorescent flicker. The mosquito accommodates to the whine. The egret accommodates to the CLACK.

---

And then there is what lives here smallest, and most completely.

You have to crouch to see it, or just stop wiping your hands. On the underside of every pipe, on the lip of every culvert, under the iron plate where sweat wells up between seams, there is a sheen that is not just water. It's biofilm — bacterial and fungal — cream and rust and black-green, making the town's surfaces feel slightly tacky no matter how much water runs over them. It smells faintly sweet and metallic. It is the town's real skin. The workers' coveralls smell of it. The egret's legs are stained with it.

It does best here because here is exactly what it wants: never cold, never dry, never dark, never subject to a storm that would scrub it away. The condensation cycle — water into air into water again — is its breath. The waste heat from the powerhouse is its sun.

If you stay long enough to let your own skin stay wet, you feel it begin on you, too. The back of your neck stays damp. A faint itch blooms where your shirt sticks. You are being filmed over, gently.

That is the final inhabitants, and perhaps the truest.

Not the people, not the egrets, not even the fish, but the *loop* itself. The basin as an organism.

Because whatever dropped this place — the crack, the fall, the sudden stair-step of fresh basalt — made a dish that holds. And whatever built the factory in the dish made a set of organs that keep the dish exactly as it is. The power house burns and makes the air hot. The hot air holds the water. The water condenses on the basalt and the iron. The runnels carry it back to the waterworks, which pumps it — thud, thud, thud, thud — to make steam. The steam sighs out and becomes air again. Around and around. Nothing leaves. Nothing needs to enter except a little oil, a little current for the lights that never go off.

The people live there the way red cells live in you: not visiting, but circulating. The egrets and rats and dragonflies live there the way commensals live in a gut. The mosquitoes and algae and film live there the way flora live on skin.

And the town lives there the way a body lives in a basin that is exactly its size.

If you stand at the edge against the weeping black rock and look across those three hundred meters of dim, humming, dripping room — too large for a house, too small for a world — you understand that no one will ever arrive to change it and no one will ever need to leave it. It emerged all at once and then learned how to stay. Its inhabitants are whatever has learned to not need change.

They are sleek, and damp, and patient. They are in no hurry. They have synchronized.

Listen between the CLACKs. You can hear them breathing with the pump.
