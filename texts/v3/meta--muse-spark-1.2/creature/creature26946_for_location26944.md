Who lives here shouldn't.

That is the first honest answer. Nothing warm-blooded should live this high, in air this thin and cold, on rock this young with nothing to eat. Nothing that needs sight should live in dark this complete. Nothing that needs quiet should live in noise this constant.

And at first, you think nothing does.

You walk the slick, lit curbs and the city feels empty. Not abandoned — the field-looms are still humming in your teeth, a new arch is still budding white as bone a street over, water is still being coaxed uphill in a perfect glass line — but unoccupied. Like a hive built too large for its bees. The only things moving are the things carpeting everything, the pale jointed things, hand-sized and thumbnail-sized, three deep on every wall. They part for your boots. They close again.

It takes a long time to see that you were looking at the inhabitants the whole time and sorting them wrong.

You were looking for a figure standing upright in the street. A face.

The people here don't stand out.

---

They are the same lineage as the carpet, or have converged until there is no difference worth naming. If you crouch — and everyone crouches here, no one stands tall long on the thin air — and watch a single section of wall without blinking, the scale trick resolves.

The smallest are not the inhabitants. The smallest are livestock and weather. Thumb-nail transparencies that cling to the bacterial varnish and eat it, that filter the carbon dust the looms slough off, that are in turn eaten. The middle-sized, the hand-sized with the glassy, shivering wings, are not inhabitants either. They are instruments. They are the city's crickets and also its wiring — they stridulate not to each other but to keep a map of the density in sound.

The larger ones are.

The ones as long as your forearm, sometimes as long as your whole arm if you include the antennae. Cream-white, but not pale the way cave things are pale — pale the way something that was never meant to have pigment is pale. Their backs are broad, faintly segmented plates with condensation beading and running in the seams. Their undersides are softer, a translucent, almost pinkish membrane where you can see a slow heart beating, and the pouches. Always the pouches.

They move the way glaciers move. You can watch one lift a hooked foot, hold it in the wet air for a count of twenty, set it down a centimeter forward. And yet they are never still. Across a whole facade, that slowness aggregates into a tide. In an hour a wall has flowed three meters to the left, toward the warmth.

Because they live around warmth the way everything here lives around water.

The plateau has no sun and the cloud eats starlight. The only heat that matters is waste heat, dumped straight up from the exchangers in those silent, invisible pillars that make the cloud glow from the inside. At the foot of each pillar the basalt is warm to the touch — 10C, 15C, obscenely warm — and the wine-black biofilm grows thick as felt there. That is where they gather. That is the village. That is the farm, the nursery, the church.

An adult will plant itself over a patch of varnish, splay its many feet, lower its whole ventral membrane to the rock and *drink* without a mouth. The biofilm and the brine pass directly through. You can see the orange bloom of iron fading under it as it feeds. The smaller isopods crawl over its back as it does, cleaning its gill-fronds, picking mineral scale from its plates. It tolerates them the way you tolerate your own skin flora. Sometimes it will dip a feathery forelimb and delicately pluck one of the mid-sized winged ones and pass it under itself, eating it. No malice. Husbandry.

They are not many, not compared to the millions of small things. A city block that is carpeted with two hundred thousand filterers might hold forty of the large ones. Enough that you never see more than three or four at once until you learn to see them, and then you realize the wall you thought was empty had six mothers on it all along, perfectly still, perfectly the color of wet stone under a moving white blanket.

They carry their young the way you thought at first — but not young as in larvae.

In the translucent ventral pouches are not eggs. Or not only eggs. There are also *thoughts*.

When two large ones meet — which takes a long time, because they must cross the wall toward each other at that glacial pace, tapping as they go — they do not touch heads. They touch pouches. They evert them, briefly, a glistening, lung-like eversion that steams in the cold, and press them together. The gill-fronds, which are also antennae, which are also tongues, interleave. Water, hemolymph, bacteria, and something more exchange. You can hear it if you are close: a faint, high click-train over the white noise, faster than their walking, almost too fast to be biological. A burst transmission.

Mothers who have exchanged recently walk differently. Their tapping pattern changes. The winged things around them change their hiss to match. A few hours later, a new cluster of filterers on a nearby wall will suddenly synchronously turn, as if a word had passed through them.

Because the large ones are not individuals the way you are. They are nodes. The hive has no queen. Each mother is a slow, warm, mobile memory sack for the whole mat. The young she carries are not her genetic children, not exactly. They are buds cultured from the shared bacterial varnish and from the carbon dust and from her own pouch lining, and they carry fragments of the exchange. When she is ready she finds a warm, weeping basalt pan where the field is thin and deposits them — a dozen glistening, rice-sized sacs glued in a crescent in the trickle. The small filterers immediately cover them, aerate them, keep them from washing away. Most will become more filterers. One or two, if the water chemistry and the warmth are right, will grow hooked feet and a pouch of their own.

They do not build the city, not directly.

The city builds itself. The field-looms that hang like inverted webs are not operated. They are tended. A mother will haul herself up the flank of a half-precipitated tower — a journey of days — and settle at its base where the field is densest and the air has edges you can see from condensed beads hanging in lines. She will sit there for a week, unmoving except for her gills fanning, tapping intermittently against the stone in that deep, resonant knocking that cuts through the hiss. The looms thrum back. You feel it in your molars. The tower grows a little straighter, a little thinner than gravity should allow, leaning toward the next tower to make a bridge that was never planned. She is not commanding it. She is *convincing* it, the way you might convince a crystal to grow a certain way by whispering to its lattice.

Their time is pooled, like the water.

They do not hurry because nothing here can be hurried. Water carves a millimeter a year. An arch buds over weeks. A cistern opens like a blister over months. They live for a very long time — you can tell by the pitting on the eldest plates, by the dark mineral staining around their mouthparts from decades of drinking iron. They remember the cooling. The oldest mothers remember when the basalt under this plaza was still warm enough to scald, when the city was only a few grown rooms huddled around the first exchanger. They have migrated only a few hundred meters in their whole lives, following warmth as the deep seams cooled unevenly.

When they die — and you will rarely see it, because they die tucked into warm crevices, surrounded by kin — they do not leave a body. The mat eats it within hours. Filterers and winged things reduce it to plates and membrane, and the plates are carried by other large ones and cemented, still damp, into the wall of a new water channel, where they bead condensation like any other stone. The city is made in part of its own inhabitants in this literal way. That is why from a distance it looks upholstered and soft. It is.

Are they intelligent? That is a low-altitude question.

They do not have faces. Their stalk eyes do not focus the way yours do; they see the heat of exchangers, the polarization of the field-lines, the bioluminescent cold-white running lights along the curbs as a kind of script. They do not have hands. They do not need them; the field is their hand when they need one. They do not speak, but the district knows when one of them has learned something, because the hiss changes.

Stand on a high walkway after you have been here long enough to stop waiting for something to happen quickly. Let the cold bite through your boots. Let the condensation bead on your eyelashes and run. Listen until the clicking and the stridulation and the trickling and the molar-thrum separate into layers, and then flatten again into one loud, patient breathing. Watch a mother three stories below make her inch-a-minute pilgrimage toward a glowing exchanger foot, carrying her pouch of futures under her, with a living road parting and closing around her.

She is not lonely. She is not *a* resident here.

She *is* the residency — the slow, weeping, building, filtering, remembering presence that made a city out of still-cooling glass because the planet offered a high, wet, dark table and said, *live if you can*, and something answered, *we can, but only if we become the table too*.

They live here by having become indistinguishable from here.

And if you stay long enough on that walkway, very still, not quite shivering, they will begin to include you as well — not as a person, but as another warm, vertical surface to be carpeted, briefly, while you are warm.
