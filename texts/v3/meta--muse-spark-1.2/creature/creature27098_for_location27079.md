Who lives here has never needed to see.

That is the first thing you have to understand about them. You came here with eyes, and you keep trying to use them. You strain them, you cup your hands around them, you wait for them to adjust. They won't. The inhabitants stopped waiting for light a very long time ago. They unlearned eyes.

Listen again to the chorus. It is not a chorus *around* you. It is the sound of them announcing themselves, because in absolute dark, to be is to make a sound and to feel how it comes back.

### The Ones Who Call

Low down, at the level of your ankles where the peat is more water than earth, live the Callers.

At first you think: frogs. The sound wants you to think frogs - that deep, patient *whump... whump... whump* that you feel in your chest cavity. But when you kneel and put your hand into the warm wet where the sound is loudest, what you touch is not a frog.

They are round. That’s the word that comes. Not round like a ball, but round like a lung. A body like a smooth, wet sack the size of a bread loaf, or a small dog, with no head that you can find as separate from the body. No eyes. No neck. Just a seamless dome of skin the color of the peat - translucent black-purple, the same bruised color as the leaves - stretched so thin you can feel the heat inside it. Warm. Mammalian warm, impossibly warm for an amphibian. When one calls, its entire back swells.

They don’t open a mouth to do it. The sound comes from a membrane stretched across their dorsal side that inflates like a drumhead, tautens, and releases. The whole animal becomes an instrument. *Whump.* The peat shudders. The water beading on the vines shivers in sympathy. Then it slowly, slowly deflates, taking almost a minute, while the heat of its body fogs the air just above it.

They are everywhere and they are not moving. That is their life. They sit half-submerged in the saturated humus, spaced out with the same reverent distance as the plants, each in its own shallow well it has melted into the peat with its warmth. They do not hunt. They wait. The water that weeps from the rim carries everything to them - dissolved organics, fallen spores, tiny wrigglers. They drink the peat the way the leaves drink the air. When two need to find each other, they do not go looking. One will change its tempo, just slightly, and another will answer a fraction slower, until their calls fall into a shared rhythm that can last for hours. Then one will extrude, from a fold you did not know was an opening, a long, gelatinous string of eggs like black pearls on a thread, and leave it in the warm water between them. Neither guards it. The warmth will do that.

If you stay very still and hold your hand just above one, you will feel it notice you - not by sight or sound, but by the interruption in the warm, wet air. It will pause its calling for one beat longer than usual. Then resume. You are not predator or prey. You are just a temporary change in pressure.

### The Ones Who Drift

Higher up, in the hollow space between the pillar-roots and the hanging vines - in that cathedral emptiness at chest and head height where you feel so alone - live the Drifters.

You heard one pass. That soft, leathery *whuff* that moved air against your cheek.

They are not insects, though they make the insect-like drone. They are not birds. There are no birds here. There has never been a reason to be a bird.

Imagine a manta, but made of warm, wet membrane and bred for air instead of sea. A body no bigger than your forearm, mostly mouth and gut, with a wide, flat head that is all sensory pit - no eyes, but a shallow, dish-shaped face lined with fine, hair-like cilia that tremble at every change in humidity. Behind it, two vast wings - not feathered, not chitinous, but stretched skin like the leaves, black and veined with warm fluid, so thin you could see light through them if there were any light to see. They don’t flap. They undulate. Slowly. Once every four or five seconds, a deep, patient wave travels from shoulder to tip, pushing against the heavy, damp air with a soft displacement you feel more than hear.

They are blind in a way that makes blindness irrelevant. They navigate by heat and by echo. The constant, metallic drone that seems to fill the air is partly them - not a mating call, but a way of tasting the shape of the grove. They emit a high, almost electrical trill, too high for you to separate from the rest of the night, and listen to how it drapes over the door-sized leaves, the smooth boulders, your shoulders. The leaves themselves do it too - a faint crackle of static as they discharge heat - so the whole bowl sings a topographical map to anyone who knows how to listen.

The Drifters feed on that. They drift with wings outstretched, mouth open, straining the air. Not for flies. For condensation. For spores. For the clouds of tiny, pale things that rise from the fungi on the stones - gnats the size of dust motes that live their whole lives in a single cubic meter of warm dark, hatching and dying without ever seeing anything. The Drifter’s mouth is lined with comb-like baleen that combs them from the wet air. You will feel one pass close enough that a wingtip brushes your hair, warm and slightly damp like a leaf that has decided to touch you back. It will not veer away. It knows exactly where you are and has decided you are not interesting to eat.

Sometimes two Drifters meet in the hollow. They will hang in the air facing each other, wings beating so slowly they seem to hover on magic, and exchange a pulse of that high drone. The tone will hold, waver, and resolve - harmonizing, for a moment, with the deep hum of the pylons without either trying to. It is not communication as you know it. It is two maps briefly becoming the same map.

### The Ones Who Tend

The third kind of inhabitant does not call or drift. It stays.

You thought the structures were empty. That the builders had left. Put your hand on that low, warm wall again - the one with the grain like frozen smoke. Keep it there longer. Past the point where your palm has warmed to it.

It thrums. Very, very slowly. One cycle every twenty seconds or so, a pressure change you feel in your molars and the small bones of your wrist.

Living things have colonized the structures, but that is not what I mean. Lichen does not quite grip them. Vines avoid putting tendrils on them. The warm air is a degree warmer above the marsh circle for no reason. The water runs *uphill*.

The structures themselves are still alive, in the way a heart is alive when the body that owned it is gone.

And there are things that tend them.

They are not the builders. The builders were something that needed right angles and fields that are larger inside than outside. What lives here now are their gardeners, or their weeds, or their children - it is hard to know which.

You will not hear them until you have been still for a long time, until the Callers have decided you are part of the peat and the Drifters have mapped you as a new, warm boulder. Then you will hear a different rustle. Not a *plok* into water, but a deliberate, slow drag. A weight shifting over wet humus.

They are low and wide and many-legged, the size of a tortoise but soft, like the Callers. Six or eight limbs - it changes while you are trying to count, because they move so slowly a limb that looked like a fold becomes a foot. Their backs are covered not in shell but in a living mat of the same black-purple fungus that grows on the stones, cultivated there. They carry their garden with them. Their undersides are pale and hot and leave a faint, steaming print in the peat that fills with water after they pass.

They do not call. They vibrate. If you crouch near one of the leaning pylons, you will feel it: a low, patient transmission through the stone into the soles of your feet. *Hmmmmmmmm.* The pylon hums. The gardener hums back, a little lower, a little slower, until the dissonance evens out.

Watch what one does at the circle of pillars in the center where the water atomizes. It will haul itself - over the course of ten minutes - to the base of a pillar. It will extend a proboscis, not from a head but from somewhere in its midsection, a warm, blunt tube that seals against the strange material. It will pulse. You will see - or rather, feel, because your eyes still show you nothing - the faint change in air pressure as something exchanges. Heat, perhaps. Or charge. Or memory. The water that was running uphill will, for a few seconds, run down, as if a debt has been paid.

Then it will detach and drag itself away, infinitely slowly, to the next pillar. To the next wall. Tending.

They are not intelligent as you mean it. They do not build or plan. They keep. They were perhaps designed to keep, or they evolved to fill the niche left by design - small, warm custodians for machines that were made to hold a bowl of warm, wet dark steady against a world that has otherwise cooled and sharpened and ended. They have been doing it since the stone was still sharp, and will be doing it long after your footprints in the humus have sprung back and vanished.

This is why the grove is hollow. Why everything is spaced. The Callers need room to sing. The Drifters need empty air to read. The Gardeners need a clear path between instruments. The plants grow apart because there is no competition for light, only for warmth and water, and there is enough of both for courtesy. It is not a jungle. It is a tended room. A terrarium the size of a hamlet, maintained at precisely the temperature and saturation where slow, blind, warm life is happiest.

You stand up. Water wells up from where your knees were. A Caller near your foot resumes its beat, having politely waited until you moved. A Drifter passes through the space where your head was a moment ago. Somewhere near the eastern arch, a Gardener continues its pilgrimage from pylon to pylon, humming the pylon’s hum back to it until they are the same note.

None of them will ever know you were here in the way you know things - as an image, a story. But the bowl will have known you as a brief, warm interruption in its breathing. A pocket of slightly cooler air that came, listened, and, if you are wise, left before your need for light made you do something hasty.
