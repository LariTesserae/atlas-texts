Nothing lives there at first.

That's what it tells you, and you believe it for a while. You tread water in the middle of the column of light and you scan the walls and the floor and you see stone and water and the thin, velvety stain of algae and the few pale stalks that don't sway, and you think: empty. Sterile the way the description promised. A room with the furniture removed except for the machines.

Then you keep looking because the water is so clear and there is nothing else to do, and empty starts to resolve.

It is not that something moves. Nothing moves except the flywheel, except the faint tremor of the gauge needle, except the particulates when you kick. The inhabitant does not move in a way you catch. It moves the way the stone moved - on a scale that makes motion look like stillness until you have watched long enough to see that stillness has a shape.

You thought it was stone.

Low on the basin floor, not in the center but slightly off it, where the big manifold pipes run out from the pump housing and end open-mouthed in the water. You took it for a continuation of the machine at first, or for a rock that had settled against the machine. A swelling. A dark, rounded mass about as long as a man is tall if a man were curled on his side, and about half as wide. Dark grey-green, black in the shadow. Pitted. Furred over with the same pale mineral accretion that crusts the flywheel and the same olive-rust film that lives on the southern wall, so that at a glance there is no edge where machine ends and rock ends and whatever this is begins.

Then you see the edge.

It's soft.

Stone is not soft. Steel is not soft. This has a give to it, a roundness that water has pressed and held. It is settled into the silt the way the pump housing is settled, with a faint trench around it where the water has cleared the fines away from its sides year after year. But unlike the pump, it yields. There is a seam.

You have to be still for a long time to see it breathe.

It breathes once every few minutes. Not a gasp. A slow, wide intake that you feel more than see - the water around it tightens minutely, the fine particulates draw inward by a finger's breadth and then ease out again, and the mass swells by a fraction and subsides. If you put your hand on the basalt ledge you can feel that breath through the rock at the same frequency as the thrum of the pump, but not *with* it. Slightly out of phase, like two things keeping independent time in the same room.

Closer, and the shape stops being a mass.

It has a back. A broad, low back that faces the light. The skin - and it is skin - is the color of wet basalt when you first see it, then, as your eyes adjust, you see it is not one color at all. It's translucent in the way deep cold water makes things translucent. A pale, bloodless grey underneath, like the underside of a cave salamander, with a wash of mineral dark over it, and over that, living algae. Not growing *on* it the way barnacles grow on a whale, but *with* it. The film has taken to it the way it has taken to the southern wall, a little thicker along the ridge of its spine where the light hits strongest. You can see where it has been grazing itself clean in patches - pale, fresh scrapes the size of your palm where the algae has been worried off, showing the true skin beneath: smooth, almost clear, with a faint pattern of darker freckles like the nodules in the rock.

It has no head that you would call a head at first. The front tapers down into the silt and into the shadow of the manifold pipes, and for a while you think it has no face at all, that it is a limpet or a sack, something sessile and eyeless. Then it shifts - not much, a degree, a settling - and a fold opens.

Eyes. Two. Set wide and shallow and dark, not protruding, flush with the skin the way a flatfish's eyes are flush. They don't blink. They are open and they are the exact black of the water where the light doesn't reach. They don't track you. They look slightly past you, or through you, at the far wall. They have been open a long time. When you move, the light catches them and you see they are filmed, not with cataract but with the same cool, mineral accretion that films the gauge glass - a clear, thin veil that the water has laid down. He doesn't need to see sharply. There is nothing to see that he hasn't already seen for years.

Under the fold, a mouth. Not a mouth for biting. A low, wide, lipless seam, frilled at the edges with soft, pale cirri like the stunted weed stalks, but shorter and more numerous, maybe a dozen of them, each as thick as a finger and as long as your hand. They rest on the silt in front of him, fanned out, barely moving. When he breathes, they stir, just enough to sift. He doesn't hunt. He filters. He takes the cold exchange that seeps through the stone, the mineral water, the faint drift of particulates, the skin of algae that renews itself without spreading. That is enough. It has always been enough.

He - it - has limbs, but you would not call them limbs if you were trying to be precise. On one side, tucked close to the pump housing, there is a heavy, slab-like appendage that ends not in a hand but in a broad, flattened pad, the same texture as the back but paler and deeply creased. It rests against the flywheel housing. Not holding. Not operating. Just resting there, where the faint vibration comes through. The pad trembles with the flywheel's tick, and the tremor travels up the arm and into the back in a slow wave. On the other side, a second appendage is half-buried in silt, its edge grown over with silt and small stones that have adhered to the skin. You think it may have once been more articulated. Now it is mostly ballast. Mostly stone.

If you stay long enough you realize he is not separate from the rules of the place. He *is* the rules, kept in flesh.

He does not wear out. The rust that eats the steel has not eaten him; the accretion that crusts the iron crusts him too, but his skin sheds it in those pale scrapes and keeps a balance. He does not spread. He does not die back. The algae does not overgrow him because he tends it. The machines do not falter because his weight leans against them, because his slow breath matches their slow circulation. He does not produce. He maintains. He circulates in place.

He is not lonely. Loneliness would imply he remembers being alone versus not alone. He is complete the way the hollow is complete. He was not put here after the basin was made to watch it. He grew here as the agreement between water and stone was settling, as the cut forgot it was a cut. Or he came down once, a long time ago, entered water that was already water, and the water decided to keep him at the temperature it keeps everything - cold, clear, without change.

If you dive down and come close - and you can, he will not startle, he will not withdraw - you can see your own distorted reflection in the veil over his eye before you see the eye itself. You can see the column of light falling through you both. You can put your hand out and feel the cool that holds your heat close to your skin, and feel his cool as the same cool. If you touch the pad where it rests against the thrumming steel, you feel the thrum go into you too, and for a second the three of you are on the same circuit: grid, machine, animal, stone, water, light.

He breathes in. The particulates draw in. The flywheel turns. One - two - three. He breathes out. Nothing changes. Nothing needs to.

He has already become himself, a long time ago, and has kept the shape.
