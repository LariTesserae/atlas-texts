They don't live here the way people live in other places. They *belong* here, the way lichen belongs to rock.

At first you think you are looking at a crowd. Then you realize you are looking at a colony.

You have to let your eyes adjust to the brightness and the wet to see them properly. From the rim they look like more texture on the cliff — another layer of dark stone and pale thatch and moving white. Then a shape resolves. Then another. Then you understand the whole north wall *is* them, or they are it.

### The People

They are short. Not stunted — compacted. Low to the ground, wide through the chest and shoulders, the kind of bodies that happen when ten generations are born at fourteen thousand feet where every breath is work. Their ribcages are great barrels. When they breathe you can see it, even at rest, the whole torso lifting. Their blood is thick with red.

They are pale in a way that has nothing to do with not seeing the sun. They see nothing *but* sun. Their skin is sun-burned and salt-burned to the same color — a weathered, wind-polished pink-brown at the cheeks and hands, almost white where the wool covers them. The salt gets into everything. It crusts their eyebrows, their knuckles, the seams of their clothes. Their hands are white up to the wrist, not from pigment but from brine, the skin thickened and fissured into soft cracks like drying mud. They don't wipe it off.

Their eyes are the first thing that tells you they are not visitors. Yours are watering, squinting, hurt by the doubled light off snow and tarn. Theirs are narrow, lidded, a pale, washed-out grey-blue that seems to have given up trying to be a color. Many have a faint second lid, a milky caul that drops halfway when they look toward the cornice. They don't squint. They look directly at the glare and see. Their hair, where it escapes from heavy felted wool caps, is coarse and dark but bleached at the tips to rust and straw, even on children.

They dress for wet, not for cold, which is a strange decision until you feel how wet it is. Layer upon layer of undyed wool, soot-black goat wool and a softer, greyer wool that must come from something you haven't seen yet — matted, fulled, never dry. Sealskin aprons, or what looks like sealskin, though no seal could live here, lacquered with oil until it shines. High, soft boots of the same skin, laced to the knee, endlessly dripping. No one wears anything that would make a noise. No jewelry that clinks. Even the children move softly.

And they are quiet because the bowl requires quiet. Their language is low and mostly hands. A palm held out means *give water.* Two fingers tapped on a lintel means *this cistern is mine for the hour.* A quick flick of salt-white fingers across the throat means *bird coming through, duck.* When they do speak, it is a murmur you feel as breath more than hear. An old woman calling to a boy across a chasm of dripping walkways doesn't shout. She clicks her tongue twice and he looks. Sound doesn't carry here. They've stopped trying to make it.

What they do, all day, without pause, is tend the weep.

You thought it was a town that happened to be in a wet place. It is the opposite. It is a machine for living with water that should not be water.

Every family, every ledge, has its cistern — a basin chiseled into the living basalt, sometimes no bigger than a cooking pot, sometimes a room-sized tank cut deep into the cliff, lidded with slate to keep the birds out. Gutters run everywhere, not as an afterthought but as the primary architecture. Every roof slab is tilted a degree or two, every lintel is grooved. The strings of water braiding down the rock are not allowed to wander. Little dams of beeswax and pine resin, tiny diverting walls of stone no higher than your thumb, coax them sideways, gather them, drop them from ledge to ledge into the next waiting mouth. On every flat surface that gets sun — and every surface gets brutal sun — are the salt pans: shallow stone trays, black wooden vats, stretched skins, all filled with dark brine, steaming faintly as the thin, furious sun evaporates them. A pink-white crust forms by midday. Women and old men and children scrape it with flat bone shovels, with a soft *shhhhk* that you can only hear if you stand very close. They bag it in wool sacks. They stack it. Salt is what they eat, what they trade down the mountain when a trail opens for a week in summer, what keeps them from needing fields. The goats give milk and wool and meat, the tarn gives shrimp, but salt is the crop.

They do not farm the lichen or the little campion flowers. They leave them. You realize after watching for a while — nobody picks them, steps on them if they can avoid it. They are markers. Where the black lichen grows thickest, the seep is freshest. Where the orange crust blooms, the brine is strongest.

They live pressed together in a way that would feel desperate anywhere else. Three, four generations in a house the size of a shed, sleeping on platforms stacked like shelves, all sharing the heat of a single small hearth that burns dung and oily bird fat and never quite warms the stone. Doors are never closed, because a closed door would block a gutter. Walkways cross directly over hearths, under beds. Privacy is a wool curtain, briefly drawn. And yet there is no jostling. In that dense, three-dimensional hive, everyone knows the choreography. A boy with a bucket of brine passes a girl with an empty bucket on a plank no wider than his foot and neither stops. A man dismantles his own outer wall stone by stone while his neighbor's child naps a meter away against that same wall. They build and unbuild constantly — not from restlessness but from necessity. The water changes its mind. A new crack opens, an old seep dries for a season, a cistern wall calcifies and must be moved. The town breathes in stone.

They are not hardy in the way mountain people are supposed to be hardy, grim and enduring. They are *busy*. Their hands are never still. Even at rest, an old man sitting on a balcony in the blinding light is mending a net of sinew, or sorting shrimp by size into a bowl, or simply holding his hands out to catch the mist as it condenses, watching the water bead and run into the lines of his palms.

And they are not alone in the work.

### The Others Who Live Here

The white birds — you keep calling them birds because you don't have another word — are not their livestock. They are their neighbors, their competitors, their roommates. Thousands of them. They have hollow, light bones and beaks too short and wide for any lowland gull, and those strange tails, not feathers but long, translucent frills that hang like strips of ice and chime faintly when they fly, though you never quite hear it. They nest nowhere else but on these wet ledges. They drink the same brine, eat the same shrimp, and leave the same pink-white guano that the people scrape and mix into mortar. The people do not chase them from the cisterns. They build little peaked roofs over the water to keep the birds out, and the birds sit on those roofs. Sometimes a woman shoos one with a soft wave. The bird looks at her with that same pale eye and stays. They have an agreement.

The shrimp are not shrimp, either, but you will keep calling them that. In the dark tarn they move as one rust-red cloud, each no longer than your thumbnail, translucent with a bright dot of blood inside. They swarm so thick the water looks solid, then part for no reason and show the black depth below. The people harvest them with fine nets on long poles, skimming without disturbing the hovering droplets. The birds harvest them by dipping their wide beaks. The shrimp harvest the mineral sweat of the rock itself — they are the thing that makes the brine into food.

On the rock between, where the water is only a film, the pale insects — each as long as your finger, with hairy legs like frost — skate and drink and are eaten by the birds and, occasionally, gathered by children who roast them on hot stones until they pop and eat them like salted nuts.

And the goats. The soot-black goats are the only thing that looks like it could live anywhere else, until you watch one walk straight up a wet, vertical face to lick a seam of brine, its cloven hooves splayed impossibly wide, its wool so heavy with water it drips in a line behind it. They belong to no one and everyone. They wander through houses, over roofs, and get nudged aside gently. In return they give the wool and the milk that steams and instantly skins over in the cold.

No one is in charge here, or everyone is. There is no chief's house larger than the others, no temple. If there is worship, it is the tending itself — the scraping, the channeling, the endless, patient catching of water that the mountain insists on giving. The oldest people — men and women with faces like the terraced stone, deeply grooved and shining — sit closest to where the weep comes strongest out of the rock, where the water runs clear inside the ice. They don't speak. They watch the flow.

When midday comes and the light goes flat and shadowless and the droplets over the tarn suspend themselves, the whole bowl pauses. Not stops — pauses. A bird hangs motionless for a second. A child holds out a bowl. A goat stills mid-step. They've seen it every day of their lives. It's not a miracle. It's just the moment when the bowl holds its breath before it lets go.

Then the droplets fall, the tarn dimples, the shrimp bloom red, and everyone resumes, ticking and dripping and quietly, turbulently living, crowded and wet and bright, exactly as this impossible place allows.
