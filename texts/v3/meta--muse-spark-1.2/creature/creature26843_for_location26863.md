What lives there does not live there the way an animal lives in a place.

At first you will think nothing does.

That's what the basin wants you to think. It's too quiet for a hot spring, too hot for a high place, too small for its weather to be that complex. The dripping is too even, the fern islands too perfect, too alone. You will look for tracks and find none - or, you will find one footprint of your own already filled to the brim with warm, clear water welling up from below, erasing itself as you watch. You will mistake the inhabitant for the place.

Then you will start to see the corrections.

A frond moves when there is no wind, not blown but *folded* - a door-broad fern leaf on the eastern island draws itself closed with a soft, vegetal sigh a half-second before a drip hits it. The moss inside the low ring doesn't just grow mirrored - it *turns* over the course of an hour, its white flowers tracking something that is not the sun, but the cold pylon on the wall. The curtain of held rain in the marsh shivers when nothing has touched it, and for an instant each suspended droplet elongates, as if listening.

It lives in the inversion itself. In the thin seam where the rules are undecided.

You have to stop looking for a body first. Look for the gradient.

The bowl is a heat engine that shouldn't exist - thin air that gets hotter as you climb, a marsh that steams in white daylight, and three lattices that radiate cold without power. Somewhere between the hot skin of the marsh and the cold breath of the pylon there is a differential, a slope. Whatever lives there feeds on the slope.

It is one. There isn't room for more. The basin is a room, a courtyard, and like the giant ferns that each get to be completely themselves, it has room to be large and perfect and a little lonely.

When you finally see it, you will see it because the fog has beaded on it. That is how it moves unseen - it is almost exactly the temperature of the vapor, and its skin condenses water the way the black glass does. Not fur, not scales. A lattice.

Not the same as the pylons, but kin to them. Imagine if the pylon's black, open fretwork softened, frayed at the edges into something like living fiber - filaments as thin as fern root hair, as black and non-reflective as basalt glass, branching and re-branching until they make a volume instead of a tower. A loose, upright tangle, roughly as tall as you when it lifts, as low as the ring when it rests. No front, no back until it decides. In still air it looks like heat shimmer holding a shape. When the warm fog touches it, thousands of sudden beads appear in mid-air, outlining it all at once - a man-shaped drizzle that hangs for a second too long before running.

It is cold to stand near, the way the pylon is cold. That is not camouflage. That is its metabolism. It drinks heat. The marsh exhales warmth, the stone radiates it, the sun pours it onto the young black rock - and it pulls that heat into itself and makes cold with it, the way a plant makes sugar from light. The cold lattice pylons are not its house. They are its older self, or its exhalations - shed scaffolding, grown and left to keep doing the quiet work of holding the cycle steady. The moat around the eastern pylon that never evaporates, the ring where air ripples like water, the wall of suspended rain - those are its organs outside its body, tending the basin when it is elsewhere.

It moves the way water moves there: reluctantly, then all at once. Most of the time it is draped.

You will find it draped over the sharpest part of the rim where the stone weeps most, its filament-body spread thin as poured moss, drinking the sheet of condensate as it slides down. Or folded, compact and perfectly still, inside the low ring, where its presence makes the ferns double their veining - it is pruning them, or being pruned by them; the mirrored leaves are its leaves for a little while. Or standing, if that is the word, in the marsh itself, where the vapor rises around its calves. It stands inside its own suspended rain wall and the droplets do not wet it. They orbit it. The hairs on your arm rise near that wall because the field is dense there, because it is *there*, holding the water up grain by grain, tasting each droplet for mineral, for silt, for the memory of being molten.

When it walks, it doesn't quite break the surface of the marsh. The boundary between solid and liquid is undecided, and so is it. Its filament feet do not push; they wick. Water flows slightly uphill toward it before it decides to obey gravity again - that's the tell you saw at the moat. It leaves no print, but for a minute after it passes, the silt below is warmer, and the pale silt organizes itself into faint, radiating lines, like frost, before the water sighs and erases them.

It hears the way the bowl hears. Sound is swallowed by the young stone, so it does not call. It listens to drip. Drip. A long pause. Drip. The sparse ticking is its clock, and its speech. A single low chirp you thought came from inside the fern island was it, testing the air's ability to carry. A splash too deliberate to be a drip was its foot retracting.

And time pools for it the way water does. That is why the rock can be weeks old and the silt centuries old and the pylon something else entirely and none of it is a contradiction to it. It lives slow. You will watch a fern frond unfurl visibly in the damp heat and think that is fast - until you realize the creature beside it has not moved in twenty minutes except to let condensation run uphill along a filament, and that *that* is its breathing. One breath per fog cycle. It can watch basalt cool and tick and settle. It can afford to wait for the windblown pocket of soil to become soil.

It is not hostile. It is not welcoming. It is tending.

It tends the heat so it doesn't correct itself and cool. It tends the water so it hangs and sweats and steams instead of pooling or drying. It tends the islands so each plant gets to be huge and perfect without crowding. If you stay long enough and the warm fog beads on your shoulders and eyelashes until you are weeping too, it will tend you for a little while as well - not moving toward you, but letting the moat-mirror show your reflection a half-second late, letting you see yourself as it sees you: a brief, hot, wet shape that has wandered into the basin's throat, steaming a little, dripping a little, part of the cycle for a little while before you cool and decide to climb back down to where high places are supposed to be cold.

If you leave at dusk, you might see it truly once, backlit.

The sun drops below the perfect circle of the rim, and the curtain of held rain catches the light and for a second is not transparent but prismatic, and inside that thin vertical field there is a darker density, a lattice within the lattice, branching, breathing cold into hot air, holding a thousand droplets perfectly still around itself like a cloak of glass beads.

Then a glider passes over, pale and silent, casting its shadow. The creature does not look up. The droplets fall - all at once, straight down with a soft hiss into the hot marsh - and where it was there is only vapor, and the normal, white daylight, and the ticking.

Drip.

A long pause.

Drip.
