Nothing lives here well.

That's the first thing you understand, standing knee-deep in warm water that doesn't know which way is down, with the roar filling your skull until your teeth ache. Nothing lives here easily, or abundantly, or for long. What lives here lives *in spite of* — clenched, thinned, made half-transparent by the need to not be torn off the rock and recirculated up into the sky-wall and poured over the lip again.

At first you think the basin is empty. And for long stretches, it is. You can stare for ten minutes at that bare, sharp basalt floor through strobing amber dusk and see only stone and warm silt and the water itself going mad. Then a current folds wrong and clears, and you see that the emptiness was never empty — only still in the way these things know how to be still.

#### The Holdfasts

Lowest down, where the stone is almost always stone and not suddenly water-wall, there is a darkening. Not algae, at first — you take it for a stain, a mineral bloom where the warm water has left iron. Then the vortex passes and the stain ripples.

It is algae. Or it is what algae becomes when there is no soil and no season and the light has to be sieved through five meters of churning, humming water. It grows as a skin, black-green and slick, no more than a few millimeters thick, stretched tight over the leeward faces of every ledge and step. It has no fronds. It has fingers. When the water is calm — the one second in twenty when it is calm — those fingers lie flat, hair-fine, feeling the stone. When the surge comes they lift all at once and whip straight out into the current, straining. They are not photosynthesizing so much as drinking light. And vibration.

If you put your hand close you see they are not plants. The mat pulses. Very slowly. A peristalsis that moves sideways across the rock against the current. And if you watch long enough you see the small, clear shrimp — later — grazing on it, and the mat closing around the grazer's legs and holding.

Above the mats are the trees that are not trees.

There are only three of them. You thought there were one or two. There are three, because one was hidden behind a standing sheet of water that never fell. They are pale as new scar tissue, the color of the blind fish, jointed and translucent. They have not grown so much as endured upward. Their roots are not roots; they are hands. White, woody knee-roots splinter into the cracks in the basalt and swell inside them, locking with pressure, so that each tree is literally wedged into the mountain. You could not pull them out without breaking the stone.

Their trunks are thin as your wrist, not barked but skinned, semi-clear, and you can see the warm water moving inside them, pumped. They have no leaves because leaves would be torn off in an hour. Instead their branches end in those waxy bladders you noticed — grape-sized, amber, full of gas. They inflate over minutes, trembling in the turbulence, until they pop with a soft *tick* you can never hear over the roar but you feel as a little pressure change on your face. Each pop releases a cloud of spores and, more often than not, a burst of smaller bladders, already inflated, that do not rise or fall but drift sideways and snag on another branch or on the rock wall and start again. The trees are filtering the water not for food but for grit — for the crushed shell and basalt flour suspended everywhere — and making more of themselves out of it, grain by grain.

They are terribly slow. You get the feeling they have been trying to make a fourth tree for a very long time and the current keeps taking the attempt away.

#### The Drifters

When something swims here, it doesn't swim.

The first time you see the ribbon you think it's debris — a strip of plastic, a shed skin. It's longer than your forearm and no thicker than your thumb, milk-white, flat, with no eyes worth the name, only two shallow pits that have sunk back into the head. It does not undulate. It hangs.

It lets the current do everything. It will go rigid and the vortex will throw it vertical, head down, tail up, perfectly still while the water screams around it. Then it will flex once, not to propel but to catch a different shear, and snap sideways ten feet like it was flicked by an invisible finger. It lives by not fighting. Its whole body is a lateral line — a long, exposed nerve down each side that reads pressure the way you read light. In a place where hearing is impossible, it feels the shape of the roar. It knows where the water is about to peel into a window-sheet, where a vortex is about to drill, where it is calm for a breath. You watch it hover near one of those hanging liquid windows and you finally see what it eats: the shrimp.

The shrimp are everywhere once you learn to see failure. They are almost perfectly clear, the size of the last joint of your finger, with too many legs that end in tiny hooks. They do not swim at all. They cling. They cling to the underside of the water-sheets, to the underside of the overhang where water pools and drips *up*, to the underside of your own arm if you hold still too long. They cling to the trees' bladders until they pop. Their bodies are full of the same warm water they live in, so they are neutrally buoyant to the point of irrelevance; gravity negotiates around them and they ignore it. When a sheet shatters, they are thrown — and they tumble, legs spread, until another surface presents itself and they hook on again. Inside each, you can see a single dark speck — gut, heart, egg — like a pepper flake in glass.

The ribbon doesn't chase them. It positions itself where the sheets are about to fail, mouth open, and the shrimp rain into it sideways.

There is also what lives in the silt, which you won't see unless a drill-vortex sucks the floor clean and then spits it back. Quick, coiled things, white and blind as the ribbon but smaller, that make the shells. They are not snails. They are soft, slug-like, and they secrete a shell not to live in but to *leave* — a heavy, calcium whorl that anchors them for a day while they filter, then is abandoned when the next surge would drag them if they stayed. That's why the floor is littered with shells at a thousand meters too high for any sea. The floor *is* shells. Generations of trying to stay put and failing, grain by grain.

#### The Keeper

And then there is the one who uses the stair.

You don't see him — her — it — at first because you are looking for an animal. You think any human here would be dead in a day. Deafened, scoured, drowned. But someone cut that stair. Someone set that bowl on the altar and left it to vibrate. Someone still does, because when you wade closer you see the bowl is not full of silt. It is clean. The water inside it is changed daily, though no rain falls here that is not already the wall.

They live in the room without a roof.

Not in it — *with* it. Against the eastern lip where the basalt makes that shelf, out of the worst churn but never out of the sound, the low walls make a windbreak for water. There, curled where two walls meet, is a hollow of woven bladder-skin and algae-mat and smooth shell, the size of a person. It is not a house. It is a nest.

He is there now. Or she is. You only see a shoulder, a knee, pale and pruned from permanent immersion, hair slicked down and algae-tinged, eyes half-closed against the strobe. A human old enough that you can't tell age. Naked except for a wrap of that same translucent skin, which is warm. They are not looking at you. They are listening with their bones.

What lives here longest has learned to go deaf early. Their eardrums gave out years ago — burst or scarred into uselessness — and now they feel the basin the way the ribbon does, through the sternum, through the teeth, through the soles of feet that have grown wide and soft and ridged for gripping sharp stone. They move the way the shrimp move: not walking but hooking, from ledge to ledge, never fighting a current head-on but letting it throw them and catching the next edge. When they need to eat they hold a bladder until it pops and sieve the shrimp from the cloud with a tongue that has gone pale. When they need to drink they drink the water that was already everywhere.

They tend the bowl because someone has to mark that the water is still high. It is a tide gauge, a prayer, a habit. Once a day they wade to the altar, tip the vibrating water out, and let the basin refill it. The act is the only quiet thing here, and even it thrums.

If you stay too long you will see them do the other thing: climb. Not down the stair into the deepest part, but *up* — onto the vertical wall of water itself. Where it hangs thickest they will press their back to the basalt teeth and lean out and let the standing wall take their weight. The surface tension there, made monstrous by whatever holds the sky-sea in place, will hold a person for a few seconds. They hang there, suspended in the side of the ocean, arms spread, in that warm amniotic blind light, not swimming, not drowning, just held — like the basalt shard that forgot to fall — while the roar goes straight through them.

Then the wall shivers and lets go, and they hook back to stone.

They will not speak to you. There is no speaking here. But if you wade in and hold still, they will eventually look at you and you will see in their eyes the exact expression of the ribbon-fish when it hangs vertical: not welcome, not unwelcome. Just: *you are also here now, holding on. See how long you hold.*

Whatever lives here lives like that — spare, tenacious, translucent, tuned to pressure instead of sound, making a home not *in* the violence but *between* its beats. Nothing flourishes. Everything endures by being small enough, pale enough, and loose enough in the world's rules to be thrown and not broken.

And when you finally climb back over the ridge and your ears start to ring with the sudden, terrible silence of normal air, you realize you have not seen many lives. You have seen one life trying over and over, in different shapes — mat and tree and shrimp and ribbon and keeper — to solve the same problem: how to keep from being washed off a young mountain that decided, for a little while, to be the top of the sea.
