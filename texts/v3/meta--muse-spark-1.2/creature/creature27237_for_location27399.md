They live dim.

That's the first thing you notice when you actually get down into it, past the lip where the wind cuts off and the light turns late. Nothing up here lives bright. Everything that lives in the Kettle lives dim, warm, and close.

At first it doesn't look like separate things at all. From the rim you see just one texture — a green-black felt thrown over stone. Then your eyes adjust and the texture resolves into layers, and the layers resolve into lives, and the lives resolve into lives on lives, until you understand there is no single inhabitant here. There is a system for catching water that happens to be alive.

Start with what holds it.

The stone itself is covered. The grey-green felt-lichen is not a plant the way you think of a plant — it has no roots, no stem. It is a skin. It grew itself flat against the undercut walls over centuries and now it holds the wall together. In the day it is thin, papery, the color of dust and ash, and you could peel it off in sheets. After midnight, when the bowl exhales and the air weeps, it swells. It goes dark and thick and spongy, doubling its weight with dew before the sun can take it back. If you press your hand to it at dawn, cold water will run down your wrist. The children do this — they go out before speaking, as the rule says, and they press cloths to the lichen and wring them into shallow pans. The lichen doesn't mind. It will be papery again by nine.

Out of that skin grow the anchors.

The rosette succulents. You thought from above they were decoration. Up close each one is as big as a shield, as heavy as a bucket of water — which is exactly what it is. Leaves fatted and overlapping like tiles, coated in that pale, dusty bloom that never washes off. The bloom is wax, and it seals. You can leave a cut leaf in the warm air for a week and it will still be plump. They grow in steps, one rosette above the next, each one catching the runoff of the one above it, each one's dead lower leaves forming a thatch that feeds the roots of the vines that grow on them. When they finally flower — one night a year, most of them — they do it all at once, throwing up a single stalk from the center that pops open into a white, waxy plate, sour-sweet smelling, that lives for one morning and is mobbed before it wilts.

And hanging.

The air-plants are what make the bowl feel like a room someone has hung with curtains. They have no business being so large. Silver beards, grey-green tassels, masses as big as a sleeping child, hanging from every crack and overhang on the upper wall where no soil could ever collect. They never touch the ground. They put out no roots. They drink only air. Each thread is covered in tiny scales that catch dew and channel it inward. During the day they look dead — dry, crisp, dusty. Shake one and dust comes off it. An hour after the condensation begins they turn green and soft and alive, and by dawn they drip steadily onto everything below. This is the real rainfall here. The people build their roofs under the biggest curtains for a reason. And the swifts build their nests inside them.

That is the floor for everything else.

Because once you have that much plant, crammed vertical, layered on itself, growing on itself, you have insects beyond counting. Not as a feature — as a climate.

The air in the lower ten meters has weight because of moths. Small, pale, almost translucent moths, the color of the lichen before dawn, that spend the day pressed flat to the undersides of succulent leaves in such numbers you think the leaf is furred. At dusk when the big white flowers pop, they rise. Not in a swarm. In a weather system. A slow, silent blizzard that fills the whole bowl from wall to wall, each one drinking, each one dusted with pollen, each one in turn being hunted. You breathe them if you don't cover your mouth.

Under them, on the rock and the fleshy leaves, the beetles work — glossy, black, blue-black, big as the first joint of your thumb, with backs like polished stone. They are the cleaners and the builders. They roll everything — fallen flower, dead moth, lizard dropping, crumb of lichen — into perfect spheres and push them endlessly across the thatch, burying them where the fig can find them. Without them the bowl would choke on itself in a season.

And the lizards and the geckos and the things that pretend to be sticks. The turquoise-throated lizards own the sun flecks — every warm rock has one, doing its push-ups, flashing, utterly still until a moth blunders too low, then a tongue — gone. The geckos own the dusk, translucent, wide-eyed, calling to each other with a sound like two stones being tapped, upside down on the house walls and the succulent trunks. And the stick insects — you will not see them until one moves. Six-legged, fourteen-inch things the exact green of the paddle cactus, with spines that match cactus spines, hanging among the spines for days without moving, then stepping, swaying, vanishing again. The children know not to grab for a cactus without looking twice.

Above all of them, the birds stitch it shut. There are no ground birds here. Everything that flies lives on the wall. Swifts with sickle wings that never land on the floor, swallows that nest in clay cups glued to the overhang, finches — five, six kinds, you lose count — small and loud and fast, brown and yellow and a red so saturated it looks artificial against the green. They are in the air at all times in a loose, constant net, cutting the moth cloud, feeding, screaming. At noon the chorus of wings and chirps is so layered it becomes a single pressure on your ears. At night they vanish all at once, as if a switch were thrown, and the crickets take the air and saw it in half until morning.

And then — in the middle of that, under that, woven through that so tightly you can't tell where plant stops and house starts — the people.

There are maybe three hundred of them. You won't see that many at once because a third of them are always curled in shade, moving slowly, speaking softly. They are not tall people. Nobody grows tall on dew. They are wiry, compact, roped with lean muscle from climbing. Their skin is the same range as the stone — rust-red, dust-pale, sand-brown — darkened and set early by the filtered sun, but with the soft, slightly damp pallor underneath that you see when they roll up a sleeve to wring a cloth. Their hair is kept short or wrapped — not for fashion, for water. Long loose hair collects dew and then loses it to the wind; they keep it cut close or bound in tight, plant-fiber wraps. Their eyes are large in the dim, lidded against glare when they have to go to the rim. Their mouths are small, their lips often chapped despite everything. They move like the lizards do: bursts of sure, economical work in the cool hours, long stillness in the heat.

You would not pick them out as separate from the bowl at first because their houses aren't separate. The houses are swallow nests. Low, round, dry-fit sandstone walls tucked into every natural terrace, chinked with pale dust-clay, roofs thatched with the same silver beard that hangs from the rim, but woven tight and dark with age and smoke. From above, a house looks like another rosette succulent. From inside, it smells like warm stone and dried leaves and people. The walls between houses are not walls — they are the rock itself, worn into steps and gutters and basins by a thousand bare feet over a thousand years. The stairways shine. The village has no streets, only that continuous, polished, branching stair.

Everything they own is for holding water.

Outside every doorway are the pans — stone pans, leaf-lined basins, hollowed-out bottle-gourds from the shrub on the floor whose trunk swells like a barrel. They are set out at dusk uncovered, brought in at first light covered with woven lids. The small cisterns are communal, big bellied clay jars sunk into the coolest shade, sealed with waxed leaves. The children — because children wake earliest and are closest to the ground — do the wiping. They go with squares of soft, felted fiber and press the lichen, press the succulent leaves, press the beard-moss, and wring. No speaking until the first jars are full. It isn't a religious rule, not exactly. It's practical: your mouth is wettest before you start talking.

Their clothes are the same fiber as everything else — air-plant beard carded and spun and woven into a loose, pale cloth that breathes and shades. Everyone wears the same cut: long, loose sleeves and trousers to keep the sun off, to keep sweat in. When they must go up onto the plain — to gather the rare hard seed that only sets on the wind-scoured edge, to look out — they go before dawn, wrapped head to foot in a damp water-cloth, faces covered, and they are back by mid-morning or they do not come back. They have no word for river. Their word for *far* also means *dry*.

They eat what the bowl permits. Strips of succulent leaf, pounded thin and dried until they are chewy and sour; beans that grow twined around cactus paddles; the seeds of that one grain that came from somewhere, sometime, and learned to store its own water in a hard, clear kernel; larvae scooped from under the lichen mats, toasted; the occasional lizard, taken only when there are many. They drink primarily dew, but also that bitter, clear sap from the bottle-trunks when a new cistern must be started or a fever must be broken. It tastes like aspirin and green wood and makes your tongue go numb. They ferment a little of it into a thin, sour drink for evenings.

They do not keep fire the way you think of keeping fire. The rule — *no fire larger than a cook-pot* — is absolute. Not because wood is scarce, though it is, but because smoke hangs in the bowl and kills the moths for nights after. So cooking is small, quick, done in covered pits at midday when the moths are sleeping and the wind, if there is any over the rim, can pull the smoke out. Most food is eaten dry or raw. Light at night is a single oil wick — cactus oil — per house, no more.

They are not primitive. They are precise. A child here can tell you, by looking at the bloom on a rosette, how many spoonfuls of water it holds. An old woman can tell you which overhang will drip fastest tonight by the temperature of the stone against her cheek. They plant two for every one they cut, they keep count of moths by ear, they know which finch takes which seed and when to leave it be. Their murals — handprints and ochre animals on the driest walls — are not decoration so much as record: here the geckos nested last year, here the lichen slipped, here the water held for three days after a cold snap.

And at evening, you understand why they do not imagine leaving.

The sun drops behind the plain, and the plain goes violet and then black and the cold comes down like a lid. In the bowl the air goes absolutely still. The stone, warm all day, begins to breathe out. First one, then fifty, then every succulent at once — *pop* — the white plate-flowers opening in the dark with a soft, audible snap, and the scent hits you like warm honey and iron. The moth blizzard rises so thick the air glitters. The birds go silent mid-note. The crickets and geckos start, louder and louder until the oval of stone rings like a struck bell.

And the people come out. All of them. They sit on their roofs, on their steps, on the warm rock, little dark figures in a green, humming room. They do not talk loudly — sound carries too well here — they murmur. They wring their cloths into jars. They watch the moths. They listen to the bowl being what it is: crowded, layered, impossible, thrumming, alive — a single pocket of wet breath held in warm stone — while for a hundred miles in every direction there is nothing at all but wind over ancient, empty rock under an enormous dry sky.
