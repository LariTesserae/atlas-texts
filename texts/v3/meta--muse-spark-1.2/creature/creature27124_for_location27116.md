Nothing lives here at first.

That is the first answer the plateau gives you, and you almost believe it. The light is too white, the air too thin to draw properly, the roar too complete. You stand on a mattress of living sponge that breathes under your boot and you think: this is a place that is only place. Rock, water, plant, steel — but no animal. No one at home.

Then you stay long enough that the roar separates, and the green separates, and you realize you were looking at the wrong scale.

The true resident is the ground itself.

It is not covering the ground; it *is* the ground. The first and most complete inhabitant is a colonial thing, meters thick, thousands of years deep but only a millimeter taller each year. Sphagnum and peat moss and cushion-plant and hair-grass woven so tight you cannot tell where one individual ends and the next begins. It holds so much water it is water that has learned to be solid for a while. It built its own soil out of its own dead, on top of rock that was still hot, geologically speaking. 

If you pull it apart — and you shouldn’t, because it sighs and takes so long to close — you see it is stratified like a city. The top inch is alive and bright, bleached almost yellow by that merciless high sun, photosynthesizing fast while the light lasts. An inch down it is already darker, cooler, saturated, reddish. A foot down it is black, anaerobic, pickled, so cold and wet nothing rots properly. That is where it keeps its past. Pollen from three thousand years ago, perfectly preserved. A dragonfly wing. A rivet that fell off a pump house in 1978 and was swallowed by summer.

That is the bulk of what lives here. Everything else lives *on* or *in* or *between* it, sparely, at intervals, the way the pump houses are placed.

### The ones in the sheet

Crouch down when the sun is most direct, when the water turns to tin and glass.

Look at the rock terraces where the basalt is still bare. The water doesn’t run here, remember — it sheets. A film two or three millimeters thick, stretched wide over the dark, jointed pavement, moving without a channel. Nothing should be able to live in that.

But it does. If you hold your breath against the thunder and look close, the sheet is moving against itself.

There are grazers there, utterly flat. Pale, almost transparent, the size of your palm, the thickness of a leaf. Call them mantle-slugs, though they are not slugs. They hold themselves to the basalt with a thousand microscopic hairs and let the sheet flow over their backs while they scrape algae and young moss protonema off the stone with a radula you can only see as a shadow moving under their skin. They are the color of wet basalt when the sun is off them and the color of bleached light when it is on them. You could step on twenty and never know.

They never lift more than a few millimeters. The wind at this elevation would peel them off. The sun would cook them. So they stay in the moving skin of water, and when a terrace lip comes and the sheet pours over in that white curtain, they simply let go, tumble in the curtain like a leaf, and re-attach on the terrace below. Some of them do this all day, endlessly cycling down the gentle stair of the plateau toward the scarp. They are how the plateau erodes, a little bit — not the water, but the mouths in the water.

In the black mirror pools, a different form waits. The tarns between moss hummocks are sky-traps, perfectly still except when your footfall makes them wobble. In each one lives one, maybe two, translators. A larva, then an adult. The larva is aquatic, feathery-gilled, the red-brown of peat tannin, hanging suspended mid-water, utterly still, filtering the endless rain of organic dust that the wind combs off the cotton grass. It does not swim. It waits for food to come to it, for years.

The adult is what you mistake for a dragonfly. It isn’t. It is larger, slower, its wings not glass but a milky, UV-opaque membrane, because here UV is punishment. It does not hawk or dart. It makes one heavy, low flight from one pool to another pool three hundred meters away — a long journey in this country — and then sits on a cotton-grass head for a whole afternoon, its wings folded like a shutter. Its eyes are small and hooded. Its hearing is gone — useless in the roar. Instead the whole fringe of hairs on its legs reads the vibration of water hitting rock through the peat.

There is one per pool because there is only enough food for one per pool.

### The ones in the mat

Where the moss gives way on the slightly drier steps to those knee-high, sideways thickets of dwarf willow and juniper — so dense you cannot push a hand through — something warm-blooded finally lives.

You will not see it move. If it moves you will think the wind moved the mat.

They are low, long, and pressed. The willow-creepers — not birds, not quite mammals in the way you think of them — about the size of a hare, but with no height to them. Their legs are short and splayed, their bellies dragging in the damp, their fur not fur but a dense, felted, moss-like fiber the exact olive-brown-green of the willow bark, bleached at the tips by the sun. Even their faces are flat, nostrils slit-valved against constant mist, eyes narrowed to dark beads under a brow ridge that shades glare.

They live sideways, inside the thicket rather than on it, tunneling through the interwoven branches in runs that stay dry even when everything else weeps. They eat the willow itself — buds, catkins, the cambium when winter comes — and they eat the peat-moss when they must, and they chew the cotton-grass heads to line their nests. Their metabolism is famously slow. At 8 or 9 degrees, thin air, they cannot afford to be quick. A heartbeat you can count if you hold one, which you never will. They breed every three or four years, one kit, blind for months in a chamber deep in the peat where the temperature never changes.

Their great adaptation is not to cold or wet but to sound.

In a place where you must shout to be heard a meter away, where water and turbine make a single low thunder that lives in your chest, calling is death — it wastes energy and nobody hears you. So they don’t call. They thump. A willow-creeper will press its broad, flat sternum to the living peat and drum, a slow, patient *thud... thud... thud* that travels not through the air but through the waterlogged sponge for half a kilometer. Others feel it through their bellies. *I am here. Keep away. I am here.* The whole plateau is quietly thumping to itself all day under the roar, a conversation you feel in the soles of your boots after you have stood still for a long time and stopped trying to listen with your ears.

Because they are so sparse, a single thumper may own a territory the size of six industrial intervals. You can walk for an hour and be inside one animal’s home without ever seeing it.

### The ones in the steel

And then there are the ones who live because the industry lives.

They are not separate from the first two kinds. They are the same kinds, learning.

A pump house is a hot, humming island in the cold wet. Its concrete footing leaks heat year-round. The riveted steel wall, sunward, might be 20 degrees on a day when the moss is 8. So the moss climbs it on the north side and on the south side, the willow-creepers make a run that leads right up to the warm concrete and stops. In winter they sleep against it. Snow never lodges there.

Inside the corrugated shed where the peat is dried, where the air is black with peat dust and smells intensely of old earth, a smaller, paler version of the mantle-slug has learned to graze not on basalt algae but on the perpetual film of condensation that runs down the inside of the iron roof and carries iron oxide with it. They line the girders in rows, breathing rust.

On the chainlink around the humming substation, where the moss has swallowed two sides and left two sides bare and buzzing, the translators sit in numbers you never see on the open bog — five or six to a fence post. The wires sing in the wind, that harsh Aeolian note that carries for miles, and the vibration puts a fine static into the diamonds of the fence that the translators can read as if it were water falling. They are a little larger here. A little bolder.

And the human.

Because someone has to tend the turbines. There is one, per station. Not many.

You meet them rarely because their intervals are as wide as the creepers’. A keeper, in a flaked-orange parka that was once high-vis and is now the exact color of primer and rust and peat. Ears muffed even in summer with heavy caps — not for cold but for the thunder. Deaf in the particular way people who work under load become deaf, able to hear a bearing going rough inside a generator through the soles of their feet. They move the way the plateau moves: slowly, without wasting height, never stepping on the deep part of the moss if a willow mat will carry them. They check the sluice, the spillway, the narrow-gauge where the black wall of peat weeps into the cut. They do not talk much, even to each other when two territories overlap at shift change at the scarp edge. They tap the rail with a wrench twice to say *all well* and the sound carries through the peat to the next keeper half a kilometer away better than a voice ever would.

They have learned to squint permanently against the white glare, so their faces are creased even when they are young. They have learned to be alone without being lonely, which is the same adaptation the creeper and the translator made long before steel was brought up here. They are not visitors. If you left for twenty years and came back, you would find a different face with the same parka, at the same turbine, watching the same sheet of water slide over the same basalt step, at the same RPM, while the moss closed another inch over the fence.

That is what lives here, layered, the way the place is layered.

Raw rock. Then the living sponge that arrived too early and decided to be soil anyway. Then the flat things in the sheet, the still things in the pools, the pressed things in the willow, all thumping quietly through their shared mattress instead of calling. Then the warm steel islands, and the things that have learned to live on warmth and rust and wire-song. Then the single human per half-kilometer, who has learned to live like them.

None of them are many. All of them are wet, all the time. All of them are a little bleached by that hard, bright, high light, a little slowed by the cold thin air, a little deafened, and a little patient beyond any patience you brought up with you.

They are not finished. Nothing here is finished. The rock hasn’t learned to be a mountain, the bog hasn’t learned to be a soil, the industry hasn’t learned to be a ruin, and the animals haven’t decided yet whether they are wild things or commensals. They are all in the middle of becoming something that will suit this loud, bright, still wet table in the sky — very slowly, a millimeter per year, a flake of basalt at a time, one pool at a time, while the water keeps pouring over every lip, endlessly renewing itself.
