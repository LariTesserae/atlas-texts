Who lives here has learned to live like the building does.

Not *in* it. *As* it.

You won't find them at first. That's not because they hide. It's because they belong too well to be seen, and because you came looking for inhabitants and they stopped thinking of themselves that way a long time ago.

### The Tenders

They are still here. The grandchildren of the builders. Or maybe the builders themselves, because it's hard to tell where one generation ends in something that grows this slowly.

They are small. Not stunted, but edited. Three and a half to four feet at the crown if they stood straight, which they never do. Their bodies have been convinced, the same way the forest was convinced, to become better at holding up empty air.

Long. That's the first thing. Long arms, long hands, longer fingers with an extra knuckle that lets the tip fold back on itself. Long, splayed feet that can grip a branch from above or below, toes as dexterous as fingers. Their chests are broad and shallow, built for the warm, wet, thin air — big lungs that move slowly. Their skin is the color of the underside of a leaf after rain, a pale, bloodless green-grey, thin enough you can see the veins in their wrists like roots. Their hair is not hair anymore but a fine, short moss that grows on their forearms and calves and crowns, that beads with condensation and keeps them from drying out. It greens a little when they are healthy. It greys when they aren't.

And their eyes. They needed new eyes for this light. Huge, amber or deep moss-green, with a slow nictitating membrane that wipes away the perpetual mist. No whites to speak of. In the dim olive dusk they see beautifully. Direct sun would blind them. They have not seen direct sun in four hundred generations and do not miss it.

They are quiet because the Weave taught them quiet. Sound dies here. A shout goes ten meters and is drank by leaf and humid air. So they don't shout. They speak in contact. A tap code on wood that travels through the living lattice faster than through air. A brush of fingertips along the back of a hand, along a mossy shoulder. A low hum, chest-to-chest, more felt than heard. When they do vocalize, it's a soft coo or click that doesn't carry, meant only for the one next to you.

You will hear them before you see them, if you are still enough: the soft creak of a bark cord being pulled taut, the quiet *snick* of a stone flake trimming a shoot, a bare foot finding a familiar gap in the thatch without looking down.

Because their work never ended. It became their life.

There is no profession here. No architect and no farmer and no priest, because all three are the same job. They tend. From the age they can grip, children are given a cord of stripped vine and taught to tie. Not to build — to *mend*. A floor sags where water has pooled? Braid a new cable from living aerial roots, guide it, tie it slack, let it thicken for a year until it takes the load. A leaf-wall grows too thick and blocks the draught that keeps a cloud from stagnating? Thin it, redirect the sap with a careful cut, coax the growth elsewhere. A cistern-leaf overflows? Pleat its edge a little higher while it is still soft.

They do not own sections. They drift. A family might tend a vault the size of a cathedral for a few years until its breathing is even, then move up or down a level where a graft has failed. Their homes are not houses. They are hollows coaxed into being — a burl swollen into a sleeping cup, lined with the down of the blanket moths; a leaf the size of a door that was persuaded to curl over and fuse at the edge, making a rain-proof tent. All of it warm, giving, slightly damp, smelling of sap.

They eat what the Weave offers without being asked to offer it. The starchy pith of pruned branches, roasted on stones heated by compost. The nectar that fills pitcher-leaves big as bathtubs after a rain — they drink it with gourds grown to be gourds. The pale grubs that fatten in the rot pockets, the fruits that are engineered by generations of selection to be handleable, sweet, and to fall only when tugged a certain way. They take, and in taking they prune. They prune, and in pruning they shape.

Their dead are not buried. There is no ground to bury in. A body is laid on an upper lattice where the air is warmest, covered with broad leaves, and the Weave takes it in days. Fungi lace it, roots thread it. Within a month there is a new swelling in the floor where they lay. They do not find this morbid. It is just maintenance.

You expect a culture that built a mountain to be grand. They are not grand. They are patient to a degree that looks like indolence. They can sit for an hour watching a single drip work its way down a vine, waiting to see where it will pool, because that is the most important thing happening. They measure time in leaf-openings and cord-thickenings. A promise made here might be fulfilled by your grandchild. They have myths, but their myths are all about knots that held and knots that slipped.

And they are not alone in tending it.

### The Fellow Keepers

Everything else tends it too, without knowing.

The **blanket moths** — you thought they were just hanging there. They are caretakers. Wingspans wider than you are tall, their wings are not colored *like* thatch; they *are* thatch, grown to mimic it so perfectly that the living floor cannot tell where moth ends and leaf begins. They hang beneath the lattices, breathing once a minute, their feathered feet constantly carding the aerial roots, spreading spores, licking up mold with long, soft tongues. At night — though there is no real night, only a deeper dusk — they drift on warm updrafts between levels, pollinating the door-sized flowers that only open for something that heavy and silent.

The **pale long-fingers**, which you mistook for the builders at first, are their cousins but not them. Smaller monkeys, almost translucent, that never come to the upper side of the floors. They live entirely inverted, walking on the undersides of the world. Their job is tying. Instinctively, obsessively, they take any loose vine tip and braid it into the nearest join. The Tenders learned their knots from watching them. Sometimes a Tender and a pale long-finger will work the same cable from opposite ends, pausing to touch hands, neither acknowledging the other as separate.

The **kites**. In the great empties — those half-kilometer voids where clouds are born — things pulse. They look like jellyfish that lost the sea, or like kites cut from wet paper. Translucent, ribbed, a meter or two across, they never flap. They flex, and air moves through them. They live on spore and mist and the smallest midges, straining the humid air the way whales strain water. They are silent because they have no way to make sound. Light passes through them and makes them briefly amber. Children of the Tenders lie on the lattice and watch them for hours, learning how to be still in empty space.

The **beetles** like polished river stones, black or deep green, that do nothing but roll. They find a bead of condensed water on a leaf, coat it in wax and leaf dust, and roll it carefully to a dry patch of thatch where it will be absorbed slowly instead of falling all at once. Millions of them, all day, moving water uphill a drop at a time. If you sit still long enough one will try to roll the sweat off your ankle.

And the predators, because a place this abundant must have them, but even they have been made polite by the architecture.

The **bark mantis** is a canoe-length of perfect imitation. It doesn't hunt by chasing; it becomes a section of trunk for days until a kite or a moth drifts too close. Then it isn't bark. Its strike is the only sharp sound in the Weave — a crack that the leaves swallow instantly.

The **closing vine** doesn't chase at all. It is just a vine, indistinguishable from a hundred others that lash floors to pillars, except its loop is a little too perfect, its tendrils a little too sensitive to warmth. It waits for the vibration of a foot. It closes slowly, inexorably, not to eat but to hold until the beetles and fungi can come. The Tenders know every one. They mark them with a braid of red bark and step over them. Sometimes they feed them when prey is scarce, so the vine doesn't starve and rot and weaken the join it is part of.

None of these creatures are separate from the building. They are its maintenance crew, its immune system, its slow, distributed mind.

### The One Who Is the Place

And then there is the inhabitant you walked in without noticing, because you thought it was the building.

The Weave itself.

It is not a forest. Forests are many. This is one, or has become one. The trunks that lift the lowest vaults are each wider than a house, but at their crowns they graft. Branch into branch, root into root, leaf edge into leaf edge, until the whole massif shares sap. It is a single organism the size of a mountain range, and it behaves like one.

It breathes. You felt it — the warm updrafts that rise through the lattice floors at noon, the cool sighs that sink at deeper dusk, the clouds forming three levels up from nothing but its exhalation. It sweats. It drinks its own sweat. It holds its voids open the way you hold your lungs open without thinking.

It has learned things no plant should know because small, patient hands taught it for two thousand years. How to make a true arch that bears weight. How to grow a leaf transparent as glass and hold it as a window. How to make a stair that is a single, spiraling frond, each step a thickened vein. It remembers these shapes now in its cambium. Even where the Tenders have not worked for a century, it continues to make them, imperfectly, like a word you keep repeating after the teacher has left.

It does not think. But it *attends*. Redirect water here because here is dry. Thicken there because there is sag. Close that gap because that draught is cold. Over centuries, that attending looks exactly like intention.

If you press your palm to one of the house-wide trunks and hold it a long time, the bark — that soft, lichenous felt — will warm slightly under your hand. The water beading on it will change course to avoid your fingers. You will feel, faintly, the slow hydraulic push of sap moving up, a pulse so slow your own heart beats twenty times for each of its.

The Tenders understand this. They do not worship the Weave. They do not command it. They converse with it the way you converse with an old, very large dog: by touch, by habit, by long association, by knowing that you will die and it will still be there, warm and breathing, holding up nothing but air because that is what it has decided to be.

That is who lives here. Small, moss-haired people with big quiet eyes who never finish tying; moths that pretend to be floors; monkeys that live upside down; kites that swim in air; beetles that roll water; vines that hold; and one vast, hollow, patient plant that learned how to be a cathedral and then forgot how to be anything else.

They all live in the emptiness. The emptiness lives because they hold it.
