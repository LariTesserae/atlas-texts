What lives there does not live there the way things live in other places.

At first you think nothing does. That is the trick of the bowl.

You hear the generators and the drill and the cable sanging and you think *those are the inhabitants*, and you hear the water ticking and the wind shredding itself on the rim and you think *those are the inhabitants*, and you push a hand into moss three hands deep and it pushes back, saturated and cold, and you think — maybe it is empty and just growing over itself. It takes a long time to understand that the green is not a covering. It is the citizenry.

No one *lives* here if by living you mean stays. That is the founding law of the cirque. The rock is too young to be still — basalt and andesite still holding the heat of their breakage, still shedding teeth and hanging slabs that ring when the wind hits them, still slumping its fresh talus apron before lichen has even found a purchase. The water does not stay — it arrives as shredded cloud, hammers vertically for ninety seconds, beads on every leaf tip and sweats out of the stone and sits in a cupped rock for an hour before the peat drinks it or the air takes it back. The light does not stay — a bruised diffusion with no source that lets you see your hands and the frond in front of your face and makes everything beyond ten meters a darker mass. Even the industry that is the loudest thing in the bowl does not live here. It camps.

And yet it is crowded. Overcrowded. Frantically, chokingly alive.

The first nation is the mat itself. Not a meadow, not ground cover — a single continuous organism of negotiation, knee to chest high, so dense your boot cannot fall without crushing something, so dense it has eliminated the very niche for anything else. It lives without being eaten and so it has become profligate, wasteful, ecstatic.

The mosses are its foundation and its elders, if anything so fast can be called elder. Cushion mosses in saturated domes the size of small animals, bright even in the dark, springy as lung tissue. They hold ten times their weight in water and then hold more. They are reservoirs with leaves. Under them the peat lives — black, sour, always cold, building itself grain by grain out of their dead while it is still alive. You can hear it sigh when it takes water after a cloudburst. You can feel heat if you press your palm to it, not from sun but from rot.

Out of that wet sponge the ferns push, but not as you know ferns. These are alpine ferns that have learned to be succulents and armor at once — leathery, low, fronds overlapped like shingles on a roof, each one waxy to shed the hammer-rain and beaded permanently with condensation. They don't unfurl in spring. They unfurl while you watch. A pale fiddlehead shoulders up through the mat, driven by a turgor pressure you can almost hear creak, and hardens overnight into something that will take a boot to dent. The saxifrage and the azalea and the nameless waxy mats with leaves like wet coins do the same — they elbow, they crowd, they slump downhill in a slow green avalanche when overwatered and immediately throw new shoots into the wet black earth they expose, steaming in the generator heat. It is turbulent growth, hyperactive in the 6-degree damp, as if the bowl's short season has taught every plant to sprint for its whole life. Pollen hangs in the still air after and never gets taken. Spores drift and settle on stone that sweats. Nothing chews them. No insect ticks in the moss. No worm turns. There is no animal sound to give it scale, so the vegetative crackle — frond against frond, moss swelling, peat settling — becomes loud as a crowd.

The second nation is the stone and the water that lives on it. The rock breathes. The fresh scar at the back wall, thirty meters of glassy andesite so clean it looks wet when it is dry, weeps constantly. Not a seep, not a spring — a sweat. The basalt teeth and the undercut steps bead and drip onto lichen that has learned to be both crust and hair and leaf at once, draping the walls in gray-green beards that drink from the air. The water itself is a resident more than a resource. It lives as a condition, moving in a tight, frantic circuit: shredded cloud caught on the rim, hammer rain, cupped pools in stone, axils of leaves, black peat pockets that the pumps hammer at with their corrugated hoses, evaporation off hot oil and diesel exhaust, condensation again on cold rock, ticking off leaf tips. At any moment half of it is airborne and half is held. It never rests long enough to become a lake.

The third nation is the one that does not belong and knows it — the machines. They are the only things in the bowl that are louder than the plants, and they live the way large, stupid migratory animals live. Two diesel generators on skids, already half-swallowed by moss, shuddering and hunting in the thin air, coughing heat and hot-oil stink into cold that wants to kill them. A tracked drill rig canted on the talus, mast folded like a sleeping neck, idling loud just to keep warm, its bit still dripping rock flour. A cableway winch on the eastern rim that screams every time its brake catches, hauling a bucket of crushed rock that drips all the way across the void. Low rectangular pumps that find a pocket, suck it dry, rattle sucking air, find it again. None of it is anchored. Everything is on pallets, on temporary mats, on steel cable that sings under tension and goes slack when the wind drops. They are not buildings. They are a herd passing through, provisional, already being taken. The moss is growing over their skids. The damp is shorting their switchbox where it spits and hums. The vibration of the drill brings down chips from the new scar. They roar and the bowl, cathedral-small and hamlet-small at once, throws their roar back flat and hard off young rock. At night — which is not night but the lid-cloud darkness — their heat makes little halos of steam where the ground exhales.

And the fourth — the most ghostly — are the humans, if they can be counted. You never quite see them. You hear a throttle blip when no one is at the rig. You see a boot print in the crushed rock already softening at the edges as the moss pushes back. They are not inhabitants. They are commuters to an altitude that will not let them stay. They come damp, stay cool, keep their light low, service a machine that is sucking a peat pocket dry, spool a cable, drill a hole that the rock will take back in a season, and leave before the cloud drops again. Their heat is taken off their skin before they feel warm. Their cairns slide away in a day. If they tried to sleep here the plants would close over them.

So this is what lives in the cirque at four thousand meters where air has weight and thinness at the same time:

A peat that builds itself alive and dead at once.
A moss that is a reservoir that pretends to be a plant.
A fern-army in shingle-plate, growing so fast it is audible.
A lichen beard drinking sweat off young, ringing stone.
A water cycle that never becomes a body.
A herd of diesel animals on skids, migratory and half-swallowed, that cough heat to stay alive and are already being digested by green.
And the brief, damp humans who tend them, who do not live here, who cannot.

Nothing has tenure. Everything has tenure. The mat will close your footprints in a day. The rock will close the drill hole in a season. The cloud will close the sky in ninety seconds. What lives here lives by not holding still, by growing frantically in the cold, by sweating, by shuddering, by passing through.
