Who lives here has learned to live on air.

At first you think nothing does. That's the trick of the plateau. From standing height it looks empty - rust, ash, dark basalt, a few starved shrubs spaced like strangers. The eye slides right over it. You have to crouch. You have to let the heat go still around you and wait for the place to decide you're not a predator, and then it unpacks itself.

What lives here lives *flat* and *low* and *inside* the rock, because there is nowhere else to live.

### The Holdfasts

Lowest in the hierarchy, and most important, are the ones that never move.

Black lichen coats every windward face in a crust so thin and tight you think it's discoloration in the stone until the morning fog comes and you see it swell, softening, turning almost green-black as it drinks. It doesn't have roots. It drinks straight out of the air, and when it dries it goes dormant again by nine a.m., hard as varnish. Without it the dust would never stay put. The dust collects on its back, the lichen holds it, and in that held spoonful of grit a seed can lodge.

The shrubs do that. They are not shrubs the way lowland shrubs are - they are fists of wire and wax. Tiny leaves the size of lentils, rolled and varnished against evaporation, gray-green and bitter. Their roots go sideways for yards through cracks, not down, chasing the line where condensation runs. They grow inches per decade. Nothing eats them if it can help it, but everything lives under them. Their shade at noon, though it's only a hand's breadth, is fifteen degrees cooler than the open basalt.

Every few hundred yards, a flat-topped tree - acacia in shape if not in species - has managed to get its taproot down to where a sandstone pothole holds water year-round. It is stunted, wind-pruned into a table, its bark thick and corky and pale as ash. It is an island. In its branches the sickle-winged swifts nest, in its roots the beetles shelter, in its coin of moving shade the lizards stack themselves at midday like coins themselves.

They are not many. They don't need to be many. Up here, one tree is a town.

### The Stone-Eaters and Water-Carriers

All the rest of the animal life is built around one fact: the water only exists for two hours a day, and then only as a film.

The beetles own the morning. They are big, thumb-sized, matte black with burnished elytra that bead water as perfectly as the harvester mesh. Before dawn they climb to the highest point near their burrow - a basalt knob, a harvester strut, the rim of a pothole - and face into the faint wind that drags fog across the basins. They do a headstand, abdomen up, and let condensation run down the grooves in their backs into their mouths. When they've drunk, they fill their legs.

They move in columns, hundreds strong, in complete silence on tiptoed legs so their bodies don't touch the hot stone. Each carries a white pellet of salt or gypsum scraped from the rim of a dried pothole. They carry it for miles to line their nests deep in the sandstone, where the salt will pull a little more moisture out of the air inside the tunnel. You can watch a column for an hour and it will only have advanced twenty feet, but it never stops. The trails they wear into the basalt are permanent — shallow, braided grooves polished by a million feet.

The lizards are the opposite. They are heat-mirrors. At seven a.m., just as the fog burns off, they emerge from every crack - flat, pale, almost translucent, with broad frilled toes - and they cover the basalt shoulder to shoulder, hundreds per square yard, all facing east. They don't move. They just breathe, slow and together, letting the sun bring their blood up to working temperature. At the exact moment the stone gets too hot to touch, they vanish. Not gradually — all at once, as if a switch was thrown, they pour back into the holes. If you blink you miss it and the rock looks empty again.

In the water itself, life is even more compressed. Every pothole, even one the size of a bathtub, is an entire world that may only last a week. In it, shrimp-like branchiopods - dun, transparent, with a dozen frantic legs - hatch from eggs that have been dry for years, filter the mineral water, breed, and lay new drought-proof eggs in a matter of days. With them are the amphibians, if you can call them that: squat, mottled things with feathery external gills, the color of wet sand, that walk on four stubby limbs. They never metamorphose fully. When the pool shrinks they burrow straight down into the mud at the bottom, curl up, cocoon themselves in shed skin, and wait. You could break open a dry pothole and find a dozen of them inside, dry as stones, waiting for a fog that might be months away. Clouds of non-biting midges rise off the larger pools at dusk, so thick they look like smoke, then settle again. Their larvae make red ribbons on the bottom.

Above them, the rock face is honeycombed.

The sandstone is soft. Everything that can dig has dug. From a distance it looks like weathering. Up close you see it's architecture — thousands of entrances per acre, from fist-sized to dinner-plate, each with a smooth, slightly enlarged lip where generations of wings and feet have polished it. Inside are the wasps, the bees, the swifts.

The wasps build papery cities under every overhang, the paper the exact rust-red of the stone, invisible until the light changes. They are small, pale, and almost silent — the thin air steals their buzz. The swifts — or swallows, you can't tell until they're flying — are the same color as shadow. They live in the holes by the hundreds, pressed together, and they leave together. Without a call, a whole cliff face will exhale birds at once, a silent puff of sickle wings that wheels over the basins catching midges, then is sucked back into the rock as if it never happened. They drink on the wing, skimming the potholes.

### The Waiters

Where there is that much prey, patiently moving and timed to the hour, there are waiters.

The long-legged canid moves only in fog. You will almost never see it clearly. Tall as a coyote but lighter, leggy as if built for wading, with ears huge and veined like leaves to dump heat, coat the exact color of lichen-spotted basalt. It doesn't run. It walks the rims of the basins with its nose low, in the white milk of dawn fog, and listens for beetles. It can hear a lizard breathing inside rock.

The kite lives off the edge. All day it hangs motionless on the updraft where the plateau falls away into the hazed lowlands, wings locked, tail fanned, not flapping once. It is the only thing here that casts a moving shadow. When a midge cloud rises or a lizard column shifts, it folds and drops two thousand feet like a thrown knife, flares at the last second, takes, and is back up on the updraft before the dust settles.

And at every pothole lip, coiled in a crack where salt has made a white mustache, is the snake. It is lichen-colored, flat-headed, thick as a wrist, and it does not move for days. It just waits where the amphibians must come to breathe. You could put your hand six inches from it and not see it until it blinked.

All of them are quiet. The thin air doesn't carry sound, and sound wastes water. Even the predators kill without noise.

### The People

And then there are the few humans, because even here, where there should be no humans, there are a few.

They are not many — a crew to tend the harvesters, maybe a dozen, maybe thirty across the whole table. You don't see them at midday because they keep the same hours as the lizards. They work from four to eight in the morning, and again from six to nine at night. At noon they are inside the pump houses, in the shade, sleeping or mending mesh.

They have adapted the same way everything else has: slowly, and by becoming part of the rock. Their skin is dark and dry, their movements economical in the thin, hot air where every breath is a conscious pull. They wear loose, dust-colored clothes that cover everything, bleached almost white by sun. They speak quietly if they speak at all — shouting is pointless up here. They know each pothole by name, the way you would know neighbors, and they take only part of what the harvesters condense. The harvesters themselves — those low, broad towers of steel and black mesh that shiver with fans all night — are built to leave an overflow. The pipes tick as they expand, the tanks ring and spill, and the spill runs down a deliberate groove in the basalt to the next natural basin, where the beetles are already waiting.

Their single narrow-gauge rail line is laid straight on the rock with no bed, rust-colored, carrying flatcars of crushed basalt dust to the smelter smudge on the horizon. Birds nest in its rails. Beetles march over it without breaking column. Laundry snaps behind a shed, a doorway stands open with a broom leaning in it, but you can stand and watch for an hour and see no one move — until the light slants, the stone pings as it begins to cool, the first beads of evening dew form on the black mesh and on the backs of the waiting beetles, and a figure in dust-colors steps out onto a catwalk with a wrench, without hurry, to bleed a valve before the night's condensation run.

They belong here now. Like the lichen, like the wasps, like the branchiopods curled in dry mud, they have come to an agreement with the stone. They take the thin water out of the thin air, and they do it quietly, and the plateau, which is crowded beyond belief and silent beyond reason, allows it.
