You think at first that no one does.

That is the trick of the way it was placed. The town is so sparse and the air is so wet and quiet that for a long hour on the rim you can mistake it for empty. Built but not inhabited. A model left in the rain.

Then you see movement and you have to retune your eyes to the scale of people here.

They are not many. That's the first true thing about them.

If the pocket could hold a few thousand without crowding, it holds maybe sixty or seventy. Eighty on a generous day. You will never see them together. They do not gather. The town was laid out to keep them apart — wide bare stone between every structure, walkways that only firm under one set of feet at a time — and they honor that spacing without having to think about it. When two of them pass each other on a stabilized path between pools, they give each other the distance of a building. A nod, sometimes, or nothing at all. It isn't coldness. It's consonance with the place. Noise is an intrusion. Density is an intrusion.

You see one cross from a low, broad dwelling to what must be a workshop on a mid-terraced ledge: hooded, unhurried, shoulders slightly forward against the damp that never quite becomes rain. Then they are gone behind a curtain of seep-water that parts without touching them, and for a long time you see no one else. The town keeps ticking to itself.

When you do get close enough to see them clearly, you understand they have not just adapted to the pocket. They have let it shape them.

They are human — still human — but thinned the way the air is thinned. Not thin as in frail. Thin as in pared.

Tall enough, but narrow through the chest and limbs, as if the body has learned not to waste heat or breath. Their skin is pale in a way that has nothing to do with sun; it's the pallor of people who live under a column of straight-down light and the pearlescent glow of their own walls, who steam gently all day. Cheeks and noses touched always with a faint red from cold, hands a little chapped despite whatever field keeps the frost from their windows. Eyes set deeper than yours, rimmed a little darker, used to looking through mist.

Their hair, when a hood slips back, is mostly dark and cut short or pulled severely back — nothing that would drip and stay wet against the neck. Some have left it to go grey early. No one here seems to be performing youth. You will see old people moving as steadily as the young, and you will see very few children. If there are children at all, they are kept indoors or kept close; the one you might glimpse is quiet beyond its years, holding an adult's hand and watching water bead and race down basalt as if it were television.

Their clothes tell you more about the tech than the buildings do. The cloth is the same family as the walls — a matte, mineral weave that takes the color of wet stone until they stand still and disappear against a seep-dark wall, then flashes that faint rainbow oil-slick when they move. Water doesn't soak it. Beads form and slide off the shoulders and hoods without a stain. It makes no rustle. They don't wear boots that track mud. The soles are soft and wide and leave no print in the black grit, even when the path behind them is still firm for half a second before it loosens again.

They breathe like everyone does up here — a little unfinished, a little deliberate — but they have stopped fighting it. You hear it when you finally stand near one: an inhale that is longer than it needs to be, a pause, an exhale that plumes and holds. They speak rarely, and when they do, they speak low and with few words, because sound behaves strangely in the pocket. It doesn't carry, it gets swallowed by the wet air and the field. Shouting would be absurd. Even a normal voice feels loud. So they have learned a quieter lexicon: a hand lifted, palm out — *go on, I'm fine* — two fingers touched to a pool's rim — *this one's warm today* — a tilt of the head that means *come look* without saying it.

That quiet isn't shyness. It's practice.

These are people who listen for a living.

You realize slowly what their day actually is. There is no commuting, no market, no bustle to observe. Their work is tending. Not farming — nothing grows here except those hair-fine white filaments in the warmer pools, branching like frost under water. No, they tend water and stone.

One kneels for twenty minutes beside a shallow, stone-lipped pool on a ledge, not moving, just watching the paper-ice skin form and crack and re-form, occasionally reaching out with a thin tool that looks like brushed glass to lift a sample of the filament without breaking it. Another stands at a wall where the basalt is weeping in a black thread, their palm held an inch from the rock, feeling the pressure and temperature gradient that tells them where the geothermal seam is shifting today. Another walks the entire circumference of the bowl every morning, not as exercise but as inspection: touching nothing, letting the town's field read their passage, noticing where a new flake of frost-shattered granite has fallen overnight, where a pool has spilled a new curtain to the next ledge, where rime has bloomed thicker than yesterday.

They are caretakers of a place that is still being born.

That's the second true thing about them, and the one that takes longest to settle. No one sent them here to extract anything, to man a station. No flag on the rim. They came — or stayed, or were chosen — because the pocket is young. A few thousand years young, basalt still sharp and glassy, still un-lichened, still sweating water that shouldn't be liquid at this cold. In most places on earth you live on old stone and you borrow it. Here you live while the stone is still deciding what it is. They record it. They regulate it, gently, with those invisible fields that let water divert around a roof without touching it. They keep the pools from freezing solid or flooding. They keep the air in its ten-degree warmer envelope without fire. They keep the town breathing *with* the seep, not against it.

If you ask one what they do — and you would have to be very close and ask very quietly — they might not say "scientist" or "monk" though both words would almost fit. They might say "we're here to not get in the way." Or "we keep the distances right." Or they might just smile with chapped lips and look back at the water.

They don't seem lonely. Solitude here isn't loneliness; it's a kind of cohabitation with the system itself. The town ticks, the water finds the sharpest path down, the air convects its cold slow cycle, and they fall into that rhythm until their own metabolism seems to match it: slow, spare, continuous. They sleep when the hard shaft of sun leaves the floor and the buildings give their soft light back. They wake when condensation starts to run again down the walls in the early afternoon. They eat sparingly — you never see smoke, never smell cooking, but in the warm light of the windows you catch glimpses of plain bowls, steam that dissipates faster than breath — and they waste nothing, because nothing here was ever abundant enough to waste.

They age differently, too. Or you think they do. Time moves in the pocket the way water does: not fast, not slow, but always. You see a woman who could be forty or could be seventy, her face wind-smoothed, her eyes unhurried, and you understand that living for years under clean high light, breathing thin air, speaking rarely, moving always with unhurried purpose, will carve a person the same way seep carves basalt. Not eroding. Refining.

If there is a belief among them, it isn't written anywhere. No shrine, no sign. You feel it anyway in the way they leave those wide stretches of bare, wet stone between every building, in the way they never try to fill the bowl. They know how intimate this place is — how you can hold the whole of it in your field of vision — and they have made a pact, tacit or explicit, to keep it that way. To remain sparse. Like the filaments in the warm pools that grow in scattered patches and never connect.

They live here the way those filaments do: fed by mineral seep, limited by cold, exactly as much as the pocket will allow and no more.

And when a high chough cuts across the open top, a black flick against the sky that does not descend, they look up for a second, tracking it, then look back down at the pool, at the wall, at the next small curtain of water finding its way to the next ledge. They don't call out. They don't wave.

The pocket contains them, and they contain it, completely and without crowding, a little world tucked into the back of a larger, much colder one, turning over quietly, water to air to ice to pool to mist, with people moving through it like breath through thin air — visible for a moment, then gone, then visible again.
