Who lives there now is not who it was built for.

Not at first, anyway. At first you think nothing lives there, because nothing *moves* the way a town should move. No traffic. No voices stacked on voices. No green. You stand at an intersection where four lanes go straight to haze in every direction and the heat makes the air over the asphalt swim, and you think - abandoned. And you are wrong, but you have to stay still long enough to be corrected.

### The Holdouts

There are still people, but they don't live there the way people live in a town. They *tend* it, the way you tend a pump or a levee that will fail if you look away.

They are few. A pump-house man on rotation who sleeps in a cinderblock room with a window unit that drips continuously onto the sill, and who wakes every few hours to check the gauges even in his sleep. A crew that keeps the canals from choking - two men in a flat-bottomed aluminum boat with a weed that isn't a weed, a brown mat of hyacinth they pull with rakes, although the description says no plants, so call it what it is: algae and trash matted together thick enough to walk on if you were light enough. The men are not light enough. They are heavy, slow, sun-darkened, salted around the collar with sweat that never dries.

A night watchman who lives on the second floor of what used to be an office for a machine shop, because the first floor floods ankle-deep every heavy rain and never quite drains. He has put everything on pallets and cinderblocks - bed, refrigerator, hot plate. He keeps the door open for air and a heron has decided the doorframe is a good perch, so they share it.

There are others scattered - families in the few houses that were built on piles high enough, who have replaced their yards with poured concrete and corrugated awnings and who move through their day barefoot because shoes never stay dry. Children who know how to read the water in the gutters - clear means rain, tea-colored means the canal is backing up, oily rainbow means don't wade. Old women who keep pigeons on rooftops not because they like pigeons but because pigeons are reliable and can be eaten.

None of them are busy in the old sense. The work that remains is maintenance against entropy: pumping water that wants to be everywhere back into the channels where it can be sent slowly to the sea. Scraping rust that will return tomorrow. Bailing the pan under the house. Waiting out the midday heat, because in that heat no honest work can be done. They are indoor animals at noon, crepuscular, like everything else. They speak quietly because sound carries too far over flat water and flat roofs. They do not try to grow anything. They tried, and it died - salt, oil, heat. Now they make shade instead of waiting for it, with blue tarps and rusted sheet metal that ticks as it expands.

They don't own the place anymore. They *inhabit* it, on its terms.

### The Citizens

The real citizens are the ones who never left and never needed to be invited. You don't see them until you stop looking for people.

First the water reveals itself. Every canal, every gutter that never dries, every lot that has become a pan of warm tea-colored water six inches deep over silt - it is *solid* with life you mistook for stillness. Mosquitofish and mollies and juvenile killifish, thousands per square yard, riffling the surface in a nervous, collective skin. Glassy shrimp flicking backward. In the deeper canals, mullet roll with a soft splash. And over them, the crabs.

The blue crabs own the pavement. They are not supposed to be there - crabs belong to the sea, to the marsh - but the salinity is right, the water is warm and everywhere, and the concrete is just another kind of rock reef. They come up out of the canals at will, dinner-plate wide, silt crusted, barnacled at the joints, moving sideways across parking lots with a patient *click-click* of legs on aggregate. They commute between puddles. They will eat anything. They eat each other without hurry. You will stand and watch one cross the road while a dog watches it, decides it is not worth the pinch, and lets it pass.

Above them, the birds have taken the high ground that industry left.

The gulls and terns and laughing gulls and herons and egrets and night-herons - you thought the white speckling on the sawtooth roofs and pipe racks was paint failure. It is birds. Hundreds of them per block, utterly still, necks tucked, eyes like glass beads, watching the water below. They have learned the grid. Each piling, each lattice tower, each rusted water tower cross-beam is a rookery, a blind, a perch. They do not cry much. In this heat and haze sound is expensive. A great blue heron will stand on a pipe joint two meters above a gutter for an hour without moving, and then spear down so fast you only see the aftermath - a fish writhing, a gulp. On the ground, the chicks huddle in nests made of plastic zip ties and rusted wire. A gull will take another gull's chick in full view of its parent and there will be no screaming. Just a brief scuffle and then stillness again. The abundance is so great it has become quiet.

Under that, the rats. Not vermin - citizens with a job. They run the baseboards of the world in lines, nose to tail along the foot of every wall where wall meets standing water, fat and sleek and unafraid because there are too many to be afraid. They eat the fish that strand when a puddle dries, the crab eggs, the pigeon's fallen young, the trash. The feral dogs trot after them but not really hunting - the dogs are lean, ribbed, tongues out, moving down the centerline of roads in loose, silent packs of three or four. They know where the pump-house man leaves water. They know not to bark; barking just tells the herons you are coming.

And on the vertical surfaces - if you put your hand on a shaded concrete wall and leave it, you will feel movement before you see it. Cockroaches the length of your thumb, pale cave isopods, black beetles with iridescent backs, all covering the walls in a living stucco that only stirs when you breathe on it. They graze on the microbial film, on the pale mineral tartar that blooms around every pipe leak, on the rust itself. They are food for the geckos - no, not geckos here, too hot - for the anoles and the skinks and the rat snakes that lie in the culverts like warm cables, thick as your wrist, kinked together, bellies bulged with the rats they ate that morning.

The air, even in that flat light, is thick. Dragonflies the size of small birds - twelve centimeters across, amber wings - hawk and hover without sound. Clouds of midges and big, slow flies rise off every pan of water when you step near it. And through them, swallows and chimney swifts cut low over the rooftops, mouths open, harvesting.

### The Reef Itself

And at the very bottom, where you might say nothing lives because nothing is green, the most inhabitants of all: the film. The dark bacterial skin on standing water that breaks like oil when a fish touches it and heals behind him. The rust-colored slick where water weeps from the levee - iron bacteria, *Leptothrix*, making a living out of dissolved metal, turning rust into a body. The pale tartar on pipes where mineral precipitates out of the steaming air.

This is the base of the food chain here. Not plants. Detritus. The town is the plant. Concrete dust, rust scale, oil sheen, human trash, dead fish, bird guano falling like snow from the pipe racks - it all washes into the warm brown water and is eaten by bacteria and shrimp and isopods and then by everything above them. It is a coral reef made of rebar and cinderblock, a tide flat made of parking lots. It doesn't need green. It needs heat, and water that never leaves, and structure to hide in, and time.

That is what lives there now: a reef town.

It is patient. It does not hurry. At midday everything stops - birds tuck heads, crabs settle into puddles, dogs lie in the thin shade under a trailer and pant, people sit still and sweat - because heat is a law and you obey it. In the late afternoon, when the rain comes hissing down on hot metal and fills every gutter at once, everything moves again. Fish jump. Crabs march. The pump house chugs louder to keep up. The herons drop one after another into the newly stirred water.

If you stay long enough you stop being a visitor who sees emptiness and become an animal who sees fullness, just at a different tempo. You learn to watch the pipe-rack the way the heron does, to hear the ticking drip from a joint as a clock. A rat passes your foot and does not hurry. A crab crosses the street in front of you, unhurried, armored, at home. Somewhere down the flat grid, rain is coming, visible as a gray curtain for miles before it reaches you, and the whole low, wet, rusted plain inhales.
