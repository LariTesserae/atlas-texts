It isn't empty.

That's the first mistake you make, looking down into it - thinking because it's new, because it's raw and unscarred and too bright, that nothing could have had time to live there yet.

Something lives there *because* it's new.

Not in spite of it.

---

Give your eyes a minute to stop fighting the light. At first you will count what you were told to count: the isopods, the light-swimmers, the pale amphibians on the floor, the stone-bird tucked into its blister. You will think *oh, fauna, spare but present* and you will try to file them away as curiosities.

Don't.

Look longer. Watch how they use the space.

None of them overlap. That's the first true tell.

In any normal pocket of abundance, even a desert spring, things crowd the water. They jostle. Here, each glassy isopod has its own condensation track and will not leave it. That dragonfly-thing that swims without wings - it patrols a single column of pooled light from one vesicle to one damp hollow and back again, a slow figure-eight that never intersects another's. The long-legged amphibians on the silt - you thought there were six or seven? Look again. There are eleven. But they have spaced themselves so perfectly, so politely far apart, that your brain refused to count them all at once. Like they have agreed to allot each other solitude.

Whoever lives here lives alone, together. That is the ethic of the place.

And then you notice who taught them that.

You won't see it at first. You will see its work. The ring of knee-high stones at the base of that paper-bark tree - basalt, same as the wall, but one side licked smooth. Not worn by wind. Licked. Tongued, patiently, over years. The stones are set not as a wall or a trap, but as a garden border - holding damp. Inside that perfect circle the silt never dries, even when the rest of the floor has gone pale. Something tends it. Something keeps it wet by tending it.

The dome on the eastern bulge - that beehive of dead, bleached stems, woven so tight you can't see the weave until the light hits it sideways. The doorway is a perfect circle, and it faces not the sky, but the place where the light pools thickest on the floor, as if to watch it. No footprints lead to it. No drag marks. Whoever built it climbs the wall itself, or swims the light the way the wingless things do.

And the handprint.

You keep coming back to the handprint on the north wall, pressed into stone that must have still been soft as wax. Fingers too long by one joint. Palm holding a bead of water that never falls, never evaporates. You put your own hand near it without touching and you feel the faint, residual warmth coming off the rock there - as if the stone is still curing around that touch.

Whoever made that is still here. Not gone. Not extinct. Just... between appearances.

Let it resolve slowly. Don't force it into a shape.

If you sit on the floor - and you will, because the air makes your legs feel heavy and the light makes you want to be low - if you sit long enough to let your breathing go quiet the way the place wants it to, it will come to the edge of being seen.

It is pale the way the amphibians are pale, but not translucent. Translucent is for things that let light pass through. This lets light *rest* on it. Its skin is the color of the dry silt when it's about to get damp - a gray that is almost white, with a faint pearlescence, like the inside of a vesicle. It is thin, and tall when it unfolds, but it rarely unfolds. You will mostly see it folded.

It favors the walls, not the floor. It moves with the same slowness as the amphibians, so slow you will swear it hasn't moved at all until you blink and its position has changed by a foot. It doesn't walk so much as it redistributes. A long limb finds a vesicle, grips its rim with fingers that have that extra joint, and then the rest of it flows after, jointless in the way octopuses are jointless, though it has bones - you can see them faintly when the pooled light catches its forearm.

Its head, if you can call it a head, is mostly mouth and mostly eye. Not many eyes - two, large, lidless, but they are not for seeing what you see. They are light-pitted. They drink. When it stops by a vesicle where light has collected and glows, it will lower its face and drink without opening its mouth, the light thinning visibly, like cream stirred into water, until the vesicle is dim and its throat gives off a very soft, brief luminescence that fades down its long neck. It sighs afterward. Without sound.

It is not one. But there are never many. Maybe three in this pit. Maybe four, counting the one that is currently the shadow near the dome. They do not gather. They do not call. If two approach the same condensation track, one will pause and wait with infinite patience until the other has finished, even if it takes an hour, and then continue. They pass each other on the wall with a careful, silent giving of space, as if the air around each one is private.

They are the tenders.

They are what lives here the way beavers live in a dam - not just inhabiting but *maintaining* the conditions. You watch one reach the ring of stones and it does something that makes your chest feel strange: it leans down and licks the smooth side of a stone, slowly, once, and where its tongue - broad, dark, the color of the wet silt - touches, condensation beads instantly and begins to run. It does this to each stone, one by one, around the circle. It is watering its garden. Or seasoning it. Or praying.

Another tends the light. It will find a place where shadow has fallen incorrectly - pale-edged and hesitant - and it will cup its too-long hands and hold them there until the light thickens between its palms like honey. Then it will carry it, carefully, without spilling, to a dark vesicle and pour it in. The vesicle glows a little brighter. The floor below gets a little less bright.

They built the dome, but not to live in. You realize this when one of them finally, after an hour of watching, flows up the eastern bulge and disappears *into* the dome without entering the doorway. It simply thins and goes through the weave, and inside, the darkness that was too dark becomes occupied - not with a body, but with a deeper cool. It is an incubator. Or a pantry. Or a place to put light that has gone stale and needs to be kept in the dark to ripen. You will never know which. When it leaves, it is fractionally brighter.

They are not shy. They are not hiding from you. Hiding implies they think you are a predator. They don't. They look at you the way the amphibians did - all at once, without moving their heads - with those light-pitted eyes, and you understand they have already allotted you your solitude. A vast allotment of empty air and empty stone around your boots where the light has pooled a little brighter, just for you, because you are now a feature of the pocket.

If you stay still, one might come to the handprint on the north wall and place its own hand over it. Perfect fit. It will rest there and the bead of water in the palm will finally, after years, roll over onto its skin and be absorbed. Then it will press a little harder, deepening the print by a millimeter invisible to you, warming the stone anew with its body heat. Keeping the rock from deciding what shape it wants to be just yet. Keeping it new.

That's what truly lives here: not a species but a stewardship. Something that moved in the moment this bubble popped and the earth failed to close, not to colonize it but to keep it unfinished. To keep the stone soft enough to remember a touch, the light heavy enough to pour, the water sourceless, the distances negotiable.

They eat light and drink stone-sweat and smooth rocks with their tongues and weave houses for darkness and they do it all alone, together, in perfect, quiet abundance.

And if you listen with the part of you that has gone quiet to match the air, you will hear - not a sound, but the lack of one - the place breathing through them. A slow inhale that never quite becomes an exhale.

They will let you stay until the mist exhales again. Then, politely but firmly, the wall will feel a little farther from the floor, the circle of sky a little closer, and you will feel the strong, wordless suggestion that your allotment of solitude has expired, and it is time to climb out, over the soft lip, back onto the wind-scoured basalt where things are allowed to be finished.

When you look back, the flat plain will be empty again. But somewhere below that thin lichen, twelve meters down in light that pools like water, a pale, long-fingered thing is licking a stone smooth again, very slowly, just for itself, and just for the stone.
