Nothing lives here at first.

That's what the place tells you, and for a long time you believe it. It's too loud to live in. Too cold to stay in. Too empty between one green thing and the next. You stand on that scoured floor with water hammering the drum of the walls until your jaw aches, and you think: nothing could hold here. Nothing would want to.

Then you stay long enough that your ears give up trying to filter the fall, and you start to see what holding really looks like in a pocket like this.

It isn't abundance. It isn't a chorus. It's one thing, really good at waiting.

If you had to give it a shape early you'd get it wrong. You'd say frog, or fish, or troll hunched under the lip of the fall. You'd picture something with shoulders. But the thing that lives here doesn't have shoulders. It doesn't need them. Shoulders are for carrying and for bracing against weather. Here the weather has already happened. The cold has already pooled and decided to stay.

No. What lives here is low and flat and spread thin, the way the water is spread thin. Then, once you have seen it, it becomes more itself.

Start with the salamander, because it's honest.

Under the lip of the black pool at the foot of the fall, under a stone the size of a loaf of bread, there is an amber smear that isn't amber. Lift the stone — you won't, but if you did — and it doesn't dart. Nothing darts here; darting wastes heat. It lifts its head a fraction, gills fanning out from its neck like two small, blood-red ferns, each filament waving in water that is four degrees above freezing and never changes. Its skin is not skin the way yours is. It's permeable, unfinished, the same wet dark as the rock, freckled with the same orange mineral stain, so that when it sits still — which is most of the time — it is not an animal on a rock but a damp place on a rock that happens to have eyes.

Eyes that don't matter much. They are lidless, clouded, reduced to light-meters. In the green dusk that never lifts, there is nothing to see at a distance anyway. The walls are too close, the slot of sky too far. So it has traded sight for the other senses that work here.

It hears through its jaw and its belly, through the stone itself. The roar that deafens you is not noise to it. It has no outer ears to overload. The fall's thunder travels through water, through wet limestone, through the thin bones of its skull as a constant, even pressure — a kind of weight. It has lived under that weight since it hatched, and it would be lost without it. On the plain above, with wind and open distance, sound has edges and direction. Here, sound has no direction and therefore no surprise. A fox's footstep on the rim, an owl's wing cutting the slot of sky, a moth hitting the water — each makes a different ripple in the drum. The salamander feels the change in pressure before it hears anything. The roar is its silence. Inside it, it can listen.

And it does almost nothing else. Its metabolism is the temperature of the pool. Heartbeat counted in minutes, not seconds. It can go a season on a dozen midges and the biofilm that grows on the underside of stones — that slick, brown-green skin of algae and bacteria that tastes of iron. When a moth blunders down through the cold column of air and lands stunned on the water, when a peat-black beetle washes over the lip of the fall, it extends — not lunges, extends — a tongue the color of the water and takes it. Then stillness again. Moss will grow over its back if it sits on a boulder long enough. Liverwort will try to root in the silt that settles on its tail. It lets it. Camouflage here is not a strategy; it is a consequence of staying still longer than the plants do.

It is not alone, but it is singular. There are not many of them — three, maybe four in the whole bowl, each keeping to its own puddle and rill, each with ten feet of empty stone around it. They do not call to each other. They don't need to. In spring, if spring means anything ten feet below the plain, the female lays six or seven eggs the size of peas and glues them to the underside of a submerged slab where the current will not take them, where the water stays black and cold and clear. She stays with them, not guarding so much as sharing the same stone, fanning them with that same slow gill-wave. Most years the water takes them anyway. Some years one hatches. That is enough. Abundance would fill the pocket and suffocate it. The place keeps only what it can keep cold.

That is the animal. But the animal is only the part of the resident you can lift a stone and see.

The rest of it is the green.

The moss is not decoration here. It is the other half of the body. Pillows fifteen inches deep that hold water like lungs hold air. Curtains of beard-moss that comb condensation out of the air one drop at a time and hand it down. Ferns that unroll only because the moss holds a pinch of soil against vertical rock long enough for a root to try. The moss does not hurry. A millimeter a year at the edge, patiently burying the fallen blocks, turning boulders into hills, turning hills into floor. It insulates. It acidifies. It makes the cold colder and the wet wetter. It makes a place where a salamander's skin never dries.

If you watch long enough you understand they are the same creature, the animal and the carpet. One is the green's way of moving, one is the green's way of waiting. The salamander carries the spores on its damp back from island to island across the bare stone. The moss keeps the air at 100% humidity so the salamander never has to leave the water. Neither could hold this hollow alone.

And then — because a pocket this complete always acquires one — there is the human that belongs to it without living in it.

Not a villager. Not someone who stays. The wall at the south end under the overhang, the flattened floor, the fire ring, the silvered spruce beams with axe marks, the three-rung ladder with the snapped lashing — those are his shape, the negative he leaves. He is sixty, or was sixty thirty years ago. He traps hare on the plain in winter and comes down here when the wind is too high to stand in, because the cold that pools is still warmer than windchill, because the water never freezes, because the moss can be wrung out and burned if you have to, because the roar covers the sound of a man talking to himself.

He doesn't live here either. No one lives here the way people live in houses. He visits, and the pocket tolerates him the way it tolerates the owl pellet and the moth wings — as something that fell in and will be slowly digested. He replaces a lashing, leaves a rusted blade, takes a drink from the ankle-deep rill and feels his teeth ache from the cold. He knows not to stay past noon when the light leaves the slot. He knows the salamander is there and never lifts the stone. He knows, without having a word for it, that the place is already occupied by something better suited, something that has learned to love the exact things he endures: the deafening, the dusk, the too-much empty air between one living island and the next.

What lives here, then, is not a monster. It is not a spirit, unless patience is a spirit.

It is a cold-blooded, slow-hearted, light-indifferent, sound-permeated thing that has made a bargain: to be small enough that emptiness doesn't starve it, to be still enough that loudness doesn't deafen it, to be wet enough that damp doesn't rot it. It lives as stone lives, as seep lives — by persisting inward. It doesn't defend the pocket. It is the pocket, the part of it that can blink.

If you come back in fifty years the waterfall will have cut a thumb's width deeper, the moss will have covered another inch of wall, the ladder will be down, the stacked stones will have slumped. Under a stone near the black pool, something amber and translucent will lift its head a fraction, fan its gills, and settle again. It will not have noticed you were gone.
