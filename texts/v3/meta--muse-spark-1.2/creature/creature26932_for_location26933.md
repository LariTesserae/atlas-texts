Almost nothing does.

That's the first truth of it. This place is too new to be inhabited, too loud to be comfortable, too empty to feed anything that needs feeding. If you stand on the lip long enough for your knees to go numb from the cold and the roar to go flat in your ears, the first thing you understand is that this is not a place that *has* inhabitants. It's a place that *tolerates* visitors.

And then, slowly, you see that even tolerance is a kind of ecology.

### The Stone Itself Is Barely Alive

Start low, on the rock, because that's where life gets its first purchase.

The basalt isn't bare, not quite. If you put your hand down you feel how sharp it still is - it will cut you if you lean on it wrong. In the pits and bubbles, in those little frozen mouths of gas, there is a dusting of green so pale you think it's a stain on the stone until you get close enough to see it has texture. Lichen. Not a plant, not one thing at all. A partnership. A fungus that learned how to farm algae inside its own body, clinging to the glass like frost.

It doesn't grow. It *persists*. A millimeter a decade, maybe. It has no roots. It drinks the mist thrown up by the churning pocket and the thin alpine air. It bleaches in the sun and darkens when wet. It is the first and possibly last thing that will ever live on the outside of this mountain.

A few inches away, in the one crack where wind-blown dust has collected enough to be called dirt, there is the grass. One tuft. That's all. A tight, hard bunch of blades as thin as needles, yellow at the tips, bent permanently in the direction the wind comes from. It shivers every time the pocket throws up a pulse of cold spray. It has no business being here - its seed must have ridden a storm for miles to find this single fist of rock in a meadow of grass - and it knows it. It is holding on with everything it has and not spreading. It won't make a meadow here. One is all this place allows.

Nothing else has managed to root. No moss thick enough to be soft. No flower.

### The Water Is Almost Empty On Purpose

Then you look into the water, which is where you expect life to be, and for a long time you think you were wrong to expect anything. The churn is too violent, the cold too absolute, the volume too clean. This is not a lake that can support a food web. There is no soil washing in, no leaf litter, no insects falling on the surface. The water came from air and stone, maybe from deep in the cooling rock itself, and it has not had time to learn how to be dirty.

So it is empty in a way that feels deliberate. Like a room swept clean before a tenant arrives.

If you stare until your eyes water from the cold and the light, until the nets of sun on the churning surface stop making you dizzy, you will see the first movement that isn't water.

They are small. Smaller than you first think, because the clarity tricks you about distance. The length of your thumb, or the first joint of it. Shrimp is the wrong word but your brain reaches for it anyway because there is nothing else to call them. They are arthropods of a kind, or something before arthropods settled on what they wanted to be.

They are completely translucent except for their eyes.

Their bodies are glass with a faint milkiness inside, like ice with a breath trapped in it. You can see the dark line of their gut, the quick pulse of whatever serves them as a heart. Their legs and antennae are threads. And their eyes are enormous, black and bulbous, far too large for their heads, turning slowly all the time. They bioluminesce - a soft, cold blue-green pulse along their backs, every few seconds - which is utterly, poignantly useless. In this white high sun, in this transparent turbulence, the light does nothing. No one can see it except you, and you are not who it is for. It is a leftover, maybe. A habit from before they came here, or from the deep water they came from where light never reached. Here, they pulse to themselves while being tumbled.

There are not many. You may count two, or three if you are lucky and patient. Never more. They do not shoal. They do not interact if they pass each other in a vortex. They simply ride.

Because riding is all you can do in here. They are not strong swimmers. Their kicks are weak, almost decorative. The water does what it wants with them - sucks them down in a spinning column for ten feet, throws them sideways into the sheer wall, lifts them in a sudden upwelling until they breach the meniscus that bulges over the lip and for a second they are *out* of the water but still *in* it, encased in a bead of surface tension, before it spits them back. They fold and unfold their tails and wait for the ride to change. They have adapted not by fighting the chaos but by being light enough and empty enough that the chaos does not bother to break them.

Sometimes, on the wall itself, just below the waterline where the chisel marks run straight down, you will see the other one.

It looks like a newt but again, your mountain brain is lying to you to make it familiar. It is white as paper. Not pale - *white*. No pigment at all. Its skin is so thin you can see the pink of its gills fanning on the outside of its head, fluttering desperately. It has four limbs too small, with fingers too long, splayed against the raw glass. It clings. That is its entire life. It clings with its belly and its toes to the vertical score in the rock, eyes closed against the roar, until a surge peels it off and it swims with frantic, sideways lashes of its tail, white belly flashing, until it can find the wall again and cling.

It is blind, or close to it. Its eyes are dots, unseeing. It doesn't need to see. There is nothing to hunt. It scrapes. When it is clinging, you can see its mouth working against the stone, rasping at the invisible film - bacteria, algae, the first slime that is trying to colonize the pit. That film is the true base of everything here. You cannot see it, but it is there, a greasy, microscopic lawn on the rock under the hammering water. The newt harvests it. The shrimp filter it from the vortexes. Without it they would already be dead.

None of them belong here for long. If you come back next season - if the mountain hasn't shrugged or sealed itself - they may be gone. They may have died, or been thrown out by a particularly violent overturn, or simply failed to breed in water so poor. Their presence here is statistical noise. A wind brought eggs. A flood brought a larva stuck to a bird's foot. This pocket is a trap that briefly looks like a habitat.

### The Third Kind Of Inhabitant

And then there are the ones who left the stakes.

They do not live here either. No one could. You can't sleep next to that roar. You can't keep a fire lit in the spray. But someone climbed all this way, with pine cut elsewhere and rawhide and stone carried up on their back, because they needed to kneel over a place like this.

You can picture them - not many, two or three at a time, breathing hard in the thin air, shouting to each other and being unheard. They wedged the pine stakes into the cracks not to build a house, but to make a place to be still. A little platform to lie on your chest and put your face over the churn without being pulled in. The cairn is not a marker for a trail. It's a prayer that says *we were here and the water was still here*. The shallow bowl with the smooth stone is the clearest. Someone chipped that with another stone, patiently, and cupped a little of the impossible water and left a gift in it. Not to drink. To ask.

They are not the first to do that at a strange water, and they won't be the last. People find a newborn thing at the top of the world that has not learned gravity yet, that holds its shape by wanting to, and they have to make an offering. They have to show the water they saw it.

They leave quickly. The stakes shudder. The rawhide tightens and dries. The stone in the bowl slowly sinks as the film of algae grows over it.

If you stay until the sun goes thin and cold, you will see the pattern.

The lichen does not grow. It endures.
The grass does not spread. It holds.
The shrimp does not hunt. It rides.
The newt does not explore. It clings.
The human does not stay. It kneels, and leaves a stone, and goes.

Everything that lives here lives by not demanding too much of it. This is not a mountain with a lake on it. It is a droplet perched on the tip of a needle, holding itself together for a little while against the pull of the whole world below. It is loud because it is small. It is empty because it is clean. It is visited because it is there.

You pull your hands out of the water and they are red to the wrist, and for minutes afterward you can still feel the unnatural way the water wanted to stack itself around your fingers.

The pocket keeps churning. It doesn't need you.
