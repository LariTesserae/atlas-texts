You don't see it at first. Not really.

That is its first adaptation. In a place so small and so full, anything that is seen quickly does not live long.

At first you think the basin is empty except for the small things — the frogs, the skinks, the fungus, the flies turning in the light column. You think what lives here is only that busy, low carpet of hunger. You crouch by one of the rock bowls near the center, the one about as wide as your chest that holds an inch of clear water, and you watch the tadpoles fleck in it, and you think: *nothing large could live here. There's not enough room. Not enough food. Not enough air that moves.*

Then you realize you have been looking at it for a minute already.

You thought the north wall, where the limestone sweats heaviest, was just more wall. A bulge of honey-colored stone gone dark with wet, upholstered in moss and liverwort as thick as a pelt. Water runs down it in a black seam and beads and falls, beads and falls. The moss there is unusually deep, hanging in a lip over the stone. Lusher. A brighter green than the other mats. And very still.

Except the moss breathes.

Very slowly. A long, shallow rise that takes ten seconds to crest. Then a hold. Then a fall. It is not wind — there is no wind in the sink. The dust in the light column is hanging motionless three feet away.

You lean closer and the shape refuses to resolve. That is the trick. You keep trying to put an edge on it and the edge keeps belonging to something else — to the rock, to a root, to a fallen fig leaf.

Then a drop of condensation falls from a fern frond directly onto it, and the moss shivers. Not like a plant. Like skin.

What lives here lives like the rock sweats: slowly, constantly, without announcement.

---

It is not large the way a bear is large. It is large the way a low wall is large — long and flat and settled into place. If it stretched out you think it would be as long as you are tall, but it never stretches out. It is folded. Tucked. Its body is pressed into the damp hollow at the foot of the north wall where two basins meet, a shallow natural trough that is always wet. It fits there exactly, as if the stone softened around it long ago and then hardened again.

When you finally see it whole, you understand why the low stone wall built by whoever camped here was broken. Not by a fig. Or not only by a fig.

Its back is the color and texture of the limestone when it is wet — not gray, not white, but that honey-amber darkened to tea-brown, pitted and fluted. The pitting is not pits. It is the animal itself. Its skin is thick, spongy, and deeply dimpled, each dimple holding a bead of water the way the rock bowls do. Liverwort and true moss grow right on it, rooted in the thin slime that covers it. Tiny ferns have taken hold along the ridge of its spine. It has gardened itself. From above, in the green gloom, it is not an animal at all. It is a continuation of the wall.

Its head, when you find the head, is blunt and wide, almost as wide as the trough it lies in. No neck to speak of. The mouth is a soft, straight seam at the front, lipless. The eyes — you look for eyes and for a moment feel relief at not finding them, then a different, deeper unease.

There are eyes, but they have been retired. Two spots, high on the head, where the skin has gone smooth and slightly translucent, like the membrane over a boiled egg. Pale, gold-flecked discs under the skin that do not open, do not blink, do not track you. It does not need to see. Light is rare here and mostly useless to a hunter who lives by vibration. In the perpetual late-afternoon green, sight is a rumor. It navigated away from sight generations ago. Dozens, maybe hundreds of generations in this one room-sized forest, with no gene flow from the outside scrub. If you touched the skin over those covered eyes it would be warm. Alive, but looking inward.

What it does have, that you didn't notice until you were close enough to smell the sweet metallic tang of it, is a throat.

Or rather, an apron of throat. The skin under its chin and down its chest is loose, pleated, enormous — a dewlap that folds out and lies flat against the stone like a damp towel. It is paler than the rest of it, almost pink-white, and veined like a leaf. While you watched, a drop fell from the wall above, and another, and another, and each one landed on the dimpled back and ran in tiny rivulets down the flanks and was channeled — you could see the faint grooves in the skin, like the fluting on the limestone itself — down into that throat apron, where it pooled. The apron was cupped. It was a basin made of living skin. A rock bowl that breathes.

That is how it drinks in a place with no river. It does not go to water. It becomes the place water goes.

It breathes the same way. You can hear it now that you know to listen, under the *tik... tik...* of the drips. A very faint, wet sigh each time that low moss-back rises. It has no lungs worth the name, or it does not use them here. The heat would dry it from the inside out. So it breathes through that vast, wet skin, through the apron, through the back. It must stay wet or it dies. And so it never leaves the sweat-line on the north wall. It has no reason to. Everything it needs comes down to it.

Gravity is its wind. Fallen leaves, dead beetles, beetle larvae, frog eggs, droppings from the bats folded on the ferns — all of it slides down the leaning fronds and hanging roots and walls and collects in this trough, in this damp compost at the bottom. The whole sink is a funnel with this animal at its spout.

It eats without moving, mostly. That is why you thought the place was silent. It is an ambush that can wait for days.

When a skink flicks too close, when a frog hops from one basin to the next, when a beetle blunders off an elephant-ear — it does not lunge. Lunging is noisy and costly. The seam of its mouth simply… opens. Not wide, not with teeth. It puckers, dimples inward, and the wet around its head seems to shiver. The air right in front of it goes tight for a fraction of a second. You only see it because a dust mote near its snout suddenly jerks sideways an inch, against all stillness.

And then the frog is gone. No snap. No struggle. The frogs nearby don't even startle, or if they do they settle again in a second. The throat apron gives a slow ripple as it swallows, and a minute later a faint, dark pellet — the indigestible chitin and bone — is nudged gently out the side of the mouth and left to melt back into the compost. Nothing is wasted because there is nowhere for waste to go.

If something larger falls in — a bird that misjudged the rim and broke a wing, a coati kit that slipped from a root-ladder — it will wait. It can wait longer than the bird can live. And when the bird stops moving, it will flow over it. Not fast. A tide of warm, dimpled skin, inch by inch, covering, digesting externally with that same sweet rot-smell that you took for leaf-rot. By morning the feathers will be a neat, wet pile a foot away, and the back-moss will be a little greener.

It is not cruel. It has no face to be cruel with. It is exactly as patient as the rock that sweats.

And it is not alone, but there is only ever one that looks like *this*.

In the smaller basins scattered across the floor — the palm-sized ones, the ones that hold water for a day or two — there are strings of eggs. Clear jelly, each bead the size of a pea, clinging to the stone under the water. Some are fresh, some are clouded, some are already wiggling with comma-shaped larvae that have the same dimpled skin, the same covered eyes, gills like red lace fanning outside their heads. They will stay aquatic as long as the basin holds water. When it dries, which it does every few days in the dry season, the largest will crawl out and make for the big trough on the north wall, following the wet seam down. The smallest will dry where they are and become part of the compost that feeds the survivor. The mother — if mother is the word for a thing that does not move from its trough — does not guard them the way a mammal would. She guards them by being the trough. By keeping the whole north wall wet with her own gathered water, by keeping the fungal blooms trimmed back with her grazing mouth, she keeps the nursery from rotting all at once.

You understand then what the niche in the east wall was for. And the smooth river pebbles. Not decoration. Not children playing. An offering, or a thank-you, or a bargain. *Stay there. Stay wet. Don't flow over us while we sleep.* The people who cut those shallow holds in the rock knew enough to build their low wall between their packed-earth floor and that north side. They knew to leave the center clear, to not sweep up every fallen leaf. They were feeding it without meaning to, perhaps, and then on purpose. And when the rains failed one year or the drip slowed, they left. The animal did not.

If you stay until the column of light narrows and cuts off at the rim, you will see it do the one thing that still belongs to the world above.

At noon the light was a hard white bar across the floor. Now, just before it dies, the bar slides up the north wall and for about ninety seconds it falls directly onto that mossy back. The covered eyes do not react. But the whole body does. The dimples seem to deepen. The small ferns along its spine, which you thought were just passengers, angle sharply toward the light, their leaves trembling. The algae in its skin — because that is what the green is, not just moss, but a symbiont living in the outer layer — greens visibly, greedily. It photosynthesizes. Just a little. Just enough that in the long months when fewer beetles fall, it doesn't have to move at all. An animal that learned, in this place where light is rationed like water, to eat light the way a leaf does.

Then the light is gone. The heat stays. The rock keeps sweating. A drop falls from the fern into the throat-basin with a soft *tik*.

The back rises. Holds. Falls.

Somewhere under the elephant-ears, a tree frog finally dares its glassy *peep... peep...* and does not know that the ripple under its feet is not moss.

From above, as night comes on, the sink is invisible again — just a darker patch of trees in the pale scrub, a breath held in cupped hands. Down inside, the wound has kept what fell into it, learned how to keep it, and grown a skin over itself that is both wound and dressing.

You climb back up the root-ladder careful not to brush any vine that might already be growing a new tendril around your boot. Behind you, the trough is still occupied. The wall is still breathing.
