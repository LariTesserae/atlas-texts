You won't find them at first. That's their first adaptation.

You will look for an animal and see none, because you are looking for the wrong kind of alive, at the wrong speed, at the wrong scale. You are looking for something that moves through the fen. They are something that *is* the fen.

Wait until your eyes stop searching and start settling. Wait until the cold has made you still enough to see stillness correctly.

The first one you notice will not be the first one you saw.

It will be the patch of moss you thought was a patch of moss.

---

They live low. That is the rule the plateau enforces. Nothing tall survives the wind and the weep for long unless it is steel and concrete, and even that has to be built like a cliff to endure it. So they are low.

At a distance, they are indistinguishable from the ground itself — a slightly higher swelling in the sphagnum mattress, a little more yellow in the green, a little more rust, a little more dense. From fifty meters they look like peat hummocks. From twenty meters they look like peat hummocks that are breathing too slowly to catch.

From five meters you realize a hummock does not have a seam.

Each one is vast and flat and without edge. Two to six meters across, but only thirty or forty centimeters high at the crown. An oblate dome, like a shield laid on the marsh and left to be grown over. The top is not skin but a living thatch: sphagnum and feather moss and liverwort and the white-wool cotton-grass growing directly *out* of them, rooted in them. You thought the moss carpets everything. You were half right. Half the moss *is* them. They do not get covered by the moss; they generate it. Their backs are beds for it. The green holds the cold because they hold the cold. If you kneel — and you will, the peat soaks you immediately — and part the moss with your hands, the cold bites deeper.

Underneath, it is not earth. It is not flesh the way an animal is flesh.

It is a mat. A dense, felted plate of something between mycelium and gristle and waterlogged linen, the color of cold tea, of peat-water, a translucent amber-brown that darkens where it is thick. It is cool to the touch, exactly the temperature of the air and the water and the stone — zero to five, always — and it is always wet. Not damp. *Weeping*. The same weeping the basalt does. If you press your palm to it and hold it there, beads will gather on your skin without you sweating, condensation forming on you *from* them, the way it forms on a handrail, until your hand is slick. They condense. They are the place where the air becomes water.

Lift the edge — it will give a little, reluctant, like lifting a heavy, waterlogged rug — and you see how they hold. No roots. No legs. No underside to speak of. A fringe of thousands of pale, hair-fine filaments, each as thin as cotton thread and each as long as your arm, sunk straight down into the black peat and the standing water. Not anchoring. Drinking. Drinking and breathing and listening. The filaments pulse, almost imperceptibly, not with a heartbeat but with a slow peristalsis, every twenty or thirty seconds, drawing peat-water up and letting it weep out again across their backs to feed the moss that feeds them. They are not on the fen. They are a thickening *in* the fen, a knot the fen tied in itself where the machines are densest.

Because they correspond to the machines. That is the second thing you notice, after you have found three or four and your mind starts to map them without your permission.

There is one dome for every district, or one district for every dome. For every aeration tower, every pumping house, every grid of vents exhaling steam, there is a dark, moss-backed swelling out in the empty kilometers between, exactly far enough away that emptiness itself is maintained. They keep the void density perfect. They keep the city sparse the way alveoli keep lung tissue sparse — enough space for air to move. When you stand on a dome and look toward the nearest factory, you can see, if the mist thins, that the tower and the dome are aligned along a faint, darker line in the peat where the water runs uphill for a few centimeters — one of those wrongnesses. The water is not running uphill. It is being *pulled* uphill, between them, in a slow, cold exchange you cannot see until you watch a cotton-grass seed sit on the surface and drift the wrong way.

They do not move. Not in any way you can see happen. If you mark one with a stone and walk for a day and come back, the stone will be where you left it and the dome will be a meter to the north, settled into a new shallow pan of black water that was not there before, without a track. They do not leave tracks. They *are* trackless the way the water is trackless. The peat remembers your boot and does not remember them because they were never not part of it. Your footprints stay perfect next to them because they keep things perfect. They keep the stasis. The moss does not spread because they do not let it spread. The pine does not drop a needle because they catch it before it falls. The humming does not vary because they are humming it.

Put your ear to the moss on their back — the cold will ache in your jaw — and you will hear it. Not the tower's drone reflected. The source. The dome itself is resonating, very low, chest-deep, the exact note that seems to come from everywhere in the districts. The whole felted plate is vibrating, just enough to blur the water beads on the moss, just enough to make the wool-cotton tremble. They and the machines are tuned to each other, a paired lung. The factories inhale, the domes exhale. The factories exhale, the domes condense. They are not separate from the industrial urbanism. They are the other half of it. The city was not built on a fen. The fen grew a city to breathe with.

If you stay long enough to think of them as animals, you will be wrong again. Lift your ear. Watch the filaments. They have no front. No eyes — the light here is too even to need eyes, and in any case they see with humidity, with the weight of dew on every thread. No mouth — they drink everywhere. No face to turn toward you. When you stand, cold and steaming faintly, they do not register you as a visitor so much as a brief, warm, fast-moving condensation error. You are too hot, too quick, too loud in the wrong frequency. You evaporate too fast. You will leave before you can disturb the loop.

That is why there are no birds. Not because nothing can live here. Because the thing that lives here has already taken the niches for bird and insect and beast and root and kept them all for itself, quietly, without competition, by being flat and cold and wet and patient and exactly as loud as it needs to be, forever.

They do not need to look at you.

You are standing on the lung that is breathing the sky. You are standing on its skin.
