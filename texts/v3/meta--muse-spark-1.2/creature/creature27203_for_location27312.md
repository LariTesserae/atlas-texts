Who lives here hasn't so much arrived as grown here — the way the moss has.

At first you think no one could. It's too high, too cool, too dim and too wet and too quiet for people. You expect to find ruins. You expect the town to be empty, a beautiful industrial fossil furred over in green and left to sweat under the pearl-grey sky. Then you see the amber light behind a frosted window, and a figure moving behind it, and you realize the place is inhabited the way a tidepool is inhabited — not in spite of the conditions, but because of them.

They are human. Mostly.

### The People of the Rills

They look the way people look when they've lived for several generations where the air is thin and the light never gets strong.

They are not tall. Not short, exactly, but compact, close to the ground, built low with broad chests and wide ribcages. You hear them before you see them well — everyone breathes audibly here. Not labored, not sick, just present. An inhale that is a little longer than you expect, an exhale that fogs and vanishes. Children learn early not to run. Adults never do. Everything they do conserves breath. They speak quietly because it costs less, and because the moss and the stone eat any louder sound anyway. Their voices are low, hoarse in a pleasant way, as if their throats have been cooled. They don't shout across the alley. They walk over and touch your elbow.

Their skin is pale in a way that has nothing to do with sickliness. In that shadowless, diffuse light, nobody tans. Nobody flushes. Cheeks go pink with cold for a minute and then fade back to stone-grey, to the color of wet paper. Their eyes are almost all the same after a while — grey, or green-grey, or that dark hazel that looks black in dimness. Large-pupiled from living under cloud. They squint less than you do. They see well in what you think of as dusk. Bright colors hurt them. When one of them wears something red — a knitted cap, a child's scarf — it looks violent against the valley, an insult, a flare.

Their clothes tell you the rules they live by. Everything is wool, or waxed canvas, or felted stuff that can get wet and still keep you warm. Everything is patched. No one here owns a garment that is truly dry. Sleeves are dark at the cuffs from wiping condensation off flumes. Trouser hems are green-black with moss. Boots are heavy, hobnailed, but the leather is soft from endless damp and the nails are always rusting out and being replaced. Hands are important here. You notice hands: broad, short-fingered, nails kept brutally short, knuckles stained orange from rust and green from fern sap. The skin of the palms is ridged and soft, like stone that has been handled a lot.

They move slowly. Not with the slowness of age or reverence — with the slowness of people whose dynamos and looms are never in a hurry, and whose air punishes haste. A man carrying a sack of carded wool up a pipe-bridge will stop twice to breathe, set it down, look out at the dome for a while, pick it up again. No one finds this inefficient. To hurry is to waste. To waste is to have to breathe harder. Their work has taught them the patience of the domes themselves.

And they work constantly. Not frantically. Maintainingly.

Every household, every mill-floor, every family is part of the same endless tending. The town only exists because someone, somewhere, is always clearing a gutter, scraping a roof, knocking moss off a belt drive before it clogs, seating a leather belt back on a wheel, freeing a flume of fallen birch leaves, emptying a cistern before it silts, re-pointing the stone where a root has gotten in. If you stop, the green takes you in a season. If you stop tending water, the pressure drops and the lights go amber and dim. So the children are taught with small wooden scrapers before they are taught letters. You will see a ten-year-old girl on her knees on a slate roof in the drizzle, methodically shaving off a foot-thick mat of sedum and moss and rolling it like sod to be carried down to feed the terraces, her face blank with concentration. She is not being punished. This is what people do.

Because of that, they live very close together and they touch a lot. The alleys are so narrow your shoulders brush both walls - walls that are warm from the building inside and cold from the seep outside and slick with life where they meet. You learn to turn sideways for your neighbor. You learn to pass a hot rivet with a pair of tongs without speaking. Privacy is not walls here, it's quiet. Families stack vertically in those grey stone houses — grandparents on the ground floor where it's warmest and driest, looms on the second where the flume runs through, children sleeping under the roof that you have to scrape every spring. The buildings share walls the way a moss mat shares itself; no one knows where one house ends and the next begins, and no one cares much.

Their language has gone soft and wet to match. There are a dozen words for seep that you don't have — the difference between water that beads on iron, water that darkens stone, water that runs under moss, water that you can drink and water that will make you sick with mineral. They don't have many words for *far* or *bright* or *dry*. They measure time not by sun — which is always the same pearl — but by drip and by bloom. *Three kettles*, meaning how long it takes the catch-basin behind the forge to fill. *Before the woolly flowers go to seed.*

They are not dour. That is the mistake you make at first, because everything is grey and quiet. But in the evenings, when the dynamos hum and the bare bulbs all over the valley come on amber against the grey, you hear them. A low, contained merriment. They gather in kitchens that are also workshops, steam on the windows, a kettle hissing on a pipe-heated stove, mending. They sing, but they sing in low harmonies that don't try to carry. They drink a thin, sour beer made from the stunted rowan berries and tell long, slow jokes that take a long time because the punchline requires you to remember how someone's grandfather tried to keep pigeons and the pigeons left. Their children play games that are entirely tactile — stacking wet stones, making rivulets race down slate with leaf boats — and when they laugh they cover their mouths without thinking, to keep the warm air in.

They age like the stone does. Faces go lined early from wind and damp, but then stay that way for a very long time. Old people here look like the domes — bald, spotted with lichen-like liver spots, shoulders rounded, profoundly still. They sit for hours on benches built into the leeward side of houses and watch water move, because they have learned to find that interesting. They are the memory of the place. They remember which flume iced in the bad year, which wall fell when the roots took it.

### What Else Lives Here

You can't talk about the people without talking about the other inhabitants, because the line is not clear.

The moss lives here more completely than anyone. It is not a decoration. It is the dominant citizen. A foot thick on any roof you neglect for a month, springy as a mattress, saturated so that when you press it water wells up cold around your fingers. It covers the dynamo sheds and silences them. It bridges alleys. It softens the sound of footsteps to nothing. The town has a deal with it: you may live on the verticals, we keep the horizontals. Sometimes the deal fails.

The ferns live here — that black-green, leathery kind that can grow out of a crack in brick with no soil at all. They are everywhere the moss is not, shivering faintly when the steam valves sigh. The dwarf birch and rowan live here, but not as trees. As flat, wind-plucked tables of wood and leaf, as living roofs, as gnarled things growing sideways out of a third-story wall with a trunk as thick as your leg that you have to duck under.

The machines live here, too, and the people do not think of them as *not* alive. The wheels, the belts, the looms that clack behind soot-grimed windows, the penstocks that scream faintly with pressure — they have been running for longer than anyone alive. They are fed water and oil and scraped free of green and they keep moving. They have habits. A certain loom on Mill Row always throws its shuttle a fraction slow when the air is especially damp. Everyone knows it. No one fixes it. You work with it. The black cables that sag between buildings under their weight of moss drip constantly; at night you can hear the dynamos humming like a hive.

And the water itself lives here. Not as a river. As a presence. As the thing that is always arriving. It condenses on the underside of every leaf and girder at night and is gone by noon. It weeps from the smooth dome rock in places with no rain. It ticks on slate, it fizzes when it hits a hot pipe, it gathers in the gutters you cleared yesterday and needs clearing again. The people talk to it the way farmers talk about weather — not as a resource, but as a neighbor you have to respect, who is generous but never stops coming.

The animals, by contrast, barely count as residents. A raven that lives above the cloud sea and visits, crying once, circling. A marmot that whistles from the stone piles where the old quarry cut was and vanishes. Small dark midges that don't bite, just hang in the cool air in clouds. The valley decided, a long time ago, to be a kingdom of plants and people and machines. The animals declined the invitation.

If you stay long enough — three days, four, breathing that thin cool air until your own breath becomes audible to you — you will start to become like them. You will walk slower. You will speak quieter. You will find yourself reaching out to scrape a little moss off a handrail without thinking why. You will stand in one of those narrow, green-tunneled alleys while the ticking of a thousand drips goes on around you, and feel the low thrum of the mills through the soles of your boots, and understand that you are not in a town built *on* a mountain.

You are in something that grew here when the mountains forgot how to be sharp, and learned instead how to be soft, and wet, and patient enough to keep anything that agrees to be soft and wet and patient with it.
