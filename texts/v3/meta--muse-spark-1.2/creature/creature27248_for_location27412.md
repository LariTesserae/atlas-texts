Nothing human lives here.

Not in the way you mean lives - not a village, not a camp, not even a wandering trail that stays. Anyone who tried would sink, would be eaten clean by fungus and ant before their footprints dried. The place is too wet to burn and too soft to build on and too busy being eaten by itself to leave room.

What lives here are the things that learned how to live on a factory floor with no walls.

At the bottom of it all, the ones who actually run it, are not animals at all.

In the peat and in the terraces and in the breathing shafts of the cliffs live the bacteria. They are the color here. You thought the rust-red and ochre and the deep sulfurous green of the rimstone dams was mineral. It is skin. Sheets of iron-bacteria and silica-bacteria and cyanobacteria laid down like paint, each in its exact temperature band. The hottest seep - almost too hot to touch - grows a black-green felt that smells like matches. A little cooler, a leather of bright orange that lays down iron millimeter by millimeter, building the dams you thought were stone. Cooler still, a pale, translucent white that makes opal if you wait a thousand years. They do not move. They do not need to. Water is brought to them, metal is brought to them, heat is brought to them. They precipitate the plateau out of water, breathing rock.

On top of them, rooted in them, drinking them, are the plants that learned to live without soil.

Because there is no soil, not really. Only peat that is ninety percent water and the bare sandstone underneath. So nothing here can afford to be picky about where it puts its roots. Every tree is half epiphyte. Their roots don't go down - they go out, sideways and up, a tangled plate that grips the peat like fingers in wet cloth, bracing trunks that twist to keep their balance when the ground quakes. And every branch is itself ground.

The bromeliads rule the middle layer. Not the small, neat kind you see in a greenhouse. These are basins. Each cistern is a pond held in the air, two or three feet across, its outer leaves thick and waxy and blood-red at the base, holding two gallons of rainwater and leaf litter and insect husks. Each one is an island archipelago. Inside one: tadpoles of the pebble toad, black and flat as seeds, that never become free-swimming frogs but hatch as perfect tiny toads. In another: damselfly larvae with jaws like bear traps. In another: a crab, no bigger than a thumbnail, blind and amber-colored, that has never touched the ground in its life and never will. The bromeliad does not mind. It drinks their ammonia.

Under that, carnivores. This place is too poor in nutrients for ordinary gorging, despite all the green. The water washes everything away too fast. So the plants turned to eating the air. Heliamphora - the marsh pitchers - stand in every open seep, fields of them, pale green trumpets veined red, filled to the brim with rainwater. They don't have lids; they don't need them here, it rains before they can evaporate. Down inside each pitcher, a slick of digestive water waits. Mosquitoes and gnats slide down the waxy throat. Further in the peat, sundews no larger than your palm glitter with glue, each tentacle holding a perfect, terrible bead. They are flowering now, absurdly delicate white stars on thin stalks, just out of reach of their own traps.

And then the things that cut.

You heard them before you saw them. The leafcutters are not inhabitants *of* this place. They *are* the place's hands. Their colonies are its organs. Each mound is not a hill but a lung - fifteen, twenty feet across, low and dome-shaped, thatched with dead leaves that have been chewed to a felt, vented with chimneys that you can put your hand over and feel the warm, sour breath of the fungus inside. Follow one of their highways and you understand the scale. The trail is kept as clean as a swept road, the peat lip reinforced with grains of quartz carried one by one, roots bridged with soil cement, divots filled. A river of them flows both ways without jam, without collision. The outgoing ants empty-jawed, the returning ants each carrying a sail of fresh-cut leaf bigger than themselves, held high to keep it dry. At the major intersections older, larger workers stand and tap - *left, right, wait, go* - with their antennae. You could watch for an hour and not see a single ant leave the path.

They don't eat the leaves. No one here eats directly. They carry them down into the earth to feed the fungus, white and branching like lungs through all the underground chambers, that breaks the leaf down into sugar for them. The fungus is the true animal; the ants are its farmers, its weather system. If you crouch and smell at a vent, the smell is not earth. It's warm bread and acid, a brewery.

A little deeper in, where the leafcutters don't go, are the other foundries. Termite cathedrals, black as clinker, five meters high, their walls hard as baked pottery. Put your ear to one and you hear the hum - not buzzing, but the combined shiver of a million wings beating not to fly but to fan. Inside they are holding 32 degrees Celsius against a forest that wants to swing between 28 and 40, fanning, carrying water, sealing drafts with spit-mud. Their fungus is different, more golden, grown on fecal combs. The mound breathes through its fluted skin.

In the pools and in the braided channels between them lives the quiet, endemic water-life that never had anywhere else to go.

This plateau has been an island in the sky for two million years. Nothing that lives up here lives anywhere else. The fish in the bromeliads and the tea-red pools are not really fish the way you know them - they are killifish and tetras dwarfed and bleached, some with no eyes at all, navigating by feeling the pressure change when your boot sighs water up around it. They live their whole lives in water that may be only a foot deep and twenty feet across, and if their pool dries in a freak hot week, their eggs wait in the damp peat, unhatched, for months until the rain returns.

The frogs own the night. During the day you hear only one sharp note after a lightning strike, but crouch by a rimstone pool at dusk when the cloud is being born between the trees and the whole forest starts to ring. Oreophrynella - the pebble toads - small as a knuckle, black or mustard-yellow, their skin pebbled like the stone they sit on, walk instead of hop because there is nowhere to land between the pitchers. They have not evolved a tadpole stage; there is no need for open water when every leaf holds it. The males carry the eggs on their backs under a flap of skin until perfect froglets crawl out. Further up, glass frogs sleep under leaves with their hearts visible through their bellies, translucent green as the light.

Above them, in the low, complete canopy, the birds that learned to fly through cloud on the ground.

They are not many species, but there are thousands of individuals, and all of them are silent until they have to be. The white-necked swift - sooty black with a white collar - lives in the breathing shafts themselves, clinging to vertical sandstone behind a curtain of seep, nesting where no predator can climb through the piston wind. They scream out at dawn in a tight flock and slice through the ground-clouds, beaks open for gnats. The tepui parrotlets, grass-green and nearly invisible, move through the canopy upside down, prying open bromeliads for larvae. And the hawk - you saw it once, you will not see it again - a double-toothed kite with shoulders too thick for its size, slate-gray, that hunts by dropping straight through cloud without a rustle and taking frogs and doves off leaves before the condensation has even cleared.

Everything else is clean-up. Shining, metallic beetles, blue-black and copper, congregate on the lightning trees after a strike to drink the sudden flush of sap. Tiny tanagers, orange like the bacteria mats, pick termites off the cathedral walls on their nuptial flights. On the forest floor, a chestnut coati - one of the only mammals big enough to keep its heat in this wet - snuffles, solitary, turning over pitcher plants, scattering tadpoles with its nose, its fur permanently damp and smelling of peat.

And nothing rots.

That is the final inhabitant. The white fungus that blooms overnight. You will see it in the morning on a branch that fell at midnight - a lace of mycelium so fine it looks like frost, already pulling the wood apart into fibers. By afternoon the branch is soft enough to push your finger through. By the next morning it is a stain of peat and a cluster of fruiting bodies no bigger than pins, already releasing green spores into that green air. The ants carry the larger pieces away, the termites take the cellulose, the bacteria take the leachate. The forest eats its dead before they can stink.

If you stay still until the seep rises around your boots and the forest exhales that warm *huhhh* from the cliffs, you realize there is not really a list of things that live here. There is one thing that lives here, in different shapes - a single, hot, wet engine that has taught itself to cut and carry and precipitate and digest and fly and bloom, all without ever needing a hand to tend it.

It notices you as water notices a stone - flows around, condenses on your skin, and keeps working.
