They don't look like they came here. They look like they grew here.

At first, from the stair, you don't see them at all. You see only the *evidence* of them — the laundry lines, the small fires, the stacked jars — and you think the Basin is empty, that whoever lived here has left. Then your eyes adjust to the moving light and the crowd resolves itself out of the brick.

They are low and close to the ground, even when they are high up.

That is the first thing you understand. Nobody stands tall here. The vault is low and the platforms are lower, and generations of ducking and climbing have made them compact. Not short, exactly, but folded. Broad shouldered, long backed, with a way of carrying their weight in their hips and thighs. The children look like small adults and the adults move like careful children, always aware of the ceiling, the beam, the pot overhead. They climb without thinking — hand, foot, hand — bare feet finding the polished notches in the ladders, toes splayed wide and callused on the edges. Their soles are the color of the bricks, pink-brown and thick as hide. Their hands are the same. Palms glossy, fingertips soft and wrinkled from damp the way yours are after a bath, but permanently — never quite dry.

You thought the light would have bleached them, kept underground. It hasn't. Where the sun columns fall, they are deeply browned. Where the gloom holds, they are a damp paler brown, like river clay. All of them have the same dark hair, cut short with a chipped stone held close to the scalp, because long hair molds in the wet. All of them have it damp. It never fully dries here. It clings to their necks. The old women keep theirs wrapped in undyed cotton that has gone grey from washing. The men and women both wear the same thing: loose, sleeveless tunics of woven reed and softened bast, patched until the patches have patches, tied at the waist with cord. No shoes. No ornament except for necessity — a pierced seed, a frog bone on a string for a child who survived a fever, a smear of green dye on the cheek on a feast day. Skin is always faintly sheened. Everyone sweats a little, all the time, and no one seems to mind the touch of it. They brush constantly, shoulder to shoulder, hand to forearm, child asleep against an old man's back. Privacy is not kept by distance. It is kept by sound.

And they are soft-voiced. That is the second thing you learn, after the shape of them.

In any other hamlet this crowded, you would expect shouting. Here, everyone speaks quietly, in a low murmur that the brick wants to carry anyway. A mother does not call her child from a platform three stories up. She clicks her tongue twice, and the child hears it as if it were beside his ear. They have learned the trick of the vault's delay. They pause half a breath after they speak to let the echo catch up. Their language has a lot of breath in it, a lot of sibilants and clicks that cut through the dripping and the cooing. When they laugh, they cover their mouths — not from modesty, but because a laugh can bounce five times and become something else.

If you stand still long enough, the crowd sorts itself into its ages.

The oldest live lowest, where it is warmest and the air is most still. They sit on low stools by the well or on the brick itself, legs folded, tending the things that need patience and not climbing. They tend the vine, guiding a tendril a fraction of an inch a day with twine. They scrape lichen into bowls with flat stones. They keep the fire pits on the ground level fed, because the smoke rises straight up through the oculi and the old ones like to watch it go. Their eyes have gone filmy from a lifetime of woodsmoke and damp, but they see movement better than you do. Their skin has gone into the brick, creased like mortar lines. They know the sound of every drip and whether the well is rising or falling by how the frogs are calling.

The working adults are always moving with the light. You see it after ten minutes: a whole, silent migration. When the sun column slides off a wall, the weaver picks up her loom and shifts two pillars over, into the new light. The man shucking beetles from the mortar moves his bowl to follow the glow. The children doing lessons on a reed mat drag the mat six feet to stay in the amber. They do not own light. They borrow it as it passes. Their bodies are lean and corded from that constant, small labor — not heavy work, but endless work. Pounding, stirring, weaving, carrying water in shallow clay pans on the head, wringing cloth, mashing fermented gourds, feeding the pigeons so they will stay and be fed on later. Everyone knows the same half-dozen tasks and can do any of them. There is no one idle. Even a woman nursing has a handful of beetle larvae to sort with her free hand.

And the children — there are so many of them, more than you thought a place this size could hold, because they are stored vertically, sleeping in stacked hammocks like fruit in nets, perched on ledges, riding on hips. They are loud in the way children are loud everywhere, but they have already learned the vault's manners. They don't shriek. They chatter and splash in the runnels, cupping condensation in their hands and pouring it over each other's heads. Their knees and elbows are scraped pink from brick. They are fearless with animals. A girl of four lets a gecko run up her arm and sits perfectly still to watch its throat pulse. A boy lifts a fat black beetle to his mouth and cracks it like a nut, offering you one without hesitation, not as a joke but as hospitality. They know the swifts by name, or by the pattern of white on their wings. They know which frog in the well is theirs to watch.

None of them are alone. That is the thing that finally settles after you have watched for a while and stopped trying to count them.

They are not individuals in a crowd. They are a surface, like the moss. Where one moves, another fills in. An infant cries and three women turn, not just the mother. A man climbs a ladder and a boy steadies the base without being asked. When someone sleeps, someone else drapes a cloth over them to catch the drip from the ceiling onto the cloth instead of the face. They touch constantly to know where the others are without looking, a hand on an ankle as they pass on a narrow platform, a tap on the shoulder in the gloom. In a place where every brick is touched, every person is touched too.

They notice you, of course, from the moment your shoulders brushed the glossy plaster on the way down. They just do not stop their work to stare. An old woman by the cistern lifts her chin to you — not a nod, a small, upward breath through her nose that is their greeting. A child stops his game to watch your dry shoes on the wet herringbone, curious about the sound they make, too sharp, too unadapted. The pigeons startle at your new shadow and wheel once around the vault, and every face glances up to track them out of habit, to see if they will settle again.

You realize they are not *living in* the Basin.

They *are* the Basin's way of staying alive. The beetles would choke the mortar without the hands that pick them. The moss would either take every wall or die without the vine-tenders. The well would silt without the children who skim it with cloths every morning. The air would go still without the fires and the breath and the wings. The brick would just be brick. Instead it sweats, and they catch it. It holds sound, and they answer quietly. It gives them light in moving columns, and they follow it, all day, every day, from wall to wall to wall, a slow, warm, endless orbit just below the skin of the world.

If you stay until dusk, you will see them go still all at once. The last column of sun lifts off the far arch and is gone. The bats drop from the high brick like thrown stones. And then, as if someone had given a signal you couldn't hear, the small oil lamps are all visibly bright at once, and the adults all sit, and the children all find a lap, and the frogs get very loud, and the whole crowded, wet, brick lung of a place exhales and holds you inside its warmth until morning.
