They don't live *in* it. They live *on* it, *of* it, *across* it. There is no "in" when the plant is the ground.

And nothing here is small and many. Everything here is few and large, and very good at being alone. The continent has selected against crowds.

### The Singers

You hear them before you can ever find them, because finding them would require you to look at something that does not want to be seen, that *is* the bark you are looking at.

The dominant mass of animal life is not mammalian. It is chitin and tympanum.

Take the Chord Beetle. You thought the gong-ring you heard was a metaphor. It wasn't. An adult is the size of a large dog, its back a single convex plate of amber chitin stretched tight as a drumhead. It wedges its six legs into the deep furrows of a Holdfast bough and braces, then draws its barbed hind femurs across the ridged underside of that plate. The whole plate resonates. One individual can throw a pure, valley-spanning note that carries for three kilometers through hot air - a territorial claim, a mating call, a warning. When two answer each other across a gap between Holdfasts, you feel the interference pattern in your chest before you hear it. They are spaced so far apart you might think they are rare. They aren't. The land is just that big, and sound is cheaper than walking when the gap between food is a mile of empty thermals.

Smaller, but more numerous, are the Glasswings. Each one is almost not there - a dragonfly the length of your forearm with wings that are clear to the point of invisibility, veined with refractive silica. Alone, it is silent. Together, billions of them hatch from the ephemeral rain-pools in the hanging meadows and rise in a phase-synchronized cloud, and their wings beat at a frequency that makes the air itself chime. It is not a swarm. A swarm implies density. This is a sparse rain that never lands, a glittering haze between Holdfasts that catches the sun and throws it back in prisms. They live for nine days, do not eat as adults, sing only to find each other to breed, and die with their wings folded into the peat of the meadows they were born in.

Everything that wants to be heard here has learned to be *loud*. Everything that wants to eat has learned to be *silent*.

### The Still Things

The Slag-Mantis is fifteen feet long when it decides to unfold, which it almost never does. For most of its life it is not an animal at all to your eye. It is a texture.

Its carapace is not colored like bark. It *is* bark - the same furrowed, lichen-spotted, resin-sealed wood, grown over with real lichen that it cultivates in pits on its back. Its legs are buttresses. Its head is a knot. It presses itself flat into a bark-canyon on the vertical trunk, aligning its body with the grain, and waits. Beetles walk over it. Condensation rivers run over it. It does not blink. Its metabolism drops until it is almost wood.

Then a glider dips. Or a human strays too far from the rope bridge. Or a chord beetle lands too close.

The strike is not a lunge. It is a failure of your eye to track. One foreleg, double-jointed and tipped with interlocking thorns like a bear trap, extends two meters in 40 milliseconds. You hear the *crack* of air collapsing around it before you see the prey is gone. Then it folds again, and for an hour you will hear the slow, deliberate sound of chitin being rasped inside a body that has not moved.

There are a dozen variations on this theme. Root-borers as thick as your thigh that spend ten years as grubs chewing spirals inside a living bough, their tunnels ventilated by the Holdfast's own breathing, until they pupate into something with wings like hot tin. Bark-mimic skinks six feet long with suction-cup toes and a dewlap they inflate to a drum for a single, concussive *thump* that stuns glasswings out of the air. All of them lean, evolved, toward stillness, toward patience, toward making a virtue of the fact that there is nowhere to hide except by being the place itself.

### The Sailors

Nothing walks between Holdfasts. To try would be to fall for half a mile and then hit buried stone no one has touched in millennia.

The air between is a country, and it has its own fauna. They are the only things here that look abundant, because the thermals force them into the same corridors.

The gliders are not birds. They are not bats. They are something older that kept the membrane. Imagine a manta made of skin and hollow bone, fifty feet from wingtip to wingtip, with a head like a hammer and a tail like a rudder. The membrane stretches from elongated forelimbs all the way to hind legs and tail, taut and translucent, veined for cooling. They do not flap if they can help it. The heat coming off a single leaf-plate the size of a field creates a thermal that can lift a ton. They lock their wings and circle, and circle, screaming the whole time.

The scream is not a call. It's sonar and status. Their throats contain paired air sacs that they inflate and deflate continuously, producing a rising and falling wail that Doppler-shifts as they bank. The whole sky between two Holdfasts is full of it at midday - a dozen individuals at different distances and speeds, their wails overlapping into a chorus that makes it impossible to tell how many there are or where any one is until its shadow cuts the sun. They hunt glasswings by flying through the haze with mouths open, filtering. They hunt each other. They hunt the clumsy young of the still things when they try to molt in the open.

When they need water, they do not land. They skim the temporary rivers that run down the trunks at dawn, dragging a lower jaw like a skimmer, scooping. When they need to rest, they hook clawed elbows over the edge of a leaf-plate and hang, panting, their membranes fluttering like laundry.

Below them, smaller sailors - needlewasps the size of hawks, bladder-floaters that are little more than a gas sac with trailing tentacles - drift. Nothing here flies *from* tree to tree. It flies *in* the weather the trees make.

### The Meadow and River People

Where water stays for more than an hour, life explodes, then compresses, then waits to explode again.

In every crotch where two boughs meet, in every healed-over hollow, there is a hanging meadow: a mattress of composted leaf-plate, aerial root felt, and black peat a meter deep that never touches stone. It is a swamp in the sky. Tubers swell there - the parasitic orchids the humans farm, pale and potato-like. But also naturally: pitcher-leaves big enough to drown a child, filled with digestive rainwater; mats of liverwort that close when touched; colonies of thumb-sized amphibians with translucent skin and throats that inflate to the size of their bodies, who spend the dry hours sealed in mucus cocoons and emerge only during the ten-minute local rain to breed in a frenzy, laying black strings of eggs in bark-canyons that will be gone by noon tomorrow. Their tadpoles have to hatch, metamorphose, and dig in within 24 hours or desiccate to a crack. Most don't. The meadow is littered with their mummified failures, which become peat.

Lower, in the hollow buttresses where the Holdfast's own breathing has left a tube, runs the wood-river. It is slow, brown, tannin-stained, roofed in living wood with slit windows where light bars cut through. Here the fauna is blind and pallid. Pale filter-worms the length of your arm anchor to the wood and wave frilled mouths. Flat, eyeless fish with whiskers evolved from some trapped river ancestor scrape algae from the walls. They line up at the windows to drink light. And things come to drink: chord beetles lowering proboscises, gliders folding wings to sip on the wing, mantids drinking without moving their camouflage. For an hour at dawn, the river-in-wood is the only crowded place on the continent.

### The Humans

They are not many. If you count, you find maybe thirty souls to a Holdfast, maybe fewer. A village is a family, plus a cousin's family on the next bough over, connected by a swaying bridge of braided root that no elder likes to cross when the thermals are up.

You recognize them by what is healed.

Their homes are hollows scooped with chert adze and fire into the living wood, never deeper than a man is tall - deeper and the Holdfast seals it with resin or floods it with defensive sap that burns the skin. Around each hollow the wood has swollen into a smooth, raised lip, a scar that says *we have been here long enough for the tree to forgive us*. Roofs are cured leaf-plate - cut only when a plate has yellowed and dropped naturally - stitched with vine fiber and weighted with stone. Nothing is nailed. Nails would be an insult.

They are short, heat-tanned to leather, lean as the grubs they prize. Children have perfect hearing. Adults by thirty have a notch of deafness right at the chord beetle's frequency; they shout at each other even when standing close, and at night they speak with hands, a fluent sign language that can be read across a windy gap. Their chests are broad. High altitude, thin air, but they have lived here long enough that their blood carries more.

Their day is water. Before dawn, the women and older children walk the bark-canyons with baskets lined with wax, scraping condensation with leaf scoops into gourds, wringing meadow-sponge. By 9am it's over. The men check the orchid tubers, pry fat white root-borers from tunnels with fire-hardened spears - one grub feeds four - and watch the sky from the leaf-edge tower. That boy with the bone horn is not ceremonial. When a Holdfast two valleys over exhales its spore cloud and births a black localized storm, he blows a low note that carries *under* the insect chorus, and everyone knows to cover the meadows and bring the baskets.

They do not think the Holdfast walks. They know it does. A grandmother will show you the petroglyph she pecked as a girl on a buttress that is now three meters to the left of where it was, the lines stretched by growth. They do not fear the breathing fissures; they press their palms to them at noon to feel if the tree is sick. They bury their dead not in stone they cannot reach but in a hanging meadow, where the body becomes peat within a season.

They are not masters here. They are not even tenants. They are a small, careful lichen on a continent that is learning, very slowly, to walk, to breathe, to make its own rain. They keep their fires small, their voices low, their bridges narrow, and they listen more than they look - because in a place made entirely of plant, where there is no ground to run on and no horizon that is not another living wall, the only way to survive is to be as sparse and as loud and as still as everything else that has succeeded here.
