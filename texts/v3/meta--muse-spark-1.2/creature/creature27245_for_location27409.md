They do not live there the way people live in a town.

At first you think they are masons. Then you think they are monks. Then you think they are caretakers, or maybe captives who have learned to love the terms of their captivity. All of those are true for a few minutes and then none of them hold. You have to watch longer, until your eyes stop looking for a finished thing and start seeing a process that has a body.

The humans are the least obvious inhabitants, even though they built everything you can see.

You miss them at first because they wear the hollow. Their clothing is undyed, unbleached — wool and heavy woven fiber the exact color of damp limestone and dry limestone and limestone dust, layered and patched until a person standing still against a wall becomes a slightly warmer arrangement of shadow and stone. Their skin, too, has gone the way of the basin. Not healthy tanned or cave-pale, but a cool pallor, matte and a little bloodless, as if the doubled light bleached them and the cool air preserved them. Their eyes are light — grey, pale amber, washed blue — narrowed against the constant brightness, lidded against stone grit. They squint even in shade. The children are born squinting.

They are quiet in a way that is not shyness. Their metabolisms seem to have slowed to match the stone. No one raises their voice because no one needs to; sound dies a few feet from your mouth in that porous, damp air. So they speak rarely, and softly, and much of what they say is said with hands. A flat palm held out: *pause, wait, the water is going this way*. Two fingers tapping a block: *this one is loose, this one wants to move*. A whole conversation can happen while passing a stone from shoulder to shoulder without a word, a chain of people who know where the stone is going without being told.

They are not thin, but they are spare. The cool never leaves — even at noon when the light is brutal, the stone under your hand is cool, and the air has that cellar-breath that kisses sweat off you before you make it. So they move without sweating, without hurry, with an economy that looks like slowness until you realize how much ground they cover. Men and women and old people and children all carry. A seven-year-old carries a head-sized block on her hip like you would carry a basket of bread, her arms chalked white to the elbow. An old man with a braided cord measures the camber of a street for the third time that morning, not because he forgot but because the street will have changed by afternoon. Their hands are the only thing about them that looks aged prematurely — broad, flat, callused into stone themselves, nails short and rimmed black with basalt dust that never washes out.

They don't own. That is the first human wrongness you feel.

No house has a door that locks. No house, really, is *a* house for long. A family will build a thick-walled rectangle with a timber lintel and a perfectly trenched runnel for the seep, and live in it for a week or a month, and then, without argument or ceremony, they will take it apart. Not destroyed — *unbuilt*. Each block brushed clean, each timber stacked, each iron bracket pried gently and kept. They carry it ten meters or a hundred and build something else. Sometimes the same blocks become a neighbor's house. Sometimes they become a low wall that impounds nothing and shelters nothing and is taken down two days later anyway. 

It isn't nomadism. Nomads leave a place. These people leave a *shape*.

If you ask — and you would have to ask quietly, and be invited to hear the answer — they will not say they are rebuilding because the water tells them to, or because the walls slump, or because the city is falling down. Though all those things are true. The seep moves. After a rain the braided silver threads cut new courses and a foundation that was dry yesterday wakes up with a dark stain spreading across its base. Better to have moved already. The great terraces, for all their ancient smoothness, still settle a millimeter a year. Better to keep the stones listening.

But the deeper answer is in the rule you felt but didn't hear spoken: *Be still and you will be built around.* Stillness here is an invitation to be covered. Permanence is the only real death. So they keep the city in a state of controlled breathing — inhale, a wall goes up; exhale, it comes down. The turbulence is not anxiety. It is how they pray, how they remember, how they keep the hollow from deciding they are rubble.

And they are not alone in that practice.

Because for every human hand carrying a block, there are a hundred other hands, feet, cilia, wings doing the same work on a different scale.

The base of everything — the thing everything else is really eating, since there is no green — is not visible until you kneel. That white and faintly pink salt-frost that blooms on the stone where water has evaporated is not just mineral. It's alive. A bacterial mat, thin as paper, faintly sour to the smell, that fixes whatever trace comes off the limestone and the seep itself. It glitters because it is half crystal. The humans do not farm it, exactly, but they tend it by not scraping it clean. They leave certain pans and lintels alone for weeks so the crust can thicken.

And then come the grazers.

The pale, hand-sized arthropods you thought were just movement at the edge of vision — low, blind, translucent white with backs like damp pill bugs — move in single-file processions along the runnels with a purpose as methodical as the humans'. They comb the crust with feathery mouthparts, harvesting the mat and leaving a faintly polished trail. Behind them, the larger slug-things, forearm-long, the color of skim milk, ooze across the wider pans and vacuum the salt with a soft, continuous rasp you can hear if you hold your breath. They are slow, deaf, utterly indifferent. A human will pause mid-step to let a grazer pass, not out of reverence but out of habit — you don't step on the herd.

The humans eat them. Not all of them, not often. You see it at midday when the work pauses without a signal: someone crouches by a pan, picks up a grazer the size of a loaf, and with a practiced twist separates the mineral-heavy foot from the clean upper flesh, which they eat raw. It is pale and salty. The children chew on strips of it dried on the warmest stones. The leathery fliers — those silent wings that circle the bright column all day — are also harvested. Not hunted. Collected. They roost on the walls in great overlapping folds, hundreds together, breathing so slowly you can barely see it, and the people climb the old scaffolds and take the fallen ones, or the addled young that never learned to cling, and carry them down folded carefully like laundry.

In return, nothing hunts the humans. Not really. There are things that wait with open mouths — the anemone-like filterers in the stronger seeps, pink and yellow, opening like lungs when the water runs — but they are filtering water, not flesh. There is no chase in the hollow. Even predation is patient. The largest fliers, doorway-wide, drift overhead for hours and then simply descend vertically onto a grazer and enfold it. No screech, no struggle you can hear. The grazer just stops. The humans watch this and keep working.

At night, when the sourceless afterglow of the stone fades and true dark comes for a few hours, you understand the last thing about whoever lives here.

They all glow, a little.

The fliers' bellies. The grazer trails. The mineral crust itself, holding the sun a fraction too long. And the humans — if you stay up — you see them moving still, dimly luminous with stone dust on their clothes and sweat, carrying a block from one place to another by the light of the rock itself, like blood cells in a body too big to be seen at once.

That is what lives here: not a population, but a tissue.

Human and arthropod and slug and filterer and mat and stone — all of them doing the same work. Taking apart and putting together, eating the crust and excreting the crust, carrying a block ten feet to the left because the water will want it there tomorrow.

No one is in charge. No one is finished. If you stand still long enough on one of those wide, cool terraces, someone will gently, without asking, begin to build a low wall around your feet, not to trap you but to include you while you are there.
