Nothing lives here the way you expect a thing to live.

At first you think the room is empty. That is its first defense — the sparsity. You stand on the warm, ticking slag and your eye slides over five meters of rust-red nothing, ten meters of black glass nothing, and you register absence. The quiet is so complete and the heat so still that absence feels like the law of the place. No buzz, no scuttle, no print in the salt. 

Then one of the absences resolves.

About fifteen feet off, exactly where you were sure there was only stone, a shape that was stone a moment ago decides to be a plant. It doesn't move. You just finally see it correctly.

That is what lives here.

Not many of them. Not a population. A dozen presences, maybe a few more if you count the ones that haven't finished happening yet. Each one alone is enough to fill the room. Each one is extravagantly, unreasonably alive in a place that has no business supporting life, and then there is a long, hot interval of nothing before the next one, as if the pocket can only afford one miracle at a time.

They are not green. Green would be a lie here.

From a central knuckle — not a trunk, not a crown, a knuckle of folded matter the color of kiln-fired clay and dark bronze — they throw up fronds that look manufactured. You think at first someone has left sheets of patinated copper here, malachite and verdigris hammered thin and oiled. The blades are broad, waxy, heavy. They droop under their own weight, not wilting, just burdened by the fluid inside them. The fluid is not water. You can see it when the noon light falls straight down and pools. The light hits a frond and makes it translucent and you see the liquid shift inside, slow, viscous as brine or pale honey or low-grade oil. It moves a second after the wind touches the blade, a slow slosh you can hear if you are close enough — *thup* — like oil in a drum.

They root without soil. Their roots are not roots. They are splayed wires of bare copper and white salt, fused directly into the hot scoria, into the pores of the slag itself. There is no dark earth around them, no mat of humus. The rock goes right up to the knuckle and then becomes the plant. If you tried to pull one you would have to break the mountain.

They don't drink. There is nothing to drink. The air is kiln-dry, twenty degrees hotter than it should be at this altitude, mineral and iron and ozone, salt without a sea. The dryness pulls the water off your lips and they do not care. They make their own wet. They breathe in heat and stone-vapor and exhale a faint metallic mist that hangs around each frond for a foot or so before it vanishes. You only know it was there because leeward of every plant the slag is crusted with a fine white salt, a pale tide-mark where their breath has precipitated. The crust crackles faintly under your boots.

The young ones live differently, or rather, they are still becoming. Near the feet of the large ones, or sometimes alone in the middle of a bare pan of scoria where nothing should be, there is a tight fist of ceramic-looking leaves, folded so perfectly you think it's a man-made thing. It hisses. Very quietly, continuously, a sound like a kettle just before it whistles. It is assembling itself out of air, out of the hot vapor that hangs dense and motionless in the pocket. It doesn't unfold. It extrudes. A millimeter a day, a millimeter a week — you can't tell until you look away and back and see a new edge has unrolled, glossy and wet-looking with that same oil-brine. They don't grow toward the sun. None of them do.

They all face the thud.

Deep below the floor, below the slag that still gives faintly under your boots and ticks as it contracts, something turns over. You feel it before you hear it. A pressure change in your sinuses, in your soles. 

*thud.*

A minute passes. 

*thud.*

So low it is almost not sound. When it happens the light pooled in the corners of the room wavers. Shadows lag a half-second behind the things that cast them, then catch up. The fronds, even the hissing young ones, are all canted, oriented toward that subsonic heart. The sun is directly overhead, brutal and bleached at this height, but they ignore it. Their god is underneath.

That is the other thing that lives here, and it is larger than the plants. The plants are its organs.

The pocket itself is alive, not as metaphor. The eastern wall — rawest, still showing the hackles and conchoidal fractures where the rock split while still molten and never weathered — is not a wall. It is a flank. In it, basalt columns that look like natural jointing are pistons. They are part of the rock and they move. So slowly you think heat is making you hallucinate. Then a seam that was sealed opens a finger's width and exhales a breath of sulfurous heat that makes the nearest frond shudder, and you understand the room is breathing. Smooth tubes of black glass run down the walls like stalactites that are not stalactites, pipes that were never built. Every few hours a bead of molten mineral gathers at a tip, glows amber, stretches, falls, and hardens with a soft *tick* on a ledge below. The plants nearest those drips are monstrous, their malachite blades stained black with fallout, heavy beyond bearing.

The rules here are not broken, just not sealed shut. Gravity is wrong by a fraction — too weak at the lip, so dust hangs too long, too strong at the floor, so it hits hard when it finally lands. Sound is swallowed by the porous stone and the dense hot air, so even the great sigh of the piston wall sounds like it originates inside your ear. The plants creak as they expand, a ceramic creak.

They do not need you. They do not need insects, birds, pollinators. The absence of animals here is absolute — no hum, no guano, no chitin, no track. If anything flew over the lip it would find no water and leave. The fronds reproduce by shivering. When the heat is right, when the interval between thuds shortens for a while, a large one will tremble — all its heavy blades shaking at once though there is no wind — and a hot nodule of salt-glass will drop from the knuckle into the slag, hiss, and begin to fold itself. No flower. No seed. Heat as a signal, as a hand.

They are not growing. That is the last thing you understand if you stay long enough to stop looking for growth. They are being manufactured. The slag floor is still cooling, still foaming microscopically, still being pushed up from below as fresh, embryonic geology is made, embryonic rock that hasn't decided what kind of rock it will be. The walls are swelling inward, a millimeter a century. The pocket is slowly closing, will in a thousand years seal over and become a hot dark inclusion, a pressed herbarium of salt and glass inside the mountain. For now it is open to the sky, lit by an ordinary sun, holding its sparse garden in the palm of a machine that grew itself, a factory with no builder, no walls, no hands.

If you stand still long enough the air holds you too. When you move, your shape remains for a breath in heat-haze, an afterimage of warm distortion that hangs where you were before dissolving. For that breath you are also what lives here — briefly, indistinctly, part of the sparse and perfect inventory.

Then it dissolves, and the fronds stay, heavy, translucent, glittering with their own salt, listening down toward the thud.
