There is nothing that *lives here* the way a thing lives in a place.

That is the first thing you have to unlearn.

Nothing moved in and adapted. No one arrived. There are no natives, no inhabitants, no population. There are no individuals in the sense that you are an individual standing on the rock looking at the rest of it. 

What lives here *is* here. The plateau is not a habitat. It is an organism that has mistaken itself for a landscape.

But if you crouch down and press your hand into the moss — which will be warm, and will depress like a lung — and you watch what moves, what works, what tends itself, you can start to sort the motion into shapes. Not species. Functions.

It takes a while for your eyes to separate them. At first it all looks like abundance without order.

### 1. The Skin

The lowest thing is not a thing at all. It's the moss, but calling it moss is a courtesy.

It lives as a felt, a single wet membrane miles wide and inches thick, made of twenty or thirty or fifty false-mosses braided through each other so tightly you cannot pull one without pulling all of them. It is translucent at the edges, emerald deeper in, rust-red where it is sporulating. It holds ninety percent of the plateau's fresh water inside its body like a held breath. It sweats on schedule to cool the rock below.

If you lift a corner, which is difficult because it adheres to the glassy basalt with root-hairs that have etched into the cooling stone, you will find the underside is not roots at all but a pale, veined mycelium that pulses faintly, warm to the touch. It is digesting the rock directly. Turning fresh basalt into soil without waiting for weather to do it. The skin eats the continent as fast as the continent extrudes itself.

It is not quiet because it has no mouth. It is quiet because it has no need to signal. It communicates hydraulically — a blush of moisture moving across acres in a wave, a chemical flush you can smell as iron and green sap. When a new ridge shoves up through it, the skin parts without tearing and then flows back over the hot stone within hours, blackening, feathering into those huge ferns at the seam.

Nothing dies in the skin. It sloughs and is reabsorbed.

### 2. The Polishers

On top of the skin, everywhere, are the beetles.

You thought there were hundreds. When you stop moving and let your eyes adjust to the dim amber light, you realize there are tens of thousands per acre. Fist-sized, low, dome-backed, with a carapace that is not chitin but a true mirror — a smooth, dark glass that reflects the dim sky perfectly. They move slowly, deliberately, antennae flicking not for scent but for heat.

They are not eating the moss. They are tending the product.

Watch one approach a cooling ingot at the base of a column-plant. The ingot is still ticking, still too hot to touch, wrapped in its peeling leaf membrane. The beetle will mount it and begin to *buff* it. Its underside extrudes a fine, acidic paste and its legs — six, jointed the wrong way — vibrate at a high frequency you feel in your teeth rather than hear. It polishes the mineral nodule until the mirror of its own back is matched by the mirror of the stone. Then it secretes a wax and leaves it.

Other beetles are lapping condensation from the knife-edges of basalt, carrying it in bladders on their bellies to the pothole pools. Others are cropping spores out of the air with combs on their forelegs. None of them ever bump each other. None of them make a sound louder than a faint *tick* when they step from rock to moss. They are not social. They are not solitary. They are like blood cells in the skin — numerous, identical, necessary, interchangeable.

If you turn one over, you will see it has no face. Just a smooth, pale seam that opens and closes.

### 3. The Air Itself

The air between the columns looks empty until you hold still and see that it isn't.

The spores hang there always — a fine, amber dust that never settles because the air is too warm to let it fall. Each spore is slightly heavier than air and slightly lighter than it should be, and it drifts with a slow, spiraling intention. In a shaft of that dim, sourceless light you can see them aligning, forming brief, geometric lattices before the next warm draft breaks them.

Moving through the spore-cloud are the gliders.

They are not birds. They are not insects. They are membranes stretched over a frame of hollow, carbonized struts, like kites that grew themselves. Two feet across, four feet, some as large as a sheet of canvas, translucent and veined black. They have no bodies that you can distinguish from wing — a slight thickening at the center where a heat-organ pulses, a pair of filament-tails that trail and taste the air.

They do not flap. They *borrow*.

Near the large columns where gravity loosens, they simply lean and slide upward as if falling in reverse, then tip and glide for a mile without once beating. A flock of a thousand will turn at the same instant without a signal you can hear, the whole cloud bruising the light as they pass between you and the sky, and the only sound is the faint displacement of warm air, like pages turning.

Their job is courier. Their bellies are sticky. Spores cling there, and the nodules of mineral dust, and the tiny blind mites that live on beetle backs. They carry the plateau to itself. When a grove of column-plants collapses inward and a new crack steams open, the gliders are already there within minutes, seeding the mist with green. They duplicate sometimes — you will see a whole formation stutter and double, two slightly out-of-phase flocks overlapping for three heartbeats before the air decides which one to keep. The gliders never react. Duplication is weather to them.

They navigate by heat alone. In this even light, there are no shadows to trust.

### 4. The Forge-Tenders

The columns themselves are alive, but not as plants are alive. Each one is forty feet of ribbed, fibrous trunk that is half wood, half cooling slag, crowned with a rosette of black-veined leaves each the size of a door. They are not growing. They are being *precipitated*.

Halfway up, the trunk dilates — a vertical seam that irises open with a wet hydraulic sigh, revealing the interior chamber. It is too bright to look at directly. For a few seconds you see a cavity lined with a living ceramic, smooth and glowing orange-white, where air is being superheated and a nodule of basaltic melt is being folded over and over. Then it shuts, and the whole column shudders and extrudes at its base: a new ingot, leaf-wrapped, steaming.

Around every cluster of columns work the pale carriers.

They are the only things here that look like animals as you understand them, and that is why they are the most disturbing. A foot long, eyeless, the color of unglazed porcelain, with a segmented back and too many legs — eight, ten, twelve, the number seems to change when you're not counting. Their heads are just a soft, heat-pitted snout. They run in endless lines along the fissure that runs straight for miles, the production-line crack that vents steam and sparks at perfectly timed intervals.

They carry slag. They carry cooling shards. They carry the waxy leavings of the beetles from one column to the next. They never stop. They never sleep. If you block their path with your boot, they will flow around it without pause or acknowledgment. If you pick one up — it will be surprisingly hot — it will continue to walk on the air for a moment before going still, legs searching. It has no brain you can find, just a knot of ganglia that pulses with the same subsonic thump as the great piston-blisters out on the flat.

Those blisters are the lungs. Ten, twenty feet across, domes of basalt so thin they flex. They rise slowly, fall slowly, not in unison but in a vast, peristaltic wave that you feel in your molars. When one rises, the air around it goes briefly clearer, cooler. When it falls, warm spore-rich air is pushed out across the moss. Put your hand on one and you will feel it is *hollow* and full of moving steam.

The carriers, the columns, the blisters, the fissure — they are not symbiotes. They are organs of one distributed animal that decided to be a factory before it decided to be an ecosystem. The distinction never needed to evolve.

### 5. The Plateau

And then there is the largest inhabitant, which does not move at all, or moves so slowly you only see it when you blink.

The plateau itself.

It lifts. It fractures. It extrudes new basalt without eruption, without magma, as if remembering rock into existence. It folds its own horizon up at a right angle and holds it there until you look away. It makes gravity shallow around the old columns because it is too busy making new ones to remember how heavy stones should be. It duplicates flocks of gliders while you watch because its memory has a stutter.

It is young, and it is already industrialized, and it is already lush, because those are not sequential stages here. It grew all of them at once, the way a crystal grows faces. The ferns cool the basalt so it can be eaten. The beetles polish the ingots so they can be seeded. The gliders carry the seed. The columns forge the ingots. The blisters breathe for them all. The condensation is the coolant loop. The silence is not absence of life but the presence of *total* life — a system so saturated with motion that it has damped its own noise, like a workshop packed floor to ceiling with cotton.

Nothing here is aware of you. Not the way a predator is aware. If you stand long enough, the moss will begin to fur over your boots. A beetle will attempt to polish a button on your coat. A glider will mistake the heat of your head for a column and circle it once, confused, before correcting.

You are not a visitor. You are a brief, cool pocket in the warm air, a temporary condensation event. If you stay, the plateau will not kill you. It will simply *include* you, slowly, without malice or notice, the way a forge includes the air that passes through it — heated, used, moved along.

That is what lives here: a continent that learned how to manufacture itself, and then learned how to cover its own machinery with green, and then kept manufacturing, silently, in the dim, warm, thin air, three miles above a sea it never bothered to make.
