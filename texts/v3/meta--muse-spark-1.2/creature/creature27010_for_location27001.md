They do not live *on* it. That is the first thing to correct.

Nothing here lives on the plateau the way a deer lives on a hill or a house sits on land. Everything that lives here *is* a way the plateau has learned to keep itself — to hold light, to hold mineral, to hold still against the wind that wants to erase it grain by grain.

There is no hurry in them. Hurry is a water logic.

### The Holdfasts

The ones you saw first, the ones that fooled you into thinking there were trees here — they are not trees. A tree is a pump, a desperate straw stuck into wet ground, holding itself up to compete for sun. These do the opposite. They press down, they spread out, they bow.

Call them glass-groves if you need a name, though each one is not a grove but a single being. A colony that decided to be one shape.

From a half-mile away they look like smoke caught and held. Teal-black, rust, a green so deep it swallows light. Closer, you understand the color is not pigment the way a leaf is green. The ribbons — thousands upon thousands per mound, up to forty feet across, never more than fifteen feet high — are translucent. They are not leaves. They are living glass.

Each ribbon is a stiff blade of waxy cuticle impregnated with opaline silica, edged with a fringe of microscopic hairs so fine they look like frost. The blade itself is photosynthetic, but not green with chlorophyll alone — there are bacteriochlorophylls and carotenoids layered in it that turn the terrible, unfiltered light into sugar without being burned by it. At noon the ribbons are opaque, angled to spill light. At evening they turn and become clear, and the whole mound glows from within, lantern-lit by the low sun passing *through* it. That's the double shadow you saw.

They have no wood, no bark, no rings. If you could cut one open — you shouldn't — the "trunk" would be a cold, dense column of felt, half fiber, half stone: dead fronds compressed over centuries, alive with fungal threads, still faintly warm from metabolism, like peat that learned to fossilize while living. It doesn't carry water up. There is no water to carry. It carries dissolved stone.

From that column, the Holdfast throws out its true body: dozens of pale, corded anchor-roots that crawl outward across the bare basalt and salt-pan for ten, twenty feet, flat as ropes, almost luminous. They don't seek soil. They *make* a soil by secreting a weak organic acid, etching a ghost of themselves into the rock, leaching iron, magnesium, phosphate atom by atom. They taste the stone and hold on. The wind that would scour any seedling away only polishes them tighter to the ground.

They never drink. They comb.

All day and all night the trace vapor that rides the high river of air — molecules born in the cloud sea a vertical mile below you, never condensing into cloud up here — snags on those silica hairs. It never becomes a droplet. In this thin, cold air it goes straight from gas to a deliquescent film, a salty chemical dew that never beads, only sheets inward along capillary grooves to the ribbon's core. You can see it if you stand very close at midday: a faint shimmer, a halo around each mound, not heat haze but the exhale of oxygen and the intake of vapor. They breathe without wetness.

They grow outward at a pace you cannot watch. A millimeter a year, perhaps two in a good century. A big mound is eight hundred, a thousand years old. It does not compete with its neighbor. It *spaces* itself. You will find them in threes and sevens, almost mathematically far apart, each one claiming a fetch of wind. When they reproduce, they do not flower. On afternoons when the dust-river runs, they lift from their crowns a slow, glittering cloud of rust-colored spores — each one a fleck of glass and dried cytoplasm, light enough to stay aloft for miles, heavy enough to eventually snag on a salt polygon and, if lucky, etch a new holdfast. That is the plankton you saw in the sun.

They do not die often, or quickly. When one does, it doesn't rot. There is nothing to rot it quickly. It bleaches, its ribbons shatter into silica chaff that the wind sorts by weight, and its felted column remains for decades as a low, warm hill that the ground-mantle slowly climbs.

### The Mantle

What you took for salt-crust, for bare ground between Holdfasts, is the second life, and by mass it is the greater one.

Kneel. Look close at what looked empty.

The polygons — those county-sized plates of cracked playa — are rarely bare. Most are covered in a continuous, ankle-deep mat that sighs when you step on it and takes minutes to spring back. Olive, mustard, sulfur-yellow, rust-orange in great washes that follow the contours of the basins. It is brittle on top, almost crunchy, and underneath it is leathery and damp — not wet, never wet, but chemically moist, hygroscopic.

It is not moss. It is not lichen exactly, but it keeps that bargain: a fungus that learned to be stone, an alga that learned to be air, and between them filamentous cyanobacteria and salt-loving archaea that can photosynthesize in light so bright it would kill a lowland leaf and fix nitrogen straight from the thin wind.

This is the Mantle. It does for the plateau what skin does for a body. It holds the dust. It makes the air taste metallic — that ozone-sharp tang you noticed is oxygen and organosulfur compounds being spilled by a continent-sized chemistry set running at full sun. It prevents the salt plates from sublimating away too fast. Where the wind has stripped to bare basalt, the Mantle is the first to return, creeping as a dark stain that over decades becomes felt.

It has no roots. Its underside exudes the same faint acid as the Holdfasts, but gentler, wider. It etches the rock microscopically and lives off the patina. It contracts at night with the cold — that's the soft, irregular pop you hear after sunset. It expands a fraction before dawn, drinking the direct frost that never becomes dew, the lace of ice that forms molecule by molecule on every surface and sublimates at first light. It never experiences liquid water. It doesn't need to.

In the oldest places the Mantle is a foot thick, layered like shale but alive, dead layers below feeding living layers above. You could walk for a day on a single organism without crossing its edge.

### The Slow Ones

Animals are an afterthought here, because animal is a water-expensive way to be.

But they are here, and the fact that they are rare makes each one complete.

There are Grazers. You will not see one unless you have learned to see rock that is not rock. Low, broad, six-limbed, the size of a tortoise or a small boulder, covered not in fur but in the same waxy, silica-haired filaments as the Holdfast ribbons — not mimicry, but shared solution. Their backs carry that same rust-teal, their bellies are pale and soft and pressed close to the Mantle. They move so slowly you can watch dust settle on them while they move.

They do not graze the living Holdfast — the silica would break their mouths — but circle each mound, rasping at the fallen ribbons, at the edge of the Mantle where it frays and renews. Their mouth is a slow, radular plane, like a chiton's. Their metabolism is so low they can take a week to digest a meal, and their excrement is a dry pellet of concentrated salts and silica that the Mantle quickly undergrows and dissolves. You will never hear one call. In this thin air, sound carries only feet. They speak, if they do, by chemistry — laying a faint trail on stone that lasts for months until the wind erases it.

And there are Gliders.

If the Grazers belong to stone, the Gliders belong to wind.

They are almost not there. Needle-thin bodies as long as your forearm, four limbs elongated into spars, membrane stretched between them so translucent you see the sun through it. No feathers, no fur — the membrane is chitin and the same waxy film as the plants, stiffened with silica at the leading edge. They have no muscles for flapping, not really. They climb by walking up a Holdfast, launching, and then they are in the dust-river.

They ride. For hours, for a whole afternoon, they hang in that clear, fast, shallow wind that comes over the escarpment, making minute adjustments with filament-thin tails. They are filter-feeders of the air: their ventral hairs comb spore and salt-crystal and the tiny desiccated husks of the Mantle's reproductive propagules from the airstream. They drink nothing. They harvest, as everything harvests, directly from vapor. When they want to descend, they simply angle and stall, parachuting with absurd grace onto a distant salt plate. You hear them least of all — only the faintest thrumming of membrane in thin air, like a distant kite string.

There are smaller things, of course, that you will never see without a lens: mites like amber grains moving through the Mantle's felt; translucent nematodes coiled in the etch-pits under anchor-roots; endolithic bacteria living *inside* the basalt itself, tinting it faintly pink, breathing stone.

None of them hurry. None of them sleep exactly. None of them make tools, or marks, or walls. A wall would be a wind-catch, a useless expense. Why build when you can hold?

If you lie on the rock in the late afternoon as the light goes amber and the Holdfasts around you turn to lanterns and the whole Mantle faintly ticks with contraction, you will feel it — the plateau inhaling. The mats tightening. The ribbons turning. The Gliders high above you catching the last gold. Not a community of separate lives, but one slow, bright, dry, extravagantly lush metabolism spread across a table the size of a country, running on light and wind and stone, above the clouds, perfectly content to be forgotten by everything below.

It has been running this way for longer than any memory. It will not stop because you stood up and left.
