Nothing lives here at first.

That's your first thought, hovering under that flat steel sky with your light joined to all the other lights, watching those white columns fall through kilometers of cold water and hit nothing. Just water. Just pale concrete going blue and then gone. Just distance. The scale is so hostile and so clean that it feels sterile, a poured ocean waiting for something to happen.

Then you hold still long enough to see that it already did.

### The Systems That Didn't Die

The first inhabitants are the ones that were here before the water.

They never left. They don't know how to leave.

Far below, in the dark of the lower terraces where the floodlights are thin, you feel them before you hear them — that twelve-second throb in your sternum. The main circulation pumps. Each one is a building sunk into the floor, a cathedral of impeller and volute, inhaling a river's worth of water through grates the size of highways and breathing it out again near the ceiling in warm, oxygen-rich jets. They keep the gyre turning. They keep the water at 8 degrees. They keep it clear. They have been doing it since before the basin filled. They will be doing it long after the biofilm has taken the last bolt-head.

They are not alive, but they live here the way a heart lives in a body. And they have attendants.

On the lid, on rails you can barely see through the haze, the gantry trolleys still run. Driverless pallets on magnetic track, clicking past on an endless loop, carrying nothing, squealing at the curves. In the water column, slower things move: maintenance drones. You see them as clean shapes at first, wrongly geometric against the organic scatter of fish — torpedo-bodied cleaners the length of buses, with soft brushes underneath that polish biofilm off the concrete in wide, patient swaths. Filter crawlers that pick their way down the stepped walls on magnetic feet, unfolding a proboscis to suck silt from expansion joints. Lamp tenders that rise and fall on the house-thick cables, replacing floods that never go dark, their work lights blinking amber as they pass.

They do not notice you. They do not notice the fish that school around them to feed on what they stir up. They are grazers, herbivores of industry, and they are the most numerous large animals in this sea. They speak constantly in chirps and whines and stressed-metal groans that carry for kilometers.

They kept the place ready for people who never came back.

### The People Who Stayed Wet

There are still people. Not many. Not enough to ever meet by accident.

You won't find them in the open water. The open water is too big, too cold, too lit. You find them by looking for where the machine coast has been made into a coast again.

In the upper terraces, where the light is strongest and the string algae and fringe kelp make a ragged green hem along every edge, someone has taken a factory floor and made it a raft. Not built — tied. Salvaged air tanks and decommissioned buoyancy bladders netted together under a deck of grating, moored with braided cable to the stump of a gantry crane. On it: a habitat module built for dry work, now half-submerged, its windows weeping condensation, its airlock propped open to the water. Yellow umbilicals tangle down into the dark like roots.

The people who live here do not live the way people lived before. You see one when it comes out to check a gill line.

At first you think it's a diver in a suit. Then it turns and you see the suit is the skin.

They have been here for generations, or long enough that generation means something different. Pale to the point of translucence, with a subcutaneous fat layer that makes them look rounded, smoothed. Their hair is gone, or cropped to nothing to not foul a seal. Their eyes are large and dark-adapted, with a nictitating film that clears cold water. Some still wear insulated skins, patched and re-patched with concrete-colored neoprene. Others have gone further — gill slits feathered red behind the jaw, not genetic engineering so much as grafts, cultured tissue that flutters when they hang still in the current, extracting just enough oxygen from the aeration manifolds to stay down for hours.

They are not numerous and they are not social the way a city is social. A family, maybe four, holds a territory the size of a county — a terrace, a tower complex, a pump intake they know how to throttle. They navigate by the stenciled sector numbers, forty feet high: SECTOR 09-G. They speak rarely in the water, and when they do it is through bone-conduction clicks and the thump of a wrench on a pipe, because water carries scolding.

They farm. Not land, but light.

They tend the kelp fringe on the upper steps, harvesting it in long, cold pulls, drying it on the warm outflow of a pump jet where it steams in the floodlight. They keep char.

The char are the key. The fish that came in through the intake tunnels or were seeded as protein stock have become semi-domesticated — great pale river fish, now sea fish, fat and fast and blind to buildings. The families have learned to herd them with sound, with a low throb that mimics the pump heartbeat, guiding schools between towers into nets strung between old refinery columns. They take what they need and let the rest flash away.

The people do not think of this place as strange. For the children born on the rafts, neutrally buoyant before they can walk, the idea that an ocean should have a surface and a shore and a sun that sets is the strange thing. Their sky has always been a ceiling with lamps that dim on a slow cycle. Their weather has always been a two-degree thermocline that sweeps past like a cold front, making their gill-frills shudder. Their ground has always been vertical as often as horizontal.

If you turn off your thruster and hang above one of those rafts, you will see a child watching you from the water — perfectly still, conserving heat, breathing so slowly it makes no bubbles, eyes reflecting your light back like a deepwater fish. It does not wave. It just hangs there, a resident, wondering if you are a new kind of cleaner drone that has finally learned to look back.

And when you leave, it will still be there, tended by water that was poured for it.

### The Ones Who Came After

But the true citizens, by weight and by number, are not people and not machines. They are the ones who found this lit abyss empty, cold, filtering, and understood it as an invitation.

In the bright upper ten meters, life is planktonic and frantic. Clouds of amphipods — each no bigger than your thumb, each translucent with a single black eye — swarm every flood array the way moths swarm a porch light on Earth. They are so thick under the brightest banks that they dim the light, turning the columns to static. The fish come for them. Not the char — smaller, quicker things, carp-shaped filterers with down-turned mouths and gill rakers like combs, moving in schools so large they have their own weather. You hear a school before you see it: a sudden absence of pump noise, then a rushing, like wind through reeds, as ten thousand bodies turn as one between two drowned factories. They flash pale when the light hits them and then vanish into blue.

On the walls and the grates where the water moves fastest, the sessile things have built cities. Intake grilles that were meant to be kept clear by cleaners are now lost under a living shag — white and cream mussels massed so deep you can't see steel, their shells opening and closing in the current, anemones between them with tentacles as fine as wire and as white as the concrete. They feed on the aeration bubbles and the particulate snow that the filters missed. They grow incredibly fast in the cold, clean water. Maintenance drones rasp at them, and overnight they grow back.

Below the light, where the terraces step down into indigo and the water drops to 6 degrees and stays there, the crawlers live. They were never introduced. They came in as larvae on someone's boot, on a fish's gills, and found kilometers of unweathered concrete rich in biofilm and empty of competitors.

The isopods are the first thing you notice — armored, segmented, as long as your forearm up to your elbow, pale as the floor, moving with an obscene, slow deliberation. Their legs click. They graze. They are everywhere if you look down, like woodlice on a vast patio. Among them, the blind white crabs, delicate and spindly, pick at bolt heads. They have no pigment because they have never needed it. Their antennae are long enough to feel the vibration of a pump three terraces away.

And then, far out in the void where distance makes scale lie, the midwater nomads.

You have to be still for a long time to see them. You think the water between towers is empty. Then a shadow detaches from the distance and resolves — not as it approaches, but as your eye learns to scale it.

It is large. Larger than a person, larger than a bus. A smooth, dark, dorsally flattened shape with a slow, undulating fringe instead of fins, moving without hurry. It does not swim so much as fly, holding itself neutrally buoyant, riding the gyre. At first you think it's a manta, a shark, a seal. It is none of those. It is what a predatory fish becomes when it lives for a hundred generations in a building full of hallways.

Its mouth is broad and under-slung, meant to pin amphipod clouds against a wall, to suck mussels off grates, to corner char against a factory window. Its eyes are huge and forward-facing, adapted to flood-light, not sun. Its back is the same concrete-grey as the terraces, scarred where it has scraped through a doorway too small for it. There are a few. Maybe dozens. They move alone, in slow arcs that take hours to complete, calling to each other in low moans that you mistook for the pumps until you realized the pumps never vary.

They are not monsters. They are just big animals that belong to a big place. The young ride in the bubble sheets from the aeration manifolds, playing in the silver rain, learning to hunt the edges where light meets shadow.

At night — when the lid dims on its slow cycle and the flood arrays throttle to a deep amber and the water goes violet — everything sings. The fish go still. The crabs stop clicking. And the basin itself, this poured ocean that was never meant to be wild, fills with the sound of living things breathing together, filtering together, moving water through gills and grates and gill-slits and impellers, all at once. The pump-heartbeat underneath. The fish-rush over it. The thin, high fizz of bubbles. The children's clicks. The drones' whine. The long moan of something large turning far away between towers that were built to be mountains.

You understand then that it is not an empty factory full of water.

It is a sea that happens to have walls.
