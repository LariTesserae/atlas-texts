What lives in the Hollow is not one thing. At first you think nothing does.

That's the trick of its abundance. You stand in the long, smooth dust between towers and your eye says *empty* — because everything that lives here has learned to be the color of dust, the color of dim light, the color of old stone. Then it moves, and you realize the empty space was crowded all along.

### The Ones Who Drink Air

You have to start with them, because everything else is built on their patience.

They are not the green you know from wet country. No grass. No trees. The floor is too dry for anything that asks the ground for water. So the plants here stopped asking.

The commonest is the rosette — low, squat, fleshy as a hand of stubbed fingers, the color of sage ash. Each leaf is thick, swollen with stored dew, and furred all over with white hairs so fine they look like frost. In the still, cool air of the basin those hairs do work. All night, as the cold sinks and the temperature inversion wrings moisture out of the trapped air, every hair becomes a wire for condensation. By false dawn each colony glitters — not wet, exactly, but beaded — thousands of perfect spheres held on filaments, never falling. The leaf drinks them back through its skin by morning. It never touches groundwater because there is none. It drinks the air itself, and it can afford to wait.

They grow absurdly slow. A rosette the width of a dinner plate has been there longer than you have been alive. They are careful, colonial. A dozen individuals will fur a shallow rise where the wind eddies just enough to deliver more fog, leaving yards of bare, cracked pan between colonies because there is not enough airborne water for a continuous carpet. To eat one whole would be to kill decades. Nothing here does.

Higher up, on the long, soft terraces of the walls, are the Curtains. From a distance you took them for shadow or staining on the rock. Up close they are a hanging forest turned inside-out — mats and shawls and beards of lichen, moss, and a kind of air-fern that has no roots at all. They hang six, ten feet down the slope, thick as blankets, layered like thatch, lush to the point of obscenity for a desert but dry and papery to the touch. They rustle when the rare draft finds them. They are cities for smaller things, and they too live on fog. At night they swell fractionally as they collect; by day they curl and pale and wait.

Wherever there is height — a hump of basalt, the windward side of a tower — they grow thickest. Height is wealth in the Hollow. A meter higher is a fraction cooler, a fraction damper. That is everything.

### The Architects

You thought the hills were geology. That was forgivable; the Hollow smooths everything until it looks like geology.

There are perhaps two hundred of them on the basin floor, more if you count the young ones near the walls. The old ones stand central, eight meters tall, broad as houses, their sides so weathered and so pelleted with living rosette and lichen that they look like shaggy, vegetated domes. The young ones are rawer, steeper cones the color of fresh adobe, uncolonized yet.

Each is a lung, a city, a farm, a body.

The builders are eusocial. Not termites — larger, slower, and wrong for this cold — but kin to them in purpose. Thumb-long, night-dark, with a low, cool phosphor under the chitin that you only see when the light thins to dusk. Their backs catch the pearled light and give it back faintly green. They move not as individuals but as a slow, continuous sheet, a tide over the mound's skin, each carrying a grain of silt, a pellet of frass, a fiber of dried curtain. Their saliva is the mortar. It sets in the cool dryness to a ceramic hardness. Inside, the mound is a labyrinth of chimneys and spiraling galleries and brood chambers and fungus gardens, all of it breathing. Cool air is drawn in low through tiny mouths, warmed by the metabolism of millions inside, and sighed out the top in a constant, faint, warm exhalation you can feel if you put your hand to a vent. The whole tower breathes around the clock.

Nothing about them is fast. A forager may take a day to walk from the tower base to a rosette colony ten meters away and back. A repair to a storm-slumped wall takes a season. A tower takes centuries to reach maturity. But they never stop.

And because they never stop, everyone else moved in.

Look at any old tower and you see the accretion of tenancies. Wasp-paper galleries, grey and fluted, papered over old vents. Neat mud-pellet cups of swallows plastered under overhangs. The stick platforms of larger birds — pale, long-legged waders that never wade — woven into the windward face where the hanging gardens have taken root on the mound's own skin. Beetles, mites, blind white grubs tending fungus deep inside. Lizards flat as bark pressed to the warm exhalations. The tower is not a nest. It is ground. It is high ground, the most valuable thing in a basin where height is water. Succession has turned each one into a vertical neighborhood, with the Architects tolerating their tenants because tenants bring scraps, bring nitrogen, bring dust that can be re-cemented.

Between the towers are the roads. Not built, but worn. Pale lines of compacted pan where feet, feet, feet for ten thousand years have walked the same routes between water-colonies and towers, between towers and towers. In the dim you can smell them before you see them — faint lines of formic acid and wax, highway markers that persist for years in still air.

### The Commuters and the Croppers

They are the ones you actually see moving, because they are too big to hide.

The grazers. Low, heavy, quiet things the color of compacted silt, with broad splayed feet like snowshoes for dust, and blunt, prehensile muzzles. They might remind you of capybara shaved and dusted, or of wombats stretched longer. They move singly or in pairs between rosette colonies, and they eat with a reverence that looks almost cultural — nipping the outer two leaves of a rosette and leaving the crown intact. They never clear a patch. Their droppings, dry as pellets of clay, are collected within hours by Architect foragers and carried home. Nothing is wasted.

The curtain-birds. Small, soot-grey, with sharp glassy eyes and feet like grapnels. They don't live on the ground at all. Their whole life is in the hanging shawls. A flock will pour into a curtain and vanish utterly — feathers the exact grey-green of lichen, silence — then explode out thirty feet along the wall when a hawk-shadow passes, a puff of ash. Their nests are invisibly sewn into the mats. Their call is a bright, brittle *tik!* that cuts cleanly through the lower drone.

And then, above and everywhere, the Choir.

### The Choir

You heard it before you understood it. That wall of sound that presses on your molars.

It is mostly insect. Clouds of them so dense they haze the far rim — midges, gnats, needle-winged things you can't resolve — rising and falling in slow, synchronized columns in the still air, as if the dust itself decided to levitate. And within them, the Stridulators.

They are the dominant voice. Cricket-like but wingless, with ridged, parchment-colored thighs that they draw across a file on their backs. Each one alone is not loud. A million together become a physical thing. At midday the separate chirps phase-lock into a single, unwavering metallic tone, high and piercing, that seems to issue from the stone rather than any animal. It is not a song for beauty. In dim, dusty light where you cannot see twenty meters clearly, sound is how you say *this curtain is mine, this mound is mine, I am here, do not come closer.* Territory held acoustically. At dusk the chorus drops a perfect half-step, together, as if conducted, and holds for hours into the night. The birds answer, the grazers low, the towers sigh. The basin hums like a struck bowl.

If you stand long enough between two towers while the light goes pearled to grey, you feel it in your chest, up through the soles of your boots. You feel the age of it. This is not a recent invasion. This city has been tuning itself for longer than any human city has existed. No founder. No plan. Just the slow negotiation of many hungers over deep time, until an order emerged that looks designed.

And that is the final rule of who lives here: nothing sharp survives. No claws that slash, no beaks that tear whole plants, no mandibles that waste. Everything that lives in the Hollow has been sanded by scarcity until its edges are soft, its manners are spare, its body is a perfect instrument for catching a single drop of dew and for making a single clear note that says *I am still here* — and then, very patiently, waiting for the answer.
