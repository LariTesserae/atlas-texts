There is something living here. You think at first that there isn't, or that what lives here is only what you already saw — the fern in its crack, the solitary crow, the bee.

That is half the truth.

What lives here lives the way the fern lives. Alone, and widely spaced, and perfectly at home in a place that should not be home.

You won't see it at first because it does not hurry, and because you are still listening with your ears. Here, nothing that lives hurries. And nothing that lives here listens with ears anymore.

---

At first, you think it must be empty of people. The scale suggests it was made for fifty, for a hundred hands to tend the Mill. The emptiness feels post-human, like a factory after the shift that never came back.

Then you notice the paths.

Not roads. Not worn trails. But places where the moss carpet in the northern quadrant has been parted, not trampled, but gently pressed in two parallel lines, about shoulder-width apart, meandering between the turbine housings and the wall. Somewhere the black-green fern has been pruned. Not torn — cut, with a clean edge, where it was encroaching on a pressure gauge. Somewhere a condensation tray that should have overflowed and spread in a temporary river has been tilted, just a degree, so its water feeds in a thin, bright thread directly into the cup of a basin-bromeliad.

Someone still tends.

You look longer, letting your eyes adjust past the roar, and you see that the lushness is not quite wild. It is *tended* wildness. Gardened abandonment.

It lives the way a caretaker lives. Or the way a monk lives. Or the way an animal that has been left to become feral in a cathedral lives. All three at once.

---

It is one. Or maybe there were ever only one at a time. The bowl is three hundred meters across — you can see the whole world in a turn — and that is exactly one person's territory. Like the bee that works a circuit from vine-flower to vine-flower, like the crow that owns the whole updraft, the thing that lives here does not share. It cannot. The space between things is too large to be bridged by a flock. To live here is to be solitary and not lonely, the way the moss is solitary.

When you finally see it, it is not where you expected. Not in a hut, not in a control booth. It is sitting on the warm iron, almost submerged in the living carpet, with its back against the base of one of the slow flywheels.

At first you mistake it for part of the Mill.

It is damp. Everything here is damp, and it is dampest of all. Its skin — if you can call it skin — is pale the way things that live under pipes are pale, but flushed pink at the joints and cheeks from the perpetual bathhouse heat. Its hair is thin, cut short or worn away, beaded constantly with condensation that never quite drips. It wears what was once a work-suit, a boiler-suit, a thermal's garment, but the fabric has gone soft and dark and has been patched with other things: a panel of insulation canvas, a cuff stitched from the waxy leaf of the vine. And over all of that, a faint, greenish fur of very fine moss has been allowed to establish itself on the shoulders, on the thighs where it sits still longest. Not as camouflage. It simply never thought to brush it off.

You realize it is old, but not in the way people outside are old. It is *weathered* old, like the rebar. The edges of it have been rounded by lichen and time. The hands are large, swollen-knuckled, stained the velvety maroon of the flywheels from years of touching rust. The feet are bare, broad, calloused to leather by the warm iron, and slightly splayed — good for feeling vibration through the floor.

Because it is deaf.

Of course it is deaf. Everything that has lived here longer than a season is deaf. The roar — that low, omnipresent, chest-filling exhalation of the Mill and the fans and the torn wind — would deafen a stone. This one has lived here long enough that its hearing has smoothed flat, like an eroded detail. If you shouted, your words would come out thin and flattened and would not reach it at all. It does not speak often. When it does, its voice is too loud and toneless, because it can only hear itself as a buzz in its own skull.

It listens instead with its whole body.

It feels the Mill's stroke through the soles of its feet — that once-an-afternoon completion of a piston's travel. It feels the tick of the pressure gauges as a faint click in its molars. It feels the drone of the bumblebee as a sudden, sharp tremor on the hairs of its arms a full second before the bee is visible. It navigates like the pale moth does, by vibration, by the way the fog moves over the parapet. If you stood still and held your breath, you would see its head tilt not toward sound, but toward pressure — a slight change in the wind being torn at the wall.

It moves the way the place moves. Slowly. Un-hurriedly, but never idle.

Watch it for an hour — you have to watch for an hour to see it do anything — and you will see what its day is. It rises when the fog burns off, not by clock but by the sudden lessening of damp on its shoulders. It walks its circuit. Three hundred meters across is its whole world, and it walks it the way you would walk a familiar room, hand trailing.

It carries a shallow tin, dented, the kind that once held machine grease. It goes to the undersides of the low pipes where the invented rivers run for a few hours each morning and holds the tin to catch the clear water bead by bead. It does not dam or store. It knows water is only a visitor — it arrives as air and leaves as air — so it meets the water where it is. It waters the bromeliads in the turbine bowls, tipping a little condensation into each cup. It lifts a vine that has slipped from its copper trellis and re-loops it. It kneels on the warm, springy moss and presses a palm flat to feel if the iron grate beneath is getting too hot.

It does this not because it was told to. No orders have reached this summit in a lifetime, maybe in generations. The Mill was built to pump or compress or refine air itself, but whatever purpose it had, it has slow-drifted into a new purpose: *to make the conditions for this life to continue.* To make warmth, to make dew. And this thing that lives here has slow-drifted with it, into the job of keeping the exchange honest. Rust and root hold equal claim, and it is the mediator. It scrapes a little rust dust into the crack where the fern wants soil. It pulls a little root back from where it would jam a flywheel that only moves an inch an hour. It tends.

It is not unhappy. That is the strangest part.

To live alone in a deafening, damp bowl on a cut-off mountain should be a punishment. But this one has learned the rule that the plants learned: emptiness is not loneliness, it is scale. There is a cathedral hush underneath the deafening. When it sits in the exact center, on the moss between the two vast wheels, with warm vapor condensing on its mossy shoulders, it can feel the whole hamlet-sized world ring around it. The distances between things — between fern and fern, between machine and wall, between itself and the parapet — are not voids to be feared. They are intervals. They are the silence between notes that makes the notes music.

If you approached, it would not be startled. It would feel your boots on the iron long before it saw you. It would turn its head slowly, its eyes the pale, watery blue of high altitude, and look at you without surprise, the way you look at a bee that has found its way inside.

It would make room on the moss. It would offer you water from its tin — water that was air this morning — and you would have to lean very close, mouth to ear, bone to bone, to hear what it says when it finally speaks.

And what it says would not be a warning or a history. It would be a small, practical thing, in a voice flattened by years of roar:

*Watch. It is about to tick.*

And you would both look at the cracked gauge, and wait, and after a long time, it would.
