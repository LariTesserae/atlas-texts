They live low because everything here lives low.

At first you think no one does.

You stand in that close, green-filtered light under the closed canopy of fleshy columns, on the springy platform a foot above the salt, and you think the plants are the inhabitants. That the place is botanical. Because nothing moves the way an animal should move — no darting, no calling, no breaking cover. The sound is wrong for occupancy. It is muffled and padded, a corn-husk rustle without wind. You can stand for a long minute and see only plant: the pale fluted trunks, the door-sized tongues shingling over each other, the mat of aerial roots combing the air.

Then your eye learns the scale.

The movement was never absent. It was the same size and speed and color as the place itself, so you edited it out.

Crouch. Put your hand on the polished runway that braids between the towers — that little avenue of cemented dust kept smooth as varnish — and wait. Let the place decide you are not a disturbance, just another warm, salt-crusted surface about to be colonized.

First, a file appears.

They are small. Most of them are small enough that you initially took them for seeds or shadows. The bulk of the population — the hands of the city, the ones whose work you have been walking on — are between the length of your thumbnail and the length of your thumb. They do not look like one species, but they do not act like many, either.

Think of them not as species but as castes that have stopped needing to share a genome.

The most numerous are the masons. They are ant-like only if you squint and only in the way a contemporary building is like a mud hut: same principle, entirely different execution. Their bodies are not black or red but the color of the material they work — in the basalt districts a matte graphite, in the salt flats a bleached bone-yellow. Their chitin is not shiny; it is dusty, wax-bloomed like the skin of the column plants, because they need to not lose water. They have no waists to speak of; they are low, flattened, broad, built to slip through a crack between salt plates or under a stitched leaf. Their heads are mostly mandible and antenna — mandibles like fine, serrated shears that can snip fiber, carry a salt grain, or scrape algae without ever opening wide enough to make a click. Their antennae never stop. They are always tasting the air an inch ahead, reading what you cannot hear.

They do not walk. They flow. A single mason alone seems hesitant, tapping, turning back. Ten together make a line. A thousand make a road, two lanes, outbound empty-jawed and inbound carrying: crumbs of white fungus, pellets of damp mortar still dark where the water hasn't evaporated, shreds of fallen leaf-tongue already wilting at the edges. They meet and touch antennae and pass without slowing. There is no jostling. The avenues are exactly as wide as they need to be.

Above them, on the verticals, are the weavers. You mistook them for birds and they are birds after a fashion, but the word is too loud for them. They are the size of sparrows, almost featherless, their skin tight and translucent over a belly that is not a belly at all but a water sac. In daylight you can see the faint amber of stored dew sloshing inside them. They have large, lidless eyes that do not blink, and beaks like needles. They do not sing. They cannot afford song — song is water leaving the body as vapor. Instead they click, once, twice, a dry *tik* that is felt more in the leaf than heard. They hang upside down under the stitched ceilings of living leaf and sew. Silk comes not from their mouths but from glands on their wrists; they pull it with their beaks, drawing leaf to leaf, punching through waxy flesh without sap loss, knotting, tightening until a seam that was two separate tongues becomes a taut, lit room. They work in pairs that are not mates so much as counterweights, one holding tension while the other stitches. When they finish, they press their damp bellies to the seam and leave a bead of condensation that the silk wicks inward.

On the ground, doing slower work, are the rollers — the ones you thought at first were stones. They are isopods scaled up until scale loses meaning, tortoise-sized, domed, plated in overlapping mineralized scales the color of basalt and salt. They move with geological patience along the bases of the carton towers, mouths underneath hidden like the underside of a mushroom, scraping. They farm the city without knowing it. Their grazing keeps the salt walls from choking with algae; their armored backs are themselves gardens, pitted and furrowed to hold dew a few minutes longer, crusted with lichen and with smaller, commensal beetles that ride them and clean them. When a roller is startled — which is rare, because startle is a luxury for animals that can afford to waste water on adrenaline — it does not roll up. It clamps down, sealing its edges to the ground with a ring of wet membrane, becoming, for an hour, a cistern. The masons will come and drink from the condensation on its dome with their mouthparts, delicately, and leave.

And deeper, where you have to kneel and look into the dark galleries that run through the root mat, are the gardeners. They are long, many-legged, ivory-pale, blind. They never come into the normal daylight. Their world is black, warm, yeasty. They tend fungus — not as a crop but as the city's gut. Everything that cannot be re-cemented goes to them: shed chitin, failed silk, leaf trimmings, the dead. There is no burial here. There is no waste. A mason that dies on the runway is not left for five minutes. Two sisters pick it up by the head and abdomen and carry it down. The gardeners receive it, disarticulate it with mouthparts softer than the masons' but more precise, and feed it to the white, brain-like masses of fungus that pulse faintly in chambers whose walls are made of chew-pulp and living root. The fungus fruits in pale crumbs, and the masons carry those crumbs back up to feed the weavers, the rollers, the queen-mothers.

The queen-mothers — if you must call them that — are not queens. There is no throne. In the thickest, most insulated towers, where the carton is a foot thick and the temperature never quite spikes nor dips, there are chambers like wombs, low and humid. Inside, something swollen and almost immobile lies half-plant, half-animal, its abdomen distended to the size of a melon, its skin translucent and veined, pulsing. It does not command. It lays. A steady, waxy bead of eggs, one every few minutes, all day, all night, for years. Attendants — smaller, nimbler versions of the masons with no mandibles at all, only soft, brush-tipped palps — turn each egg, coat it with wax and salt and antiseptic spit, and carry it to its incubation niche where the temperature is exactly right because the living trunk above was coaxed sideways a generation ago to shade that exact spot. The mother is not loved or worshipped. She is tended like a well is tended.

None of them drink.

That is the physiology that unites them more than any ancestry. Whether chitin or feather-skin or plate, every inhabitant is a closed bottle. Their cuticles are waxed. Their spiracles can close tight. They excrete dry pellets, or nothing at all, reclaiming urea as water inside. The rollers store a night's dew visibly in their bellies; the weavers carry theirs in throat sacs; the masons hold theirs in a honey-crop that makes their abdomens slightly distended and amber at dawn, flat by dusk. They harvest water not by finding it but by being it — combing it from the air with haired legs, with velvety root-nets, with the broad, cool domes of their backs. At midnight, when the glitter settles on every edge, the whole city inhales. You can feel it if you stay still: a faint coolness as ten million hairs and membranes go from dry to beaded, a hush even deeper than the daytime hush as every forager pauses to drink the air itself.

And they do not speak.

Or rather, they never evolved to waste water on sound. The city speaks in chemistry and touch. A mason that finds a newly exposed plate of salt does not call. It drags its abdomen and leaves a streak of pheromone that is not a smell to you — you smell only resin and yeast — but to the next mason is a bright line that says *build here, this needs covering before the sun takes it*. The next mason adds its own pellet of mortar a millimeter to the left of the last, and the line becomes a wall, and the wall becomes a dome, and no one ever held a plan. A weaver that finds its stitching place too dry taps the leaf twice with its beak; the vibration runs through the living platform and three other weavers converge. This is called stigmergy by those who study it from outside — coordination without communication, building without architects. From inside, it is just the way matter wants to go.

There is no center to this. There is no edge. An inhabitant born in a salt district will live and die within twenty yards of its hatching place, never knowing that over the horizon the same lattice repeats for a thousand miles. It does not need to know. Its world is the gradient of humidity on its antennae, the angle of daylight through a slit in the leaves, the taste of the wall that tells it whether the fungus behind it is hungry. The continent-wide city is not a city the way we mean cities. It is a reef. A single, low, warm, continent-sized organism that happens to be made of many bodies, many species, all agreeing — through evolution, through necessity — to keep the water inside the circle.

If you lie down on the springy platform and make yourself low, as low as the land itself, you will feel them accept you briefly as topography. A mason will walk across your boot without deviation. A roller will graze algae that has colonized your shadow. A weaver will click above your face, judging whether your exhaled breath is worth harvesting. You are too big, too loud, too wet — you leak water with every breath, you radiate heat like a furnace, you make noise when you shift. They will not attack you. Predation here is quick and quiet, reserved for the soft-bodied interlopers that blunder into the fungus chambers. To them you are not prey, not predator. You are weather. A brief, warm, clumsy front moving through. If you stay until midnight and stay perfectly still, the dew will bead on your eyelashes, and in the morning you will find that a few grains of salt and fiber have been mortared to your sleeve, not as decoration but as repair — you were, for a night, a wall that needed covering, a crack that needed closing, another small part of the endless, almost-silent work of turning raw, new mineral into something that can hold water, and hold life, and hold itself together.
