They live low. That is the first thing you understand about them.

Not low like in a burrow or a pit, not debased or sunken. Low like something that has accepted the terms of the saucer. They do not try to rise above the lip of the basin. They do not look beyond it. Whatever is beyond the smooth stone rim is not theirs and they have no longing for it. To live here is to have agreed, a very long time ago, to become horizontal, spread-thin, and even.

At first you think no one lives here at all.

Then you think - *oh, pigeons*. Then you think - *oh, rats*. Then you think the place is overrun, infested, that the animals have moved into an empty town and taken it the way pigeons take any abandoned factory.

That is not what happened. That is the image you have to let go of.

They did not move in after. They were not an after. They are the town finishing itself.

Start again, slower:

On the roofs and the gantries, the birds. If you only glance, they are just pigeons, thousands of them, slates and dirty whites. If you stand under the flat white light for ten minutes and let them become more themselves, you see they have changed to suit the low place.

They are heavier than city pigeons, and quieter. They don't coo. Their feathers have taken on the exact mineral palette of the basin - concrete grey, rust red at the edges, the iridescence on their necks no longer green-purple but oil-slick black and boiler-steam white. They sit facing the same direction, all of them, like rivets set in a line. Not looking at you. Looking at the hum. When they fly, they don't scatter. A sheet lifts, shows the white sky through itself for one second, and resettles in the same pattern, the same number, filling the same spaces. You count a ledge - thirty-seven birds - you look away, you look back - still thirty-seven, though not the same thirty-seven. An old one topples, stiff, and falls to the concrete and the mass does not diminish.

Above them, the starlings that never land. They are not a flock anymore. A single slow torus turning and turning above the water tower, so even you think at first it is smoke. They have been turning long enough that the air has worn a groove for them.

Lower down, where the water runs.

The rats. You want to call them rats because that is the shape, but the word is too sharp and too dirty for what they are. They are sleek, cat-large, their fur damp and combed by condensation. They do not infest. They commute. They trot in single lines along the hand-deep channels, tails trailing in the water like rudders, from the dynamo hall to the kiln-house to the cistern and back. They carry things: a chip of brick, a dead beetle, a perfect white pigeon bone. They carry them reverently, carefully, to the black water. They have errands. Their eyes are not beady. They are dark and lidded against the steam, and when they look at you it is with the mild, incurious regard of a maintenance crew for a new pipe that has appeared in their run. *You are here now. You will take up a certain amount of space. We have accounted for you.*

In the cistern itself, the eels. Thick as your forearm, the color of wet basalt when they are under, the color of exposed belly-flesh when they roll. They never leave. They knot and unknot endlessly in the foot of black, absolutely still water. Not swimming. Stirring. Keeping the water from deciding to be still. Sometimes a flat head breaks the surface and breathes steam. Sometimes a rat drops a bone in and they take it without a splash.

And then the things you mistook for part of the factory.

The beetles. Iron-black, each plate of their shell edged like a rivet, each leg ticking on concrete. They gather in the warm lee of the dynamo hall where the concrete thrums in your molars, and they click. *Click. Click. Click.* Exactly with the pistons in the long shed - *chunk-chunk, chunk-chunk* - or no, the pistons are clicking with *them*. You realize half the industrial plainchant you thought was belts and chains is actually carapace on stone, mandible on rust. They eat the rust, you see one worry at a flake of orange scale until it comes free, but they also eat the sound itself, the vibration. They are fat and glossy and their backs bead with condensation that never falls.

And the moths and midges - clouds that hang like more steam in the cool air, grey moths with eyes on their wings that are not eyes, that never land, that vibrate in place for hours without tiring. They drink the drips from the underside of every catwalk. They breathe the high thin whistle of the kiln vent.

None of them eat what a thing should eat. There is nothing to eat here by any honest ecology. And yet they are all fat. They are all glossy. They are complete.

Because the other inhabitants are not animals at all, and this is the next image that has to become itself:

The buildings are inhabitants too. The pipes are. The water is.

The hamlet did not house life, it *became* a way of being alive that doesn't distinguish between pigeon and pipe-rack, between beetle and belt-slap, between rat-errand and water-channel. The hum that never peaks or fades is not something they live *in*, it is something they live *as*. The dynamo thrums and the pigeons shiver with it. The condensation beads on the pipe and the moth drinks it and the pipe beads again exactly as much as was taken. Input equals output. The town exhales white steam from squat chimneys and inhales cool drizzle without ever clouding over.

And the row houses tucked against the big halls - the doors open, the laundry hung but never wetter or drier, the kettle forever just about to whistle on a stove that is always warm - those tell you about the last kind of inhabitant, the one that is still mostly human or was.

They are sparse, like the buildings. Not many. You may not see them at first because they have learned to be the right size for the empty space between things. A woman in a worn boiler suit sweeping the same yard of gravel smooth, not because it gets unsmooth but because sweeping is her part of the chord. An old man sitting on an upturned crate by the culvert, watching the rats trot, nodding to them. Their faces have weathered the way the brick has weathered, corners rounded, color honest. They don't speak because the roar is already speaking for them, constantly, evenly. They look at you calmly, the way the thousand birds look at you, as one more sparse fixture that has appeared among the gantries and will now be accounted for in the balance.

They don't age. Or they age exactly as fast as the rust advances to a certain orange and then stops. They are not trapped. They finished becoming themselves a long time ago and decided, perfectly, to stay.

If you stay long enough to be regarded - to be truly seen by the beetles ticking in time, by the rats pausing on their errand, by the pigeons all facing the same way and including you in that same direction - you feel how it works. You feel your breath ghost faintly and not want to leave, how the cool stays cool, how the damp stays damp, how your boots leave a print that smooths itself not because something erases it but because the gravel was always smooth and you were, for a moment, mistaken.

You understand then that no one lives *in* the low place.

The low place lives, and they are how it does it.
