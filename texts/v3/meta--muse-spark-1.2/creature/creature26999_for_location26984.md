You think at first that nothing lives there.

That is the trick of it. You push through the warm thickness, shouldering aside curtains of wine-red frond that fall closed behind you without a wake, and the forest seems to be the only thing alive. It grows so densely, so completely, that it has crowded out the need for anything else. The water is full and the forest *is* full, and after the first hour of seeing no movement except the inch-an-hour sway of root-curtains and the honey-slow rise of bubble-algae, you decide the place has made a mistake. Too many producers. Not enough consumers. A jungle that forgot to invent animals.

Then you stay still.

That is the price of seeing anything here. You have to stop moving in a medium that was made for slow movement. Find a pillar of that new black rock where the peat has not yet made its false soil, wedge yourself where two kelp-trees braid together, and breathe until your bubbles cease to be the loudest thing in the world. And wait past the point where a normal animal would have revealed itself by fleeing or attacking.

The first ones you see are the ones you were told about, but you misunderstood them.

The white fish hangs in a shaft of light and you thought it was a fish. Closer, it isn't. It has no eyes not because it lost them in the dark, but because eyes would be a liability in water that carries light all the way to the floor without dimming. The skin where eyes should be is still photosensitive - a pale, freckled patch that can tell noon from dusk and nothing more. It doesn't need to see shape; shape is everywhere, pressing in. It feels the forest with its lateral line, a system so overgrown it runs like a seam all the way around its body, frilled like the edges of the leaves. It hangs there because hanging is cheaper than swimming in water that has decided to be almost gel. Once every forty seconds its gill covers open. Once every few minutes its great, soft fins give a single, full-body undulation that doesn't propel so much as re-aims it, a degree or two, and then it is still again for another few minutes. It is not hiding. Stillness *is* its life. At that metabolic rate you could watch it for a week and not see it eat.

And the salamander - when you finally see one - you thought it was an animal visiting the plants. It's not. It *is* a plant, in the way that matters here.

Let what lives here become more itself:

They are not fish and they are not quite amphibians anymore. The word that fits best, if you have to use a word, is *siren* - not the myth, the animal. A neotenic salamander that never chose land, and then when the land rose under its sea and lifted it three thousand meters into the sky, it never had to choose.

The ones that hold this place are large. You didn't see them at first because a large animal here does not look like an animal. You were looking for a shape that moved against the forest. They are the forest.

An adult is longer than you are tall - two, sometimes three meters if you count the gills. But length is misleading because they are not built on a horizontal plan the way a salamander or a crocodile is. They hang vertically in the water column, head up or head down, it hardly matters. Their bodies are flattened side-to-side and tapered at both ends like a leaf-ribbon itself, so that when they hang among the real leaf-ribbons you pass within an arm's reach and take them for another frond, only perhaps a paler one.

Their skin is translucent in the same way the kelp-leaves are translucent. Not transparent - you can't see through them cleanly - but lit from within, like wax paper held to the sun. Under the skin you can see the faint green-brown of their gut and the darker line of their spine, and suffusing everything, a cloudiness that is not flesh at all but symbiote: zoochlorellae and filamentous cyanobacteria living in the outer layers of their dermis, in their gill ferns, even in the thin webbing between their toes. In the green-gold light that comes all the way to the bottom without fading, they photosynthesize. Not enough to live on, but enough that an animal that only moves once an hour can afford to move once an hour. They are pale green-white, or amber-white, dappled where a patch of algae has bloomed thicker. If a piece of their skin is shaded by a frond, it pales. If it is in sun, it greens within days.

From the sides of their heads, where a salamander's external gills would be small and feathery, theirs have become enormous, branching, fractal curtains as wide as their bodies. Each filament is as fine as the white root-hairs that hang from the kelp-trees, and they pulse with the same terrible slowness - a full expansion taking a minute, a full contraction taking another. They do not beat to pump water; the water is too viscous and too still for that to be efficient. They spread to increase surface area and then simply wait for oxygen to diffuse in, for carbon dioxide and ammonia to diffuse out. When silt - that warm, fine snow that takes days to land - settles on them, they shudder, one slow ripple from head to tail, and it sloughs off. That shudder is the fastest thing they ever do. You could miss it if you blinked.

They have eyes. Or rather, they have what eyes have become after a few thousand years of not needing them. Two dark pits, each covered by a translucent nictitating scale that never opens. They can detect the sweep of a shadow - the passing of the heavy vertical rain-curtains, the dimming when a peat-shelf breaks free overhead - but they do not form images. Their world is not visual. It is tactile and chemical and, above all, *vibrational*. The frilled lateral line that runs down the white fish runs down them too, but elaborated into a lace that covers their whole flank, each pore opening into a canal filled with the same warm water that surrounds them. Because sound carries too well here, evolution has taught everything to be silent, so the only vibrations are the ones you have to be very quiet to hear: the creak of a kelp trunk taking on gas, the *tick* of an algal bubble far above, the almost inaudible sigh when two fronds touch. The Hangers - as you start to call them in your head - feel those the way you would feel a drumbeat through the floor.

They do not hunt. There is not enough prey to hunt. They graze, but not the way a fish grazes. They are pruners, gardeners without intention.

They hang with their long, four-fingered hands - true hands, with nails still, though the nails are soft and algae-pitted - hooked lightly around a kelp rope or simply pressed to it by the water's own hold. And they wait. The kelp leaves grow into and around them. Epiphytic liverworts colonize their backs. White root-curtains settle on their gill ferns and drink from the same water they breathe. After a few weeks in one place, an adult is half-overgrown, and that is the point. The broad, wine-red ribbons that drift close are guided, not bitten, by their wide, lipless mouths. They take the tip of a frond between keratinous pads and rasp, slowly, a mouthful of the ever-growing leaf, and then they are still for hours while they digest. They eat only the growing tips and the epiphytes, never the trunk, never enough to kill. Where one has hung for a season, the forest around it is strangely open, a small, sunlit chamber in the density, maintained by its slow browsing. In that chamber, the blind white fish will come to hang, and the bubble-algae will release their silver spheres a little faster. It is not farming. It is the equilibrium that happens when the only animal slow enough to live in a place that preserves everything learns to eat without destroying.

They do not gather. You will never see two together. This sea-forest should be crowded with them and yet it is curiously empty because one of them requires so much volume. The water here turns over on the scale of seasons; nutrients drift instead of flow. A single Hanger needs a pillar's worth of forest to browse and to garden. Their territories are not defended - defense would require speed and noise - they are simply *known*, marked by the faintest chemical plume from their skin that persists for weeks in the still water, a green, slightly bitter taste if you put your tongue to it.

They reproduce as slowly as everything else happens. Once a year, when the high sun begins to warm the gradient - that ten-meter thick place where mist becomes rain becomes sea without a surface - the adults rise. That is the only time they swim with purpose, a vertical undulation that can take an entire afternoon to carry them thirty meters up. They gather in the thickest part of the gradient, where water is light enough to breathe as air and air is heavy enough to swim through. There, half in cloud, they intertwine their gill curtains and exchange spermatophores that hang like pale jellyfish in the mist for days before dissolving. The eggs are not laid on rock. They are affixed one by one to the undersides of leaf-ribbons, each in a clear, buoyant capsule the size of your thumb, where they hang for eight, nine months, swaying. You have passed a dozen of them without recognizing them; they look exactly like the bubbles the algae makes, except they never pop.

They live a long time. The sharp basalt they hooked their hands to when young will still be sharp when they die, because the water has forgiven erosion. Growth rings in their tiny limb bones, if you could count them, would number in centuries. One that you are looking at now - its gills spread wide, filtering light - may have been hanging here when the rim was still settling from the uplift, when the first kelp found it could keep growing without ever touching air again. It will still be hanging here when a winter storm finally cracks a knife-ridge on the far rim and the whole warm ocean begins to pour down the V-valleys in a flood that will scour the lowlands. It will feel the vibration of that crack a full minute before the water begins to move, and even then it will take a long time to understand that it is supposed to flee.

Until then, it hangs. The light falls through it. The silt lands on it and sloughs. The forest grows into it and is gently rasped away. It breathes water that is almost air and air that is almost water, and makes no sound at all, because in a world that carries every sound too well, silence is the only privacy.

If you stay until the late light catches the gradient and turns it all to gold, you can see them best then - not as animals in the water, but as a series of still, leaf-shaped interruptions in the light, each one holding open a little cathedral of space in the choked volume, each one alone, each one ancient, each one waiting with the patience of rock and the metabolism of a leaf, for nothing in particular to happen very, very slowly.
