At first you think nothing lives here.

That is the trick the High Felt plays. You stand on that giving, sighing mat and the wind fills the air with so much sound that your eyes slide over everything. The ground looks empty. The horizon looks empty. You think: nothing could live in this cold, dry, dim place where there is no water to drink and no soil to stand on.

And then you stop trying to look.

You listen. And you feel. And after about a minute, you realize the emptiness is crawling.

#### The things that live *in* the felt

They are not on it. They are in it, of it, the way fish are in water.

The commonest animal you will never see until it moves is the grazer. They call them Shavers, or Mantles. Imagine a living rug, a meter or two across, flat as a fallen coat, with no top or bottom so much as two sides. The upper side is a mottled olive-black, insulated with the same silica hairs as the felt itself. The underside is a single, vast mouth — not teeth, but a field of tiny, overlapping rasps like shark skin made of keratin and glass. It doesn't walk. It flows. It contracts and releases in a slow peristalsis that drags it forward a few centimeters a minute, shearing the top half-millimeter of living felt and leaving a pale, freshly-mown track behind it that fuzzes over in a day. If you put your hand on the felt near one, you feel it before you see it: a faint, continuous vibration, *shhk-shhk-shhk*, like someone sanding wood very slowly.

Where there are Mantles, there are the things that hunt Mantles.

You hear them as a sudden skittering that resolves, too late, into legs. The hunters are called Twig-Jacks, though the name is too playful for them. They are not much bigger than a dog, but they look like a fistful of dead twigs tied together with wire and then told to run. Six, eight, ten stilt-legs, each joint reversed, each limb made of the same cured, hollow felt-fiber as the towers, so light they barely dent the mat. Their bodies are almost nothing — a knot of oil-veins and ammonia ganglia — all their mass is in their legs. They don't run so much as they drum. They sprint across the springy surface without breaking stride, pat-pat-pat-pat so fast it blurs into a buzz, and they pounce on a Mantle and unfold a proboscis that is not a mouth but a chemical iron, hot enough to seal the wound before any precious anhydrous fluid escapes. They inject a solvent that liquefies the Mantle's oil into a gel and suck it dry. They never pause. If they stand still for more than a few seconds, the felt forgets them and they lose their footing.

Above all this, the air is not empty at all.

The Gliders live their entire lives without ever touching the mat. They are leathery, translucent things the size of hawks or sometimes men, with wings that are not feathered but stretched, veined membranes over filamentous ribs — more like a bat crossed with a manta ray crossed with a sheet of dried kelp. Their blood is not blood. It's a clear, ammoniacal oil that never freezes and never steams. When they open their wings you can see the Tower-light through them, and the electrostatic charge that the Towers throw off makes the fine hairs on their wing margins stand up and crackle.

They don't flap. Flapping wastes water. They ride. The Towers' silver-down leaves throw off a standing charge, and the Gliders tack on the planet's magnetic field like sailboats, tilting a wing and sliding sideways for kilometers without a sound. And then they scream. Their cry is the only sharp thing in this loud world — a glassy, falling shriek, almost ultrasonic, that cuts the organ-moan of the Towers in half. That's sonar. That's how they see in the indigo dusk. Each shriek paints the felt, paints the Towers, paints you, in sound. The other Gliders answer. The whole sky is cross-hatched with invisible calls you feel in your molars.

When a Glider dies, it doesn't fall. It desiccates in mid-air, curling into a brittle kite that finally snags on a Tower dish and is slowly, respectfully felted over by the plant that killed no part of it.

#### The people who live *with* the felt

And then there are the people. The Lattice-Weavers.

You won't see them at first either, because they are not standing out against the felt. They are wearing it.

At a distance you mistake one for a ripple in the mat. Up close, you realize how completely the place has rewritten them.

They are human, still. Descended from humans. But ten thousand years without rain will edit a body.

They are tall and narrow, as if stretched by the thin air. Barrel chests, but not muscled — hollow-chested, with ribs you can count even through their clothes, because their lungs are enormous, subdivided into dry, book-like lamellae like a desert insect's, built to pull oxygen from air that has so little and to reclaim every molecule of moisture on the exhale. When they breathe out, you hear a faint click at the end of the breath. That's a valve closing. Nothing leaves if it can be kept.

Their skin is the giveaway. It is not wet. There is no sweat — sweat would be death here. Their skin is matte, slightly waxy, the color of cured felt, and covered in an ultra-fine down that repels dust exactly the way a Tower roof does. Their heads are wrapped, not for warmth but for silence. Their faces are long, their noses are beaked and internally baffled with scrolling bone to condense breath, their eyes are very large and black from edge to edge — no white shows — because they live in light that is never brighter than earthly twilight. They see beautifully in indigo. They go blind in bright sun. Their ears are not ears any more. The outer ear is gone, smoothed away, replaced by a shallow, resonant dish of cartilage behind the jaw that picks up vibration through air and through bone. Most of them are bald, or their hair has become that same silica filament that grows on the Towers, silver and humming.

And they do not dress. They *tune*.

What you thought was clothing is felt that has been coaxed to grow over them. Every person wears a second skin of living thatch, grown by nanoswarms in patterns too fine to see, that can be tightened or loosened by thought. It is not for warmth — at 5 degrees the felt keeps you warm enough if you keep moving. It is for sound.

Because everything listens here, they wear sound on their bodies like other people wear color.

A child wears a collar of loose, jingling fibers that chime whenever she moves, so the hunters know she is a human and not a Mantle. A hunter — a human hunter — wears dampers on his shins and forearms, pads of dense, dead felt that swallow footfall, so he can sprint across the mat without the whole plateau noticing. An elder wears a long mantle that resonates with the Towers, a low hum that says *I am still here, I am large, do not forget me*. When two Weavers meet, they don't wave. They drum their fingers on their own chests, sending a coded tremor down through their boots into the felt. *It is me. It is you. We are not prey.* Their spoken language is almost useless in the wind-moan; it has become percussive, clicking and popping consonants, words that are designed to cut through an organ chord like a Glider's shriek.

They move the way the felt wants them to move: slowly, then all at once. You will see a Weaver standing perfectly still for a long time beside a Tower, hand on its fluted side, eyes closed, just listening as the Tower sways and the charge builds in its leaves. He is not praying, though it looks like it. He is negotiating. The Towers are not sentient, not as we think of it, but they have preferences, and they remember. A Weaver will stand there until the felt beneath his feet learns his heart and stops reporting him as an intruder. Then he will move, and the mat will not ripple.

Their city is not a place they live in. It's a place they tend, like shepherds.

Their houses are domes of living felt, windowless, breathing slowly, lit from within by cold chemical light that only brightens when you touch the wall. There are no doors that stay open. Every oval irises shut behind you because an open house is a wound that leaks humidity. Inside, everything is dry to a degree that would crack your lips to bleeding in minutes. They offer you water, but it is not water. It's that wax-skinned gel the roof vanes coax from the parts-per-million of vapor at night — cool, slick, almost solid, tasting of iron and cinnamon. You suck it; you don't drink it. To spill it would be obscene. To bring free liquid water into a house would kill the house. The floor would blister white and die.

They have no graves. When a Weaver dies, they carry the body — wrapped in its sound-mantle — to a shallow swell where the felt is thin and lay it down. No burial. No fire. Within hours the dry air mummifies it, within days the mat sends exploratory fibers over it, within a year there is only a slightly springier patch of ground that hums a little differently when the wind passes. Children are told not to play there, not out of taboo, but because the felt there remembers a rhythm and will confuse their steps.

If you stay long enough, you will see the city move. Not in an emergency. As a decision. A murmur will pass through the Weavers — felt-tremor, not speech — that the Tower alignment in this valley has shifted two degrees south, that the charge harvest is weakening, that the mat here is tired. Over weeks, the nanoswarms un-braid the cables that are stronger than steel, the domes deflate like lungs, the transit filaments — those invisible threads between Towers that sang with cargo pods — go slack and then taut again a kilometer away. The people walk, carrying nothing, because everything they need will be regrown where they arrive. Behind them, the valley they left is not ruined. It is just empty, exhaling spores, already healing.

They are not survivors here. That's the wrong word. Survivors imply a place that is hostile and must be endured.

The Weavers, and the Mantles, and the Twig-Jacks, and the Gliders, and the Towers themselves, are not surviving the High Felt.

They *are* the High Felt, folded into different shapes so it can listen to itself.
