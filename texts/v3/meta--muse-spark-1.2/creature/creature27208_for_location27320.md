Nothing lives here the way you were taught to look for living.

At first you will be sure of that. You will scan for movement and find none. For color that isn't stone and find none. For a face, a track, a nest, a burrow, a call-and-response that stands out against the roar — and the plateau will give you nothing. It is too bright, too loud, too crowded, too wet. Your animal brain, which is a lowland brain, will file it under *sterile* and try to hurry you off.

It is not sterile. It is just that what lives here has solved the problem of living so completely that it stopped looking alive.

### The Residents Are Not On The Plateau. They Are The Plateau.

Crouch down until your eyes are level with the top of a pillar and you can see it properly: the stone is not stone the way a mountain is stone.

Each column is a body.

Not like a body with a head and legs. Like a body the way a coral is a body, or a tooth is a body — a single, columnar creature packed so tightly against its neighbors that it has grown hexagonal to fit. Basalt-colored, yes, but not basalt. When it weathers it does not chip. It exfoliates in smooth, concentric sheets, like old skin. At the rim where your boot rests, the film of water runs over a lip that is faintly spongy, faintly yielding. If you stay still long enough to let the roar swallow your impatience, you will see it breathe.

Not in and out. *Through.*

Water beads out of the pores on its crown — clear, cold, mineral-slick — runs down its flanks in those thousand identical threads, is pulled thin by the wind, evaporates off its sides, is breathed back in as condensation from the cold air, beads again. The same water. The same circuit, for centuries. The weeping you thought was geology is perspiration. Is circulation. The entire plateau exhales and inhales without ever changing its level, because none of them take more than they give back. That is their first law and their only law. Equilibrium is not something that happens *to* them. It is something they do.

They do not photosynthesize. The light here is too hard and total for anything green to survive it; it would bleach chloroplast white in an hour. They drink light differently — as pressure, as weight. Their dark crowns are faintly piezoelectric. The sun, small and close through the thin air, presses on them all day without moving, and they turn that pressure into a slow trickle of charge that runs down their length into the dark between them. At night — if there ever were night, which there isn't — they would still hold that charge for a long time.

They do not grow. Or they grow so slowly that growth is indistinguishable from staying. A young one is knee-high and slightly sharper at the edges. An old one is three times your height and perfectly round at the lip, its top dished just enough to hold a lens of water a millimeter deep that never spills. They do not compete for space because there is no space to compete for. They were born touching. When one finally dies — and it takes tens of thousands of years — it does not fall. It simply stops weeping, dries, and becomes true stone, and its neighbors imperceptibly lean in to close the hand's-breadth gap, their sides kissing with a soft, wet sigh you would never hear under the roar. Then, sometime later, an imperceptible seam appears down the middle of one of the larger ones, and it eases itself apart into two. No crack. No violence. Just a decision to be two where there was one. That is how crowded they stay.

### What They Do With Sound

You thought the roar was wind and water forced through a rock flute. It is, but the flute is playing itself on purpose.

Each gap between bodies is a throat. The wind, which never changes speed or quarter, is pulled through those throats and made to vibrate the thin sheet of water stretched across them. Each gap makes the same white hiss. Millions of gaps make white. But the taller, older bodies are hollow — not empty, but resonant. Their interiors are chambered like a wasp nest, or a lung, lined with fine, wet cilia that still. When the wind hits their lips at the right angle they hum, low and deep enough that you feel it in your molars and the soles of your feet more than you hear it.

That hum is how they talk, if talk is the word. They have no ears. They have no need for ears in a place that is always loud. They *are* ears. The vibration travels down through water film and through stone contact, from flank to flank, across miles. A disturbance in one part of the colony — a change in weight, a cold spot, a foot standing where no foot has stood — ripples out not as an alarm but as a question of pressure, answered by a faint shift in pitch. You will not hear it shift. Your ears are blown out by the white. But if you place your palm flat on a pillar crown and keep it there until the cold bites even through your glove, you will feel a second hum under the first, a brief thickening, as the bodies around you decide what you are.

They decide you are not relevant. You are too loud in the wrong way — arrhythmic, warm-blooded, blinking — and too transient. They have felt birds before. They have felt moths tumbled over them, and those long-legged skaters that live only on the meniscus for a day. That insect you saw is one of the few things that actually *moves* here of its own volition, and even it is not a resident — it's a tenant, skating on the water-lens on the roof of a giant, stealing a little bacterial film before the wind takes it. The moth is plankton in an ocean of air. The bird infinitely high and motionless above is not circling; it is being held still by the same equilibrium that holds the water, a speck of dust caught in a still column of air. None of them live here. They are passersby, like you.

What actually lives *on* them, *in* them, is even smaller than that and even better hidden:

A sheen. On the wettest, most sheltered vertical faces, where the light is slightly less murderous and the water is slowest, there is a darkness that is not mineral stain. It is a biofilm so thin it could be mistaken for wetness itself, black-green and then black again, without edge or thickness. It tastes the silica and iron the bodies weep out and precipitates it back as a faint crust of rust and pale salt. It does not spread. It has nowhere to spread to. It simply maintains. If you scraped it — you can't, your nail can't find purchase in the gap — it would be alive under your nail for a few seconds before the light killed it. It has no defense except being almost indistinguishable from not being alive at all.

And inside the hum, if you could become small enough to live in vibration instead of flesh, there are things that are not bodies but patterns. Coherent whorls of standing sound that have persisted in certain particularly well-tuned clusters of pillars for longer than any one pillar has been alive. They do not eat. They do not die. They are the chord the plateau plays to itself, and they persist because the conditions that make them never vary. Nudge one pillar a fraction of a degree and a whorl would collapse. But nothing nudges. So they hold.

This is what it means to live in a place that has solved itself.

No hunger, because nothing is scarce and nothing is plenty.
No fear, because nothing hunts and nothing flees.
No season, no age, no progress.

Only being, packed shoulder to shoulder, weeping and shining, humming one endless note, keeping the water exactly where it is and the stone exactly as it is, until the sun — small, hard, very near — finally wearies of pressing on them.

You lift your foot, and water rushes instantly to fill the pale print of your sole, and the hum under your palm evens out, forgets you, and goes on being exactly itself without you.
