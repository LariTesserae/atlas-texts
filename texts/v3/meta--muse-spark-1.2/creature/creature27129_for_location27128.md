Nothing lives here at first.

That is your first reading, and it is almost correct. You stand on that warm, newborn basalt under the held late-morning light and you feel the scale of the absence correctly — kilometers of poured, wrinkled stone between anything at all, salt blooming in its cracks, air so dry it takes the wet from your mouth, silence so complete it makes a tone behind your ears. There is no track. No burrow. No shell or husk or spore. No animal has ever walked here. Nothing has ever needed to hide or hunt or rot. If you use the old definition of *alive* — the wet one, the one that drinks and eats and leaves a body behind — then truly, nothing lives here.

You have to stay longer than that first reading. You have to let your eyes adjust to a different kind of lush.

The ones who live here do not arrive. They erupt. They do not spread. They clump.

From a distance you thought the plain was empty. Then the light catches a grove a kilometer off and it flares — not green, not the dull green of leaves, but a lit, metallic amber, a rust-red shiver against the dark stone. You walk toward it, and the walk is long and quiet and your boots make no echo on the unweathered rock, and as you get closer you understand that what you took for a forest is one thing.

One life per grove. Not many.

What lives here is sessile, rooted, and it lives the way a factory lives when it is left on idle.

At its base is the only real body it has: a root-plate, a nodule of iron-black about as wide as a small house is long, fused directly into the basalt where the die pressed. It has no bark, no skin. It is still partly stone, still partly cooling, though it will never cool. From that plate ten thousand filaments rise at once.

Each filament is fine as copper wire, thicker than a hair, thinner than a nail, and each one is the organism extending itself tip-first into the air. It does not grow by cells dividing. It extrudes. It pulls. The warm, thin, absolutely clear air is full of invisible mineral vapor — iron and aluminum and silica made volatile by the heat held in the stone — and full of static, a low, permanent charge that never discharges because there is no water to carry it away. The filaments drink that. That is their water. They draw the haze of metal and charge down their length and spin it out at the tip, a little longer, a little longer, branching without pattern, without leaf, without flower.

Up close you can see it happen if you are patient and hold your breath against the silence. At the very tip of a copper-bright thread, a bead of light, a soft hiss like flux touching an iron, and the thread is a fraction of a millimeter longer than it was. The whole grove is doing that at ten thousand points at once, all the time, so slowly you would swear it was still. The air around the crown smells faintly of hot ceramic and ozone. You hear the ticking as lengths cool, the faint transformer whine that is not a sound so much as a feeling in the teeth.

Fifteen or twenty meters up, where the filaments have branched until they can branch no more, they fray. They become tassels of down so fine they would float if there were any wind, but there is no wind. Copper-wool, brass-moss, steel-thistledown, aluminum hair so white it hurts to look at. Each grove chooses an alloy and keeps it. One will be wine-dark with iron oxide fur, the next brassy and green with verdigris, the next a pale, almost luminous white. That tassel crown catches the normal sun that never moves and glows from within, dense, lush, impossibly soft-looking for something made of metal.

That is its head. Its lung. Its mind, if it has one.

It is not a mind that moves quickly. A thought, if you can call it that, is the slow equalization of charge across the whole loom of threads. When a filament grows on the east side, the whine shifts a quarter tone and the entire grove compensates. When a gust of mineral vapor drifts through — not wind, just a denser patch of air — the tassels lean without leaning, extruding a little faster on one side. It is aware the way a field is aware, or an idle engine is aware: it monitors its own idle. It does not need to do anything else.

And it sheds.

This is the part that makes it *lush* and not just strange. Constantly, impossibly slowly, the crown lets go. A length of filament, a tuft of metallic down, detaches and falls. It does not fall like a leaf. It falls like wire, like steel wool, catching on other threads and tumbling in slow motion to the ground. Around the base of every grove is a deep, springy drift of its own fallen self — meters deep in the oldest groves, a duff that never compacts, never decays, never feeds anything. No insect lives in it. No fungus breaks it down. There is nothing to break it down. It just lies there, copper and brass and soot-steel, until the salt that blooms out of the basalt itself slowly buries it, grain by grain, in white. Production without product. The grove makes more of itself and then lets the extra lie down.

This is what it means to live here.

Not to eat. Not to breed. Not to migrate or evolve or die. The filaments do not make seeds. They do not need to. They are not trying to get anywhere. The equilibrium is perfect and therefore still: the grove pulls a little mineral out of the air, makes a little more thread, drops a little thread, the salt covers a little thread. The warm light does not change. The temperature does not change. The grove has been alive for a very short time — because this whole plateau has only just been made, still showing its press-marks — and it will be alive in exactly this way for a very long time, growing a millimeter a year, humming.

There are perhaps a few dozen of them in your sightline, perhaps a few hundred across the whole small-country plain, each a little dense country of fiber surrounded by kilometers of nothing. They do not call to each other. They do not need to. One does not shade the other. One does not compete. They are spaced as if the die that stamped this plateau had a sparse pattern of pins on it, and where a pin touched, a life started.

If you stand inside one — and you can, the filaments part for you without breaking, they are springy and warm to the touch, not sharp — you are inside the only interior this world has. Outside is the hard, bright silence of the basalt. Inside is a dim, golden, ticking room made of the organism itself, the tassels overhead filtering the held sun into a hundred warm sparkles, the transformer whine now audible as a low, patient sigh. It is lush in there. It is intensely, densely alive in there, in a way that has nothing to do with wetness or green. You can feel it making itself around you, tip by tip, without hurry, without hunger.

That is whoever lives here.

Not a herd. Not a people. Not a forest as we mean forest.

A set of solitary, loom-like beings that replaced what we call biology with metallurgy and kept the other half of the bargain: to be lush anyway. To be many-threaded and glowing and dense where they are, and absent everywhere else. To take the dry chemistry of a newborn, high, held world and spin it, quietly, endlessly, into more of themselves.

If you stay until your mouth is completely dry and your ears have fully adjusted, you will hear it: far off across the plain, another grove flaring gold, making its small electric tick as a filament cools, continuing to weave itself out of nothing, needing nothing else, perfectly at home on a table of stone that was poured just for it to stand on.
