They live there the way crystal lives in a solution — not crowded, not lonely, but *spaced*.

At first you think nothing does.

That is the trick of the pocket. For the first few minutes you stand in the warm, brassy drone and think it is empty because nothing is doing what living things are supposed to do. Nothing moves to greet you. Nothing flees. The rose-white fronds do not stir. The black beetles on the salt spears do not startle. Even the dust under your boots doesn't puff, it squeaks and settles again. You think you've found a beautiful, dead room — a mineral diorama someone forgot to finish.

And then your eyes learn the spacing.

### The Ones Who Drink Heat

The plants are the easiest to misread, because they don't behave like plants. They don't lean toward light — there is no light to lean toward, only their own faint, internal phosphor, the color of cooling embers. Each one stands alone as if by politeness, ten or fifteen feet from its neighbor, roots sunk not into soil but into the warm basalt itself.

If you crouch next to one of the low ones — the lichen-plates that spread in overlapping scales — and hold still long enough that your own heat stops overwhelming it, you can feel it breathe. Not with air. With temperature. The edge of its fan ripples a millimeter, and the warm air around it goes briefly cooler against your cheek, as if it sipped. That is what lives there first: thermovores.

Their trunks are not wood. Put your palm against one and it's like touching a warm beehive. Braided paper, chaff and wax, dry and faintly giving. Inside that braid there are no rings, no sap. Only a dense, waxy pith that slowly converts the 38-degree blush of the rock into sugar and structure, sublimating a little salt as it goes. Their leaves are not leaves at all but aerogel lattices, rose and bone and rust, so light a breath would shatter them if there were ever a breath. They exhale scent instead of oxygen when broken — cinnamon, hot iron, old paper — because there is nothing to rot here, only wax to be made and remade.

They do not grow. They *maintain*. A frond snapped off fifty years ago is still snapped off in exactly the same way. A new filament is extruded exactly as fast as an insect rasps one away. They will be this tall, this wide, this luminous forever, unless you burn them, and then the smoke would hang in the still air for a century.

And there are hundreds of them, sparse and lush at once. You can see through the whole forest and still feel crowded by life.

### The Ones Who Hold the Note

You hear them before you see them correctly. The drone is not background. It *is* them.

The beetles are not beetles the way desert beetles are, armored against sun and thirst. These are dry to the point of being ceramic. Fist-sized, obsidian-black, each one clinging alone to a salt spear six feet above the floor. They don't eat. Not really. They press their belly plates to the halite and vibrate, very slowly, converting the same heat the plants do into that low, brassy Om that lives in your teeth. If you touch one — and you can, they will let you — it is hot, almost too hot to hold, and it doesn't move its legs. It just thrums a little harder under your finger, and your fingerprint comes away dusted with a fine, salty pollen.

A few feet away, another one. And another. The pocket could hold thousands and you would still think it was sparsely populated, because each keeps its exact interval. No swarming. No mating flights.

The moths are stranger. They hang from the underside of the fronds like wet handkerchiefs left to dry. Their wings are made of the same rose aerogel as the leaves that they mimic, dry and leathery. Every three or four minutes, without cue, all of them at once — *tick* — open and close. Not in unison, but in rhythm, like breathing. That tick is part of the chord too.

On the floor, the crickets. Pale, eyeless, legs like compass needles so they never quite touch the hot salt for long. They pick their way across the anhydrite powder on stilts, and where their feet touch, the salt crust re-knits behind them in tiny cubic stairs. They leave no trail. They rasp — not chirp — a continuous filament of sound from a file on their back. They have no mouths as you understand mouths. A dry proboscis that scrapes wax from a frond edge, takes a few molecules, and is done for a day.

Nothing hunts anything else. Nothing decays. The beetle that dies — rarely, very rarely — does not rot. It just stops humming and stays there, polished, on its spear, until the salt slowly grows over it like frost.

### The Ones Who Learned to Be Quiet

And then there are the people. Or what people become after they have lived here long enough.

You will not see them at first because they are sitting. They have learned that in a perfect resonant chamber, any unnecessary movement is a kind of violence. There are three of them, maybe four — you never see them all at once, because to see them all at once you'd have to move quickly, and the pocket discourages that.

They are not dressed. Not really. Their skin has gone the color of the wax paper trunks, cream and amber, dusted over with a fine, dry bloom of salt that never sweats off because they never sweat. They are thin, long-limbed, attenuated the way cave things get, but not pallid. Warm. If you sat beside one, your shoulder would feel its heat before you felt its presence. Their hair is long, uncut, and stiff with wax and gypsum dust, braided or matted into ropes that hang without moving. Their eyes are open but filmed, not blind, but dimmed — sight is not useful here where distance collapses and everything glows faintly anyway. They listen with their whole bodies. Their ears are large, almost translucent, veined like the aerogel fronds.

They do not drink. There is nothing to drink. Their bodies solved water the way the plants did — they closed the loop. What water they have they keep. Their urine, if you could call it that, is a dry white powder they tap into the mortar you saw. Their mouths are small, pursed, lined with a dark, chapped wax. Once a day, maybe once every few days, they scrape a little rose powder, a little halite, a little pollen into that mortar, grind it with a basalt pestle, and place a pinch on their tongue. It is enough. Heat does the rest.

They do not speak. You understand why when you try to whisper and hear your own voice hang in the air five minutes later, a faint harmonic ghost. Speech pollutes the chord. Instead, when one needs another, they tap. A fingernail against a salt spear, a knuckle against basalt. A clean *tok* that joins the drone and decays with cathedral slowness. Two taps: *I am here.* Three taps: *move aside.* A long, soft scrape: *come see.*

They live as sparsely as everything else. One tends the salt-brick circle and its perfect spiral, brushing it with a frond-fiber broom not to clean it, but to remake it exactly. One sits at the loom-frame, adding one filament to the rose blanket every few hours, never finishing, never needing to. One balances stones, wrapping each in wax thread so they never topple, not even from vibration. They do not sleep together. They do not gather. Each has its interval, ten or fifteen feet from the next, just like the plants, just like the beetles. If you sit in the circle, one might come and sit opposite you, not looking at you, and match your breathing to the drone until you both disappear into it.

They are not trapped. The mouth above is still open, narrow as it is. They could worm back up onto the white salt flat. Some did, once. Most came back down. Up there, time moves. Things rot and blow away. Sound dies. Here, nothing is lost. A footprint stays forever until you choose to erase it. A thought, hummed once into the air, stays audible for an hour. For some people, that is unbearable. For the ones who stay, it is the only honest place to be.

If you stay long enough — an hour, two — you stop being a visitor. Your sweat dries and does not return. Your mouth goes salty-bitter. Your ears adjust and you become part of the chord, your heart thudding in tune with the beetles. One of the pale crickets will pick its way nearer and pause, tasting your heat. One of the people might tap once on the salt near your foot, not in welcome, not in warning — just to mark that another interval has been filled, that the lush, dry, loud, still jungle now has one more sparse and solitary thing in it, holding its space, adding its note, and keeping, with perfect fidelity, the warm, breathless equilibrium that has held since the earth hiccupped and sealed this pocket shut.
