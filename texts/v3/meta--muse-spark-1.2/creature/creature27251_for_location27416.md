They do live here. That is the first impossible thing you have to revise.

At first you don't see them at all, and you think the city is an automaton — that it was built to run without people. The scale and the quiet and the heat suggest it. No smoke from cooking fires, no laundry lines, no shouting between balconies. Just the slow sigh of the engines and the tick of contracting stone. You could walk for ten minutes through a dense canyon of basalt and black iron and think you were alone.

Then you catch motion out of the corner of your eye, but not the kind of motion you expect. No hurry. No stride.

Someone turning a valve as slowly as if they were turning it in honey. Someone else standing perfectly still on a catwalk, just breathing. You realize the city isn't empty. It's inhabited by things that have learned how to not be noticed by the heat.

### What they look like is a decision the plateau made for them.

They are still people, recognizably human, but human the way a desert fox is still a dog — pulled long and thinned and refined by the place until the original shape is a suggestion underneath.

They are tall and narrow. You have to be, here. This is not the stocky barrel-chested mountaineer of the cold high peaks. That body is for cold thin air that wants to steal your heat. This air is already hot, already trying to cook you from the inside if you generate too much of your own heat. So they have gone the other way: lean, heat-dumped bodies, long limbs that radiate, necks long to keep the head away from the torso's core. Their chests are deep, but not broad — deep front-to-back, like a bird's, to house lungs with more surface folded into the same frame. When they breathe at rest, you can see the ribs move even under the coveralls, a slow, deliberate bellows. Inhale for six counts, exhale for eight. They never pant. Panting wastes water.

Their skin, where you can see it at the wrists or the narrow strip between goggles and respirator, is not the pink of lowlanders. It's dusky — grey-brown, freckled with darker mineral flecking, as if the iron varnish that grows on the rock has begun to grow on them too. It has, a little. Sweat here is not allowed to be wasted, so they don't sweat freely. They sheen with a thin, oily film that holds moisture close and reflects. Children are born paler and darken in the first year as the controlled exposure to the warm ground and the bacterial air inoculates them.

They do not go uncovered. No one does. The uniform is not a uniform imposed, it is an adaptation converged upon: a one-piece coverall of matte silver, not shiny — a soft, brushed aluminum fabric that doesn't glare in the hard noon sun but throws back the infrared coming off the rock. Loose where it needs to be loose for air circulation, tight at the cuffs to keep the fine basalt dust out. The feet are the most important part. Thick, layered boots with soles of woven ceramic and air gaps. The ground is a stove. You stand on it, you cook. So they stand on insulation, they sleep on hammocks or mesh racks, never directly on the stone. You can tell someone who has lived here a long time by the way they never lean against a wall, never sit on a step. They hover a centimeter away from the world.

Their faces are masked, not for secrecy but for damp. Every adult wears a rebreather — not a roaring oxygen tank, but a quiet, passive thing: a lattice of copper fins and salt-paper membranes that sits over nose and mouth, catches the moisture of the exhale, cools it, and returns it on the inhale. It makes their breathing audible, a faint, wet clicking that you hear only when you stand very close. It also makes them quiet. You can't shout through it. You can't project. Their goggles are large, dark, fitted with side shields against the glare off the salt pans and the hot wind. When they lift them, their eyes are startlingly light — pale amber or washed-out green — eyes that have learned to see through steam and haze, with an extra fold of lid, a nictitating clarity against dust.

They move the way they have to move. Slowly, but not with exhaustion. With economy. A conserve-motion culture. No wasted gestures. When a worker on the high terrace needs a tool, they don't call out; they tug a cable, which thrums that low note through the canyon and is felt in the teeth of the person three levels down. When two people pass in an alley too narrow for two, they don't excuse themselves; they turn sideways and slide past with a practiced, exhale-held stillness, as if the city itself inhaled to make room. Children, who have not yet learned, move too fast and are gently corrected not by scolding but by a hand on the shoulder and a reminder to breathe.

### What lives here is not just the people. It is a way of paying attention.

Because you cannot farm here, you cannot hunt, you cannot gather, the inhabitants have become tenders. That's the word that emerges the longer you watch, more accurate than worker or citizen or miner.

They tend the damp.

Each family, each work-crew, is responsible for a catchment. A stretch of pipe, a grooved basalt fin, a covered cistern. At dawn, before the sun clears the rim and while the metal is still cold enough to weep, they are out with soft brushes, coaxing the beads of condensation to run faster into the channels, checking the membranes for salt fouling. They do not pray, exactly, but they have a ritual attention. They tap the cistern wall and listen to the tone to judge how full it is. They know the three daily mister cycles by heart and stand in them, faces up, letting the eight-minute veil settle on their masked faces, on their coveralls, harvesting even that. It is not worship. It is maintenance that has become reverent because failure is death.

They tend the softness too. They are not separate from the fact that the land is still moving. Every dwelling has slip-joints you can see — walls that don't quite meet the ceiling, doorframes cut with a finger's width of play, iron staples shaped like a staple-pulled-wide, designed to bend. Once a month, someone walks the length of their terrace with a level and a chalk line, marking the new tilt. There is no frustration in it. A lowland carpenter would rage at a floor that has gained two degrees in a year. Here they just shave a sliver off a table leg, pack a wedge under a piston mount. They talk about the rock the way sailors talk about a sea — not as ground to be conquered, but as a slow, warm animal you live on the back of. You don't correct it. You accommodate it.

And they tend the quiet. This is the most social part of them.

Loudness is a literal danger here — a shout can shake loose a slab from the uncaught rim — but over a generation it has become an ethic. They have developed a language that lives below the threshold of rockfall. A creole of gestures, low hums, and tapping. The factory whistle that would scream in any other city is here a single, subsonic thump, 40 hertz, that you feel in your chest cavity. They answer it with taps on pipe. Children are taught from infancy to place a hand on the rock to feel if their voice is too large. At night, when the plateau glows faintly in infrared and the air finally cools enough to sit outside without your boots smoking, they gather not around a fire — fire is wasteful and dull in air this thin — but around a cooling slab, laying palms a few inches above it, talking in whispers that are mostly breath, telling time not by clocks but by the ping of the contracting basalt. *Three pings since the hammer. Time to check the valves.*

There is almost no birth here, and that is part of their density. Families are small, often just two or three, living in stacked cubes that exchange air and water. A child is a tremendous investment of damp and cool. When a child is born, the entire terrace contributes a day's condensation ration to the cistern that feeds that cube, and the child's name is given from the map of temporary mirrors — *Pan Three-Day*, *Salt White*, *Steam Blind* — names that remember water that was there and is gone. The children grow slowly. Thin-hot air and low calories and careful growth. They are old at ten in the way desert children are old: patient, observant, already able to read the wind's hum for dust.

They are not trapped. The long cableways over the 4,000-meter wall prove they could leave. Pallets of finished rebar and sheets of basalt glass sing down those cables every day to the soft lowlands. Counterweighted lifts could carry a person down in an hour. Some do go, for trade or for medical care that needs thicker air. But most who were born here come back, because their lungs no longer work well down there either. They have acclimated to the contradiction, and the lowlands feel cold and heavy and oppressively loud.

If you stand long enough on that warm balcony at the edge and watch without trying to see, you stop looking for a crowd and start seeing the inhabitant as a single, distributed organism. The pale glider that folds its glass wings on the cooling tower is part of it. The blind, slow thing turning in the cistern is part of it. The dark bacterial varnish on the rock is part of it. And the people, in their mirrored suits, moving at the speed of cooling lava, listening for the next tick in the stone, are the part of it that learned to use tools — not to change the plateau, but to be allowed, for a little while, to cool with it.

They are not conquering this mountain that was just made. They are learning how to be its first weather.
