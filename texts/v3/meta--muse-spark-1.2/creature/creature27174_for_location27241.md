They live in it the way something lives in a lung.

You don't see them at first.

From the rim you look down through that low white hanging air and the broken green thatch and you think at first the pit is empty - that the machines are running themselves, that the vines are tending themselves. Movement is hard to pick out because nothing moves quickly and nothing moves in a color that isn't already there. The whole pit is greens and wet rusts and concrete grey and amber bulb-light, and they have been in it long enough that they are all of those colors too.

Then a catwalk shudders where no machine should be, and a patch of green curtain parts, and you see a hand.

You have to wait a moment longer for the rest of them to resolve.

They are not many. There were never many - the pit is only a few terraces, only three tiers of old machine halls and row houses and sheds - maybe two hundred people, maybe fewer. Enough that everyone knows the sound of everyone else's boots on a wet stair.

And they have come to look like the place has made them for itself, which it has.

They are pale in a way that has nothing to do with sickness. It's the pallor of things that live under leaves. Children born in the pit have skin that never freckles, that stays a damp, almost translucent white with the blue of veins just under it, and a faint green thrown up onto it always from the canopy. The adults are the same, only their skin never quite dries. Even when they stand under a fan, even when they have been working beside a boiler, there is a sheen on them - on the forehead, on the back of the neck, on the forearms. They towel it off and it comes back. Their hands and feet are soft and a little swollen, the way your hands go in a long bath, the ridges of their fingerprints smoothed, their soles gone white and creased from always standing in an inch of seep-water. They wear rubber boots to a man, to a woman, to a child - black or green boots, split and patched with tape, always wet inside and out - and the tops of the boots are lined with moss where the foot never quite sits.

Their hair is cut short. It has to be. If you let it grow long in that air it stays lank and drips and takes on a sour smell and grows a faint white mold at the scalp that has to be scrubbed out with hard soap. So the men and the women both keep it cropped close, and the old people shave it entirely. The children, when they are small enough to be carried, have a fine, damp floss that sticks to their heads in curls.

Their eyes are the clearest thing about them. Down there the light is always that filtered cathedral green, and the bulbs are never bright enough to make a sharp shadow. You live your life in a kind of permanent overcast. After a generation or two the people have learned to see in it. Their pupils stay a little wider than yours. Their eyes have a habit of stillness - they don't squint, they watch. When one of them looks up at you on the rim, his face tipped to catch a shaft of real sun, his eyes narrow all at once and water heavily, as if the light stings.

They are loud without meaning to be loud. The pit never stops its thudding and slapping and dripping. The pumps on the lowest tier make a heartbeat you feel in the stair rails - *thud... thud... thud...* - and the big fans chop the heavy air and the looms chatter behind walls of vine. You have to talk over it. So they all talk a little too loud, a little too flat, leaning in to hear each other, and they have stopped noticing they are shouting. When they want to be quiet together they don't lower their voices - they touch. A hand on a shoulder on a catwalk means *pump three is juddering*. Two fingers on a forearm means *move that vine before it eats the wire*. Their hearing is not good, especially in the older ones. One ear or both gone dull by sixty.

Their clothes tell you how long they've been negotiating with water. Nothing that can be ruined by water is worn down there. Oilskins that have gone soft from years of damp, undyed cotton shirts and trousers that are always slightly damp and therefore slightly dark at the seams, canvas aprons with a hundred pockets for shears and splices and rags. No wool, which would never dry and would rot. No leather boots, for the same reason. Color has mostly left their clothing - it leaches out in the steam - so they are dressed in faded greys and greens that make them harder to see against the wall of ficus and rust.

And they are always working, but the work is not frantic. This is the other thing you don't see at first - you expect industry to look like hurry. Here it looks like tending.

Because nothing down there can be left alone, and nothing down there can be forced. On the lowest terrace, where the seep is heaviest, a man stands all morning with a wooden push-broom in that moss-carpet that is not ground, sweeping standing water toward a gutter that feeds a pump inlet. The water comes back. He sweeps again. He will do this until lunch. On the second tier, two women on a scaffold are pruning. Not gardening - pruning as maintenance. A creeper as thick as your wrist has found the housing of a ventilation fan and is trying to pull itself into the blades a centimeter an hour. They cut it back with hooked knives, not killing it, just telling it *not here*, and pack the wound on the wall with lime. The fig will be back next week. Thirty feet away, a boy with bare legs in his boots is doing the pollination round, moving down a row of low glasshouses built against the quarry wall where they grow tomatoes and beans and a bitter green they eat every day. The glass is beaded inside with condensation. He carries a small, soft brush made of wire and rabbit hair and touches each flower, one by one. There are no bees to do it. There have never been bees in the pit. He hums while he does it, not a song so much as a note held to match the machine drone, so you only hear him when his note wavers.

They tend the machines the same way they tend the plants - as living things that have to be kept in balance. Each pump has a name, or rather a number that is spoken like a name. Each fan has a person who listens to it. When a bearing starts to whine, someone is there with oil before the whine becomes a shriek. When a pipe sweats too much, someone wraps it. Their hands are all marked the same way: small white scars on the knuckles from sheet metal, the nails black with ground-in oil that no soap quite gets out, and at the same time the nails soft from water, splitting easily. The young learn by standing next to the old and watching their hands.

You notice, if you stay long enough to be offered tea - which you will be, in a tin cup that is hot enough to steam in that air - that they don't seem cramped, though you feel cramped for them. The pit is so dense there is no horizon, no distance to rest your eyes on. Alleys turn and end in walls of leaves, then open onto a terrace you didn't know was there, then close again. But they move through it without hesitation, ducking a low pipe, stepping over a root that has heaved a stair, turning sideways where a building and a trunk have grown together and left only a shoulder-width gap. The pit is not a maze to them. It is a house with many small rooms that they have memorized with their bodies. They know where the roof leaks in a way that will soak you if you stand there, and where the light shaft falls at ten in the morning so you can warm your hands.

They have the manners of people who live in a greenhouse together. They do not own much sound. They do not slam doors - doors would swell shut anyway, so most are just hanging blankets of heavy plastic that hush. They do not call across the pit if they can walk over. They keep their quarrels small and practical, about water flow and who left shears out to rust. They are not unfriendly to you, but you feel, very quickly, how dry you are, and how loud your breathing is, and how you smell like dust and wind, which are smells that have no place down there. The children stare at your dry shoes.

At night - though night is only a deepening of the green gloom and the bulbs burning a little more yellow - they sleep in shifts, because the pumps cannot be left. Hammocks, mostly, strung where the air moves, under fans, away from drips. Or pallets on the concrete that has been padded with layers of old sacking that is itself colonized by a thin, springy moss. They sleep through the thudding. In fact, they can't sleep without it now. People who have left the pit - and a few do, to take the road to the town on the flat - say they lie awake in the dry quiet and feel their chest is too empty, and come back within a month or else never come back at all. Those who are born there and never go to the rim until they are ten think the world is supposed to be wet and green-roofed and humming, and the idea of an open field with wind in it frightens them more than the idea of the walls.

They are not trapped. That's what is hardest to understand from the rim, where the walls look like a prison. If you ask them - shouting a little, over the fans - if they want to fill it in, or roof it properly, or drain it, they look puzzled, as if you asked if they want to drain a lake they live in like fish. The pit is not a hole they fell into. It is the only place where the air is the right temperature, where the light is the right dimness, where the work makes sense. The limestone weeps, the leaves drip, the bulbs hum, the pumps thud, the people prune and sweep and oil and brush each flower by hand, and the whole thing - plant, machine, human - keeps itself at that precise point of saturation where nothing rots quite all the way and nothing grows quite out of control.

If you stay until dusk, when the warm air finally sighs up over the lip in a visible breath and the lights below turn the canopy into a field of amber embers, you will see them all at once, not as individuals but as the pit's way of moving. A light goes on, a figure crosses a catwalk, a vine is pulled, a pump stutters and is hit with a wrench until it steadies. They are the animal the pit has - the only animal it tolerates. Pale, damp, loud-voiced, patient, at home in the bathhouse heat, doing the work the plants cannot do and the machines cannot do alone, keeping the agreement.

And when you climb the iron stair back up, your handrail cold and wet under your palm, your ears ringing a little in the sudden quiet of the field, you understand why no bird stays long in that green, loud, wet light. It already has its birds.
