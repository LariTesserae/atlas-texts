Who lives there doesn't stand out at first.

That is the trick of the place. You look for inhabitants and you see only the mat, only the weeping towers, only the water and the green. Your eye slides over them the way it slides over a vine that is also a snake, because they have learned the same camouflage as everything else there. They belong too well.

Then one moves.

You see a figure wading thigh-deep between two islands, and suddenly you can see a dozen more — not because they appeared, but because your brain finally learned the search image. You thought the buildings were sparse for a reason. Now you understand: the people are spaced like the buildings. As sparse and as intentional. Each person with their breadth of marsh around them, like a territory of water.

They look human at a distance. That's the first lie the place tells you. Closer, you see the edits.

They are low people for a low world. Not short, but *settled*. Long in the torso, short in the leg, wide in the foot. Everything about them has been pulled down toward the water table. Their feet are bare always — not by custom but by anatomy. The toes are splayed and webbed to the first knuckle with translucent skin, the sole thickened into a soft, ridged pad like a frog's palm that grips wet root and doesn't slip on algae. Their ankles bead water constantly. Their calves are faintly bowed from a lifetime of wading. They never hurry the way you hurry on dry ground; they push, they glide, they part the water with their shins.

Their skin tells you the rest of the story. It is not quite skin.

In that honest light that makes all colors true, they are aggressively colored too — but subtly, like bruised fruit. Under a base tone of brown or copper or black, there is an undertone of green you only see at an angle, a dusting of engineered chloroplasts in the outer dermis. Freckles that are not freckles: small, flat lichenous rosettes, rust-red or sulfur-yellow, seeded deliberately along the shoulders and forearms where the sun hits. They photosynthesize. Not enough to live on — no one pretends they are plants — but enough that they never burn, enough that a child who falls asleep on a floating mat wakes up faintly sugared and not hungry. Their sweat smells green, crushed-leaf sweet. If they stand still too long in that gold afternoon light, the air really does try to grow on them. A faint biofilm forms on their forearms, their scalp. They wipe it off without thinking, the way you would wipe condensation from a glass.

And they *are* always wiping, always pruning. That's what you notice second.

Every adult, every child old enough to walk the board-roots, carries the hook. A long, light sickle of grown composite — cellulose edge, lignin spine — curved like a question mark, sharp on the inside only. It hangs from a loop at the wrist or thigh and is never put down. It is not a weapon. It has never been a weapon. In that hyperactive bazaar where a vine crosses a canal in an hour and a wall will seal a window overnight if it feels like it, the hook is manners. You trim your path as you go. You bow to a neighbor by clearing the suckers from their tower's base. You apologize for stepping on a root-mat by hooking away the algae you disturbed. Children learn the draw-cut before they learn letters — the soft *shhk* of severed air-root, the way sap that looks like milk but smells like iron bleeds and then clots where the building wants it to.

Their faces are what gives them away as post-human.

They are still expressive, still quick with laughter — you hear them calling to each other across the water, voices oddly clear in the wet air, overlapping the cicadas — but the eyes are wrong in the way a heron's are wrong. A second, translucent lid flicks sideways when spray hits them, a nictitating membrane. The iris is larger than it should be, often amber or deep violet, engineered for a life under leaf-filter. The ear is small, close to the skull, with a fleshy operculum that can close against diving. When they submerge to clear a canal — and they do, often, disappearing entirely for a minute to wrestle a clog of water lettuce and those silver lily-pad filter machines — they open their mouths slightly and you see the gill slits, three fine pink seams behind the jawline, fluttering. Not lungs replaced. Added.

Their hair, when they have it, is sparse, kept shaved or braided into damp ropes threaded with living filament. Many cultivate it out entirely and grow instead a cap of fine moss or liverwort directly from the scalp follicles, a soft cranial garden they oil and trim. It holds water for them. It keeps the sun off. The old woman who tends the easternmost tower — you think she is old, anyway; their age is hard to read because their skin heals without scarring — has a head of hanging Spanish-moss that she has not cut in decades; small bright mites live in it, the same sigil-forming colony that lives on her house, and she talks to them.

Because that is the rest of who lives there: you cannot count only the soft, bipedal things.

The hamlet is inhabited on at least four overlapping levels, and they all negotiate.

First, the people as described: perhaps forty adults and a scatter of children, sparse as the towers. They are not a tribe. They are not a colony. They are more like tenants who have learned to be good tenants. They speak in a patois that is half human language, half horticultural command — short, tonal phrases, a whistle that is exactly that two-note bird call you thought was a machine, which tells a wall to soften, to grow a little faster here, a little slower there. They do not own the buildings. They *convince* them. You do not order a tower to make a door. You offer it nitrogen, or shade, or a promise to divert a gutter, and it considers. Sometimes it sulks. The children know to pat the walls when they thump them. To apologize.

Second, the buildings themselves are inhabitants. Each of the thirty islands is a thing that drinks and weeps and remembers and has preferences. Press your palm to one and the faint capillary light that blooms under your skin is not a reaction — it is recognition. They keep a slow, chemical memory of every hand that has touched them. They trade water among themselves through root grafts under the marsh. At night they pulse very faintly, respiring. One on the western edge has been allowed to go a little feral — its membranes are thick with epiphytes, its balconies have become nests for the deer-things — and the people leave it alone out of respect, or caution, as you would leave an elder alone.

Third, the tool-animals, who are not animals and are not tools. The heron-assembler that stands for hours in tea-dark water filtering heavy metals with its beak and spreading solar wings to hum — that is a resident with a vote, in the way a town's well is a resident. The dragonflies with circuit-etched wings that catch sun and carry pollen and carry data packets identically. The schools of fish that are really fish plus sensor-filament. The vine-snakes that are really grown peristalsis pumps keeping the ditches from stagnating. The shimmering mite sigil that dissolves and re-forms — it is a distributed mind that the people consult about water quality the way you would ask a neighbor. None of them are pets. None of them are wild. They are colleagues with different metabolisms.

And fourth, the mat itself. The moss-mattress, the root-braid soil, the fern-oaks, the banyans that became architecture. It is not background. It is the continuous bionegotiator that sets the terms. The people cut it, and in return it feeds them. Figs drop the size of fists directly into woven baskets the towers extrude. Protein-rich nodules like peanuts swell on aerial roots and are harvested with the hook. The water lettuce is skimmed, pulped, fermented. Nothing is hunted that is not also farmed.

Their temperament matches the turbulence. They are not the meditative swamp sages you expect from people who live on the oldest ground in the world. That ground says *we are done*, but they are hyperactive, bazaar-loud, always carrying something — a bundle of cuttings, a child on a hip, a dripping armful of filter sludge to trade to a tower for a new growth of handrail. They argue while wading. They laugh loud enough to startle the real birds. Their sociability is intense and brief, like the dragonflies: they converge on a boardwalk that a house has just decided to grow, exchange news and nitrogen and gossip while the wood is still soft, then disperse again to their separate islands because crowding lets rot set in. Privacy is water. You are alone until you choose to wade over.

They die rarely and well. When one dies — and you see how rarely, because there are so few and the green crowds so much — they are not buried. There is no ground to bury in that isn't already root. They are laid on a mat in that shallow twenty-foot basin and the mat takes them. Within days the liverworts have braided over the face, the moss has lifted the limbs, figs send down a curious root to taste. They become, grain by grain, the same black silt that the mountains became, holding a fingerprint. The buildings dim a little for a day. The heron goes still.

You understand then that this is not a settlement on a peneplain. It is the peneplain's latest way of eroding something — not mountains now, but the very distinction between what lives and where it lives. The people are how the low place stays low and wet and alive. They are the low place's hands, with webbed feet and green-flecked skin and hooked knives that never leave the wrist, keeping the wound of the town open just enough that the jungle can't quite close it, and never wide enough that the water can finally decide where to go.
