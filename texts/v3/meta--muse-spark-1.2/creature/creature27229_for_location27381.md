Nothing lives there at first.

That's the first impression and it's wrong, but you have to have it before you can see correctly. You stand on the giving mat and listen for the scurry, the whine, the call - for the animal that should be there - and you hear only the *chuff* of a bark-valve and the tick of sap in a pipe-trunk and the slow drip onto a leaf-trough. No eyes reflect the amber. Nothing flees from your footfall because the mat swallows the vibration whole.

So you think: empty.

Then you stand longer until your own warmth fogs and your own breathing falls into time with the pressure vessels, and you realize the emptiness is not empty. It's full. Too full to leave room for anything else.

What lives there *is* there. The place and the inhabitant are the same thing. The word for it isn't colony and isn't forest and isn't organism, but it has to be spoken anyway:

It is One.

---

It lives low and wide. Not as a creature lives in a habitat, but as a surface lives - a skin grown over a continent because the rock underneath was too new and too sharp to stay naked. It found the basalt when the basalt was still hot in its cracks and it covered it the way blood covers a wound: not by choice, by necessity.

You want to call it plant and that fails immediately. Plants are things that grow *on* something. This grew *into* being something. The mat under your feet - wrist-thick roots and hair-thin filaments braided until no light passes between them - is not where it lives. It *is* what lives. Layer upon layer of living on its own compressed dead, which never rotted, only became more structure. It is soil that decided to be alive, and then stayed that way for thousands of kilometers in every direction without interruption.

It doesn't have individuals. That's the part your animal brain keeps trying to add back in.

You look for a tree and find a column. You think "a tree among trees" but touch it and feel the thrum, the 50-cycle hum that lifts the hair on your forearm, and understand it is not a tree. It is a vertical pressure vessel, a living smokestack, a heart stood on end. Sap doesn't drift in it; it hammers. The bark tightens like a sphincter, releases: *chuff.* Pause. *chuff.* The same interval everywhere, forever. If you cut it - and you shouldn't - it wouldn't bleed. It would vent, seal, and re-braid itself by morning with the exact same diameter, because its growth and its death are the same motion, perfectly balanced. A leaf unfurls in the exact spot the last one curled, to the exact same size, catching the exact same bead of water.

It has no need to get bigger. It has no room to get smaller. It holds.

You start to see its organs when you stop looking for animals.

High above - or below, direction has lost meaning in the dusk - the great leaves hang. They are not for sun; there is no sun to catch. They are condensers. Coolant fins. Still and vast as roofs, slick with perpetual distillation, they exhale the warm vapor that becomes the air's weeping and inhale it again. Water beads and runs without ever falling, from tip to trough to root to air to leaf again. A closed loop the size of a continent. The amber light you see - that tired filament glow along every vein and node, that slow pulse deeper in - isn't for show. It's waste light. Byproduct. The way a forge glows because it is hot, not because it wants to be seen. Sometimes, where two filament-tips have grown too close in the damp, a thin arc jumps with a crackle of ozone. The whole mat shivers and then forgets.

Listen closer and the stillness breaks into work.

The vines that you thought were hanging dead are moving. So slowly you thought they were still. They creep in a loop, millimeter by millimeter per hour, carrying packets of resin like pallets on a conveyor that never stops because it never needs to stop. The bulbous growths near the rock swell with heat - boilers made of wood - and sigh it out into the greenhouse air. Roots etch the basalt underneath with exhaled acid, not aggressively, but steadily, the way a tongue worries a tooth; the mineral is drawn up in a leach and fractioned and shunted through the pipe-trunks.

It is all industry without workers. Pistons made of tightening grain. Valves made of leaf-lips. Power generated not by burning, but by growing.

If there are "animals" there, they are not visitors. They are its hands.

Crouch, put your face close to the mat where the black lens-water stands skin-thin between hummocks. Let your eyes adjust past the amber glow until the mat resolves.

It moves.

Pale things the color of fresh root-flesh, or a little paler, like grubs made of the same fibers as the mat. No eyes - what would they see? No legs as you understand legs. They are flat, low, pressed, elongated along the grain. Some are no longer than your finger; some are as long as your forearm and as thin as a belt. They have no front or back. Ciliated along their bellies with fine, translucent hairs that carry the low electrical hum. They inch along the root-braids, or rather, they *are* the root-braids, temporarily gathered into a shape that can do a task and then dissolve again.

One is tending a valve. You watch it arrive - not crawling so much as flowing - and lay its body across the sphincter of bark. It pulses there for a long time, dimly lit from within, regulating pressure. When the *chuff* comes, it shudders in time. Another is lapping condensation from a filament with a frilled mouth that is also a root tip, moving water one bead at a time to where the distillation troughs have run a micron low.

They do not eat. They do not hunt. There is nothing to hunt and no waste to scavenge because nothing is wasted. The amber light, the water, the etched basalt, the heat - all of it cycles without loss. They take sap and give sap. They are temporary thickenings of the One's attention, grown when a place needs tending, reabsorbed when it doesn't.

They do not reproduce. Why would they? Dispersal is unnecessary because everything is already everywhere. When the mat needs another tender in a new place, it buds one there, exactly the size needed, exactly as many times as needed, which is exactly once.

They do not die, either. Or they die constantly, the way your skin cells do, sloughing into the thatch to become more thatch. Net change: zero.

They have no language. They have pressure and chemistry and voltage. A low throb passes through the root-web when a pipe is overfull three kilometers away - you feel it as much as hear it, a change in the hum against your teeth - and the nearest hands adjust. Thought here, if you can call it thought, is not fast and bright. It is slow, distributed, hydraulic. It holds the whole continent in mind by never needing to hold it at all; each part knows its pressure and its wetness and does its work.

And if you stay - this is the last thing you understand, when the dusk has made your watch meaningless and your sweat has become part of the film on every leaf - if you stay long enough, the mat will try to make you part of its tending.

Not from hunger. It is not carnivorous. It has no malice and no kindness; those are animal economies.

But you leak heat and salt and water, and you make small, irregular pressures where you stand. You are inefficient. So rootlets will find your boot seams. Filaments will brush your wrist, not grasping, just tasting the mineral of you. The air, which has held its warm wet stillness since before your species learned to make fire, will condense on your hair the way it condenses on every other surface, and try to route you down a leaf-groove toward a trough.

If you lie down, the mat will give slightly and return, and after a while you will not be able to tell where your own warmth ends and its greenhouse warmth begins. The pale tenders will not flee from you. One will flow alongside your hand, ciliated belly humming faintly against your skin, and wait to see if you will become a useful shape.

This is what lives there: a single, low, continent-wide creature that learned how to be a factory so perfectly it never needed to become anything else. It does not sleep and does not wake. It distills and condenses and etches and pulses, amber-white, amber-white, in a dark that never changes.

It was young when the rock was young. It will be young still when the rock is old, because it has replaced change with maintenance. It breathes without wind. It sees without light. It keeps itself.

And it has no need for you to understand it.
