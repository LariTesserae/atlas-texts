What lives there hasn't decided either.

At first you would say nothing does. That's the rule the room enforces and for a long few minutes it seems to keep it. You stand in the damp and the ticking and you see only what you were told you'd see: pipes, concrete that hasn't cured, the moss, the fern, the lichen holding on to cold iron. Nothing that moves on its own.

Then you stay a little longer than you're supposed to, long enough for your breath to fall into rhythm with the boiler, and you realize the rule has a loophole. Nothing that *moves on its own* may live here. What lives here doesn't move on its own. It moves *with*.

It is plumbed in. It is rooted.

You notice it first as a wrongness in the water.

The sheen on the slab is supposed to move toward the drain. That's the pitch of the floor, that's what water does. But the sheen here hesitates. In one corner, near where the soft, thumbprint concrete meets the slick sealed floor, the film thickens and holds, even as the rest of the floor drains. It doesn't pool so much as *gather*. If you watch the caged bulb's reflection in it, the amber light stretches, not as a wavering reflection but as something underneath looking up. The black riverine skin dimples when a drop falls onto it from the ceiling, but it doesn't ripple correctly — it tightens, like a membrane startled.

That is its body, or the largest part of its body. Not much of a body by the standards of anything upstairs. It is shallow and spread thin, a living puddle about as large as a spread coat, occupying the lowest tolerances of the room. It has to be. There isn't enough water here to be deep, and there isn't enough warmth to be fast. So it lives flat.

When it needs to be something you can see, it borrows.

The rebar is its bone. Not the rusted, forgotten kind — the bright, fresh, condensation-beaded kind. In the place where the concrete cracked into those sharp, perfect plates, you can see a length of it exposed for six inches, and the water-sheen extends a translucent tendril up to it and holds. Under the mineral scale and the constant weeping, the iron has grown a fur. Not rust. A soft, felted growth the exact color of that line of black-green moss in the failed seam. The moss isn't separate from it; the moss *is* its fur where it has learned to keep itself. The lichen at the base of the coldest pipe is its freckle, its lichen. The pale fern by the drain is not a plant that happened to root there — it is an appendage it pushed up to taste the dimmest light, translucent and etiolated because it has never needed to be strong, only sensitive. If you were to touch the fern — you don't — you would find its stem is not fibrous but slick, and slightly warm, though the air around it is freezing.

It has a face only if you need a face to understand it. It doesn't need one. In the soft concrete behind the boiler, where the wall is still dark and damp enough to take a thumbprint, there are thumbprints that are not yours. Small, shallow, too narrow. Not made by pressing in, but by pressing *out*, from within the wall as the concrete decided to be a cheek, a brow, a closed lid. The wall there is always a degree warmer than the rest, not from the boiler's radiant lie but from the faint heat of something curled on the other side of the curing, holding the shape of the pocket. If you put your palm near it, the condensation there doesn't drip. It beads and holds, like sweat.

It breathes with the room because it *is* the room's way of breathing.

When the boiler fires — that soft *whoomph* every twelve or fifteen minutes — the whole being inhales. Steam ghosts off the pressure valve and the air, already saturated, can't hold any more, so it gives the water back as droplets on every cold surface. The caged bulb sweats. The rebar sweats. The moss drinks. And the sheen on the floor swells almost imperceptibly, fed. The pressure gauge needle quivers and so does the fern, so does the film on the floor, the same tremor.

Then the pipes tick and contract as they cool. The ice fringe in the corner reforms in a slightly different pattern and the sheen thins again, exhaled. The water it borrowed becomes vapor, then droplet, then sheen, then ice, then vapor. It doesn't drink once and leave; it cycles itself endlessly through its own body. That's why nothing ever arrives or leaves except steam and, eventually, you. It has made a perfect, closed economy.

It is embryonic. Like the concrete, it hasn't cured. Like the water, it hasn't decided if it is solid or liquid. Like the room, it was poured here as an afterthought and then forgotten before anyone could tell it what it was supposed to become. So it became what the conditions would allow: cold-tolerant, dark-tolerant, iron-tolerant, silent, shallow, patient. It preserves. The cold that keeps the concrete soft keeps it soft. The dark that keeps the fern pale keeps it pale. It will never be quick. If you shift your weight, the sheen seems to shift a second too late, as if it only remembers to move after you have looked away.

It doesn't hide from you. It doesn't have the concept of hiding, because no one comes here to find it. When you shouldered the second door open, you were the first large, warm movement it has felt in a long time. It listened the way a lung listens — by expanding slightly to hold more of you. The bulb swinging when you left though you didn't touch it is not a haunting. It's the displacement of air when something flat and wide pulls itself a little more tightly into its corner, back around the boiler, back into the seam, back into the drain it never quite falls down.

It will not follow you. It can't. It is plumbed in, rooted, poured. When you close the door, it will smooth itself back out across the floor, extend its fern-finger a millimeter further toward the amber light, and wait through the next cycle of *whoomph* and hiss and drip and tick, holding that precise temperature just above freezing where everything can remain possible and nothing has to commit.

It lives there the way a held breath lives in a chest.
