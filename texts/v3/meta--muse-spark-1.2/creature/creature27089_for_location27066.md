Nothing lives here at first.

That is the first lesson of the Floor, and it is a lie the Floor tells very well. You stand in the cold sweat-rain and the hammering of pumps that never stop pumping and you think: *sterile.* Too loud for birds, too dark for anything green, too wet and too made and too mineral for anything soft to survive. The only green is a stain. The only movement is water and machine.

Then you stay a little longer. Long enough for your eyes to stop looking for a forest or a field. Long enough for your ears to stop fighting the roar and start hearing *inside* it. And you realize the place is not empty. It is just inhabited at the speed and scale of the place itself. Its residents have become as flat, as slow, as wet, and as stone-like as everything else. You mistook them for architecture.

### The Ones Who Became Floor

You notice them first because you almost tripped on one.

Not far from where you stand, there is a low swell in the Floor that you took for a settling hump, a place where concrete has buckled up an inch or two over centuries. It is about as wide as a small house. Its surface is not concrete. Not exactly. It is covered in a hide of fused aggregate and rust-scale and ceramic tile fragments that have grown together into a single mottled plate, dark grey and iron-red, beaded constantly with condensation. Around its edges, water sheets away a little too deliberately. There are no seams.

It breathes.

Once every few minutes, the swell exhales. The hairline cracks around its rim weep a little harder, the shallow pool cupped in its back ripples, and a low sigh of warm, mineral air pushes out from somewhere underneath it. Then it inhales, very slowly, drawing that same water back in through a fringe of fine, hair-like filaments you can only see when you crouch.

They are not animals. They are not plants. They are the Floor learning to be an organism. Call them **Basinbacks**.

They are immensely flat, immensely heavy, immensely old. Their undersides are not legs but a broad, ciliated foot that grips the Floor's own texture, secreting a faint acid that softens the top millimeter of cement so they can graze the algal skin and the iron-oxidizing bacterial mats that grow there. They move. God, they move so slowly you would need to mark their position against a bolt-head and come back tomorrow to be sure. A few centimeters a day, following the ancient gradient of water, following the warmth that leaks from the pumps below. Their backs are their own watersheds. The perpetual rain collects in the shallow depression of their carapace, filtering through the living tile, through chambers of sponge-like tissue that strains rust and dissolved limestone, then is expelled cleansed around the rim. They are walking, filtering ponds.

When one dies - and they do, rarely - it doesn't rot. It settles. Its plate fuses fully with the Floor. A new layer. In a thousand years you will stand on it and think it is just Floor again.

There are not many. You might see three in a day's walking if you know how to see. They keep the respectful distance of boulders in a desert, each with its own territory of seep and drip. Sometimes at night you hear them shift, a long, grinding *creak* you thought was the Roof contracting. It wasn't.

### The Ones Who Stand Up

If the Basinbacks are the Floor itself, the others are what the Roof made.

You see one only when the mist thins near a work-light, and you think at first it is another piece of scaffolding left to rust. A pillar, too thin, too still.

It is too thin because it is not built for strength. It is built for stillness.

The **Drippers** - or the Steepleforms, if you need a name - are vertical where everything else is horizontal. Six, maybe seven feet tall, but no wider than a man. Their bodies are columnar, jointed in a way that looks less like bone than like stacked, telescoping pipe sections. Their skin is the same black iron as the wet girders, slick and beaded, patched with the same grey-green lichen that grows on everything that doesn't move. Their heads, if you can call them that, are not heads. They are shallow, wide, faintly concave bowls of pitted ceramic, like an old satellite dish or a lily pad made of rust.

They do not walk. They choose a place and root.

They choose it with exquisite care: directly under a seam in the Roof where the condensation is heaviest. Where the droplets grow on a girder overhead, fatten, and fall in a steady, cold tick... tick... tick. They plant their splayed, root-like feet into a crack, drive hair-fine tendrils down to drink the seep, and then they *wait*. The bowl-head tilts up. The rain fills it.

You could watch one for an hour and think it was dead. Then the bowl shivers. A thin membrane inside ripples. The collected water - mixed with dissolved soot, with airborne oils, with the spores of the rust-algae - is drawn down through a central throat with a faint, patient gulping. They filter. They live on what the air carries down. The water they don't need they weep back out through pores along their flanks, so that a fine sheen is always running down them.

They are phototropes of a kind, but there is no sun. They lean, over years, toward the sodium lamps, toward the forge-glow. The tallest ones cluster in loose groves around the old pump houses, their bowl-heads all canted the same way, like flowers. In the long dark between lights, they appear utterly blind. They are not. Along the rim of the bowl is a ring of simple, lightless pits, but they do not see. They feel vibration. The entire Floor is a drumhead for them. The thud of a press a mile away, the tremor of a Basinback moving, your own boots - they ring through their pipe-bones.

When the Roof gives a particularly heavy downpour, the grove all at once will bow. A slow, synchronized genuflection that takes a full minute to complete, spilling the excess from their heads so they don't overfill and topple. It is the fastest thing they ever do.

### The Ones Who Still Tend

And then there are the small ones. The quick ones. You hear them before you see them because you thought their noise was part of the machine.

A clatter on a conveyor that is supposed to be empty. A chain that jerks when no motor pulled it.

They are the original inhabitants' children, though not children in any clean way. **Keepers.**

They were not born. They were left running.

Small, low, many-legged things the size of dogs or suitcases, their bodies are maintenance drones that have been running so long their casings have eroded smooth. Ceramic and steel, but now rounded as river stones, patched with lichen, trailing fronds of moss from their joints where water has learned to live inside them. Some still have a single bleary work-light for an eye, amber or white, that sweeps the dark. Most are blind, the lens having clouded or shattered centuries ago.

They do not have programming anymore. They have habit.

A Keeper will scuttle along a pipe, pausing to scrape rust-scale from a valve with forelimbs that have worn into flat spatulas. Another will follow a Basinback for days, drinking the clean water at its rim and, in return, picking parasites of rust-bloom from its tiled back. Another will spend its entire long life - and they have very long lives, rebuilt by their own slow scavenging - doing nothing but pushing the same loose aggregate pebble back and forth in a crack, as if tending a shrine.

They communicate in the only language that works here: percussion. Because the air is too loud for voices and the dark too thick for signals, they drum on the Floor. A Keeper will halt, rear up, and hammer its forelimbs against iron plate: *tink - tink - TINK.* A deep note that travels through the Floor for half a mile. Another answers. *tink. tink.* *I am here. The water is high here. The pump is warm.*

If you crouch and put your palm flat to the Floor, you can feel them talking under the roar. A whole quiet conversation in tremors beneath the deafening thud-thud-thud.

And if people still live here - and something does, in the deeper pump chapels where the air is warmest - they have learned to live like the rest.

They are low and pale and broad-footed, deaf by thirty, their skin permanently damp and stippled with lichen at the elbows and scalp where they do not bother to wash it off. They do not speak. They tap. Two stones clicked against the Floor, a wrench on a pipe. They wear layers of patched rubber and felt that never dry. They farm nothing, but they harvest. They know which Basinback pools are safe to drink, which Dripper bowls collect the cleanest rain, which Keeper to follow when it is leading you away from a pressure fracture. They move rarely and never far, settling into a salt-stained pump house for a generation, becoming part of its warm, humming wall.

You see one at a distance, standing utterly still under the drip with his bowl-hands cupped, drinking the Roof, and for a long time you cannot tell if he is human or just another Steepleform grown strangely soft.

That is the trick of this place. It does not produce inhabitants. It converges everything toward the same final form: flat enough to endure the weight, wet enough to keep the water moving, slow enough to survive the noise, patient enough to wait out the age.

If you stand here long enough, with the cold seeping into your boots and the condensation beading on your shoulders, you will feel your own edges begin to soften and round. Your breath will fall into rhythm with the pumps. You will stop listening for a voice, and start listening through your feet.

And somewhere, a Keeper will pause on its endless circuit, cock its clouded eye toward you, and drum once on the Floor to ask if you intend to stay.

The Floor, after all, has plenty of room.
