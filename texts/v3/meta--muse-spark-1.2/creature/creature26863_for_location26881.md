They live so densely you don't see them at first.

You think you're looking at the rock. At its texture.

Your lamp catches what you take for a strange mineral skin on the walls — a black-red scale, overlapping plates, dull gloss like cooling slag or dried blood, pitted and smooth by turns, completely covering the stone. It looks like the chamber itself has a scab. Like the basalt sweated out some iron bark as it cooled. You reach out and the bark moves.

Not all at once. A single plate near your knee lifts a jointed edge, takes a step, and sets down again. Then another. Then a ripple pulls across the whole surface, a slow shudder of adjustment, and suddenly you can't unsee it: the wall is not wall. The floor is not floor. Every surface is alive and covered, edge to edge, with bodies.

The first ones you learn to pick out are the beetles.

They are not like the beetles you know from meadows or rotting logs. There is no iridescence, no quick skitter. They are low and flat and heavy-looking, as if they were pressed down by the heat and the closeness. About as long as the last joint of your thumb, oval, with no waist between thorax and abdomen. Their backs are a single domed shield, wing-cases fused shut forever — they will never fly, never open. The shield is the color of black iron just before it begins to glow, a deep maroon-black that drinks your light, enameled, hairless, hot to the touch even before you touch it. Around the rim of each shield the edge is turned under, so each animal seals to the rock and to its neighbors like a tile.

Underneath, their legs are a surprise — too thin for their bodies, like black wire, six per animal, splayed wide to carry weight in the press. Their heads are small, tucked under, all mandible. The mandibles are not curved like predators' but broad and blunt, like tiny scrapers or adzes, pale at the tips where the chitin has been worn and regrown, worn and regrown. They have no need for big eyes. In this sealed dark, eyes are almost nothing — a pair of clouded pits on either side of the head that can perhaps tell glow from not-glow. They navigate by feel and by taste, by the trembling of other feet through stone.

Among them, paler shapes move. At first you think they are juveniles, but they are something else entirely.

The crickets. They are thinner, longer in the leg, the color of beeswax or old paper, almost translucent in the dull red. You can see the dark line of their gut through their back. Their bodies are soft where the beetles are hard, and they move differently — not so much crawling as threading, slipping through the gaps the armored bodies leave, antennae always working. The antennae are twice their body length, white as thread, never still, combing the hot air a millimeter above the press of beetles, tapping backs, tasting salts, reading vibrations. They don't chirp. They have no song that carries in that dense, hot air. They have no wings to rub. When they want to be known they drum lightly on a beetle's shield with their forelegs and the beetle pauses.

There is no space between them. Not the way a forest floor has space between ants. Here the press is absolute, the way scales press on a fish or cells press in tissue. They stand on each other. They walk over each other. A beetle crossing the wall will climb the back of a stationary beetle without hesitation or aggression, and the one underneath simply lets itself be stepped on, adjusting its legs minutely to take the weight. If you brush one with a fingertip, the mass does not scatter like insects should. It flows — slowly, reluctantly — around your finger like hot tar, and then closes again behind you. No panic. No flight. Outside is cold death for them. They have nowhere to go.

And they are working. That is the thing that finally resolves out of the slow tide once you have knelt long enough to stop looking for a single animal.

No individual looks busy. Each move is ponderous, almost lazy. A beetle extends its head, scrapes its mandibles sideways across the bare basalt in a spot where the stone looks bare but isn't — where heat has sweated out a faint efflorescence, a dusting of yellow-white and mustard, sulfur and nitrates and iron bloom that reforms invisibly hour by hour. It gathers a few grains, consolidates them in its mouth with a bead of dark saliva, and begins to walk, three body-lengths a minute, toward the center of the chamber.

That center is warmer. You can feel it on your exposed face, a dry furnace breath coming off the floor where the rock thins over whatever still smolders below. There the beetles are thickest, in a churning, slow mill. They deposit their grains onto a growing cake the size of a dinner plate, already damp and dark. Other beetles are there tamping it down with their flat heads, turning it, folding it. The pale crickets follow in their wake, bending over the cake to paint it with a clear gloss from their abdomens, a secretion that smells faintly of vinegar and hot metal even in this air. It binds. It cures.

A little further along the cake has hardened to a black biscuit, crumbly at the edges. Different beetles — or the same ones later in their circuit — bite it up again, chew it finer, and extrude it behind them as a pellet, hard as cinder, almost perfectly round. Those pellets are then pushed, one by one, to the margin of the chamber where the living floor ends and raw stone begins. There, a line of beetles receives each pellet, fits it against the others like masons without mortar, and begins to tap.

One tap. A pause. Another tap. The head brought down like a tiny hammer against the pellet to seat it. On its own the sound is inaudible. As a chorus across ten thousand hammers it becomes the chamber's constant weather — a dry, encompassing clatter, chitin on stone, a stamping mill running without pause, echoing off the close curve of the walls until it feels less like sound and more like pressure in your teeth and chest.

That is how the floor grows. How the walls grow. Millimeter by millimeter, season by season. They are not building nests. They are not storing food. They are enlarging the only world they have by smelting the mountain itself, turning its sweat into the ground they stand on. Their dead are not carried away; there is no away. A beetle that dies simply stops moving and is slowly chewed from underneath by its neighbors, its shield becoming part of the next cake, its softness rendered back into gloss. Their eggs — tiny amber grains you only see if you catch a cricket gently lifting a pellet — are tucked under the fresh floor, where the heat is steadiest.

They need nothing you would think life needs. No light, no green, no water to drink — they make their water inside themselves, metabolizing hydrogen from the mineral and oxygen from the air, keeping it all. No rain has ever reached here and none ever should. The dark is total except for that deep earth glow. The heat is constant. They breathe slowly, you can see it if you watch one for a long time: the faintest pulse under the enameled back, a bellows-beat that takes ten seconds to complete.

They are not a hive with a queen, not a colony with soldiers. No one directs. There are just enough rules — scrape here, carry toward warmth, tamp, gloss, chew, press, tap — that order appears out of the crowd the way a current appears out of water molecules pushing. The factory has no foreman. The assembly line is made of the workers themselves.

If the seam ever widened, let in wind and cold and rain, they would all stiffen and die in a day. If the mountain finally cooled, they would starve with stone still around them. But for now, sealed in its new, hot pocket, the ridge has made a perfect, rare bargain: a little room, held shut, given over completely to a dense, patient industry that asks for nothing but heat and time, and which fills it absolutely, so that there is not one bare handprint of stone anywhere that is not tasted, tamped, and hammered by something alive.
