They live low.

Not low like they crouch. Low like they were made to match a world that has been planed flat. Everything that lives here has given up on height.

At first you think nothing lives here at all, which is correct for a few seconds. The pan is empty. The stone shines. A hundred meters of water an inch deep reflecting pearl-grey nothing. Then your eyes drop down to where the water is actually happening, to the film, and the film resolves into motion, and you realize the plain is not empty, it is *covered* - it only hides its abundance by spreading it perfectly thin.

### The ones who eat the stone

They are the true residents. The others are just living off them.

They are small. Most of them would fit in the palm of your wet hand and you would lose them there, because they look exactly like wet pebbles until the pebble walks.

Call them pips, or buttons, or pebble-bearers. Domed, smooth-backed, translucent grey-brown shells the size of a thumbnail to a thumb-joint, with no eyes at all - just a soft, pale lip underneath that constantly tests the water, and a ring of absurdly many jointed legs - twelve, sixteen, twenty - fringed with fine hairs that row and grip at once. Under the dome is a radula like a circular file that they press to the rock and drag.

There are millions of them. Billions. You don't see the billions because they are never piled up. They are smeared across the peneplain in a living varnish, one or two per square hand of rock, each scraping. Only at the weeping rocks - those low, barely-there rises where water wells out of the gneiss itself without a spring - do they collect into tides. There the bacterial varnish - the black and rust and sulfurous yellow that is the only color in this mineral world - grows thickest, slick as oil, and the pips converge from every direction in a slow, clicking flood until the stone disappears under a restless mosaic of shells clacking against shells.

That is their sound. Alone, one pip makes a faint *tik-tik*, a scrape, a creak as its shell flexes. Together, across a plain the size of a county with no tree or hill to break it, the sound becomes a continent. It has no source because it is coming from everywhere at once. It rises at what passes for noon - when the pearl sky brightens by a fraction - until the shallow pans vibrate with it and you feel it in your molars. It is not a background. It is their way of being counted. They chirp as they scrape, almost continuously, a side-effect of the radula rasping and the shell resonating. Where a pan is thickest grazed, the chorus is loudest; where a hunter has passed, there is a hole in the sound that fills back in within minutes as neighbors flow in.

They do not need plants. They never knew them. They farm the stone itself. They eat the bacteria and the bacteria eat the stone and the water that sweats from it. They eat each other's dead and each other's eggs and the film that settles after condensation. If something larger dies, they can skeletonize it in a day - you will find the white, paper-thin stilts of a hunter picked perfectly clean, the rock around it polished to a dark mirror from the frenzy of a thousand domes converging and then, just as fast, dispersing, leaving no rot, no smell, only clean stone.

Among them live smaller relatives that have leaned further into the water that is never quite rain:

*The skaters* - flat as a sliver of mica, six-legged, translucent as glass, that never break surface tension. They dimple the film of the pans and shoot across it in straight lines, trailing a V-wake that catches the shadowless light. They filter the water column with a basket of bristles under their chins.

*The mud-writhers* - longer than your finger, segmented, the color of wet grit, living half-buried where fine sand has collected in the faintest hollow. You only see the feathery red gills that twitch above the surface like small ferns, opening and closing, opening and closing, pulling oxygen from water that is always cool and always barely moving.

All of them are pale. All of them are wet to the point where wet is not a condition but a tissue. Cut one - though the people here rarely do without purpose - and it barely bleeds; the inside is mostly water held in a membrane.

### The ones who eat the stone-eaters

Because the grazers are so evenly spread, the predators have had to become sparse and tall and perfectly still.

The most common is the stilt-walker. From a distance in that light without shadow you mistake it for a trick of distance - a cairn, a dead bush, until it moves. It stands a meter at the shoulder but weighs almost nothing. Its body is a folded sheet of grey flesh, like paper left in the rain, stretched between four stilt legs as thin as reeds and twice as long, jointed backwards. Its head is a long, eyeless wedge with a beak that works like a pair of tweezers. It stands in an inch of water and does not move for an hour, its feet making only the faintest dimple, its color the exact grey of sky-on-stone. The chorus washes around it. Then, when the flow of pips beneath it reaches a certain density - when the clicking becomes a single tone - it stabs. So fast your eye leaves a streak. The beak spears three or four domes, crushes, and flicks them into a gullet. It swallows shell and all. It will take another step - a slow, high, dripping step - and become still again. Every few hundred meters there is another one, each with its own circle of silence that it carries with it, each acoustically isolated so the others cannot hear its clack and come steal.

Lower, in the pans that are a hand deep and therefore count as lakes here, wallow the humpbacks. They look like seals that forgot how to be seals. Barrel bodies, no eyes, no ears, just nostrils that close like valves and a broad, flat tail that sculls. Their skin is mottled slate and ash, thick and wrinkled, holding water against the cool air. They roll and grunt - a low, wet, surprisingly mammalian sound under the insect-roar - and suck at the grit, filtering pips and writhers and water together through baleen-like plates. They are solitary too. If two meet at the edge of a connecting pan as the water breathes and links up their lakes, they bellow and slap until one subsides.

Everything that hunts here hunts by vibration, by hearing the chorus and listening for the break in it. Sight is useless in a world without shadow. Sound is heavier than air here; you can see it ripple the pans before you hear it on a still day.

### The ones who stack stones

And then there are the people. Or near-people.

They live low too. They have to. If you stood tall here the wind that never stops - the low, cool drag across water films that makes them hum - would take the heat from you in minutes, and there is nowhere to hide except behind what you build.

They are scattered the way the stilt-walkers are scattered, which is not by accident but by necessity. A family, maybe three or four, per few kilometers of pan. No villages. Villages would starve. The rock does not grow enough at any one point to feed more mouths than that. They move, but not far. Their territory is a slow circuit of weeping rocks and cups and weirs that they tend.

You see them least first and last longest. Small, pale to the point of translucence themselves, with hair that is thin and plastered to their heads by condensation, with skin that is always damp and slightly reddened on the knuckles and knees from the 8-degree cold that never relents. They wear - not clothes, exactly. Cloaks sewn from humpback hide, scraped thin and rendered with rendered pip-fat until it is supple and waterproof. Nothing woven; there is nothing to weave. Their feet are bare and wide and calloused from grit and cold water, their toes splayed.

They do not speak loudly. No one can afford to speak loudly for long where you have to shout over the chorus and the chorus never stops. They have a sign language of hands and splashes. When they do call, they use a low, sharp whistle that cuts through the high clicking, or they tap stones together. Their ears are good for a long time and then bad very young; the old are deaf and watch the water for the ripples of sound instead.

What they do all day is tend the stone.

With a flat piece of banded chert flaked to an edge - the only tool they need and the only tool they leave behind in bright scatters of debitage - they scrape. They have ground shallow cups into the gneiss, smooth basins the size of a stew bowl where water lingers a little longer than on the open plain. They sit at those cups for hours in the cold, scraping the bacterial varnish with a practiced, circular motion, collecting the black-green paste in a hide pouch. That's their staple. That, and the pips themselves, gathered by the handful when the tide is on a weeping rock, boiled briefly - if they have fuel - or eaten raw, crunchy and salty and faintly iron.

They build low. Always low. An ankle-high wall, two stones thick, twenty meters long and then stopping, not because it is unfinished but because it is *done*. It is a fish weir for creatures that are not fish - a way to cup the breathing water as it rises. When the pan connects, the skaters and pips flow over it; when the water sighs back down into the rock, a few are stranded behind the wall, easy to collect without chasing them across a hundred meters of slick. A cairn of balanced slabs is not a burial or a boundary. It's a condenser. Water beads on its vertical faces faster than on flat stone and drips into a cup at its foot. A windbreak of three leaning slabs is a house. Inside it, a blackened circle shows where they burn the only thing that will burn: dried mats of biofilm and cakes of pip fat and humpback blubber that smoke terribly and give a low, sullen heat that just takes the ache from your hands.

They are primitive in the way a tidepool animal is primitive: not stupid, but perfectly reduced. No metal, because there is no ore that is not locked in the shield and no fire hot enough to smelt it. No pottery, because there is no clay, only grit. Every fabric is hide or gut. Every edge is stone. They do not carve, they stack; they do not plant, they harvest what grows on rock. The varnish grows back over their oldest walls, black and red, reclaiming the stack one bacterial layer at a time, and they let it.

If you stay still long enough, one will notice you not by sight but because your weight changes the pressure under plates and the hiss of the weeping fissures shifts. It will look at you - not directly, because its eyes are milky and narrowed against the diffuse glare - and it will not be surprised. The plain teaches that anything that stands up and casts no shadow is either a hunter or a cairn, and you are neither quite.

It will return to scraping. The chorus around you will rise again, the stilt-walker two pans over will take its high step, the humpback will grunt and roll, and the water that was not there an hour ago will be inch-deep at your ankles, full of mica-slivers shooting back and forth, reflecting a sky that never gets bright and never gets dark.

That is who lives here: a film of grazers that *is* the ground, a few thin hunters tuned to hear the holes in that film, and a few thin families who have learned to listen the same way, to live inside the noise and drink the sweat of the stone and make small, stubborn shapes against a flatness that has been trying, for a billion years, to be perfectly smooth.
