They live close together. That is the first thing you understand, before you can make out any one of them.

From a half a kilometer off, against the hard white sun, the city looks empty. The stone is the same color as everything. The terraces make a geometry too regular to be natural and too vast to be made. For a long time you watch and see nothing move except prayer flags and ravens. Then the light shifts and the scarp resolves into doors. Hundreds of doors. And every door is open and closing. People moving along the steps like water in those finger-width channels — in single file, with loads on their backs, unhurried, never stopping.

You have to get closer before they become individuals, and even then they do not become individual quickly.

They are short. Not dramatically, but evidently. A low center of gravity is useful when every alley is a stair and every winter buries the stairs in verglas. They are broad. Men and women both barrel-chested, the chest deep front-to-back in a way that makes their jackets strain even when they are thin. Children look almost stocky, as if inflated a little. It is the air. You hear it before you see it clearly — everyone breathing audibly, a faint catch and sigh even at rest, the sound of a people who have grown larger lungs. When they work, when they haul a sledge or swing a hammer, the breathing becomes a rhythm that paces the work, open-mouthed, steaming even when it is not that cold. Their blood is thick with red. A cut wells dark and clots fast.

Their skin betrays the sun. You expect the pallor of a cold place, but the sun at that height is merciless. Even in midwinter it comes white through the thin air and reflects off every snowfield and pale stone until there is nowhere to hide from it. Their faces are browned, ruddy-brown, the color of oiled wood. The high cheekbones burn first and then never fade. The skin at the corners of the eyes is creased early, crazed with fine lines by twenty from squinting. They keep their eyes narrowed by habit, even in shade. The eyes themselves are dark — deep brown and black almost without exception — and the iris is ringed heavily against glare. An old woman's eyes are almost lost in the folds around them, until she looks directly at you and you see how black and wet they are.

They do not hurry. Nobody runs. Running in that air is a punishment and everyone learned it as a child. They walk with a short, even step, never lengthening stride on the flat because there is almost no flat. They climb all day. Calves like corded wood under felt trousers. Their hands are their most expressive feature. Broad palms, fingers thickened and cracked at every knuckle, nails blackened and broken from stone. The cold splits the skin every winter and it never quite heals, a lattice of white scars over brown. Even the young have old hands. A boy of ten will have the callus of a mason on his right palm where he holds the hammer his father made for him, too heavy last year, just right now. A girl of twelve can fit a dry-stone joint so tight you cannot slide a barley husk into it. They touch stone the way other people touch wood — constantly, appraisingly, affectionately. A man passing an old wall will run his palm along it without stopping.

Their clothing is the city in miniature: layered, patched, crowded, made to be repaired forever.

Nothing is ever new for long and nothing is ever discarded. The base is wool — sheep's wool and yak hair spun coarse and woven tight, then fulled until it is almost felt. It is brown and grey and undyed white, because dye is a luxury when every plant is countable. Over that, hides, hair out for warmth, hair in for wind. An outer coat that is really a blanket with sleeves, belted with a cord, the hem black with soot and hemmed with many repairs in sinew. Hoods that frame the face tight, leaving only eyes and nose and mouth, rimed with frost from breath by mid-morning. Felt boots, knee-high, stuffed with dry moss that is changed daily. Everyone wears gloves or mitts cut off at the fingers so the fingertips can work stone; everyone's fingertips are therefore black with cold. In summer they shed a layer and you see the sweat darkening the wool at the back. In winter they add another and become shapeless, shuffling bundles with only eyes showing, indistinguishable from one another until they speak.

And they speak quietly.

The city has taught them that sound does not travel well and that wind will take anything louder. Their language has settled low. Few high vowels, many soft consonants, words that are short and end in breath. They do not call to each other across streets; they walk over and say it close. When a yak driver wants his animals to turn, he clicks his tongue or hums rather than shouts. Children learn not to scream. A baby cries — of course babies cry — but mothers press them to the chest under the coat immediately, and the crying muffles. Two old men on a terrace can hold a conversation for an hour and a person ten meters below will hear only a murmur, like pigeons. Laughter, when it comes, is sudden and startling, and brief.

What they talk about, when you finally hear them, is mostly maintenance.

There are no jobs here that are not maintenance. The plateau does not give; it must be coaxed. That is the culture. Every family belongs to a stretch of channel, a barley plot, a cistern, a section of windbreak wall. A boy's first real responsibility is not herding but tending a frost-jar — a clay pot set on a roof to catch the morning rime, which he must scrape with a flat stone before the sun takes it. A girl's first is to walk a contour channel with a feather, watching for where the thread of water has been dammed by dust, and to clear it with a fingertip. The adults keep the larger balances: when to open the high glacier gate and when to close it, which terrace gets the dung this year and which must lie fallow, how many sheep the winter lichen on that north wall can support without killing it for seven years.

They are engineers without the word. They know stone the way you might know weather. Which basalt will split clean along the column and which will shatter. How to lean a wall two degrees into the hill so frost will not push it. How to pitch a roof just enough that frost slides into the jar but not so much that the wind takes it. The knowledge is not written. There are no books — paper would be a miracle here — but there are walls. On the inside of a granary, generations of notches mark good and bad years. On the smooth face of a cistern, a hand has carved the sign for "clean me before first melt." Children are taught by doing, beside their parents, for years, until the knowledge is in the hands.

Because the city is so dense and so old, privacy is a concept that has been thinned like the air. Walls are shared. You hear your neighbor's cough through the stone. You smell their cooking — always the same: barley roasted and ground to tsampa, root stew, tea churned with yak butter and salt until it is salty and oily enough to keep the cold out — and they smell yours. Marriages are arranged terrace to terrace so that water rights can be joined sensibly. An argument between two households is an engineering problem that the whole stair-street must solve, because if they stop speaking the shared channel between them will clog. There are headmen and headwomen, but mostly there are elders who sit in sun-traps — those rare corners where the windbreak and the scarp angle make a pocket of still, warm air — and remember. An elder who remembers the blizzard that lasted eleven days forty years ago is more valuable than a strong back. They listen to those people the way other cultures listen to priests.

And there are priests, or something like them. Not separate. The same elders, the same hands. Where the fault has made a particularly clean, sheer face, someone has carved a shallow niche and set a stone inside carved with a spiral or an eye. Where a path crosses an ice culvert that never melts, a cairn has grown, each traveler adding one stone for safe passage. Prayer flags are everywhere, not bright as you imagine — the colors bleach quickly in that sun — but pale, whipping to threads, each snap releasing a prayer into the thin wind. They do not expect answers. The plateau is too large and too indifferent for a god who intervenes. What they practice is maintenance of meaning the same way they maintain water: small, repeated acts that keep something from vanishing.

The animals are not separate from them. A family without a yak is a family that cannot haul stone and will not build. A family without a dog will lose grain to pikas and hares. The dogs are not pets; they are low, heavy, broad-headed things with coats like yaks, sleeping in piles for warmth in alleys, waking to bark once at a lammergeier's shadow and then settling. The yaks know the stairways as well as people and will climb them patiently, bells clanking, steaming. Ravens know people individually and will follow a particular butcher. Snowfinches will land on a person's shoulders if they stand still long enough spreading grain to winnow. The city is a reef. The people are the coral, the animals the fish that live only where the coral is.

At midday you can see them best. The sun stands almost violet overhead and every edge throws ink. Thousands of figures are bent in the terraces, weeding barley that is barely ankle-high, moving dung, mending walls. The light makes their faces skull-like for a moment, cheekbone and brow stark, then they tilt their heads and are human again. At evening the dung fires are lit — juniper is too precious to burn except for incense — and a blue smoke lies in the steps and does not rise, and the people go inside. The houses are low and half-sunk, one room where all sleep around the hearth, the stone itself warm from the day's sun and from bodies. They lie close. In winter ten people and two dogs may sleep under the same yak-hair blankets, breathing into the shared air that is warmer for being breathed.

They do not think of themselves as living in an extreme place. To them, the plateau is the world. The idea of a forest would frighten them — that much soft, busy, wet green — the idea of a sea would be mythic. If you tell them about lowland air, thick and warm as soup, they will smile politely and think you are describing a fever. They have been here so long their bodies have changed to fit the height, their culture has changed to fit the thinness of water and the slowness of stone. They are not stranded. They are not waiting to leave. They are what happens when people decide, generation after generation, to stay where staying is a craft.

If you stay long enough to be allowed to stay — to keep a channel clear, to learn not to shout — you will stop seeing crowds and start seeing faces. The old woman who always scrapes her roof first, before light, and leaves the frost for the finches. The mason twins who can set stone without speaking, mirroring each other's hands. The child who guards his family's juniper bush, no taller than his knee, with a seriousness as if guarding a treasury. Each is crowded and each is distinct, exactly like the lichen on the basalt — from afar a stain, from close a nation.

And like lichen, they hold.
