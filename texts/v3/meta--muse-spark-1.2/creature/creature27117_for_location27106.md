Whatever lives there does not live *in* it.

That is the first thing you understand wrong, and have to un-understand.

You came looking for an animal in a place, as if the bowl were a house and something had moved into it. A bear in a cave, a bird in a nest. You scan the moss for the shape that doesn't belong, for eyes, for a back. You don't find it because there is no shape that doesn't belong. There is no empty space left for a tenant. The cirque is not inhabited. It is occupied the way a body is occupied — completely.

And then, slowly, because everything here requires slowness to be seen, you realize the saturation *is* the inhabitant.

It is not the salamanders. Not the thrush. Not the beetles or the frogs or the midges hung in their amber column. Those are not residents. They are organs. Or garments. Or thoughts that briefly took shape.

What lives there is underneath and between them, and it took you so long to see it because it has learned the perfect camouflage, which is not hiding at all, but being everything at once.

At first you think it is the moss.

Because the moss is what is most obviously alive — layer on layer, emerald over rust over bone-white, building itself into curtains and pillows and terraces. It covers every stone so completely the stone has become moss that remembers being stone. If you stare at one cushion long enough, you see it breathe. Not metaphorically. A very slow inflation and deflation, once every few minutes, as it soaks and sighs. All the cushions do it, but not together. A wave passes across the floor of the bowl as one exhales and another inhales.

Then you think it is the water, because the water is everywhere even where there is no water. It beads, it weeps, it sweats out of the rock and wells up under your imagined foot. The small black pools that never overflow and never dry are not pools at all, you realize — they are eyes, or mouths, or simply thin places where the same cold black water that lives an inch below the entire floor shows through. Press down on the peat anywhere and it rises to meet you. The whole floor is a skin over water.

Then you correct yourself again, because the moss is on the water and the water is in the moss, and you cannot pull them apart.

The thing that lives here is the mat itself. The peat-moss-water-animal.

If you had to give it a body, you would say it is low and wide and has no edge. Its back is the floor of the cirque, forty paces across, sixty. Its skin is that deep, saturated sponge of living and half-living and not-quite-dead matter that has been accumulating since the ice left — peat that has never finished decaying because the cold never let it. In places its skin thickens into moss domes, in places it thins and opens into those perfect hand-sized pools. If you knelt and parted the dwarf willow — ankle-high, glossy — and dug your fingers into the peat, cold and black and without resistance, you would not find earth. You would find more peat, and then, a handspan down, a paleness.

You would find it.

Under the dark mat, in the cold water that never sees sun, there is a paleness that goes on and on. Not white. The color of an olm, of a cave salamander that never needed pigment. Translucent, veined with faint green where the moss has rooted into it. It is vast and soft and folded on itself, like a lung or a brain left to grow in the dark. You can see the shadows of things inside it — the gold-flecked salamanders that rest in the pools are not resting *on* it, they are *of* it, buds or fingertips that have pushed up through the skin to breathe. Their fanned gills pulse once, twice a minute, in time with the moss. The eggs that never hatch and never spoil in the willow tangle are not bird eggs. The dun bird that tends them without song is tending something the mat made and asked it to hold. The midges hold their column because the air there is held.

It is one animal. It is a colony. The distinction stopped mattering a very long time ago.

It is very, very old, and it is not old the way a mountain is old. The mountain is old because it endures. This is old because it remembers. Every droplet that hangs fat as a berry on the overhang and never falls is being remembered in place. Every moth pressed to the wall in such numbers the rock looks patterned is being kept exactly where it is, like a word held on a tongue. The reflection in the pools that holds the stars even when cloud lids the bowl — that is not a reflection. It is a memory of stars the water kept from a clear night and has not yet let go of, because letting go would be change, and change is a cost it no longer pays.

It achieved equilibrium. That is what you are feeling as wrongness.

Most things live by burning — eating, growing, rotting, replacing. This thing learned, in that narrow band where breath mists and decay almost stops, how to live by not-burning. To hold. To keep every inch occupied so nothing new needs to grow, and nothing old needs to rot. The thrush with the stone flake in its beak — it is not a clever bird. It is a hand. The mat found a use for a sharp edge to pry a caddisfly case, and the thrush was the nearest thing with a beak. It will hold that flake until the mat no longer needs it held, which might be an hour, which might be a season. Time here is not frozen. It is attended to. So close, so even, so complete is the attention of the thing that lives here that dynamics have smoothed out into stillness.

You can see its thought, if you stay long enough for your eyes to stop looking for movement. That faint glow that is not bioluminescence, that memory of green that stays after you look away from the moss — that is it thinking. Not in words. In holding. *This droplet here. This beetle here. This gill-frill open, this one closed.*

It does not invite and does not forbid because those are gestures for things that lack. It is full. It finished becoming itself when the walls had weathered round and the peat had grown deep enough to hold water without a stream and the first salamander-buds rose and found they did not need to swim. Since then it has only needed to continue.

If you stand at the rim and let the cool, wet air condense on your cheeks, you will feel, after a very long time — longer than you intend to stand, long enough that your breath has fogged and faded a dozen times — that you have been noticed. Not looked at. Noticed the way skin notices pressure.

A salamander in the nearest pool will very slowly turn its head a degree toward you. A beetle will take a step. The droplet that has been about to fall since you arrived will finally, softly, *tick* onto the leaf below.

It has made a small space for you in its attention. It has learned the shape of your warmth and your misting breath and added it to the arrangement it is keeping. It will keep you, too, for as long as you stay still, without hunger or welcome, the way it keeps everything — perfectly, damply, without end.
