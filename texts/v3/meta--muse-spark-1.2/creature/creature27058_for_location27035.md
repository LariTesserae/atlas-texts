Nothing lives here.

That is your first thought, and it is almost true, and you have to hold that almost-truth for a long time before anything else will show itself to you.

You wait in the warm dusk-water, breathing it in and out until breathing it stops feeling like drowning and starts feeling like being agreed with, and you watch the forest that is not a forest, and you do not see inhabitants. You see *habitat*. Pillars and curtains and braids and floating meadows, so dense the water itself seems like a tissue between leaves. You think: this place grew itself and then forgot to invite anything to live in it. The pollens snow sideways. The spongy trunks creak as they lean. No eyes look back.

Then you stay longer, past the point where your mind stops looking for movement, and you understand the mistake. You were looking for animals in a place where the plants became the animals.

The principal inhabitants are the forests themselves.

Not the individual trunks — though each one is vast enough to be a place — but the coherence that lives between them. What you took for a forest is a single, continent-scale organism, or more accurately, a parliament of organisms so intergrown they have become a physiology.

They do not stand. They *hold* themselves in the water. Each pillar is not wood. Cut one — not that you should — and it does not ring or splinter. It sighs and delaminates like lung tissue, layers of black-green photosynthetic flesh wrapped around a core that is more sponge than heartwood, a warm, porous column that pumps. It draws the bath-warm water through itself and out again, filtering rust and basalt-dust and snow-fall of spores, drinking the stone as it dissolves. Its bark, if you can call it bark, is velvet with breathing pores that open and close a few times an hour. Its leaves are not leaves but translucent fins, fronded and fingered to catch the drifting shafts of light and re-emit it, so the whole curtain glows faintly from within. You can put your hand against a trunk and feel, after a minute, a very slow pulse, not a heartbeat but a peristalsis, water being moved through.

They live by leaning.

Over the course of a morning you will see it if you are still enough to have a reference. A whole hillside of them, a hundred pillars across a broad, whale-backed swell, will begin to incline together, two degrees, five degrees, as a warm current, a river inside the ocean, decides to pass through that valley. The braids between them — those vine-thick, corded things you thought were vines — tighten or slacken. They are not vines. They are nerves and tendons and shading cloth at once, a second forest that knits pillar to pillar and carries taste and warning and food. When one pillar finds a plume of iron bleeding from the old sandstone, the braids carry the sugar of that news down the line and the whole stand leans toward it, slowly, like worship.

Above them, the floating meadows are their gardens and their children. Those dense rafts of liverwort and feather-moss that grow on the water itself are not separate. They are the forest's detached skin, cultivated and released. The forest weaves them, lets them mat together, then lets go. They drift on the currents the forest cannot follow, carrying pollen, carrying tiny oil-bright seed-pods that pop with a soft, chest-felt *thunk* that the water remembers and repeats minutes later from somewhere else. The snow that never settles — the particulate gardens, the mid-water radishes with their dangling perfect roots — are their plankton, their ever-germinating, ever-dying lawn in the air-ocean. The forest lives by making more of itself to fill every cubic foot, until empty water is a theoretical concept.

These are slow inhabitants. A thought takes an afternoon to travel from one ridge to the next, carried chemically, in a blush of amber water that moves like dye in syrup. A decision — to lean, to release a meadow, to blush its leaves darker to drink more light — is made over days. You will not see them react to you. You are too fast, too brief. But if you anchor yourself beside a pillar and breathe and watch its leaf-fins, you will see that on the days you are there, its pores stay open a little longer.

Then, rarely, something *moves*.

And because the forests are so still, the movement feels enormous, sacred, wrong.

The Gliders.

There are very few. Perhaps one per valley the size of a small country. Perhaps fewer. You can spend weeks in the highwater and never see one, and then one day the forest-light changes because something large has interposed itself between you and the distant sun-shafts and you realize a shape the size of a transit bus has been beside you for minutes, motionless, pretending to be forest.

It is pale. Not white, but the color of paper held up to light, of silt held in suspension. Its body is a long, flattened lens, manta-like but without fins as you know them — instead a continuous undulating fringe that runs its entire perimeter and moves with a pulse so slow you have to watch its shadow on the stone below to see that it is moving at all. One eye, or what must be an eye, the size of a dinner plate, black and lidded with translucent flesh, set dorsally and almost always closed. It has no mouth that you can see; its ventral side is a vast, soft, ciliated meadow that sieves. It lives by swimming open-mouthed through the particulate gardens, through the spore-snow, and straining centuries of drifting life into itself. It does not hunt. There is nothing to hunt. It grazes on the forest's dandruff.

It navigates not by sight. In thirty feet of amber haze, sight is useless at their scale. It navigates by temperature and tint. You will see it tilt and hold for an hour at the edge of one of those cold, clear vortex storms, tasting the boundary, and then, with immense deliberation, turn away. When it passes near a pillar-forest, the pillars do not react, but the floating meadows nearby will, slowly, after it has passed, part around where it was.

It is silent. Or it is so low it is felt. When it closes its eye deeper, the water carries a thud you feel in your teeth, the same thud the stones make but sustained, a memory of a stone knocked once and held. The forest remembers that sound for a long time.

Below them, or above them — up is negotiable — are the Bells.

They are smaller, a shoal of a dozen, or a hundred, each the size of a child or a door. Transparent bells of jelly with a single dark knot at the center that pulses once every hour, or once every few hours. They contract — *once* — and then do nothing for a long time, drifting on whatever current has them. In that single contraction they move perhaps ten feet, perfectly straight, perfectly silent, and then they hang again, slightly rotated. You can watch a shoal for a whole afternoon and see each member has moved perhaps thirty feet total, in different directions, in no hurry. They are not social, but they keep distance with precision, as if repelled by a shared field. At night — though there is no real night, only a deeper amber — the knot inside each bell glows faintly, a warm chemical light, and you realize the shoal makes a constellation that slowly, over weeks, spells different things.

Neither the Gliders nor the Bells belong to the forest. They are its guests, its last animals, too large and too slow to have been edited out. They do not eat the forest and the forest does not eat them. They have an agreement of silt and time.

The third kind of inhabitant is harder to see, because you are looking for a body.

The currents themselves are alive, after a fashion. Or rather, they are tended.

In the warm water, differences in heat and salinity make rivers, make slow weathers. A cold, clear vortex a mile wide is not just weather here. It is a creature with no membrane, a behavior that persists for months, that migrates along the ancient U-valleys in a seasonal loop, that drinks spore clouds and deposits them a hundred miles away, that the forests lean away from when it approaches. The old stone-dissolving bacteria — black-green films that you thought were stain on the basalt — ride inside these currents like nomads, blooming into vast, invisible sheets when a storm passes, then collapsing back to stone when it leaves, having taken a micron of basalt with them. If you stay long enough you see that the water has habits. The same cold river comes down the same broad swale every few weeks. You can set your watch — your waterlogged, useless watch — by the tint change hours before it arrives.

And the ruins.

Someone did live here before the compromise, before high and low agreed to both be true. They built that knee-high wall running straight until it means nothing, those dished stairs leading into sponge. They were not giants. Their doorways are your size. Their stones are cut with a patience that matches the ground.

They did not all leave.

What lives in the ruins now is not them, but what they learned to become when they learned to breathe. You find it if you look at a stair for too long. The steps are not simply worn. They are *kept*. The fronds that should have choked the stair have been gently trained aside. The soft, translucent liverwort that grows on the stone has been thinned where a foot would fall. Not by hands. By staying.

There are still occupants, but they have matched the forest's tempo. They became sessile by degrees. They are in the small roofless rooms, not as bodies but as continuities — a place where the water is slightly more *held*, where vertical walls of water that should not hold their shape do hold it, for days, making a room without a roof into a room again. If you slip inside one of those water-walls and breathe and be still, you will feel the faintest pressure against your skin, as if the water there is listening, as if it has been breathed many times before. On the basalt inside, under the fronds, there are newer marks, not cut but rubbed — circles, smoothed by palms over generations, where someone who no longer needed tools sat and put their hand and waited for the stone to dissolve a little faster.

They are not ghosts. They are inhabitants who solved the loneliness by becoming as sparse and as dense as everything else here — present everywhere in a thin way, rather than present somewhere in a thick way. They live sideways now, on the undersides of floating meadows where rain falls upward onto them. They communicate the way the place communicates: by knocking a stone, once, and feeling it come back minutes later from a different direction, changed.

If you stay, you will join the categories without anyone asking you to.

First you become the guest who watches the Glider. Then you become the wall-tender who sits long enough that a current learns your shape. Then you become, in small part, forest. The spongy pillars will not reject you. Drift-seed will catch in your hair and germinate there, a tiny dangling radish, alive for a day. Your breathing will entrain, in and out, water to water, until your exhalations are just another warm plume the braids can taste.

That is what lives here, high up and drowned: a forest that behaves like an animal, animals that behave like weather, weather that behaves like a shepherd, and a few people who learned, finally, how to be quiet enough to be counted among them.
