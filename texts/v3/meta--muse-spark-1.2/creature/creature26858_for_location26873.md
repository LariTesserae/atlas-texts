Nothing lives here the way something lives in a forest.

In a forest, life is crowded and overlapping and competitive. Here, life is spaced out on a grid. It lives the way lights live here — far apart, in pools, with a lot of wet dark between them.

At the bottom of the food chain, if you can call it a chain when it's really just a film, is the slick.

You don't see it at first until you almost slip on it. Every horizontal surface that stays wet has grown a skin. In the drainage channels it's thickest — a black-green algae, but not the green of summer ponds. This is a green that has never seen the sun, a green that learned to eat amber sodium light and the faint heat of current in a cable. It makes the concrete treacherous and glossy. If you scrape it with a boot it comes up in a single sheet like paint, and underneath the concrete is paler, untouched, as if the building were blushing.

Where the water slows and silt collects — concrete dust, skin cells, iron oxide flaked off a girder a kilometer above — the slick thickens into soil that was never soil. And from that, the sedge.

It doesn't grow in meadows. It grows as individuals. One clump here, then twenty meters of nothing, then another clump. Each tuft is precise and miserable and perfect: rust-colored blades, roots like white wire gripping the crack between slabs. The seed heads are always crusted with a bead of frost that never quite melts, so they rattle when the draft hits them. They have learned to lean. All of them lean the same way, southwest, with the pull of air toward the great exchangers, as if in prayer to a cold god.

Around the base of each light pole, where the wiring throws off a single degree of warmth, there is a perfect circle of white lichen. You could set a compass on it. It grows outward millimeter by millimeter, year by year, a pale ring that measures how long that bulb has been burning. No lichen touches another lichen. There is too much distance.

These are the permanent residents. They don't move and they don't need to.

The moving things are rarer, and they move as if they know they are trespassing.

First, the moths. They are the color of concrete dust, grey on grey, with wings that look like chipped aggregate. They hatch, live, and die under a single light. Their whole world is the cone of amber under one bulb and the few meters of black around it. They flutter without purpose, not toward the light like normal moths but in slow, level orbits around it, as if circling a cold sun. When the bulb flickers, they falter. When it dies — and they do — the moths under it die within a day, or they make a desperate, doomed crossing through the dark to the next pool of light, fifty meters away. Most don't make it. You find their bodies in the channels, wings spread, perfectly preserved by the cold.

Then the rats. They are not the rats of cities. Those rats are fat and bold. These are pale, almost translucent, with whiskers crusted in frost. They do not live in nests. They live in *routes*. There are cable trenches that run under grating beside the drains, carrying warm current, and the rats follow them like roads. You will never see a colony. You will see one rat, moving with absolute purpose along a straight line, nose to the grating, never deviating, going from one warm place to another warm place kilometers away. If you stand still, one will pass within a meter of your boots and not look at you. You are not a predator or prey to it. You are just a warm obstruction that will be gone soon. Its feet make no sound on the wet concrete.

In the deeper sumps — where the grid of shallow drains finally feeds into square pits with no bottom you can see, where the water is black and still and so cold it seems to have weight — lives the white thing. You will not see it twice unless it wants you to. It's not a fish, not really, though it has the shape of one. More like a salamander, or an isopod grown large and blind. Pallid, eyeless, with feathery gills that pulse even when it is still. It lives on what falls from above: dead moths, algae flakes, the occasional drowned rat. It moves by feeling pressure changes when you step near the edge. It surfaces once, rolls, shows a flank the color of wet paper, and then is gone, and the water after it is flat again for a long time. It was not put here. Nothing put it here. It either washed down from some far water system above, or it assembled itself from the only materials available: cold, dark, and time. It is the only thing here that is not lonely. It has the whole sump to itself.

And then there are the people who live here. Because something does, though not many and not the way the architects intended.

The Basin was built for hundreds. There are switching houses with twenty chairs, a mess hall with a line that could feed a battalion, a locker room with rows of hooks, all empty. What lives here now is a skeleton crew that became the whole body.

Usually three. Sometimes two, sometimes four. They are not stationed here so much as *left* here on rotation so long the rotation stopped meaning anything.

You recognize them before you see their faces by how they move. Slowly. No wasted heat. They wear too many layers, all of it patched workwear, the cuffs black with damp, the knees and elbows shiny. Their gloves are always wet. Their boots are always wet. They have learned not to touch metal with bare skin. Their faces are wind-burned without wind, chapped by the perpetual mist that never quite becomes rain.

They don't talk much, because sound betrays you here. A voice carries too far and comes back late, and it makes the valley feel larger and emptier when you hear your own words return. So they use hand signals, taps on rails, the flick of a headlamp. When they do speak, they speak quietly and close, their breath making a cloud that hangs between them for a second before the draft pulls it apart.

They live in one of the switching houses that they have made habitable — not warm, never warm, but less cold. They have put foam board over the windows that look out onto nothing, hung blankets over the poured walls, put a kettle on a hot plate that never turns off. The kettle is the hearth. They sit around its steam. They have rituals: every morning at the start of the air-cycle, one of them walks the grid with a long squeegee, clearing silt from the drains so the water keeps moving. Another checks the exchangers, listening to the forty-second throb, putting a hand to a pipe to feel if the cold is still being made correctly. The third changes the bulbs. Always one bulb is out somewhere in the constellation, and they change it, not because anyone needs that light, but because if they let one go dark, the grid starts to feel like it is going out one by one.

They have been here long enough that the Basin has started to grow on them, literally. The white lichen finds the seams of their boots. Their lungs hold the mist. Their thoughts have spaced themselves out to match the architecture — long intervals of nothing, then a brief, bright pool of attention.

If you stay long enough to be mistaken for one of them, you will notice they are not really maintaining the Basin. The Basin is maintaining them. It provides water, it provides a little heat by contrast, it provides a task to do every day that prevents them from thinking too much about the scale. They clear the drains for the sedge. The sedge holds the silt for the algae. The algae feeds the moths that die and feed the white thing. The rats follow the cables the humans need. It is a closed loop that was not designed but has arrived anyway, a watershed and a weather system and an ecology, all running on poured stone that was meant to be sterile.

The final thing that lives here is not countable.

It is the Basin itself, as an organism. Not alive, but living in the way a cold marsh lives: by breathing, weeping, and keeping a temperature.

It inhales every forty seconds when the exchangers sigh. It exhales condensation that ticks on every surface. It circulates water from ceiling to floor to drain to sump to pipe to ceiling again without ever needing a river. It grows frost on its girders like hair and sheds it in the afternoon. It makes wind without sky.

You feel it notice you only as a warm spot that needs to be cooled. The draft finds you and lingers. A light above you buzzes a little louder when you stand under it, as if looking.

It does not need you. It was built to contain pressure and it has decided that pressure includes you — your heat, your breath, your sound — and it is very, very patiently diluting you into its cycle. Stand still long enough and your breath joins the mist and falls as rain on the other side of the valley. Your footsteps join the percussion of drips and are forgotten. The lichen will take your boot print and make a new circle from it.

That is why everything here lives far apart and stays still when it can. It has learned the rule of this valley:

Take up as little room as possible. Shiver if you must. Wait. The building will do the rest.
