Who lives there is not obvious at first.

You come up through the hatch expecting to have to search for it. You slip into the warm water and hold yourself still in the center and look - and at first you think the cistern is empty except for the mechanics and the plants and the small quick things.

Because what lives there doesn't dart.

It hangs. It suspends. It belongs so completely to the lit water between things that for a long few minutes you look *through* it.

Then one of the empty spaces moves. Slightly. Not against the current but with a different kind of current.

---

At first it is only a paler part of the water. A long, off-white vacancy the size of a person, folded in on itself. You catch it out of the corner of your eye when a school of silver fish breaks around something that isn't there - parting, reforming, without ever seeming to touch it.

You turn your head and it resolves by degrees.

It is not one shape. But it has a center.

The center is soft and old and built for exactly this. If you had to give it a name from the outside world you would call it a salamander, or a newt, or an axolotl that forgot to grow up. But that is only the shape it borrowed. This one kept its childhood and then kept going.

She is about as long as your forearm and twice as wide when she spreads herself open. Her skin is the color of the tiles after a century underwater - not white, but that deep, tea-stained ceramic cream, translucent at the edges. You can see the map of her inside: the faint rose of gills, the darker line of gut full of silt and lily petal, the small, hard knot of a heart ticking very slow, in time with the pistons. Her outer skin is not smooth. The warm mineral water has given her a texture. A fine fur of that same velvet moss that carpets the walls has colonized the high points of her - the crown of her head, the ridge of her back, the feathery external gills that fan from her neck like three pairs of water-vines, pale and veined and waving even when she is still. Shrimp live there. They pick.

Her eyes are small and black and not very useful. She does not need them. Light is everywhere above, sound is everywhere through the tiles. She navigates by feel, by the way the pump gyre presses on her flank.

When she moves, she does not swim so much as she *unfolds*. A slow undulation that starts at the tip of her tail - broad, flattened like a lily pad - and travels forward through her body until her forelimbs, absurdly small and hand-like, with four fingers that end in black nails, paddle once, twice, and then she is gliding again. She hangs in the sun shafts to warm her blood. She lets the big slow circular current carry her. If you watch long enough you realize she is never really swimming at all; the room is swimming her, turning her in that lazy gyre past the hanging vine-curtains, past the wall of pearling moss, past the black iron shoulders of the pump.

She is the caretaker.

Not because anyone appointed her. Because the room made a job and she grew into it.

She tends the lilies. You will see her nose up to one of the huge white flowers that has opened underwater - a flower that should not open underwater - and worry at it with her soft mouth, not eating, but pruning. She takes the spent petals that would otherwise rot and clot the spillway and lets them go, and they spiral down around her like snow. She crawls along the tiled floor on her belly, between the grout valleys filled with black silt, and roots there, fanning silt with her tail to keep the anemone-filters from choking.

And she tends the machines. This is the strangest thing until you understand that to her they are not machines. They are the warm springs. The brass pipe that exhales a constant plume of hotter water, the one with a lily rooted directly in its mouth, is her fireplace. She will wedge her long body against it in the coldest hour before dawn and let it breathe over her gills. The great pistons - CHUFF - hiss - CHUFF - rise and fall, encrusted in lime and rust and the same moss that grows on her. She climbs them. She rests in the yoke where the piston meets its housing, where the vibration is strongest, and the shrimp that live on her commute over to clean the iron, and the shrimp that live on the iron commute over to clean her. The chain conveyor that clanks endlessly, lifting silt and leaf litter from the bottom - she rides it. She hooks a limb over a rung and lets it carry her up, up through the water column, and then she lets go at the top and drifts down again through the falling debris, mouth open, feeding.

She is not alone, exactly. What lives there is not a single animal but a *condition* that has learned to keep itself.

She is the large, slow thought. Around her are the small quick thoughts: the hundreds of silver fish that are her attendants and sometimes her food, the blind white eels that are really just extensions of the pump-works, thin as cables, that slip in and out of copper joints and never leave the dark, the plum-sized diving beetles that row from vine to vine carrying oxygen bubbles like lanterns, the carp as long as your arm who have grown barbeled and thoughtful and territorial and who give her a wide berth. Above, in the dry lip of the vault where water never reaches, the swallows live - four, six at a time - mud nests tucked into bolt-holes, lined with rust flake and vine fiber. They are the only part of her that leaves. They punch down through the mirrored surface like needles, trail bubbles, snap a fish, and burst back out through the sky into the thin air. She watches them from below, eyes up, seeing them as dark, swift tears in her ceiling.

What lives there is amphibious in the oldest sense. It has gills *and* lungs. Like the newts crawling on the tiles, she can breathe water and air. On warm afternoons she will haul herself - impossibly slow, with great effort - half out of the water onto the sloped lip of tile just under the oculus, and lie there with her forelimbs folded, her mossy back exposed to normal sun, breathing the thin mountain air in great, sighing gulps while her gills collapse wet against her neck. She looks, in those moments, poured. Like the mountain. Resolved. Not ruined. If you were to come upon her then you might mistake her for a piece of the cistern itself, a smooth bone of handrail, a fallen vine.

She has been there long enough that she no longer feels introduced, even though every inch of her was introduced. She was carried once, perhaps, as an egg on a swallowed leaf, or as a larva in a maintenance worker's bucket. Now the cistern is not a tank that holds her; she is the way the cistern holds itself together. Without her grazing the moss would close over the tiles, without her pruning the lilies would mat the surface and block the sun, without her weight on the conveyor the silt would settle too thick and smother the filters. The pumps would still CHUFF and hiss, but they would be alone in their work.

If you stay very still in the center, suspended, warm to the point of not knowing where your skin ends, she will eventually forget you are a visitor. The gyre will bring her around again. She will not see you. She will feel the displacement you make in the water and correct for it as she corrects for a vine. She may pass within an arm's length, gills fanned, tail undulating, shrimp riding her like pale seeds, eyes on nothing, going about her unhurried circuit of vine and iron and flower and light.

You will feel, in that pass, that the room has a resident not *in* it but *of* it. That the warm water, the light shaft, the thrumming tile, the swaying vine, the slow piston breath - all of it - has learned how to be one animal, and that animal is hanging right there in front of you, looking through you, pearling a single bubble of oxygen from its mossy back that rises, wobbling, up to join the sky.
