No one lives here the way a person lives in a house.

At first you think nothing can. That's the initial wrongness you feel in your chest with that warm, shallow breath — the place should be sterile. Too high, too young, too sharp. Too loud with iron.

And then you correct yourself: not nothing. Not *no one*. But nothing that lives *like you do.*

### The One Who Tends It

He is the easiest to see because he almost isn't here.

You find him in the negatives — the warm rubber boots, the tin cup with the skin still soft on the tea, the wrench vibrating inch by inch toward the edge of the transformer. Someone has to keep the heat on. Without the heat, the bowl dies.

He doesn't live here. No one could live here full-time. The air, even warm, is still thin; sleeping here would pound your heart to pieces. So he lives below the clouds and commutes up. A day-man. An old mining tender, maybe, or the son of one. You can picture the type of lungs he must have, the type of calm. He comes up three times a week on foot or on a mule that knows the knife-ridge trail, checks oil, rakes slime off the cistern grate, bleeds the boiler, writes numbers in a logbook that swells and cockles in the humidity. He has a way of moving that the plants have learned.

He is not adapted to the altitude. He is adapted to the *room*. His skin is always a little damp here. His joints ache less here than down below because of the warmth. His ears ring for hours afterward from the 50 hertz. He has stopped trying to keep the place tidy. Early on he laid that trail of flat stones, strung that clothesline. Now he lets the vine take the pegs. He knows better than to prune what grows against the pipes — last time he pulled a root off the hot-water line, the pipe sweated and then burst. Now he understands: the moss is insulation.

He leaves before dark if he can. He doesn't like to hear the pressure valve shriek when the mist has closed in and he can't see the walls. He doesn't like how the tadpole pools go still when he steps near them.

He is the only human animal that can stand to be here for more than an hour, and even he only borrows the place. He is not what lives here. He is what *feeds* what lives here.

### The Green That Lives Here

The true resident is the mat.

Not the species — the *mat itself*. Singular. At sea level you would call it ground cover, understory. Here, in a bowl thirty meters across with no outlet and no winter, it has become a colonial body. It has no center and no edge, only thicknesses.

It lives the way a reef lives. Layer on layer. The giant moss at the bottom holds water like a sponge and makes peat out of its own dead. The fern grows out of the moss's back. The bromeliad grows out of the fern's crotch and holds a perfect cup of sky-water for the tadpoles. The vine runs horizontally across all of them, not climbing toward light but toward heat, feeling its way along the black cables and the sweating pipes and stapling itself to them with aerial roots that drink condensation.

It knows where the heat is. That's the uncanny part you saw — the fronds all combed toward the boiler, like compass needles. Away from the sun. It has learned the shape of the machines faster than any animal could. Where a pipe runs under peat, the peat is a degree warmer and the moss there is twice as thick, a raised green welt. Where the transformer hums, the leaves vibrate faintly and grow smaller, tougher. It has incorporated iron into its diet. You can see it: leaves with rust freckles, roots that have followed a weeping rust-stain down the scarp and turned the color of dried blood.

It is not growing quickly. It is growing *inevitably*. If you stood still for a day you would feel its explorers: a tendril testing your boot lace, a film of protonema greening the toe of those warm rubber boots by evening. It does not compete. There is no need. It just fills any volume that is not hot iron or moving water. It closes around the headframe until the shaft is a throat lined with green. It is young, like the rock, and so it has not yet learned to be limited.

It lives by drinking the air itself.

### The Iron That Lives Here

And then there is the other body that never leaves.

It sits against the scarp and breathes. Boiler, diesel, transformer — you think of them as three things but here they are one metabolism. The boiler makes steam, the steam keeps the peat from freezing, the peat holds the water that feeds the boiler. The diesel mutters and makes the charge that makes the transformer sing, and that 50 hertz drone is its heartbeat — you feel it in your molars, in the peat through the soles of your feet. It never stops. Even idled down at night it is warm to the touch, ticking, sweating, letting off that sweet sulfurous haze that hangs knee-high and makes the lowest leaves curl.

It lives the way a furnace lives, which is to say it has to be fed and it excretes heat. The tender feeds it diesel hauled up by hand, and it excretes the single condition that allows anything else to live up here: frostlessness. Without its low fever, the bowl would be what it should be — a sterile melt-pool six months a year, ice-cracked basalt, lichen. With it, it is a greenhouse cut into the sky.

It is not yet overgrown. It is *being* overgrown. Roots have found the felt lagging and pried it. A flowering vine has grown *through* the corrugated shed, in one hole and out the other, using the perforated steel sheets as a trellis. Green slime has sealed a leaking joint better than any gasket. The distinction you thought was sharp — plant vs. machine — is not holding. They are grafting.

### The Children That Never Leave

The water holds the last residents, the ones that prove how closed the room is.

The tadpoles. There are hundreds of them in every bromeliad cup and peat pool, hanging motionless in water that is always the right temperature. Too many. No dragonflies. No frogs calling back to that single thin bird that crosses and never returns. In a normal mountain pond they would be frogs by August and gone. Here there is no August. No season at all, just warm mist and transformer light. So they stay tadpoles. Some are as long as your thumb, with feathery gills still waving, eyes too large. Neotenic. They never metamorphose because they never need to. The bowl holds them.

Under the cistern, in the green-slime lip where the water overflows without rippling, something else moves water from below. Not wind. A slow, rhythmic push. If you crouched a long time you might see the slime pulse. A bacterial mat, iron-oxidizing, breathing. It stains the concrete orange-black and smells faintly of blood and hot rock. It lives directly on the mineral breath that comes up the shaft — that steady, too-hot upwelling you felt over the headframe. It was here before the tender, before the peat, riding the geothermal sigh. Now the cistern gives it a perfect mouth to live in.

And the beetle, that too-large iridescent green-black thing working its way across the pipe — it is not a resident. It is a tourist that got lost above the clouds, found the heat and stayed a little too long. Its shell is beaded with condensation. It will die here. The mat will grow over it in a week.

---

So this is who lives in the room:

A man who only visits to keep a fever going.

A green colonial body that drinks air and eats rust and orients to heat like a prayer.

An iron body that breathes heat and sings at 50 hertz and is slowly being digested by the green.

And a nursery of things that never grow up, suspended in warm water, tended by a slime that breathes rock.

None of them could live here alone. Together they make a small, complete world, sealed above the white sea of clouds — a terrarium that built itself around a wound in the mountain. Young, raw, sharp and soft at once, ticking as it cools, sighing as you step on it, steaming gently while it learns how to be alive.

If you stay until the mist closes and the boiler cycles down, you will hear all of them at once and briefly, for an evening, you will be one of them too — another warm body in the room, beading with condensation, already being measured by a vine.
