Who lives there hasn't finished arriving yet.

That's the first thing you understand, even before you count them.

You expect when you come up the cut — lungs still rinsing themselves with that thin, cold air, hands still cold from the raw basalt lip — to find either no one, or a ruin. A place this new should be empty. A place this planned should be full. Instead you find something in between, and at first you can't see them at all because they are the same color and speed as everything else.

Then the cloud thins for a moment and you catch movement. Slow movement.

### The Thirty

There are perhaps thirty of them. Maybe thirty-one if someone is sleeping behind a shutter. You will never get an exact number because they don't gather.

At a distance, you might think they are monks. Then you get closer and you think, no — gardeners. Then closer still, midwives is closer. Caretakers is closest.

They are human. Entirely human, but uneroded, the way the mesa is uneroded. No sharpness worn off them yet.

Physiologically they have become a high place. Broad chests under thick, felted wool the color of wet lichen — undyed greens and grays and the pale brown of sphagnum in winter. Cheeks always a little red, not from exertion but from the perpetual cold and the work of breathing air that has so little to give. They are short and solid, not stunted but compact, like the alders — built low to keep from being taken by the wind over the edge. Their hair is kept cut short, or bound back, because otherwise it would never dry. Their hands are the most telling: wide, damp, the fingernails soft and always a little dark at the rims from stone dust and moss. No callus the way a lowland quarryman would have it — their callus is even, spongy, from handling wet wood and wet stone all day. Joints that ache early. Lungs you can hear if you stand near them in the quiet — a faint, patient wheeze on the inhale, then a long, complete exhale that fogs and vanishes.

They do not hurry. They can't. The air forbids it and the stone teaches against it. A man levering a basalt block on the eastern wall will spend an entire morning dressing one face with a stone maul and an antler pick, tapping, listening, tapping again. A woman will sit on a plank walkway above a bog-pan and card wool for hours with a pair of wooden combs, her breathing synchronized to the ticking of water from the moss-furred rowan above her. When they speak to each other across the too-wide streets, they don't shout. They wait until they are close. Their voices are low, a little hoarse from the damp, and they say little. There is no need to fill the acoustic.

Because the town is an amphitheater, you hear them anyway. The scrape of a slate blade on alder. The soft thud of bare or felt-booted feet on saturated moss. A child's laugh — there are four or five children — that bounces off the empty two-story houses and comes back uncannily loud, as if the town itself laughed back.

They live dispersed in that grid made for ten times their number. No crowding, no center of gravity. One family to a row where six were planned. They sweep the empty houses as well as their own. You will see an old woman open the door of a house no one sleeps in, sweep its clean floor, wring out the moss that has crept in over the sill, and close the door again. Not out of fear of ghosts, but out of housekeeping. The houses are not abandoned; they are *kept*. Doors are left ajar. Floors have a single mat, a single low fire-bowl, a single carving on the shutter. The rest is waiting space.

They are not lonely. Loneliness requires an expectation of fullness. They have the opposite — an understanding of extensiveness. You see it in how they use the plaza. The cistern in the middle is always full to spilling because the cloud never stops weeping into it, and someone — different each day — without being asked, lifts the wooden sluice to let the excess sheet across the paving stones toward the edge, where it will find its first, tentative way off the mesa. It is not a chore assigned. It is just what you do when you notice the water is high.

They share work the way water shares a surface. There is the Wall Crew — three people, sometimes two — who have been building that eastern wall for months, block by block. There is the Weaving Shed where two women and an old man keep a loom going, making that thick, water-shedding wool. There is the Granary on stilts, which holds far more than the thirty need, its bins meticulously dry despite the damp, checked every morning for condensation. They store for a future they are not anxious about. They talk about it the way you talk about a season that is coming next year. *"When there are more hands, the north terrace will go up faster."* Not *if*.

Their children are the most adapted. They do not play in the streets so much as in the moss itself. They know which mats will hold their weight and which bog-pans will quake and suck at an ankle. They pull cotton-grass heads apart and let the wind take them over the edge. They know the exact note a basalt column makes when you tap it — a clear, glassy *ping* — and they run along the cliff edge tapping, making music no adult would think to make. They are not kept from the edge. The edge is the boundary of their world, sheer and absolute, and they understand it the way a child raised by a shoreline understands surf. They lie on their bellies and watch the thin braids of water go over, disappearing a thousand feet down to the warm haze below.

At night they sleep shallow and together in the lower floors, because the upper floors are damp and because heat is scarce. No hearth roars here — alder burns cool and a little sour — so they sleep close, under felt. You can hear the town breathing then: the constant percussion of drips from leaf to leaf to plank to stone, the low creak once or twice a winter when the whole uplifted plate settles a fraction of a millimeter deeper into itself, the wind scouring the empty streets.

### What Else Lives There

It would be wrong to say only the thirty live there.

The actual dominant resident is moss. Not as decoration but as citizen. It thatches every roof until you can't tell where building ends and ground begins. It seams the walls and softens the quarry marks. It swallows sound and then releases it as water. It is tended, not removed. People pull it from drains when it chokes them, and press it back onto a wind-scoured corner where the basalt shows through. They understand that without it, the thin new almost-soil would wash right off the plate in a single storm.

Under and over the moss, everything else that can tolerate cold wet light has come obscenely fast. Ferns the length of your forearm unfurling from every crack, each frond beaded and ticking with water. Pale lichen scaling the north sides of houses. Sphagnum and cotton-grass making perfect, quaking miniature bogs between paving stones. The stunted alders and rowans, each one a hanging garden, their bark invisible under three inches of green fur, their branches dripping even when the cloud lifts.

Animal life is still deciding whether to join. Midges appear in a silent, hanging cloud over the warmest bog pan at noon and vanish by evening. A raven crosses — always alone — and its croak is an event that makes everyone look up, because sound carries so unnervingly well between the hard new cliffs. And somewhere in the moss, the wide, splayed, three-toed print of a high grazer — *takin* or something like it — presses deep and fills slowly with water. You will see the print fresh in the morning and never see the animal that made it. It came up the switchbacks in the night, drank from the spilling cistern, and left.

So what lives there, finally, is a collaboration of the too-early and the too-patient.

A new mountain that hasn't learned to be a mountain yet.

A forest that arrived before soil did.

An infrastructure that arrived before its people did.

And thirty slow, damp, broad-chested people who arrived in time to be early — who build with hand tools at the speed of moss, who keep empty houses clean, who listen more than they speak, who have made a pact, whether they know it or not, to live at the tempo of geology until biology and demography catch up.

They are not stranded. They climbed. Or they were born to those who climbed. They chose the plate with no rivers because the water stays, and the light is good, and the stone rings, and there is room.

If you stay long enough to be offered tea — alder-smoked water, bitter herbs grown in a cistern-side box — one of them will ask you, without irony, how many came up with you. And when you say, "Just me," they will nod as if you have answered correctly, and look past you toward the clean, sharp, empty edge of the mesa, where the water is still learning how to fall.
