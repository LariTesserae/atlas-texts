No one lives there at first.

That's what it wants you to think, and for the first hour you believe it. The room is too empty, too evenly lit. The distances are too great. The few things that move - a cricket, a swift cutting through the column, a beetle polishing its way across the silt - all look like visitors. Like they blew in over the lip and haven't found their way out yet. If you stand at the center where the sun hits, your own shadow feels like the only thing that belongs to you.

Stay longer than an hour. Stay past the time when the novelty of the light wears off and your eyes adjust to the actual scale of things. That's when the room stops being empty.

The first residents you notice are the small ones because they are allowed to be seen.

On any given meter of wall there are ten lives. The cream cave crickets folded into the flutes, their antennae testing air that hasn't moved in a decade. The geckoes pressed so flat to the limestone you think they're a stain until one blinks with an eye that is too wet, too white, a little moon that catches the reflected glow and throws it back. The paper wasps in their single cells under the lip, each cell a perfect hexagon built grain by grain from chewed stone and fig fiber. The swifts are not residents, they're commuters, but their mud cups are - dark crescents tucked high where the wind can't scour.

On the floor, the pill bugs like bleached pearls. The black beetles. The ants.

None of them live *there* the way a tenant lives in a house. They live *on* it. They occupy surfaces. The place tolerates them the way a large, warm rock tolerates lichen. Which means there has to be something else underneath. Something that lives *in* it.

You start to see the signs that there is a single life the whole room is organized around. Not a species. An individual.

First: the water. The rock sweats, beads, runs, falls *plip* into a palm-sized hollow. A dozen micro-springs, never in the same place twice. But the moss only grows where the drip lands, and the moss grows in coins, perfect circles - and if you map them, they are not random. They form a loose spiral around the center, widening out. Each coin is exactly as far from its neighbor as a long, slow reach would allow. As if something has been tending them by drinking from one hollow until it dries and then moving - millimetrically - to the next.

Second: the workshop. The rust-red ants at the base of the sideways fig with their midden of chert flakes. Watch them long enough and you'll see they aren't using the flakes where they found them. They're carrying them. Always inward. Always toward the deepest part of the floor, where the travertine concaves and the scalloped rimstone has formed a shallow, terraced basin about as wide as a sleeping man. They carry a flake, they saw at a leaf, they abandon the flake. The next day a different ant retrieves the same flake and carries it a little closer to the basin. Over weeks, the flakes migrate. It's not efficient. It's devotional.

Third: the light. You wave your hand through the column and your shadow on the far wall lags. The dust hangs a fraction too long, as if the air had thickened. It's not the heat. It's not a trick of the eye. If you crouch for a long time in the basin's shadow and hold your breath, you can see why.

She is the color of the wall.

That is her first defense and her final one. When she is still, which is almost always, she is not an animal at all. She is a continuation of the limestone. A low, broad upwelling of travertine that shouldn't be there, tucked into the undercut where the wall curves inward and the seep-stain runs darkest. Her back is cream and powdery, pitted with the same pocks and flutes as the rock around her, crusted at the edges with the same pale sage lichen that grows on the stone. She has been growing that lichen for years. It grows on her.

She is not large the way a predator is large. She is large the way a piece of furniture is large. Wide and settled and low to the ground. If you stretched your arms out you could not quite span her.

She is most of the way to being a salamander, and the rest of the way to being something the surface forgot. Imagine an olm or a cave newt that never stopped growing. Her skin is translucent at the margins - when the light is right you can see the faint rose of blood beneath it, and the slow pulse of a heart that beats perhaps four times a minute. Her head is flattened, blunt, wider than it should be, with no external eyes as you understand eyes. Where eyes should be there are two domed, lidless moons, white and opalescent, sunk deep and covered with a fine, dusty film. They reflect too much light. That is what you saw in the geckoes; you were seeing her in miniature. She does not look at you. She senses the interruption in the warm pooled air, the change in the hum over the oculus when a body blocks wind.

She has four limbs, but they are not for walking now. They are splayed and thick, their toes webbed and fused into broad pads that have half-melted into the rimstone. She has not walked in a very long time. She breathes without opening a mouth - a slow, almost invisible inflation along her flanks, a drawing in of the warm air that pools in the bowl. When she exhales, ten minutes later, the dust in the column shivers.

Her mouth is on the underside, close to the stone, a soft, wide slit that rests directly over the central hollow where all the *plips* eventually find their way. She doesn't hunt. She doesn't need to. The beetles come to her. The crickets, when they molt and are soft and stupid, blunder toward the condensation she exhales. The ants bring her flakes - not as food, but as tools she has taught them to bring. Once, every few days, she extends a tongue the color of wet silt - impossibly long, impossibly slow - and draws a flake into that slit, using it to scrape the lichen from a patch of her own back where it has grown too thick. Then she lets the flake fall. An ant will come for it tomorrow.

She is why the place erases craft. She borrows it.

She is why the light is viscous. From the rim of the fig, from the undercut where her brow ridges, she has strung a few nearly invisible lines - not a web to catch, but a harp. Single threads of silk, or something like silk but wetter, strung vertically through the entire volume of the chamber from lip to floor. You will never see them straight on. You see them only at noon when the dust hangs on them. They are the reason your shadow lags; it catches for a half-second on the lattice. She laid them when the roof first fell, and has tended them grain by grain ever since, tightening when the wind hums too loud, loosening when the stone ticks with heat.

She is old. Older than the fig, which fell as a seed fifty years ago. Older than the swifts' great-grandmothers. The scalloped terracing on the floor is her doing, in part - mineral deposited from the water she has breathed on, evaporated, breathed on again. Each hair's-width rim is a year. You could count them like rings if you could get close enough, but you won't. The air near her is several degrees warmer than the rest of the bowl, and it smells faintly of wet stone and fig sap, and your body understands before your mind does that you are in a mouth.

She is not hostile. She has no concept for it. She is patient in a way that is not patience but geology. When the column lifts off the floor in late afternoon and climbs the opposite wall, her white moons catch the last ellipse and glow for an hour after, and that is the sourceless luminosity you thought was the rock remembering the sun. It is her, exhaling light.

If you stay until that hour and you are very quiet - not the held-breath quiet of a watcher, but the real quiet of something that has decided to belong for a little while - she will open her mouth a fraction. Not to eat. Just to let a single bead of condensation fall from her lower lip back into the basin with a *plip* that is exactly, precisely, the same sound the rock makes. An answer.

The beetles keep polishing. The ants keep sorting. The fig keeps reaching, a millimeter a week, for a sun it will never quite touch.

And under the undercut, the color of the wall, she waits for the next drip to find her.
