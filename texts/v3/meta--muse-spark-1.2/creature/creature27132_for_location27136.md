It lives alone.

That's the first thing you understand, before you see it. It follows the rule of the room. Everything in here is set apart from everything else with a halo of empty water around it — the pump housings, the plinth, the chain fall — and whatever lives here has learned to keep that distance. It doesn't crowd. It doesn't school. When the small fish part, when the shrimp freeze, they are parting and freezing *around* something.

You don't see it at first because it isn't moving. And in this water, you see movement.

You hang in the middle of the aisle, letting your bubbles rattle up to that trapped lens at the ceiling, and you let the silt settle again. The pumps thump. *Thunk* — seven seconds — *thunk.* The flywheel squeals. The water makes its slow north-to-south circuit, pulling fine particles in a deliberate orbit around the room. And then you notice that one orbit is wrong.

In the lee of the middle pump — the biggest one, the one with the eel living in its throat — there is a shape that should be shadow and isn't. A long, low mound on top of the housing where there should be flat iron. Silt has settled on it, the same pale blanket that covers everything, so its outline is softened. For a long minute you think it's just accretion, mineral growth, a place where the concrete and lime have built up into a false form.

Then the silt puffs.

Just once. A small, deliberate puff from one end, as if something exhaled through the silt and disturbed it. The cloud hangs, and you see two filaments move.

Whiskers.

They are longer than you thought anything's whiskers could be. Two, then four, then six — pale, fleshy barbels as thick as your finger at the base and tapering to nothing, as long as your forearm, maybe longer. They uncurl from the silted mound and taste the moving water. They don't wave randomly like the asbestos ribbons or the little kelp. They *seek*. One traces the edge of the pump housing. One lifts and combs the current coming off the flywheel. One sweeps down and brushes the black shell of a mussel and the mussel shuts.

And then the mound breathes.

It heaves — a slow, whole-body contraction that lifts silt off its back in a sheet — and settles again, heavier. You realize how big it is. It is draped over the top of that streetcar-sized pump housing like a blanket thrown over a machine. Its head is at the front, broad and flat as a shovel, wider than your chest. Its body runs back along the housing for two meters, maybe more, tapering down where the pipework disappears into the wall. It is the color of the room itself — not fish-silver, not eel-black, but that mottled, mineral grey-pink of old concrete under silt, of rust bled to nothing, of ceramic tile gone to cream. Mottled with darker blotches that match exactly the pitting on the walls. When it doesn't move, you could look straight at it and see only pump.

This is the one the room was made for, without ever being made for it.

It's a catfish. Or something that became a catfish after it came down here. A wels, maybe — the kind that can live a hundred years cold and dark under reservoirs and dams, that never stops growing if nothing stops it. The kind they say lives under the locks and gets as big as a man.

Its skin is not scaly. It's naked, smooth, and covered in that same hard lime accretion as the walls, in patches, so that small white anemones and even a couple of tiny mussels have taken hold on its back and shoulders, riding it like it is just another piece of the floor. Its eyes are there — two small, dark beads set wide on that flat head — but they are clouded, shrunken, filmed over. It doesn't use them. Light from the shaft never reaches this corner anyway. When you drift a little closer, pulling yourself hand over hand on the ceiling rail so you don't kick silt, you see the frill behind its head pulse.

Not gills like a normal fish. External, feathered, pale pink-grey branches that open and close with its breath, fanned in the current from the pump. Like the ribbons of lagging. They wave even when it is still.

It lives here the way the flywheel lives here — by keeping in rhythm.

Every seven seconds the pipes hammer. *Thunk.* The whole vault shudders, the pressure changes, the trapped air lens at the ceiling shivers. A split second before each *thunk*, those barbels stiffen. The head lifts a fraction. It has learned, over decades, to read the hammer as a clock and a tide. When the hammer comes, food is stirred. Shrimp lose their footing on the grating. Small fish tumble. The current reverses for half a second. It doesn't chase. It waits. It will make one slow sweep of the aisle in an hour, maybe two, moving with a lazy, whole-body undulation that doesn't seem to hurry at all, its vast tail barely flexing, its whiskers combing ahead of it for inches, for miles in this water. It will pass the workbench, the control pulpit, the place where you are hanging, and it will not deviate. The small fish part around its head the way they part around pipes. The shrimp go still.

It has made a nest without making a nest. The depression on top of the pump where it lies is worn smooth, polished by the weight and slow slide of its belly. The iron there is shiny, free of silt, free of mussels. It fits. That pump housing puts out a faint heat — friction and those old relays — and in twelve-degree water, faint heat is everything. It lies in that warm spot and breathes the water the pump pushes over its frills. It is not the eel's predator, not exactly. The eel lives *in* the pump's mouth, the catfish lives *on* its back. They have an agreement you cannot parse.

How long has it been here? Long enough to smooth. Like every corner in the room, it has no sharp edges. Its fins are ragged and then rounded, its barbels are nicked, its lip is scarred where it once scraped a bolt head. You can see layers of growth in its blotches, the way you can see lifts in the poured concrete. It came down as something small — perhaps it slipped through the shaft when the place was still trying to be dry, a fingerling carried in on a flood, or someone's abandoned pet — and it found a stable new thing. A dry hall inverted, keeping water moving forever, full of food that circulates and never quite leaves. A cathedral with a slow current and no seasons. It grew to fit the volume that was available, to fill the emptiness without filling it up. If you drained this room, it would die in air. If you turned the pumps off, it would die in stillness.

It knows you are here, of course. Everything in here knows by vibration.

While you have been watching, one of those long barbels has turned and is pointing directly at you. Not wavering. Still. Feeling your regulator bubbles, your heart hammer transmitted through water. The flat head hasn't moved. It doesn't need to look. It just tastes the change you have made in its circulation.

It doesn't come to investigate. It is too old to be curious quickly. It lets the current bring you a little closer if you drift, and the water carries the faint, cold, metallic smell of it to you — mud and iron and old skin.

If you stay still, it will decide you are another piece of sparse equipment that has fallen into the room, another control pulpit or chain fall. It will breathe, silt will settle back over its back, and it will disappear again into being pump-housing, being wall.

If you kick, if you puff silt toward it, the whole, impossible length of it will uncoil with a single, powerful flex that pushes a wall of water at you, and it will be in the middle of the aisle where you were, hanging empty, tasting the water where you were with all six barbels at once, before you have even had time to find a handhold.

It lives here because nothing else could live here so completely. It is not a visitor like the eel, not a grazer like the shrimp. It *is* the room now — the soft, living counterpart to the slow flywheel, the thing that keeps moving because it was never told to stop.
