Nothing lives here at first.

That is the first and most honest reading of the basin. Whatever made the city left, or finished, or died trying to be jungle where jungle cannot afford to be. The buildings are empty bowls. The stalks are failed plants. The water moves by itself. You wait for a scuttle, a call, a footprint that isn't your own, and nothing answers except another drip into another canal. The place has the patience of something that has already outlived its occupants.

Then you stay a little longer, and that reading thins.

You realize you have been looking for the wrong kind of living.

It does not move the way an animal moves. It does not separate itself from where it lives. In a place with no sun and no dryness and no quickness, the idea of a body that walks around inside a house becomes absurd, an affectation. Why would you build a shell to leave it?

The buildings are not houses. They are bodies. Or they are the excretion of bodies. Both are true at different times of year.

Who lives here lives *in* the walls.

If you put your palm to the pitted, breathing bio-cement in the dark and hold it there until your own heat goes quiet, you feel it. Not a heartbeat. Slower. A peristalsis. A warm, interior give, like pressing on a closed lung. The thick walls are not solid. They are layered, accreted, thumbprinted because they were pushed into place while still soft. Living plaster, then, and living still. Each dome is a single organism that has learned to be architecture.

In the damp heat you can almost see it if you stop trying to see. The mass is not limestone, not clay. It is a mineralized sheath secreted outward. Inside, the inhabitant is still there.

It is not small.

Imagine a thing the size of two or three men curled together, but without any hard parts to give it that size. No skeleton, no shell, no joint. A mass of dense, translucent muscle the color of old water, dark amber and veined with the same black silt that rims the canals. When it is at rest - which is most of its life - it fills its dome completely, pressed to the inner curve like a snail that has grown to precisely fit its shell, its outer skin fused to the inner wall in a hundred places where it secretes and drinks. Its underside is a broad, flat foot that rests on the warm silt and stone beneath the dome, not moving, exchanging water endlessly. You can see the ghost of it through the thinner parts of the wall where the bio-cement sweats - a darker shadow, a slow roll.

It has no face. That is the next thing you have to unlearn. There is no front. Along what you would call its back, if it had one, are shallow pits that open and close with the drip-weather, drinking condensation directly from the air that weeps through the porous wall. Along its belly are frilled gills - pale, feathery, endlessly fanned - that lie in the inch-deep black mirror under the dome, filtering the mineral water that never stops arriving. And scattered across its entire surface, like freckles, are simple, lightless eyes. Not eyes that see. Pits that feel pressure and heat and the nearness of other domes. It does not need to see the basin. The basin is dark for everyone. It feels the whole dish as a map of wetness and warmth and weight.

It does not walk. It extrudes.

When it needs to be larger, which might be once a decade, it softens a section of its wall with a warm, acidic sweat and pushes. A ripple travels through that dark amber mass and a new lip of living mud is laid down from the inside, pressed smooth by a broad, blind pseudopod that leaves those rippled thumbprints you thought were human. Then it cures in the hot dark, hardens, becomes wall. The dome grows a centimeter. The organism inside grows with it. This is how the city was built, not all at once but over a time so long the hills had time to slump while it worked. You thought the buildings were spaced too far apart. They are not. Each needs its dish of empty flat around it to collect enough condensation to drink, to keep its water table breathing. If two domes grew too close their drinking pits would compete and both would dry. The emptiness is load-bearing because it is *territory*, not planning. The avenues are the truces between thirsts.

What you took for plants are parts of them, too. The pale, paper-colored stalks with the closed buds are not plants at all but sensory tendrils the domed ones send out through cracks in their own walls, a foot or two into the open air, tasting the humidity. The bud never opens because it is not a flower; it is a closed nose. The black lichen that pulses flat over stone is shed skin, alive on its own for a while, still exhaling moisture after it sloughs. Even the strange stilt-roots with no tree are the oldest foot-edges of domes that moved - or rather, flowed - a few meters in a century and left their lifted footprints behind like empty ribs.

They do not speak. The dripping is their speech, or the part of it you can hear. A drop falling from an eave hits a canal at a particular tempo and the vibration travels through the continuous inch-deep water that connects every street that was never a street. The gill-frills feel it. A slow pattern - plink... plink-plink... plink - means *I am here, I am wet, keep your distance.* A longer, irregular gurgle where water impossibly climbs uphill is an old one shifting in its sleep, redirecting flow the way you turn over in bed. They have been conversing this way since before the sky became a lid.

You will not see one move. If you watch a dome for an hour you will swear it is stone. Come back after a year and it will be a hand's breadth to the left, and the silt before it will be furrowed as if something infinitely heavy had been dragged without lifting. They move at the pace of mineral and heat. They remember gradients that no longer exist and follow them because their bodies are water diviners.

And at the center, where they leave no building, is why.

The perfect circular pool that is too still and too deep is not empty. It is the communal place. It is where they go, very rarely, to divide. When a dome has drunk enough heat and enough iron-tanged condensation for long enough - a century, two - it loosens its foot, becomes briefly more liquid than solid, and flows, incredibly slowly, downhill toward the center, leaving its old dome behind as an empty, slowly weathering bowl that you mistook for a ruin. In the black pool it meets others. They do not mate. They *pool*. Edges dissolve in that water that reflects a depth that is not there, and for a time there is one thing in the basin instead of many. Then it separates again, and one more dome than before crawls away in a new direction to find an empty flat and begin secreting a new wall around itself. The pale stalks around the pool lean away because the water there is too full to drink.

If you stand at the edge of that pool long enough, perfectly still, and let your own breathing become the only animal sound again, you will see the surface which had no ripples finally break without a drop falling.

Something vast, dark-amber and slick, will crest just enough to expose a field of those dim pits to the dark air, tasting you. It will not hurry. It has nowhere to be that water is not already going. It will regard you the way a hill regards weather - as a brief, interesting change in condensation - and then sink again, without a ripple, because ripples here are only for speaking.
