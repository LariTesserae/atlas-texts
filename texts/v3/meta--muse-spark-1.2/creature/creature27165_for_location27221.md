Who lives there is not obvious at first.

If you come over the rim at midday, when the light is at its most plain and high, you might think at first that no one does. The bowl holds its shape so perfectly - the terraces clean, the walls plumb, the yard swept - that it looks tended in the way an abandoned shrine stays tended for a while after people leave. By momentum. The goats standing perfectly still in their pen look like part of the stone. The smoke from the low chimneys is so thin in that clear air you have to look for it.

Then a door thuds. A broom scrapes. And you realize the quiet was not emptiness. It was occupancy at a very low volume.

They are not many. Thirty structures would suggest thirty families anywhere else, but here a structure is just as likely to be a cistern housing, a byre, a wall that is just a wall. There are perhaps forty or fifty of them in total, old and young together. Enough that every house has people in it, but not enough that any house is crowded. You can count them by the lamps at dusk - one warm shallow bowl of light per dwelling, seventeen or eighteen lights in the two crescents, and then dark between.

They look like they were made for the bowl, or by it, over a long time.

In build they are spare and compact, not tall. Men and women both broad through the chest and shoulders - you see it in the way their undyed wool coats hang straight from yoke to hem. It's the air. At first you breathe a little too fast and feel a little light-headed until you learn, as they have, to do everything more slowly. They walk slowly. They lift slowly. They speak slowly, in low voices that do not try to fill the bowl, because sound dies here anyway. Children learn early not to shout - a shouted word just goes flat against stone. They run for a few steps and then stop, hands on knees, laughing without much breath, and then walk.

Their skin is weathered in a particular way. Not sun-browned - the light here is cool and thin - but wind-burnished, the cheeks and the backs of the hands reddened permanently as if by a cold burn. Their eyes are pale, narrowed a little against that high plain light and the glare off the limestone. Their hair goes grey early and is kept cut short, for practicality. You won't see ornament. Wool the color of stone, or the darker grey-brown of the goats they keep; a little soot at the cuff, a little lichen-green at the hem where a coat has brushed a wall for years. Everything they wear has been mended. A knee patched, a sleeve re-hemmed, a goat-hair shawl darned until the darn is thicker than the original weave.

Their hands are the truest thing about them. Even the children's hands look older than they should - broad palms, short nails kept trimmed to the quick, the skin creased with stone dust that never quite washes out. The pock-marks of the stone hammer are on their knuckles as well as on the walls. If you watch an old woman mend a slate, you see how little force she uses. She taps, listens to the stone, taps again. She is not imposing a shape. She is finding where the stone already wants to split. They all have that patience. It is not a virtue to them. It is just how work goes at this altitude, where hurrying only leaves you breathless.

They are an urban people, in their own account. Not villagers. Villagers scatter. These people have agreed to a density and an order. The houses step down the inside of the bowl facing inward, toward the common yard and the cistern at its center, and so does their attention. They are not private in the lowland way. Every roof feeds the same water. Every gutter runs to the same center. A quarrel between two houses is a problem of plumbing as much as feeling - you cannot divert a channel out of spite without everyone hearing the cistern tick more slowly that night. So they have become careful, and formal, and quiet.

No one raises his voice in the yard. When two neighbors need to discuss the sharing of a terrace or the turn to clean the spouts, they do it standing side by side looking at the work, not face to face. The speech is sparse. The language itself has worn smooth here, like the ridges. Few adjectives. Many words for the states of stone and water - stone that sweats, stone that drinks, stone that holds cold, water that beads, water that ticks, water that sleeps under the slab. A child who spills a ladle will be corrected not with scolding but with a demonstration of how to pour more slowly next time.

There are very few children, and they are not noisy. A baby here learns early the bowl's hush. They are carried close in undyed slings, they watch. When they are older they are given small, real tasks - sweeping a length of gutter with a broom made of twig, guiding a goat by a rope that is too long for them, watching the cistern mouth for the drip-rate after rain. Nothing is pretend. No toys that are not also tools. They age without a visible break between playing and working, because no one taught them the difference.

The old are old for a very long time. The thin, cool air and the slow pace preserves them. They sit in doorways where the sun hits the stone and warms it, wrapped even in summer, eyes half-closed against the light. They do not govern so much as remember. This wall was re-laid in the year the pine was struck by frost. That channel was cut two grandmothers ago. Their memory is vertical, like the stratigraphy of the limestone - layered, compressed, each generation a thin page. They do not tell stories of elsewhere. Elsewhere is down the long grey slopes where the world drops away, and it is not relevant.

What they do all day is maintenance, which they do not think of as maintenance but as living. Sweeping. Re-setting a slipped slate. Clearing the fine, gritty dust that the wind sifts over every surface. Leading the goats out to the tuft grass between the terraces and back. Ladling water from the cistern - never pumping, just a bucket on a rope, hand over hand. Mending the one iron pot. Trimming the wick. At dusk they light those shallow oil lamps and the light falls on walls that have held that same small circle of light for longer than any one person can count, and it feels correct, not strange, that nothing new is being made.

Nothing changes, and they do not expect it to. Not with urgency, not with resignation. It is simply the shape of the world. A house does not fall, so you do not build a new one. The cistern never empties, so you do not fear thirst, but it never overflows, so you do not waste. A goat dies and another is born and the number stays at six or seven. A person dies rarely, is carried to a cleft in the rock on the windward side beyond the bowl where the lichens are orange, covered with slabs. And the intervals between the buildings - those deliberate pauses of bare rock and empty pen - stay empty. They like the empty spaces. They could build there, but they don't. The emptiness is part of the structure, like the silence between words.

If you stay long enough to slow your own breathing to theirs, you start to see that they are not so much inhabitants as continuations. Like the moss in the sheltered angle where wall meets bedrock, like the lichen mapping the north faces, like the goats themselves, they are what can live here without altering the agreement that water and frost and time made long ago. They fit so well they are hard to see, until a door thuds, a broom scrapes, a low voice says something brief and practical about water, and you realize the bowl has been holding people the way it holds damp - intermittently, patiently, without ever showing it obviously - for as long as it has held any shape at all.
