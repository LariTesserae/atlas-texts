You don't see them at first.

That's the trick of the place. You look for inhabitants and you see architecture, and you see plant, and you think the city is empty. The bowl is so loud with the chime and clatter of those glass-leather leaves, so full with the slow heave of green, that anything human-sized disappears into it. Then a curtain moves wrong. A patch of pale cloth shifts where there shouldn't be wind. A doorway that looked like shadow resolves into a person standing perfectly still, watching you.

There are not many of them.

That's the first true thing about them. In a city built terrace on terrace to hold a thousand, there are maybe sixty. Maybe eighty. You count and recount and you never get the same number, because they don't gather. They are spaced. One here, tending a wall of vine three houses down from the next. One there, small and alone on a vast, swept plaza that was clearly meant for crowds. They live like birds that will not flock — or like something that has learned not to crowd its water.

At distance, they look like anyone would who lives under that slot of white fire. They are wrapped. From head to foot in undyed, salt-pale cloth, loose and layered, bleached by sun until it is the exact color of the brick. Only the eyes show, and even those are narrowed behind a veil of woven fiber, a second, finer curtain pulled across the face. They move slowly. Deliberately. Not from age — though some are old — but from economy. In that dryness any quick motion costs moisture. You see it after an hour: no one hurries, no one gestures large. Even the children, and there are very few children, walk as if they are carrying something full to the brim.

Closer, you see they are not quite desert people as you know desert people.

They are taller and thinner than they should be. Not starved — stretched. Long limbs, long hands, long necks. The cloth hides most of it but the hands give it away, and the ankles where the wrap rides up. Skin that is not browned by sun but cured by it. It has a sheen. They anoint themselves constantly from small gourds of that clear, lime-sharp oil rendered from the hanging leaves, working it into knuckles and elbows and cheeks until the skin takes on the same waxy translucence as the plant. It doesn't sweat. You realize after watching for a while that none of them sweat. In heat that should leave a man dripping, their skin stays dry and faintly glossy, like polished wood. Their sweat glands, over generations or by design, have learned to stay closed. What water they have, they keep.

Their faces, when you finally see one unveiled in the shade of an awning, are startlingly plain and startlingly inhuman at the same time.

The eyes are large, dark amber, set deeper than yours, with lower lids that are thick and fleshy and can draw halfway up like a lizard's. They blink rarely and slowly. The lashes are pale and almost absent, replaced by fine pleats of skin that hood the eye against dust and glare. The mouth is small, the lips thin and pressed, perpetually closed. They breathe through the nose, slow and shallow. When they do open their mouths to speak, you see teeth stained faintly green-gold from chewing the waxy seeds, and a tongue that looks dry. They don't lick their lips. They have taught themselves not to.

Their hair is not hair as much as a dry, fine felt, cropped close to the skull — some shave it entirely — the color of dust. It doesn't oil or grow fast. The scalp beneath shows through, oiled, protected.

They don't look frail. Turn an arm over and the muscle there is corded and flat, lean as rope. The people who garden the vertical need strength. You watch an old woman, her face a map of fine cracks sealed with oil, work a hooked pole of black bone-wood twice her height. She leans into it, catches the edge of a hanging sheet of leaves as thick as a saddle, and pulls. The whole curtain shudders. The clack it makes is deafening. She trims with a short copper knife, slicing the stiff, jade leaves at the holdfast. The clear oil beads on the cut and does not drip. She catches it in a shallow pan, careful as a beekeeper with honey. Every drop matters. Nothing is wasted, nothing is allowed to fall to the salt and hiss away.

This is what they do. They are not farmers. They are pruners, barbers, midwives to a plant that does not need them but tolerates them.

The plant is the other inhabitant, and you cannot understand the people without it. They live inside it the way you live inside weather. They read its sound. In the morning, when the light is still white and the bowl is at its loudest — that dry insect roar of a million leaves trembling and seeking — they are indoors, behind leaf-curtains, behind heavy fiber drapes, letting the fastest growth happen without them. You can hear the runners moving then, the audible *tack* as a pale green whip finds rock. If you left your door open, you would find it stitched shut by noon with a cord as thick as your wrist. So they close their houses. They wait. Around midday they go out with poles and knives and cut back what has grown overnight, clearing the lanes, the rope bridges, the kiln mouths, the loom frames. They train it the way you would train a river, not to kill it but to keep its channel.

They eat what it gives. The papery seed husks that shake loose when the wind funnels down — each the size of a thumbnail, rattling, light as ash — are parched and ground into a fine, nutty flour that smells of turpentine and citrus. The leaves, sliced thin and soaked in salt and then roasted, become a chewy, translucent sheet that tastes of lime rind and wax. The oil is everything: food, fuel, skin, lamp. They burn it in shallow cups and it gives a clean, white, almost smokeless light. They have no wood to spare. The only true wood is the dark core of the oldest hanging stems, hard as bone, oiled for generations until it shines.

They speak with their hands.

Not because they cannot speak — when they do, their voices are hoarse and low, used to being forced through dry throats — but because the bowl never stops talking. To shout over the leaf-roar at noon costs water with every breath. So they have a language of signs, flicked across the plaza from terrace to terrace, precise and economical: *cut here, leave this runner, water? — no water, bring pan, child inside.* You see two figures a hundred meters apart hold a full conversation without a sound, their pale wrappings flashing in the sun. When they are close, under an awning where the plant muffles the chime, they will murmur, mouth to ear, a few words at a time. They laugh rarely, and when they do it is silent, shoulders shaking.

There is no crowd to be. No market crush, no festival throng. The plaza cistern — a beautiful, vast, white basin that has never held water — is sometimes used as a sleeping floor on the hottest nights, when the whole bowl radiates heat and a single family will lie spaced apart on mats, looking up at that thin ribbon of stars. But even then they do not touch. Children are few because bodies that hoard water cannot make many bodies easily. You see perhaps four children in the whole bowl, and they are watched by everyone. They are taught early to keep the mouth closed, to oil the skin, to listen for the tick of a runner approaching a window. An elder — sex indeterminate under the wraps until you hear the voice — will sit with a child for an hour and together they will watch a single vine tip extend, millimeter by millimeter, saying nothing.

They are not unhappy. That's what unsettles you at first. You expect a people so sparse, so quiet, so pressed down in a salt pocket to seem mournful or desperate. They don't. They are patient in a way that has nothing to do with waiting for rescue. They have been here long enough that the corners of their city have gone soft. They mend, they sweep, they prune, they weave husk-fiber into cloth that never rots. They know the bowl will not get bigger and they do not want it to. When one terrace finally is lost — when a family dies out or moves and the green takes the house completely, filling it with hanging curtains that root to the hearth — they do not fight it. They cut a new doorway elsewhere, exactly as large as needed. The city breathes in and out, green and salt, inhabited and empty, and the balance holds.

At that brief hour before sunset, when the sun clears the lip and pours sideways gold through the slot, everything changes, and you see them best then. The translucent leaves light from within and the whole hollow becomes a lantern, amber and jade, and the roar softens into a hush. The heat lifts just enough that faces come uncovered. Old and young step out onto their flat roofs, their oiled skin catching the light, their veils down. For twenty minutes you can hear the small sounds: a mortar's thud, a copper pan set down, a child humming through closed lips, an old man's dry cough that he immediately smooths away, conserving.

They glow. Not metaphorically. The oil on their skin, the wax in their cloth, the light through the leaves — they look for a moment like they have grown there too, like another form of the same translucent, waterless flesh that has learned how to drink sun directly and keep every drop of itself sealed inside.

If you stay, they will offer you oil for your skin without asking if you will stay. They will show you, with hands, how to wrap the cloth so your mouth stays closed. They will not ask where you came from. The desert above is not a place to them. It is a lid.
