Nothing lives here at first.

That is the first honest answer your eyes give you when you open them under the cold, bright weight of that water. Nothing with a face, nothing with a voice. No flicker of a school turning, no shadow passing overhead. The light-nets race across raw basalt and find nothing to illuminate but stone. You hang there, breathing thin air in thin air and then cold water in cold water, and the roar is so continuous it becomes a kind of silence, and in that silence the absence of other bodies becomes absolute. For a long hour you could be the only soft thing for a hundred kilometers.

And then you learn to see lower.

You have to drop your gaze — not out, but down. Not to the horizon but to your feet. To the top of the column you are standing on, where the water is shallowest and the sun, made strange by passing through its ocean-sky, finally hits something solid.

It is painted.

That is the first life, and you almost miss it because it does not look alive by any contract you have made with the word before. It does not rise. It does not branch. It does not wave. It lies flat against the black glass where the turbulence is just briefly tired, a skin of dark olive and dried-blood red and deep, lightless green-black, so thin you think at first it is a mineral stain, a discoloration in the basalt itself.

It is not.

If you put your hand close — and you have to brace against the slow, continent-wide pull of the gyre to do it — you see it has a texture. Leathery. Pitted. Like old leather left in the sun, or the scab over a wound. It is a mat. A crust. A colony that decided that height was death and chose to become a surface instead. Algae and lichen and bacteria and something engineered that is not quite any of them, braided together until they are one organism.

This is what lives here. Almost only this.

It clings.

That is its entire philosophy, its entire technology. On stone so new its edges could still cut you, where any current would peel a kelp forest like tape, where standing waves collapse like mountains, this thing holds on with a grip that is chemical before it is physical. It excretes a clear, almost invisible cement from its underside that welds itself to the basalt at a molecular level. You cannot pry it with a knife. If you try, the basalt gives before the mat does. It has to, because the water never stops trying to take it. The low keen you hear — stone being filed down for the first time — is the sound of currents dragging silt across these mats and failing to move them.

It eats light, but not as you know eating.

It does not wait for a steady sun. There is no steady sun here. The surface above is a breathing lens, and the light arrives as caustic nets, lattices of brilliance that sprint across the stone, contract to a white-hot filament, then shatter and reform. Any leaf that tried to photosynthesize here would burn and starve in alternating seconds. The mat does something else. It is dark for a reason — its pigments are almost black to catch every photon when the net is elsewhere, and when the net passes, when that searing lattice sweeps over it, its surface shivers. Not moving, exactly, but blanching, dispersing the excess through its body like a bruise spreading. It stores light as much as it uses it. In the brief, flickering noon when the whole plain glitters, these colonies glow faintly from within, as if they have swallowed a little of the sun and are holding it.

It does not grow so much as *creep*.

A millimeter a week if it is lucky. It advances as a blind, flat tide over the naked basalt, its leading edge a pale, almost translucent lip where new cells are testing the stone. Behind it, it thickens, darkens, becomes that leathery crust. It has no front or back. It is all edge. In the lee of a hexagonal column, where an eddy makes a pocket of relative calm, one of these mats may have had centuries uninterrupted — by its own slow clock — and has grown to the size of a table, a low dome. And there its strategy betrays a little softness. The edge lifts. Just a hair. A frayed hem, no thicker than paper, that trembles in the flow.

And that is its second form of life.

The filaments.

They tear. They are meant to tear. The hem lifts and thins and sends out threads, rust-red and olive, as fine as hair, that flutter and then snap. You see them if you hold still long enough — a cloud of them, drifting like dandelion seed, but underwater, in that startlingly clear kilometer-visibility. They do not swim. They are not propelled. They are *taken*. The gyre takes them and shears them and carries them for kilometers, for tens of kilometers, until by chance one snags on another sharp edge in another pocket of calm. Most never do. Most are ground to nothing in the cavitation crackle around a basalt pillar. But a few catch, and cement, and begin again as a new stain, a new painting on new stone. The ocean here reproduces not by spawning but by eroding itself.

If you watch very close, you will see the third thing that lives here, because where there is a mat there will, eventually, be something that eats the mat.

It is not a fish. Nothing here has the arrogance of a spine, or the speed to outrun a gyre. What lives on the mats is flatter than the mats themselves.

At first you think it is a ripple in the crust, a blister. Then the blister moves.

It is the color of wet basalt, a mottled black-grey with a faint olive cast from the mat it has just grazed. It is the size of your palm, or your handspan, oval and rimmed with a fringe of cilia so fine they are invisible until silt catches on them. A limpet, if a limpet had been told to become a shadow. A chiton, if a chiton had been pressed under glass. It has no eye you can find, no head. Just a low dome, a foot that is all foot, that makes a perfect seal against the mat and the stone beneath. It moves at the speed of rust forming. An hour to cross its own length.

It lives by being unliftable. Its shell — if you can call it a shell — is not a shell at all but a flexible plate of overlapping mineral scales it grows from the basalt it scrapes, thin as mica, flexing to follow every micro-contour of the column top. The current pours over it and finds no edge to purchase. The cavitation that would rip a barnacle off a pier breaks and slides over its back. Underneath, it rasps. A mouth like a soft, toothed wheel that kisses the mat and shaves off a cell's thickness, never enough to kill the colony, just enough to live. Behind it, it leaves a pale grazing scar, a faint trail that the mat will heal over in weeks.

There are not many. You might see one per colony, sometimes none. Sometimes two, centimeters apart, never touching, spending their whole lives on the same table-sized world, never knowing the next world exists a kilometer away across a valley of empty, roaring water. They do not call. They do not need to. The chemical tang of a fresh grazing scar is enough to tell another of its kind, decades later, that someone was here.

And then, if you stay until the cold seeps into your bones and the light-nets begin to stretch long toward evening, you may sense the last inhabitant, which is not an inhabitant at all in the way the mats and the grazers are, but which lives here more completely than either.

The water itself.

Not the water as water — though even that is not quite water — but the lattice inside it. When the sun hits at a low angle and you catch that oily, too-crisp iridescence, that pixelation of rainbow that shifts a little too sharply, you are seeing it. The structure. The machine that was poured here to hold the ocean up. It is not alive as you are alive. It has no body, no hunger, no mat to cling to. But it *does*. It holds. It circulates. It decides, at every point along that impossible vertical cliff at the plateau's rim, to keep holding. It shepherds silt along invisible field lines. It suspends spray a fraction of a second too long. It repairs itself when a standing wave tears a hole in its logic.

If the mats are the first tenants, patiently preparing the stone for a future soil that does not yet exist, and the grazers are the first guests, then this is the landlord — invisible, distributed, awake across the entire drowned continent at once. It does not notice the mats, or rather it notices them the way you notice the moss on your roof: as a condition, as weathering, as part of the work. *Erode, but not yet,* is its only thought, repeated ocean-wide, pump-wide, for as long as it takes the raw planet to be ready.

You never see it arrive or leave. There are no doors opening, no drones rising. But sometimes, after a big collapse, after a whole valley of basalt has been briefly illuminated and the water rushes to fill it loud enough to feel in your chest, you will see, in that pinpoint-clear water, a perfect sphere of stillness — a meter across, utterly calm while everything around it roars — traveling slowly against the gyre, as if something is looking, and then it is gone, and the current closes without a ripple.

That is everyone.

A paint that eats flickering suns. A shadow that eats the paint, so slowly you have to time-lapse it to be sure it moved. A cloud of torn threads between them, trying to become new paints. And the ocean that is also a building, holding itself up in the thin, cold air, waiting.

Nothing sings. Nothing darts. The world is profoundly, intentionally empty, so that when whatever was meant to live here finally is invited, there will be stone softened enough, and water clear enough, to receive it.
