They do not live there the way something lives in a desert.

Nothing *visits* this room. Nothing passes through. What lives here belongs to it the way a color belongs to a stone — not added on, but grown out of the conditions that made the room possible in the first place.

At first you will not see them as animals at all.

From the rim, in the weighty twilight, the floor of the bowl looks like a texture. Like the salt pan itself has gone rough — has scaled over, has scabbed. A dark, pebbled leather stretched rim to rim. You have to watch it for a long time before the texture resolves, because there is no space between one and the next. No ground showing. No individual to pick out.

Then one near the center shifts.

A plate lifts at its edge — that dull amber margin catching what little light there is — and a fringe underneath flexes, pale as new rootlets, and sets down again a finger's width to the left. And once you've seen one move, you see that they are all moving.

They are low. That is the first correction your eye makes. You thought they might be raised, like beetles, like roaches. They are not. Each one is flattened almost to nothing, a long oval the length of your hand from wrist to fingertip, but no thicker than a knuckle. A shape made for pressure, for sliding under and over. On top: a back of overlapping plates, matte black, not shiny, not chitinous in the familiar way. More like cooled basalt themselves, like slag that has gone to scale. The plates are edged all around with a thin band of amber, or rust, or cooling iron — a color that is not pigment but heat made visible. When they are still, that edge darkens. When they have been still a long time, near the warm cracks, it pales, almost to straw, as if the color has been drunk inward.

Underneath — and you only see it when one tilts, when one is lifted — they are soft. Pale, segmented, almost translucent. A dense comb of fine legs, too many to count, too fine to be legs in the way you know legs. Cilia, perhaps. Oars. They never stop. Even when the animal above is otherwise motionless, flattening itself to the salt to drink heat, that fringe is in a slow, constant tremor, tasting, holding, pulling mineral to the mouth you cannot see.

They have no face that you would call a face. No eyes. No antennae waving. The front is only the slightly narrower end of the oval, where the plates come to a blunt point. They find each other and find heat and find the direction of the turning not by sight but by the whole surface of themselves. They know the gradient of warmth through their backs.

And they are so many that the idea of "they" starts to fail.

You try to count and the number slides. A thousand is too few. Ten thousand is still a shape you can hold in your mind. This is more. This is rim to rim, wall to wall, shoulder to shoulder, plate over plate. A thatch. A shingle. A living pavement that breathes. When they move they do not walk like animals crossing ground. They flow. One climbs a third of its body over the next, plates ticking softly against plates — that faint, dry susurrus you mistook at first for wind — and settles, and the one beneath slides forward in turn. It is circulation without hurry, without panic, endless. You understand after ten minutes that you are not looking at a crowd. You are looking at a single thing that has learned to be many pieces so it can turn itself over.

Because it does turn.

If you fix your eye on a lighter-colored patch near the outer wall, you can see it creep, impossibly slowly, clockwise along the basalt. While at the same time, near the hot center where the salt cracks are breathing, the mass is moving the other way. A convection cell made of bodies. Heat rises in the middle, the fed animals grow restless and push outward toward the cooler stone to cool, the hungry ones on the perimeter are drawn inward to drink. The whole bowl stirs itself, once an hour, once a day — time is hard to keep in the dark — a silent, vast, slow stirring that never stops.

This is how they eat. There is nothing else to eat.

They do not graze. There is no lichen, no algae, no wind-blown detritus worth mentioning. They press themselves flat over the warmest fissures and go utterly still, and the amber edge goes pale. Heat is passed directly into them. Chemical heat, mineral heat, the deep exhalation of the old vent. Some chemistry you smell as iron and ozone runs in their plates instead of photosynthesis. When they have had enough they shiver all over, the plates tightening, and rejoin the flow.

Not all of them wait.

Among them — fewer, so you miss them at first — are longer forms. Same palette, same matte black, but stretched, perhaps a hand and a half, and with a high, smooth ridge down the spine that catches the dimness like glass. They do not flow. They *thread*. They move with a purpose that cuts the slow circulation, slipping between the ovals without making them part, until they stop.

They poise. The ridge tenses.

Then a quick, downward drive — a wedge, a knife — and the thatch parts around them in a perfect, fluid circle, as if it knows this shape. When they lift, the underside is exposed for the only time: soft, pale, legs kicking once, twice, still flexing. The long one does not tear or chew. It pins and absorbs, mouth somewhere along that ridge, and within seconds the circle closes. The plates resettle. The floor is whole again. No gap, no stain. The economy does not allow for waste.

And elsewhere, the same economy deals with growth.

You will see one shudder. Not the flip you heard earlier — that bright little *clink* that carried across the bowl — but a deeper tremor. A seam appears down the center of its back where you swore there was no seam, and the plates part. Something slightly larger, slightly more amber, slick and new, hauls itself forward out of its own back, leaving behind a perfect, empty husk — the exact shape of itself, but papery and light.

It does not stay empty for a minute. Two, three neighbors turn their blunt fronts toward it and begin to take it apart, edge by edge, with mouths too small and too ventral to see. The husk is unmade methodically, to nothing. Salt would otherwise accumulate. Shell would pile. In a room with no water to wash anything away, everything must be eaten, even the self you have outgrown.

If you stay long enough to let your eyes truly adjust to the bruised dark, you see that none of this is frantic. No frenzy, no swarm. It is established. Weathered. Old. The molting, the hunting, the drinking of heat, the slow turning — all of it has a practiced, unhurried rhythm, like a tide in a bowl that has been tide-pooling for a very long time. They do not need you to see them. The cairns on the rim are the proof: something stood here before you, watched the whole floor sigh with warm air, understood that nothing built down there would stay uncovered for an hour, and left the stones as a marker not for others, but for itself. *I was here. I did not stay.*

If you disturb them — a pebble tossed, a breath too hot — they do not scatter. The thatch ripples out in a perfect ring, plate ticking on plate, legs catching and releasing, and then smooths. The hunters pause. The drinkers flatten again. The turning resumes.

They live as the mineral lives: densely, patiently, completely sealed into the room that made them.
