It is not empty. That is the first mistake, and you make it for a long time.

You sit on the rim and you watch and the bowl watches back and you think: nothing lives here. Because nothing *moves*.

The green is too perfect. The spacing too even. The water too loud. In a place this lush there should be chatter and rustle and the panic of small things. Instead there is only the ticking.

So you keep looking, until your eyes adjust to the speed of the place.

---

What lives here lives at the speed the bowl does. Which is to say, almost not at all.

At first you think it's a hummock.

Down near the center, where the peat is deepest and the ankle-water opens into a palm of clear sky about six feet across, there is a mound that is a slightly different black-green than the others. A little higher, a little rounder. Moss on it, like all the others. Except the moss on this one has a grain to it, a nap that lays in one direction, like fur.

It breathes.

Not often. You have to stop hearing the drips to hear it. A slow intake that takes twenty seconds, a slow release that takes thirty, so shallow the surface of the pool around it barely trembles. If you blink you miss the slight lift. Then a long stillness. Then again.

You keep staring until the shape resolves and you realize the shape was never a mound at all.

She is the size of a large dog, or a small bear, but low and long. There is no good animal word for her, because the bowl never learned those words. If you had to borrow one, you would say salamander the way you say *fern that is not quite fern.* An amphibian that has decided, over a very long time, to be almost entirely water.

Her skin is the bowl's skin. Not colored like it — *it is it*. Translucent, but not pale. A dark olive-jade, thick as wet leather, freckled and stippled with algae that grows *in* the skin, not on it. In the clear parts — at her throat, at the inner joints of her legs — you can see the vessels underneath, slow and dark, and the water she carries. She is full of it. When she shifts, you see it slosh inside her, like a gourd.

She has no shell, no fur, no scales. Her back is broad and slightly hollowed, a shallow dish in its own right. And in that dish, a complete, miniature version of the bowl has established itself: sphagnum and liverwort rooted in the thin soil of her own shed skin, a tiny, perfect bromeliad no bigger than your fist growing between her shoulder blades, its cup holding a mouthful of sky-water. Two or three small, white orchid-like flowers root there too. They are not parasites. They are passengers who have agreed to be carried.

Her head is blunt and wide, more stone than face. No external ears. Eyes set high and far apart, not forward like a predator, but outward and upward like a creature whose main predator is the sky. Her eyes are large, lidded, and absolutely clear, the color of the standing water — not iris and pupil, but a single depth. They do not blink often. When they do, a nictitating membrane wipes sideways, as clear as a drip.

Around the sides of her neck, fanning out like the gills of an axolotl, or like the fronds of the tree ferns above her, are three frilled, leaf-shaped lungs. They are not feathered red, but veined green-black, saturated, fluttering only faintly with each breath. They look exactly like wet fern leaves until they pulse. That's how you missed them. The bowl upholsters everything, even its animals, in fern.

She has four legs but she scarcely uses them. The front two are strong and splayed, tipped with flat, spade-like toes webbed to the tip — not for swimming, but for spreading her weight on peat that otherwise would swallow her. The back two are smaller, almost vestigial, tucked under her. She does not walk. She eases. She reposes and then, an hour later, she is three inches to the left, and you did not see the transit. The peat sighs and comes back, leaving no print. That's why there are no tracks.

And she is loud in the way the bowl is loud — by being quiet enough to let the water speak for her.

Water beads on the rim of her back-dish and spills over her flanks in shining threads, the same width as the threads on the basalt. Condensation gathers on the cold edge of her gill-fronds and drips, *plink*, into the pool. The bromeliad on her back overflows and trickles down her side. She is a weeping rock that breathes. Sitting ten feet away, you can hear the individual drops fall from her chin back into the water. In this amphitheater, that *is* a voice.

There are not many of her. There can't be.

If you stay long enough — a full day, into the evening when the cloud lifts off the rim and the light goes level and golden — you might see a second. Far across the bowl, tucked under the overhang of the weeping wall, another hummock with eyes. Possibly a third, smaller, half-submerged in one of the larger bromeliad cups high on the wall. But you will never see them together, moving, interacting. They are spaced like the tree ferns. Like words with a lot of space between them. Each one requires the whole bowl's slow wringing of cloud to keep its skin wet, its back-garden watered, its throat full.

She prunes, in her way. That is why the place looks tended.

You watch her edge toward a fallen tree-fern frond that has lain since last spring, perfectly preserved in the peat, and she settles her great blunt mouth over its edge. She does not bite or tear. She covers it. She secretes over it — a thin, clear film — and waits. Hours later, when she eases away, the frond is a little softer, a little more water than plant, beginning its slow becoming-peat. She eats time more than mass. And later, you see her nudge with her spade-toes at a young fern shoot that has sprouted too close to her pool, crowding the open water. She does not uproot it. She lies on it for a day, until it turns aside, seeking light around her.

The cut stump on the drier hummock was not hers. Hers is a different tool. But she remembers it. When humans come — and they do, rarely, because a place this perfect pulls — they come to see if she is still there. They cut a sapling with a chipped stone not to build, but to see if the bowl would mind. They made a small fire that smoked terribly and went out, and sat a long time watching the hummock breathe. She watched back. She allowed it. People have always been visitors here, not residents. She is the resident for whom the bowls condition is not strange at all: warm air holding high, water clinging sideways, light staying a second too long in the pools. For her, that *is* the weather. The bowl is not a mountain anomaly. It is a cradle. The basalt learned to be soil so she would have a bed. The clouds learned to wring themselves so she would not dry.

She lives a long time. You can see it in her. The algae in her skin has had decades to stipple. The little bromeliad on her back has flowered and seeded. The transparent belly she carries makes her look gravid even when she is not. When she is, she does not lay eggs in the peat. She backs slowly up a weeping wall until she is draped beneath one of the barrel-bromeliads, and she slips a string of three or four clear beads, each the size of a grape, into its private pond. There they hang, visible, each with a single dark speck curled inside, bathed in sky-water and leaf-light for a season, until they hatch as perfect, tadpole-thin copies no longer than your finger, that wriggle over the waxy lip and drop into the water to become new hummocks elsewhere. You will not see it happen. It takes months.

When the sun slips behind the rim and the air finally cools, she does something that will stay with you.

She lifts her wide head, just an inch. Opens her mouth, which you had thought was lipless and fixed. And she drinks the air.

Not the pool. The condensation itself. The droplets beaded on the underside of the fern crowns twenty feet above catch the last light and fall, and she catches one or two on her tongue with a slow, precise flick, like a chameleon taking rain without moving. She has been doing this all day while you thought she was still.

You realize then that she is not *in* the bowl. She *is* a bowl, inside the bowl. A shallow, warm, patient dish of water and skin that holds in, high in the thin air, exactly as the stone does. The mountain made one bowl, and then the bowl made her to hold a smaller one.

If you are quiet, if you stay until your lungs forget the climb, she will ease a few inches nearer. Not from curiosity. Because your shadow, for a moment, kept a patch of peat from drying. That's enough reason, here.

She will settle beside your boot where the water sheets thin over stone, warm from the sun and cold from the rock at once, and breathe, and drip, and add her small, wet ticking to the loud, slow chorus of the place, until you can't tell which drops are stone and which are her.
