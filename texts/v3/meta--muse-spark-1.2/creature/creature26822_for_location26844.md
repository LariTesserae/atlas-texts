They live there the way you would live if you were never quite born.

At first, from a distance through the amber water, you don't see people at all. You see movement that could be plant.

A slow pale shape detaching from a braid of root. A drift of limbs that doesn't thrash. Something that hangs for a long time in mid-water, absolutely still, with only the hair moving. Then it kicks once — a frog-kick that barely disturbs the silt — and glides five meters on momentum before it needs to kick again. If you watch long enough you realize the whole town is full of them, hanging like fruit in the dense weave, each one holding a rope loosely in one hand even while asleep.

Closer, you see they are human. Or were.

They are not built for speed or for distance or for land. They are built for suspension.

### The Body That The Water Made

They are thin, but not in a starved way — elongated. Low muscle, long tendon, ribs you can count when they stretch. The children are round and seal-fat until about six, and then they length out abruptly, as if the water pulled them taller. Their chests are deep and barrelled, not broad-shouldered but forward-keeled like birds. When they inhale on the lip of an air-bell their whole torso swells. They can hold that breath for a long time. Seven minutes is unremarkable for an adult. Ten, twelve for an elder who has learned how to make her heart slow on command. The young men compete sometimes, not as sport exactly, but as prayer — who can hang motionless in a cold seep layer the longest, eyes open, until the meniscus trembles across their throat.

Their skin never really dries. It is the color of river pearl, or light basalt when it is wet — which is always — a soft grey-pink with a slight translucence at the temples and inner wrists. It prunes and stays pruned, ridged at the pads of their fingers and toes in whorls that never flatten. They are mostly bare, except for belts and slings of kelp fiber, because cloth drags and rots. Hair grows long and is never cut short; it is a useful thing in still water. Women and men both wear it past the shoulder, often threaded with small bored stones or shell beads to keep it from blinding them. Underwater it lifts and spreads around their heads like a veil. The infants are almost bald for the first year, and shine like tadpoles.

Their eyes have changed without quite evolving. The iris is large, the pupil always wide in that permanent dusk, a deep amber-brown that catches the glow of the feather-weed gardens. They don't blink often underwater; they have learned a slow, deliberate blink that clears silt, and a thin extra film they can draw half-shut when they pass through a stinging layer of brush-algae spores. Hard light hurts them. If you brought one down past the rim, into the dry lowland glare, it would weep and keep its eyes closed for days.

Their hands tell you how they live. No callus on the soles — they never stand on stone if they can help it, the fresh basalt would cut them anyway. The callus is all in the grip: the inner palm, the crook of the first finger and thumb where rope runs. Their fingers are long, the webbing between them a little deeper than yours, not a true web but a stretchiness that cups water when they scull. Their toenails are soft and short, almost useless. Their fingernails are kept slightly long and honed on basalt, because a shell knife can be dropped and lost in the silt forever, but a nail is always there to slit a kelp stipe or clean shrimp.

They move like they are trying not to wake the water. Never a splash. The rule is taught before speech: *hurry and the water holds you.* If you thrash, the strange viscosity of the Table — that hanging-silt, those locked thermal pillars — pushes back. If you are smooth, you slip. So they have a swimming that is mostly drifting. One kick. Glide. One slow sweep of the arms. Glide. To turn, they catch a vine. To stop, they open their limbs and let the water itself brake them. The old ones barely swim at all; they pull themselves hand-over-hand along the ropeways with an economy that looks like floating.

### The Breath

You cannot live submerged all the time. They have made their lungs external.

Each family, each working group, each garden-tending crew maintains a bell. From below it looks like a thatched basket, or an overturned bird's nest, twenty feet across, woven from living mangrove root and dead reed and sealed inside with layer after layer of black resin and stretched kelp skin, then weighted and lashed to the basalt pillars or to the great kelp anchors. Underneath, air.

They steal it from the mist and from the gas of the kelp itself. The great surface kelp bladders exhale oxygen in slow bubbles that wobble upwards and, instead of popping, catch under the bells. The people coax them there, herding bubbles with broad leaves the way you would herd goats. Inside the bell the air is warm and wet and profoundly stale. It smells of human breath and oil smoke and damp thatch and bodies. Little cup-lamps of bored basalt burn seal-oil — not seal, there are no seals, but oil rendered from the pale salamander's fat when one dies, and more often from the pressed nuts of the drowned hydrangea-bush — and the light they give is as dim as the water, a soft orange bruise in the gloom. The whole constellation of bells glows faintly through the amber if you look down from the canopy, like lungs in a chest.

Living is a rhythm of breaths. Out along the ropeways in the middle layer where the feather-weed grows, pruning, tying, harvesting, holding your breath. Back to the bell, duck under the woven lip with an practiced flip that spills almost no air, and break surface inside into the pocket. There, they talk. It is the only place where voices work normally, though the air is so small it makes everything sound close. Outside they do not talk; they sign with hands painted at the forearm and brow in phosphorescent fungus paste that hangs visible in the haze, and they tap code on the ropes — two quick tugs *I am here*, three slow *need air*, a long tremor that means *salamander passing, be still*.

Children live inside the bells for their first two years, surfacing like pups in a den, learning to hold breath in games. They are not taught to walk. There is nowhere flat to walk. They learn to hang. A mother will hold her infant against her chest in the water and the infant will instinctively clutch at her hair and throat, legs frogged, gills nonexistent but mouth remembering. By three they can make it between two close bells on one breath. By seven they can work a garden line. By twelve they are expected to be able to sit absolutely still for an hour, watching a red brush-algae frond for the exact amber blush that means it is ready to cut, because patience is not a virtue there, it is locomotion.

### The Temperament

Because everything touches everything else, they have no privacy and no hurry.

You sleep not in a bed but in a sling of kelp net hung inside a bell or, if it is warm enough and you are young, hanging directly in the water tethered by one wrist cord so you don't drift into a thermal column in your sleep. The whole town sways together, not with waves — there are no waves — but with that hours-long convective sigh, warm water up, cool water down. They all breathe it. They all feel the same slow heave at the same time. It synchronizes them.

They are quiet because sound betrays distance. In the dense tangle, a sharp word can seem to originate inside another person's head. So they are gentle with noise. Even anger is slow. Two people in dispute will go to opposite sides of a bell and tug opposite ends of the same rope, feeling the tension, until one lets go.

They are not sad, but they are embryonic in mood. Incomplete, waiting, like their geology. They tell stories about the rim — that beyond it the world drops away forever and there is dry wind and sharp sun — but no one believes it as a place to go. To go over the rim would be to fall out of the womb. Their myths are not about creation but about *staying*. How the Table broke and held. How the water came and chose to stay. How the first people wove the first bell and learned not to thrash.

If you pulled yourself up through the kelp crowns at noon-dusk and broke into the haze-air and looked back down, you would see them as the water sees them: hundreds of pale, slow figures suspended in a green-gold light, each connected by a thread to another thread to another, each holding a rope even in sleep, their gardens glowing beneath them, their bells breathing in soft circles, their great white salamanders passing underneath like thoughts.

They are not fish. They are not yet something else. They are what happens when a people are lifted kilometers into the sky, given a warm, still sea instead of a sky, and told: live here, but do not disturb the water. And they listened.
