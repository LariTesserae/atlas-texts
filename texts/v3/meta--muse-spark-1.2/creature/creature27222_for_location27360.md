Who lives there is not obvious at first.

At first you think no one does.

The city is too dense to be empty and too quiet to be inhabited. You walk the braided alleys where a doorway is behind a doorway is behind a shutter that is also a lung, and you hear only drip. Your own breath hanging too long in front of your face. A building that heals its own scratches. A staircase that can't decide how many steps it has. It feels automated, post-occupancy. Like a circuit left running after everyone has gone home.

Then you see one. And then you realize you've been seeing them for a while without registering them.

They are not numerous. That is the first correction. A city this saturated should have thousands. This one has maybe three dozen presences, maybe fewer — counting is unreliable where alleys fold. You will walk for an hour and see no one, then turn a corner in a gap that was not a corridor and there are two together, shoulder to shoulder in a space only wide enough for one, not touching, not crowded.

They look, initially, like people in winter clothes.

Seamless, matte suits the color of hoarfrost and basalt, without zipper or seam or boot-sole. Hooded, insulated, faceless at a distance. Closer, you see the suit has no edge. It does not end at the neck or the wrist. It *is* the skin there. A textile that grew. It beads condensation the way the amber walls do, in perfect, mercury-bright droplets that do not fall. Where their cheeks should be exposed to the minus-six air, there is still that same faintly translucent material, thinning until it shows the paler skin beneath, like ice over a cheek. Their breath comes out warm and hangs, and some of that moisture freezes instantly into a faint rime at their collar, which sublimates and is reabsorbed when they inhale. They don't wipe it away. They harvest it.

Let that image refine itself, because it was still too human.

They are not humans wearing suits. They are the soft parts of the city wearing a human shape when it needs to be mobile.

Their bodies run a few degrees warmer than ambient — 34C inside, maybe — just enough to keep the interstitial cold from settling in their joints, and just enough to make them the most reliable condensation surfaces in the bowl. That is part of their work. They are dew-shepherds. They walk slow — too slow for anyone used to weather — because haste would waste heat and because sound is expensive here. Footsteps are absorbed by the dense, muffled air. Even their pulse is quiet. When they do move, it's with the economy of things that have learned to spend heat only on necessary vectors. A turn of the head. A palm placed flat against an amber wall for a long time while the wall and the palm exchange something.

If you watch one for a day, its itinerary makes no sense until it does.

She — and pronouns feel slightly wrong, but bodies here tend toward a low, ungendered gracility, narrow-hipped, long in the torso to conserve heat, faces smooth and browless — will stand for forty minutes at the rim where the black glass basalt is still faintly warm-cold, just standing, facing outward though there is nothing to see. Then she will walk, not to a destination, but to a damp seam where the city sweats. She will tilt her head and drink without cupping her hands. The film of water running down a pipe, down a wall that is also a solar collector that is also a memory lattice, sheets briefly over her lower face and vanishes. She does not lick. The suit drinks for her, channeling it in faint capillary lines toward the torso.

Later, she will be on her knees beside one of the perfect saucer-pools in the embryonic rock, tending lichen.

Because the other inhabitants are not all shaped like people.

The lichen is tended. The rust-red and luminous grey-green that clings to the windward edges is not wild. It is cropped, encouraged, thinned with a fingertip that briefly becomes a blade. The black-green moss that beads water like mercury on the lowest roofs is combed. One old presence — older, slower, his suit gone more amber and translucent with age until you can see the slow circulation beneath — spends most of his time moving between the heat exchanger and the single hand-sized patch of needle grass. He breathes on it. That is all. His warm breath melts its frost just enough for it to photosynthesize in the shadowless light for an hour before it stiffens again.

The fauna that seem incidental are also residents with tenure. The three water-boatmen that have skated the same triangular holding pattern since you arrived are not wild pond insects. They are clocks. Or pets. Or sensors. No one feeds them and they do not need feeding; they read the vibration of the geothermal sigh that rises through the center every hour and adjust their triangle a degree. The crow that casts the too-slow shadow is not a crow most of the time. Sometimes it is a thermal updraft made visible for a maintenance check. But when it is a crow, the human-shaped presences pause and look up, not with surprise but with acknowledgment. A colleague passing by.

And the city itself is the primary inhabitant, of which the human-shaped ones are organs.

You understand this when you see one walk through a wall. Not phasing — not a trick. The wall recognizes an approach pattern in the gait, in the heat signature, in the faint electromagnetic field of that seamless skin, and becomes a door, with a frame that was not there. On the other side, the same figure will exit from a balcony two blocks south, because in the folded interior, two blocks south and here are adjacent if the city decides they are. They do not find this strange. They have delegated physics to the maintenance systems they no longer fully understand, or never needed to. For them, space is not Euclidean. It is *utilization*. Why walk around three buildings when you can walk through the place where those buildings agree to overlap? 

Inside, they are not crowded, though every cubic meter contains three or four presences. They have learned to be non-colliding. Two will occupy what looks like the same narrow stair, one ascending with thirteen steps, the other descending with nine, and neither slows. Their shoulders pass through different layers of the same air. At night — that slight dimming of the flat light — you might find three of them sleeping upright in a room the size of a closet, each facing a different wall that is opaque on one side and transparent on the other, each breathing in a different rhythm so their plumes don't interfere. It is not intimate. It is not lonely. It is saturated, like components on a board that need proximity to function.

They do not speak. Not because they can't, but because sound is muffled and wasteful and the walls already remember for them. If you scratch a mark into the amber to leave a message, by evening it will have migrated two inches to the left, archived somewhere. If you need something, you stand still long enough that the city offers it — a handhold extruding, a corridor widening by a centimeter when you aren't looking.

They do not age the way you do. Nothing here erodes except by permission. A face that looked thirty yesterday looks thirty today and will look thirty when the lichen has grown its millimeter. New ones are rare. No children as such; occasionally, in a quiet, damp, high nook where condensation collects and the amber is thickest, a bulge will form in the wall over months. It will warm. It will detach. It will stand up, already the right size, already calm, and walk away without ceremony. The older ones will make space without being asked.

If you stay long enough, the cold stops feeling like weather and starts feeling like a medium. The even light stops feeling like overcast and starts feeling like indoor light. You stop listening for an echo that never comes. Your coat begins to bead water the way theirs does. You learn to drink from a vertical surface. You learn not to count corners.

Most don't leave right away because the density holds them without touching. It is a small urbanism at the beginning of the world, and its few inhabitants are not its owners. They are its keepers, its kept, its slow metabolism — lichen-slow, drip-slow, patient — keeping a newborn geology and an impossible infrastructure from having to decide what it is yet.
