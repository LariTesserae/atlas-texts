They don't live here the way people live in a place.

At first you think no one does. That the marks are old, or wind-made. The basin is too loud, too crowded, too unfinished to belong to anyone. Anything that tried to claim a corner would have it taken back by water or rock or hunger within an hour. So you expect transients. Visitors from the desert above who fell through a skylight and left a fire before they died.

Then you see the paths again and understand they are not random.

No one here *settles*. That is the first principle of whoever lives here. Settlement assumes the ground will stay where you left it. Here it won't. A ledge you slept on last night is a salt pan by noon, a steam vent by evening. So they don't build houses. They make *interruptions* — places where the chaos hesitates for a little while.

You find one occupied only after you've looked right at it for a long time.

High on the second terrace, where the stone is still hot through your boots and the condensation never quite dries, a windbreak of basalt slabs leans against the native rock. It is low — you would have to crawl to get behind it. The slabs are not mortared, not shaped, just chosen for flatness and stacked with a care that only shows up when you try to pull one loose and find it won't move. The cracks are chinked with a paste of white salt-mud and powdered chitin that has dried as hard as plaster. Under the overhang beside it, hides are stretched over a frame of long bones and black chitin plates. The hides are not leather as you know it. They are translucent, damp, stitched with sinew that shrinks when it dries to pull the seams tight. The whole shelter is the size of a badger's den. It sweats. Inside, the floor is covered in layers of shed carapace — the pale, papery molts of the shrimp-lice — pressed flat for insulation. It smells of brine, smoke, and living skin.

Nothing in it is made to last. And that is intentional.

The people who made it are still there, and you mistook them for part of the fauna.

They are on the wall above you. They have been watching since you entered the basin, which is why the ticking of nails on rock never stopped when you approached.

You thought they were animals. That is an easy mistake because they move like animals when they want to. They are small. If one stood upright on the flat — which it almost never does — it would come to your chest, maybe 1.2 meters, and it would look wrong doing it. Upright is not its shape. Its shape is *crouch and cling*.

Call them the Basin folk for now. They have no name you can pronounce without clicking your tongue against your palate. They are not human, though they are built on the same few solutions. Two legs, two arms, a head. But the proportions are pulled long by a life lived on vertical, unfinished stone and in air that is a fraction too light.

Their skin is the first thing you notice when one finally drops down to the terrace level to tend the fire. It is hairless, damp, and the color of wet shale — a mottled grey-pink that darkens to near-black when they are dry and pales when they have been sitting in a pan. It has to stay damp. Like the biofilm they harvest, like the grazer things they eat, they are water-animals that have learned to live in air by never quite leaving the water. Glands along their flanks and throat bead constantly with moisture; in the dry hour after a pan evaporates they retreat to shade and go still, conserving. In the wet hour they are everywhere. You can see salt rime crusted at their temples and along their spines where their bodies excrete what the water leaves behind. They drink constantly, lapping from films with a protrusile tongue.

Their hands are what tell you they are people. They are all hands. The fingers — six on each, long, double-jointed, tipped with a flat nail that is half claw — make up nearly half the length of the arm. The thumb is not opposed so much as *central*, a finger that can swing to meet any of the others. When they climb, which is always, those hands splay and grip the sharp basalt without being cut; the skin of the palms is thick and pebbled like the grazer carapaces. When they work, those hands are surgical. You watch one pick up a flake of black glass, turn it, strike it once with a rounded hammerstone, and produce an edge you could shave with. The motion is casual, practiced a thousand times before the stone was even cool. Another hand holds a thigh-bone awl, its point polished by use to a yellow gloss. A third scrapes salt into a hollow stone with a rhythmic, circular patience.

Their faces are tucked. No snout, no muzzle, but a flattened, almost fetal profile — large, lidless eyes set wide, covered by a clear nictitating membrane that flicks sideways when steam gusts. The eyes are not light-blind like the dart-fliers. They are light-*hungry*. Amber, faceted, they reflect the bacterial glow even in daylight. They see the biofilm as brightness. They see heat as color. Ears are not external at all but shallow pits behind the jaw that open and close. They do not need to hear well over distance; the basin is too loud for that. They speak, when they speak, in close, low sounds — clicks, taps, breathy whistles — and more often they speak with touch, drumming fingertips on stone or on each other's shoulders. A whole conversation can travel through the rock as vibration while to you it looks like two of them are just sitting together.

They live in bands that are not families the way you count them. Eight, twelve, twenty individuals sharing a terrace and the pans on it. No chief. No houses to own. The old ones — you can tell by the way their skin goes pale and almost translucent, and by the salt-bleached crust on their backs — sit closest to the fire and knap and grind, while the younger ones, leaner and more nervous, run. Always running. A child no bigger than a hare will sprint on all fours across stone that would slice your feet, snatch a double-handful of shrimp-lice from a pan, and sprint back, the whole trip taking twenty seconds, before a dart-flier can stoop on it. They play in the shallow pans, half-submerged, filtering gnat-clouds through baleen-like fringes on their lower lip — you realize late that the gnats are not pests to them, but soup. They laugh by puffing air through those fringes.

Everything about them is fast. Their lives are fast because the basin is fast. You find no graves. A body here would be eaten or salted or steam-scalded within a day. Instead you find the three-stone cairns at every path crossing, and now you see a young one stop at one, add a fourth stone, take the bottom one away, and leave. Not a monument. A message. *We were here. Water this way. This pan soured. Be careful, the wall cracked here this morning.* The cairns are their writing, their prayer, and their map, all at once, constantly being rewritten because the land keeps revising itself.

Their economy is gleaning, not hunting, not farming. They do not need to farm. The basin does it for them. They tend the biofilm the way you might tend a garden that grows while you watch. A patch that was scalded by a new vent in the morning will be amber and pulsing by evening; by midnight they will have scraped half of it into mouths or into flat stones to dry into chewy, iron-tasting leather. They know which grazers to eat — the black-armored bulldozers are left alone, too tough and too full of mineral — and which to crack open and roast. The carapaces left in the fire pit are from the mid-sized, soft-bellied ones; the children chew the legs like gristle. They collect the shed chitin plates of the larger things and lash them into shelter and into shallow bowls that never leak because the chitin sweats and swells shut.

They are not awed by the weirdness. They use it.

Where the gravity goes light near the deepest, hottest updrafts, the dust hanging too long, they have learned to jump. A youth will launch from a high terrace with limbs spread, the loose skin between forearm and flank catching air like a meager wing, and glide — not fly, but fall very slowly — for fifteen meters to the next ledge, where hands find holds that shouldn't have been reachable. Where sound arrives before motion, they have learned to flinch *before* the crack, their whole band going still a half-second before a slab calves off the wall, as if listening to the future. Where the shadows under the skylights don't line up, they don't look at shadows at all. They navigate by the feel of warm stone under their palms, by the smell of brine, by the taste of the air.

They are not many in any one place and they are *everywhere* if you count. Four kilometers of basin, perhaps three or four hundred of them total, spread so thin that from your shelf they looked like lichen. But the rock ticks with them constantly. At night — if you can call it night when the bacterial veins never go dark and the skylights show stars — the whole basin clicks and taps as they drum on stone to talk across terraces: *pan full here, vent opened there, lost a child to fliers, found a new dry ledge.*

They will not approach you unless you sit and be still and let condensation bead on you until you look like part of the stone. If you do, one will come close enough that you can see your distorted reflection in its amber eye, and you will smell iron and salt and warm hide. It will reach — not for you, but for the salt crust on the rock beside your foot — scrape it with a nail, taste it, decide. Then it will look at you for a long time with an expression you cannot read, because its face does not move like yours. Then it will leave a single chipped flake of black glass by your boot, edge out, an offering or a warning or simply the thing it had in its hand and no longer needs.

They do not belong to the basin the way an animal belongs to a burrow. They belong the way foam belongs to a wave — a temporary, intricate pattern that the energy of the place throws up because it is so full and so turbulent that *something* has to be that shape for a little while. If the roof finally finished collapsing, if the water stopped weeping, if the stone cooled, they would be gone. Not dead, just impossible. Unwritten.

For now, while the rock is still being born and the pans still fill and empty and the air still hangs too light, they are perfect. They scrape, they climb, they breed quickly in warm, hidden crevices — clutches of soft, translucent eggs the size of grapes tucked into damp cracks, guarded for weeks until they hatch as miniature, already-clinging copies — they die quickly too, and are eaten, and are replaced before the salt has even re-formed.

The basin breathes steam around them. A new crack opens with a gunshot report behind the farthest terrace, and for half a heartbeat every living thing goes silent — fliers, grazers, the folk on the walls. Then the roar returns, louder, and you see them already moving toward the fresh break, hands outstretched, to see what new film has been exposed, what new pan is forming, what new place there is to live for an hour or a day before it changes again.
