What lives in the hollow is not at first visible as something living.

That is its first adaptation.

When you come down over the soft lip and stand with the warm air pooled around your ankles, you think at first that nothing does. That is what the hollow wants you to think, and what it has taught its occupant to perfect. Life here is not what moves or calls or crosses the middle distance. Life here is what has learned to be indistinguishable from the stillness itself.

You have to stay a long time to see it. Longer than is comfortable. You have to let your own breathing even out and become as infrequent as the drip from the stone.

At first you only notice the damp patch again. The soft, dark earth in the lowest concavity where the condensation gathers. It stays soft to the touch. It never quite dries, never quite floods. You think it is just earth.

Then you notice that the earth breathes.

Not quickly. If you watch it directly you will miss it. It lifts - a fraction of a millimeter - and settles, on a cycle so slow you would swear you imagined it if you counted. Once every few minutes. A shallow rise and fall that moves the mineral ring around it by nothing at all.

And then you notice that the patch has an edge.

Not the irregular, feathered edge that damp makes as it seeps into dry. This edge is too even. A faint, close seam where the dark meets the dusty brown-red, like the hem of something laid down. If you crouch - and the air is warmer down there, close and still - you see that the surface is not granular. It is skinned.

It is a skin.

Whatever lives here lives flat. It has to. There is no depth for anything else. It holds the lowest place in the hollow the way water would if there were enough of it, spread thin across the concavity, not deep at all. At its largest after a rain when the temporary sheets collect and sink, it might be as wide as a low table, an oval that fits itself exactly to the shape the water makes. In the ordinary dry, it pulls in and thickens, no larger than a folded blanket. You could step over it without knowing, and most things do.

Its color is the color of the place. That is not camouflage as an effort, but as an identity. The dorsal side - if up can be called dorsal when you spend your whole life pressed to the earth - is dusty brown-red freckled with pale pebbles that are not pebbles but warts of calcified skin, and patches of that dark green moss grow *on* it, not beside it. Not parasitically. The moss has rooted in the thin film of silt that collects along its back. When it is very still, which is almost always, you cannot tell where stone ends and it begins. Its edge blurs into lichen. Its center, where it is thinnest over the damp, is the same sweating sandstone tan, darkened by moisture.

Underneath, it is paler. Soft. Wet. It does not have feet, or rather it has one foot, like a slug on a scale that would be monstrous elsewhere. A broad, muscular sole that seals itself to the coolest stone and drinks. There are no tracks because it does not drag itself. It flows. When it does move - once every few hours, once a day - it does so by pouring itself a few inches toward the seep, a slow tide of itself, so gradual the pebbles around it do not shift.

Its face, if you can call it that, is at the margin nearest the weeping rock, where the condensation beads and slips. There, the skin thins and lifts just enough to make a lip. No eyes, or not eyes as you would recognize. On either side of that lip are two shallow pits the size of lentils, matte black and always wet, that do not see light so much as feel its diffusion. In this dim, shadowless place sight would be useless. They sense the dimming when a cloud thickens, the faint change in brightness from hour to hour. Mostly they wait for the vibration of a drop.

That is what it eats, or rather that is how it eats. It does not hunt. It does not graze, though algae and the tender edges of moss dissolve slowly against its underside when it rests over them. It waits for water.

You can watch it feed if you are patient enough to forget you are watching.

A bead gathers on the sandstone, swells, and lets go. It falls - *tick* - onto the dark skin. The skin dimples. For a long moment nothing happens. Then a slow, concentric ripple passes across the entire surface, from the point of impact out to the pebbled edge, as if the whole animal is registering the gift. The lip at the stone-side ever so slightly opens, a crescent of paler tissue, and the film of water on its back is drawn, impossibly slowly, toward that mouth. It drinks the hollow's sweat. Minerals and the microscopic life that lives in that single drop - that is its meal. One drop every few minutes, a few dozen an hour in the cool morning, fewer at midday. It is enough, because it needs almost nothing.

It does not need to move because the water comes to it. It does not need to breathe quickly because the air is still and warm and the hollow holds its own atmosphere. Its heart - somewhere deep in the thin oval, not centered - beats perhaps six times an hour. Between beats it is listening. That is the only word for its state.

It is old. Not old like the shrub is old, twisted and stunted over decades. Old like the hollow is old. The runnels stopped cutting deeper and it arrived, or emerged. Perhaps it is the same age as that low line of three stones on the southern slope, which were set by hands that did not stay because the place does not support the kind of life that builds and leaves. The stones sank, and it spread over the damp that those builders had come to drink from, and it remained. You can see in the way the lichen has grown onto its back without interruption that it has not moved far in a very long season.

It knows the hollow not as a place it inhabits, but as its body extended. The slope is its cool side and warm side. The overcast is its sky because it never looks up. The tick of water is its clock. The faint arrangement of stones at the southern edge - that almost-geometry - it will sometimes cover and sometimes uncover over weeks, flowing over one of the toppled markers and then receding, not from curiosity but because the damp shifts a hand's breadth that way in winter, which is not winter.

It does not invite or repel, just as the hollow does not. If you sit on the rim with your feet in the warmer air, it does not know you as predator or prey. You are a weather, briefly. A pressure, a shadow, a little more carbon dioxide in the still air. It will hold its breath - that long, minute-spaced breath - a little longer while you are there. When you leave, it will ripple once and continue.

This is what can live where nothing seems to move: something that has made a perfect agreement with stillness. Something that requires so little - a dim light, a stone that sweats, a patch of soft earth that never dries - that it never needs to break the equilibrium. It has become the hollow's own damp, its own listening.
