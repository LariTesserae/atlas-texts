Nothing lives here the way a city is supposed to be lived in.

That is the first answer, and it holds for a long time after you arrive. You walk the center line of an avenue that was poured for eight lanes and hear only your own shoes, and the hiss from a vent stack three blocks over. You look into a factory that could have employed a thousand people and see empty air and a single chain swaying. You wait for someone to appear in a window and no one does.

Then you stay a little longer, and you realize you were looking for the wrong scale of inhabitant. You were looking for people.

The city is not empty. It is thinned to what can actually live here.

### The Weather That Lives Here

The first resident is the one the city was redesigned to house without ever admitting it. Not people. Weather.

The whole mesa is a lung. The deep rock underneath runs hot — whether the heat is still coming up from the basalt or left over from the furnaces that used to be here no longer matters, it is the same heat now. It pushes up through every slab and culvert and pipe chase. The lid above — that amber-gray overcast that never moves — stays cool. Between them, the city sweats.

And the sweat stays. You understand now why the buildings stand so far apart, why the streets are too wide, why every roof is a shallow pitch feeding a half-pipe, why chains hang instead of downspouts. They are not intervals for traffic or light. They are clearances for a herd of invisible animals to move. Columns of warm air rise off every spillway and cooling fin, condense on the cool air, and fall. They rise again.

If you stand still long enough on an elevated walkway and let your eyes unfocus, you can see them. Not steam as a thing coming out of a pipe, but a system of slow, standing convection cells, each as big as a neighborhood, turning over and over. They have territories. They have habits. One will always form over the basin plazas at 14:00 when the sub-slab pumps cycle. One lives permanently in the lee of the largest tenement stack and never leaves, a pale pillar that leans a little south. They drink from the settling basins and graze the wet sides of buildings. They are the true citizens. The streets are their riverbeds and the buildings are cliffs built to make them rain.

You can hear them breathing. That low 60-cycle thrum through your soles is not just electricity — it is the city inhaling through intakes the size of garages and exhaling through a thousand lips.

### The Skin

Where the weather touches stone, the second resident lives. 

You thought there was no plant life. That is true. Nothing grows upward here. Everything that lives has learned to lie flat.

Crouch down at the edge of one of those shallow, hot basins. The water is only three or four inches deep, perfectly clear and hot as a bath, sitting over basalt gravel that has been rounded and polished by decades of being underwater. The rim is crusted with a pale travertine beard, soft as chalk. And on every surface that stays wet — the underside of the lip, the side of the gutter, the shady face of a concrete channel where water runs in a film no thicker than a coin — there is paint.

Black-green when thick, coppery-black when thin, sometimes rust-red where iron is being eaten. It looks like an oil slick that decided to become solid. It does not have leaves or stems. It does not even look alive until you watch the way its edge advances a millimeter a year, leaving behind a faint etched line in the mineral it has deposited.

This is what won. A mat, a felt, a collaboration of bacteria and archaea that does not photosynthesize so much as *thermosynthesize* and *chemosynthesize*. It eats the heat itself. It eats dissolved basalt and concrete and the sulfur in the steam. It is not in the city, it *is* the city’s skin now. Acres and acres of it, covering every wet runnel like lichen on a scale a forest would envy. If you scrape it with a fingernail it comes away as a greasy, layered sheet, and underneath the concrete is pitted where it has been slowly digesting it for longer than you have been alive.

In the places where it is thickest — below the big spillways where water has run for forty years without stopping — it has built its own geology. Travertine and biofilm have laminated together into soft, living stalactites, hollow curtains that drip constantly. Put your ear close and you can hear them fizz faintly.

This is the dominant life. It holds the basins so they don't leak. It seals the cracks. It drinks the condensation before it can pool too deep. The engineers who sealed every soil surface did not mean to make a petri dish the size of a county, but they did.

And where the Skin is thickest, it feeds the others.

### The Slow Animals

Once you learn to see the Skin, you start to see what grazes on it.

They are not many. The carrying capacity is low, like a desert. Everything moves slowly to conserve water and keep from overheating.

There are the blind things — pale, segmented, the length of your thumb, not ants and not roaches but something that came from one of them and changed. They do not build hives in the dry. They build a paper city smaller than your hand tucked into the expansion joint of a wall that never dries, and they farm the Skin. You find the empty ones first, delicate as ash, abandoned when the mat underneath died back. Then, if you are quiet, you find one that is still tended, a soft traffic of white bodies moving over black-green felt, harvesting it with mouthparts too small to see.

There is the heron you saw. There are others like it, but never more than one at a time. They are not really herons. When you see a second one from a gantry, closer, you realize it has too many joints in its legs, that its skin is translucent enough to show the hot mineral water moving through its gut, that its head is mostly a tube for siphoning and straining. It folds itself down like origami to drink. It stands for an hour without moving in the steam, waiting for the blind things or the film to thicken enough to be worth scraping. It looks at you and does not recognize *animal* because nothing else here is shaped like you. Then it unfolds — impossibly tall, impossibly thin — and steps between two tanks that are fifty feet apart and seems to take a long time to get there because the scale is so large it fools your eye.

They are not visitors. They are just rare. This is their correct density: one large grazer per several square kilometers of Skin.

And then there are the things that were built and never stopped.

You hear one before you see it properly: a soft squeal of metal on rail, so slow you think you imagined it. A gantry crane the size of a house inches along its track over an empty yard, carrying no load. It stops. An hour later it moves back. Elsewhere, a valve opens on its own and a plume of steam jumps thirty feet and vanishes; a basin drains with a throat-clearing gurgle and refills at its own pace. A pump kicks on somewhere under your feet, muttering for fifteen minutes and then going still.

At first you think these are automatic leftovers, dead processes cycling. Then you watch a maintenance servitor — not a robot the way films show robots, but a squat, flat-topped crawler on wide, heat-proof tracks, the size of a laundry cart, with a single articulated arm. It has no lights, no face. It trundles out of a service bay you thought was bricked up, stops at a clogged half-pipe, scrapes travertine crust away with a wire brush until water flows clean again, and trundles back. It does not hurry. It does not look around. It knows this runnel the way a crab knows its tide pool.

There are dozens of them, maybe hundreds, spread so thin across the city that you could walk for days and only see three. They are not intelligent. They are not being controlled. They are the city’s other grazers, tending the Skin and the Weather the way the blind insects do, only their Skin is rust and scale, and their Weather is pressure.

They are not abandoned. They are *feral*. The city thinned, but its metabolism did not stop. The pumps and gutters and basins and Skin and crawlers and herons and steam cells keep each other in balance, predatorless and purposeless and persistent. An ecology made of concrete and water and heat.

If there ever were people here — and there were, you can see the dimensions of door handles and stair risers, the height of railings — they either left when they realized the mesa could only be comfortably inhabited by something that can drink 50-degree mineral water and lie flat, or they stayed and changed. You find a place where a basin has been cupped a little deeper, where a towel hangs over a pipe that is cooler than the others, where the travertine has been scrubbed off a sitting ledge. Someone was here recently, or something that was once someone. But you never see them during the day. The light is too dim and too even to hide in, and yet the intervals are so vast that a single figure could always be one block over, just out of sight, moving slowly through the steam.

If you stay until the flat, shadowless dusk, when the overcast dulls even further and the basins reflect the bruised sky more perfectly than the sky itself, you will feel the heat settle and the condensation pick up. Drops gather on every edge. The Skin gives off its faint metallic smell. The heron drinks. A crawler brushes its track. A whole avenue exhales at once and the water, which had been holding its breath, starts to run.

It is then you understand the city is not waiting to be reinhabited. It is inhabited, completely. It just isn’t inhabited by you.
