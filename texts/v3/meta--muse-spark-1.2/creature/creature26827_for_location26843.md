Nothing lives there.

That is your first thought, and it is almost true. This room is too young to be inhabited, too loud and too bright and too sharp. It has not had time to be lived in. It was made to hold water and now it does, and that is all it does. Anything that tried to live here would be scoured clean.

And then, if you stay on the steps with your ankles in the trembling line of the water and let the roar settle into a rhythm, you begin to see that something does — but nothing that has finished becoming itself yet.

The first residents are not residents at all. They are volunteers.

In the seams where the plaster failed to take, a few threads of algae hang and shiver. They are thinner than hair, a green so pale it is almost white, each filament anchored by a single holdfast to the raw lime. They do not grow in mats or clouds. There are maybe a dozen of them on the whole north wall, waving in the current like antennae. They are not thriving. They are *trying*. Near the overflow lip, where the stone is always wet and the sun never quite hits, a clump of reed has found a crack. Three stems, no more, translucent and jointed, the color of young celery. It has no business being here — its seed must have blown down the oculus or ridden in on a workman's boot — but it has rooted, and it holds. When the inflow surges the stems bend flat and then spring back, patient.

They make the room feel larger, not smaller. That is how you know they live here: they emphasize the emptiness they have not filled.

The second resident is not in the room when you are.

But they have just left, or they are about to return. You can read them in the things that are not stone and not water.

The bucket drifting in its slow circle is not debris. It is too deliberate, half-sunk and bailing itself with every lap against the wall. The coil of fiber rope on the submerged ledge is placed where a hand can find it without looking. The scaffolding, which should have been struck and taken away once the vault was finished, still leans against the east wall, and its poles are dark and swollen, and the lashings have left pale ghosts on the stone where they were moved, and moved again. And on the west wall, under the water, there is a thumbprint in the curing plaster. Someone pressed there to test if it had set. The lime clouded, settled, and the print stayed, soft-edged now, filling slowly with silt.

Whoever lives here is the person that print belongs to. Not an owner. A keeper.

She is young, because the room is young. She would have to be.

You imagine her before you see her — and then, because this kind of place makes imagination real, you do see her, or the shape she has taken on after weeks of tending it.

She is somewhere between twenty and indeterminate, the age of an apprentice given a job too simple and too important to give to anyone else: *keep the water moving*. She comes down the sharp steps barefoot because shoes would be ruined in an hour and because the stone still cuts. The soles of her feet are white and softened, wrinkled even when dry. Her calves and forearms are always a little pink, not from sun but from lime, a faint chemical burn that never quite becomes a rash. Her hair is cut short or tied back severely — long hair would be a liability near the sluice — and it has been bleached almost colorless by the reflected sun that pours down the oculus and then comes back up again off the water. Double sun.

She lives here the way a lighthouse keeper lives in a lighthouse. That is to say, she doesn't live here at all, and she never leaves.

There is nowhere dry for her to sit except the top two steps, so she sits there with her feet in the flow. Or she floats. She has learned to float for hours in the column of light without being deafened by it, to let the roar become a kind of silence. The water is warm as skin, so after a while she loses the edge of herself. She hangs in the middle of the chamber, suspended, hair fanned out, eyes open to the chisel marks on the floor three meters down, breathing shallowly while the lattice of light crawls over her. She has to be careful not to drift into the drain. She has learned to move with a single lazy flick of the wrist, the way you would in a bath that is the size of a room.

She talks sometimes, because the vault makes any sound huge and immediate, and because no one can hear her over the pour. Or she sings, badly, loudly, to hear a human note inside all that water-noise. The stone throws her voice back at her, wet and doubled.

She is not native to this kind of brightness. No one is. But she has adapted the way cave fish adapt in reverse — not to darkness but to absolute, scouring light. Her pupils are always pinned small. She squints even when she smiles. At night, when the oculus is a circle of stars and the water goes black and finally quiet, she sleeps on the scaffolding, on a plank thrown across two poles with a folded sack for a pillow, just above the water line, and the sound of the sluice becomes her whole dream.

If you asked her what lives here she would say *I do*, and then correct herself and say *we do*, meaning herself and the three reeds and the algae threads. She sweeps silt off the steps with her hands. She clears a leaf from the drain before it can catch. She watches the plaster cure. She will be here until the stone softens, until the edges round, until green takes the walls properly and fish find their way in and the water is no longer so painfully clear. Until the room is old enough to live in without her.

Until then, the room itself is the other thing that lives here.

Not metaphorically. The chamber breathes. You saw it. The inflow pulses with the wind over the catchment you cannot see; the light lattice twitches; the fine stone dust lifts and settles like plankton, like breath made visible. It holds heat, it takes light and throws it, it keeps its level at the notched lip with a trembling exactitude that is almost attention. It is a small, vigorous weather system that has been given walls. A bright, loud, warm animal made of architecture and held water, still curing, still learning to be a pool.

And it is lonely in the particular way of anything that was made recently and for a purpose, and is now doing that purpose perfectly, waiting for something to use it.
