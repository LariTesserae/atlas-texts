No one lives there.

That's your first thought, and the place wants you to think it.

It is too loud and too wet and too cold for anyone to live. Whatever lived there would have to be mad or made for it, and nothing looks made for it at first. You see the mosses and you think *oh, the plants live there.* You see the cloud of midges over the vent and the snow buntings flicking after them and you think *oh, the birds live there.* You see the fox prints and you think *oh, something passed through.* But nothing *lives* there. Nothing stays. The pocket is too busy being itself — sweating, dripping, clanking, venting, freezing and unfreezing — to house anyone. It feels inhospitable the way a workshop feels inhospitable when the saw is running and the floor is wet.

You have to stay until the cold gets into your molars. Until you stop looking *for* something and start looking *at* the pace of it.

Then what lives there starts to un-hide.

First, you notice that the machine is not abandoned.

Not tended, exactly. Not *cared for.* But not abandoned either. The belt that drives the small turbine should have snapped years ago. The flywheel should be seized with rust. Valves that slap and vent should have scaled shut. But they haven't. The moss is stripped clean off the flywheel on the spray side, but left bearded on the lee side — something has been wiping it. The mineral bloom on the big pipe — that beautiful rot of white and green and orange — is scraped clean in one place, a patch about the size of a hand, where the metal shows through darker and wet. And fresh. The pressure gauge, its glass cracked, has been tapped clear of condensation. The submerged pump chugs just a little too evenly for a thing left to its own devices; someone has cleared the sphagnum that would otherwise have choked its intake.

You think: *a person.* A hermit, a watchman, some old surveyor who stayed with his drill. You look for a bed, a tarp, a fire ring, a boot print that isn't yours. There is none. No human sign at all except the absence of failure.

Then you see that the wiping wasn't done by a hand.

She is the size of the space between two drips. Which is to say, you keep missing her because she is exactly the color and speed of the things around her.

She lives tight against the north wall, where the big pipe enters the basalt, where the heat is greatest and the rock weeps the fastest. At first you took her for a continuation of the wall — a bulge of black basalt made darker by water, bearded with the same star moss and cold fern that covers the ledge. But basalt doesn't breathe.

She breathes with the piston.

When the barrel-wide piston coughs up, she inflates. When it thuds down, she sighs. Not in mimicry. In time. Her sides — if they are sides — rise a half inch, fall a half inch, so slowly you thought it was steam moving over her.

She is low. She is wide. She is not tall enough to have ever needed to stand. If she stood, she would be up to your chest, but she never stands. She sprawls. She is built like a seal made out of peat, like a great, smoothed boulder that decided to be soft.

Her back is the floor's carpet carried up onto her. Sphagnum living on her, and liverwort, and the black-green fern. Not growing *on* her like a costume — growing *from* her. The moss is rooted in a pelt so dense and matted it holds water the way the bog does. It is olive and rust and luminous green, and it sweats. Droplets form on the tips of the moss on her back and run down her flanks and fall into the marsh with the same soft *plip* as the condensation off the walls. In winter, frost furs her and then melts from the heat of the pipe. She steams.

Underneath that, where the moss parts along her spine where the spray hits hardest, you see skin. Not fur. Not feather. Skin the pale, translucent white-pink of the amphipods boiling in the water, of something that has never needed sun. Thin skin, with a tracery of dark vessels underneath, and beneath that, a slow pulse that is not hers alone — it is the double heartbeat of the two out-of-phase pumps, made flesh.

Her head — if you can call it a head — is mostly mouth and mostly nostril. No neck to speak of. The face is pushed flat and broad, like an otter's or a catfish's, whiskered with long, white, mineral-stiffened filaments that quiver in the steam and read the air. Her eyes you miss for a long time because you expect eyes to shine. Hers don't. They are small and black and wet as the snails' shells, set deep under a brow of moss, each covered with a nictitating membrane that blinks sideways, slowly, to clear condensation. She watches without looking. She looks at heat, at vibration.

She does not fear you. Fear would require that you be relevant.

What she does — what her life *is* — is tending. Not consciously. Not lovingly. The way your tongue tends the inside of your mouth without thought.

She scrapes. With a forelimb that is not an arm and not a flipper but something in between — broad, spade-like, tipped with black keratin that has grown long and chipped like old basalt — she reaches out, so slowly it looks like the rock itself is shifting, and draws it down the bloom-crusted pipe. *Scrit.* A curl of mineral falls into the water where the snails will find it. She clears the intake of the submerged pump with a sweep of that same spade, pushing sphagnum aside, letting the water gulp. She leans her bulk against the belt when it slips and screeches, and the added tension catches it again. She does not know this is maintenance. She knows that when the shuddering stops, the warm water stops, and when the warm water stops, the midges stop, and when the midges stop, the film she grazes on thins.

Because she grazes.

She is the grazer the whole pocket is built to feed. She extends a lower lip — vast, pale, frilled — and drags it across the basalt, across the pipe, across the film of iron bacteria and algae that the heat and the iron bleed make so brilliantly green and rust-red. She leaves behind the bright trails you thought the snails made, but wider, cleaner. The snails follow *her* trails. The buntings follow the snails. The fox comes to drink where she has been because the water is warmest there.

She mothers, too, though not as you understand it. Along her lower flank, where her heat meets the cold marsh, there are pockets in the moss-pelt, deep, warm, wet pouches. If you stare long enough — and the piston gives you a rhythm to stare to — you see them move independently. A dozen, two dozen small, pale shapes, like oversized amphipods, like blind tadpoles with whiskers, squirming in the fur-moss, slipping out to snap at midge larvae in the water and then squirming back into the mother's warmth. They never go more than a hand's length from her. She shifts, and they shift with her, a tide in her coat.

She is not old. She is not young. She is as old as the pocket, which is to say she was born a few hundred years ago when the sea left and the crust rebounded and the split opened and the warm water found the cold air for the first time. She may be the only one. There may be other pockets along this flat coast, each with its own one. You will not find them by looking for her. You find them by finding the steam that shouldn't be there.

When the valve slaps open and vents a cloud that rolls along the floor and does not rise, she closes her membrane-eyes and opens her mouth and breathes it in, condensation beading instantly on her whiskers, on her moss. She is the reason the pocket has not frozen shut or dried out or choked on its own moss. She is the contrast made animal. She eats cold and drinks hot and makes a life out of the difference.

She does not notice you leaving, except as a brief shadow in the heat. When you haul yourself back up the sharp, glassy basalt and your boots leave the living water for the thin black peat, the sound of the piston thudding and the turbine whining stays behind, and underneath it, if you listen on the wind, that slower, wetter sigh.

From the flat, once you step back, there is nothing there at all. Just peat, and knee-high willow growing sideways, and a wisp of steam like any other wisp of cold air.

But down there, under the brow of birch, the moss on the moving rock gathers another bead of water, and lets it fall.
