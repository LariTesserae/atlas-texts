They are not there when you first look.

Not really. You see the town first — the density of roofs, the pots, the wet stone, the lamps — and you think it is empty. Abandoned, maybe. Preserved. The sound is so loud and the light so even that your eye slides over them. They move like the place moves: slowly, without hurry, without breaking the pattern of drip and gurgle. Camouflage is not something they try for. It happens.

Then you see a hand.

A hand on a pot. The pot was already shining and you hadn't noticed that someone had set it exactly where the ceiling bead would fatten and fall. The hand is wet. Everything is wet. You only see it because it moved — lifted, tipped, poured — and then it was still again.

At first you think: people. Small people, cave people. And that image will hold for a while because it is the easiest one to hold.

They are short. They have to be — the ceiling in the center just brushes the top of your reach, and near the walls you must stoop, and they have stooped for so long that it has shaped them. Not bent, exactly. Settled. Shoulders rounded forward, necks tucked, heads carried a little low as if they are always listening to the stone just in front of their feet. Their clothes — if you can call them clothes — are not cloth the way you know cloth. There is no cotton here, no flax, no sheep. No plant to make thread from. What they wear is skin and softened hide, and felt beaten from whatever sparse hair and down can be saved and saved again, dark with damp and fat and years of handling. It never dries. It hangs on them like a second, looser skin, cold to the touch.

They are pale the way things under stone are pale. Not white. The color of the cream mineral when it is wet, or the underside of a fish. Their skin has a sheen to it, almost translucent at the temples and the wrists, where you can see the blue-grey of veins very close to the surface. Their hair, where they have it, is thin, colorless, cut short with a stone blade or pulled back and bound with sinew — nothing for the drip to catch on. Their eyes —

You expect them to look at you. They don't, not directly. The light is dim enough that you have to be close to see their faces properly, and when you are, you understand they have been looking with other senses for a very long time.

Their eyes are small, and pale, and unfocused. Not blind the way the little salamander in the basin is blind — they have eyes — but the eyes have gone quiet. Lids often half-closed against the perpetual mist. They narrow against glare that isn't there. They water constantly. In the amber-grey mineral glow they seem to reflect nothing. You realize after watching one pass within an arm's length of you without turning his head, that they navigate by sound and by touch far more than by sight. This place is too loud for shouting and too small for calling anyway. They tap.

A knuckle on a slab. A fingernail on a pot rim. Two stones clicked together. The sound cuts through the composite roar in a way a voice never could, and it carries cleanly off the ovoid walls. You see one old woman — you think woman, though it is hard to tell, they are all slight and similarly wrapped — tap a pattern on the lip of the central basin and a younger one on a roof walkway two alleys over taps back, not an answer but an acknowledgment: *I am here. This channel is clear.*

They do not speak much. When they do it is low and close, mouth to ear, hands cupped around words to keep the damp out of them.

There are not many of them. You count and lose count and count again because the alleys fold and the roofs overlap and the same figure seems to appear in three places at once. Thirty, maybe forty. No children running. No old ones bedridden. They all look roughly the same age, which is ageless — thirty and sixty at once — skin smoothed by wet air rather than wrinkled by sun. If there are births here, they are rare and quiet. If there are deaths, they are rarer. The town has found its number the way the basin has found its level. It does not press.

Watch them longer and the first image — cave people — starts to thin and become something else. Because they don't behave like people who *live* somewhere. They behave like people who *are* somewhere's way of keeping itself.

No one is making anything new.

You wait to see a new wall go up, a new pot coiled. It doesn't happen. Instead you see the same pot mended. A hairline crack traced with a fingertip, packed with the grey clay that never dries, smoothed, set back precisely under its drip. You see a man — again, you think man — lift a slab that has not fallen, check its seating, dust the stone-on-stone contact with a pinch of grit, and lower it again to the exact same place. You see a woman wipe down a wall that is already clean, her rag leaving a darker wet streak that vanishes in seconds as condensation sheets over it again. You see someone bail the central basin with a small stone cup — one cup out, poured into the perimeter channel — and then a moment later the level is exactly what it was, and he does it again. Not because it needs doing. Because it is the doing that keeps the place from forgetting how.

They are maintenance without progress. Repair without decay. Ritual without belief, or belief so old it has smoothed into ritual the way the stone has smoothed into curve.

Their tools tell you how long this has been going on. Nothing is new and nothing is discarded. A stone hammer's handle is polished to a dark glass where hands have held it for generations, the head re-pecked and re-ground until it is half its original size. A bone awl is worn to a needle. An iron knife — there are only a few — is kept in a niche wrapped in hide and fat, taken out, used, cleaned, put back, the edge touched up on a slate hone that itself is dished from centuries of use. Every pot has a chip, a mend, a soot mark from the fat lamp it once sat beside. No one throws anything into the slit where the water drains. Everything is kept, because what would you replace it with? There is only stone.

If you watch long enough you stop seeing them as inhabitants at all. That is the second image giving way to a third, slower one.

They are not *in* the town. They are part of its physics, like the drip. Like cilia. Like the pale isopods under the pot that only move when exposed. They have the same patient, unhurried tempo as the condensation itself — bead, swell, slip, fall, catch, pour, return, evaporate, bead again. You begin to see a person empty a pot into a channel not as a chore but as a circulation. You see the tap on stone not as language but as a signal pulse. You see the careful, endless wiping of walls not as cleaning but as keeping the sheet of water unbroken so the mineral light stays even.

They were not built for the surface that you came from. They could not live there. The sun would be too sharp, the air too dry — it would crack them. The wind-scoured basalt flats above would be a desert to them, though you thought of them as merely barren. They belong to this degree of cool, this degree of wet, this degree of dim, this degree of loud. Take one out through the fissure and he would dry out before he reached the daylight.

And the most unsettling thing, after an hour or after a day — the time dilates here and you lose the count — is that they do not seem to mind you. Or to need you. Or to wonder why you came down. You are not a stranger to them; you are a temporary extra volume of warm air that will cause a little extra condensation and then leave. Someone may move a pot two inches to catch the drip your shoulder is deflecting. That is the extent of your impact. They do not gather. They do not stare. They just keep tending.

There is a moment — if you are still, if you crouch near the central basin and let the roar become your own hearing — when one of them will stop beside you to bail his cup, and his wet, cool forearm will brush yours, and you will feel how smooth his skin is, polished like the lips of the pools, and you will see, close up, the tiny, almost invisible fine hairs on that arm beaded with mist, and the faint, rhythmic pulse at his wrist, and you will understand all at once that what lives here never arrived.

It condensed.

It was left, like the bubble itself was left when the world cooled. It grew until it fit exactly and then stopped growing because fitting exactly was the whole point. It is not a colony. It is not a remnant of people who got lost. It is what the stone does when it is wet and enclosed and given time — it makes hands to wipe itself, feet to wear its paths smooth, a small number of patient hearts to keep its water moving in a circle that never fills and never empties.

They are the pocket's way of touching itself. Its way of remembering its own shape.

And if you stay long enough to feel the damp settle into your own clothes, into your own hair, and your own eyes start to water and lose their focus, you feel the town gently, without malice or welcome, beginning to count you as part of its number — not as a person, but as another pot set out to catch the drip.
