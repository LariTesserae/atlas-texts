You don't see them at first.

That's the trick of the shelf. You come up through the cloud and you see houses that are not houses, and bridges that are branches, and roofs that are meadows, and your mind files it all under *forest* - green, wet, dense, unpeopled. You think the place is empty because no straight line announces *human*. No quarried block, no sawn plank, no smoke straight up. It takes a minute for your eyes to learn the weave again and pick out what is deliberate.

Then someone moves where you thought was a wall.

And suddenly the hamlet resolves - not as buildings with people in them, but as people with buildings growing around them. You can't separate them afterwards.

### What They Look Like Up Close

They are not tall, not spare, not ethereal. Mountain air and this kind of work doesn't make willowy mystics. It makes compact, broad people. Low centers of gravity for branch-ways that sway. Short legs with big calves from stair-steps bowed by centuries. Shoulders and forearms like root-burls from pulling, twisting, hauling green wood that doesn't want to go where you want it yet.

Everyone looks a little damp. Not wet - *damp*, as if the cloud has settled into them the way it settles into the basalt. Skin is weather-pale at the start of summer and then freckled and ruddy by the end, always a little chapped at the cheeks and knuckles from cool air. Hair is kept short or braided tight back, because anything loose will collect leaf litter and small insects in an hour. Old men have beards that never quite dry. Old women have hair still dark with green in it - not dyed, just stained, from years of brushing against leaf and moss until the color leaches in.

Their hands give them away before anything else does. You hear them - loud, laughing, arguing over the price of smoked frogs - but you *know* them when you see their hands. Broad palms, nails cut to the quick and black at the rims no matter how recently scrubbed in the rill. Fingers stained a faint tannic brown-green. Cuts that have healed around splinters. They are gardener's hands, not carpenter's hands. Sensitive at the fingertips. They don't grip an axe so much as they test the give of a withy, feel where a stem wants to bend and where it will snap if you force it.

Their clothes are the same logic as their houses. Nothing that will rot if it stays damp, nothing that minds being rained on every afternoon. Thick, felted wool the color of lichen and bark and peat - greys and yellow-greens and rust brown - beaten and fulled until it can shed water for a while, then patched where it finally soaks through. Pockets everywhere. Leather only for feet and belts, because leather has to be carried up and so is hoarded. Most fastenings are wooden toggles or bone. Everyone wears something living without meaning to - a sprig of selaginella caught in a cuff, a fern spore that sprouted in the seam of a hat and was allowed to stay because it looked right.

They don't look like they live *in* the forest. They look like the forest grew a kind of fruit that learned to walk around and prune itself.

### How They Move and Sound

They are loud. That's the second failure of imagination you have to discard. You expect cloud-forest people to be quiet, reverent, whispering in moss cathedrals. They are not. To be heard over water and bird and insect and over the next layer of the hamlet above or below you, you have to project. Their voices are hoarse and bright. Children shriek with the exact pitch of jays and you learn to check which is which. A woman calling her goats on the roof-meadow above you sounds like she's shouting from the canopy, because she is. A man laughing two lanes over sounds like he's next to your ear, because the lanes are shoulder-width and the living walls bounce sound back wetly.

And they are never still. Stillness here is how you lose ground. So they fidget, tend, nip, tuck. An elder sitting on a root-buttress mending cordage will reach without looking and pinch off a runner trying to colonize his knee. A girl carrying a basket of tubers will lean and redirect a vine over a doorway as she passes, a flick of habit, the way you might push a stray hair behind your ear. They move with a three-dimensional ease that lowlanders lose. They don't think twice about stepping from moss-ground to a branch-way six feet up with a hand on an air-root for balance, or swinging a leg over a living rail to drop to a lower tier where the black pools are. Toddlers scramble. Old folk with bent backs go hand-over-hand along vine handrails as casually as you would walk a hallway. Goats do the same, and so do martens, and no one thinks this overlap strange.

Their time sense is the strangest thing about them. They can be frantic - shouting, hauling, beating wool before the next cloudburst - and patient beyond comprehension in the same hour. The man guiding a sapling into a future doorway will tie it with soft bark, nudge it half an inch, and tell you, not joking, "Come back in eight years, it'll be ready to hold your weight." A mother will plant a hazel wattle for a daughter not yet born and let it thicken until the girl is old enough to marry and cut a window where she wants it. They prune what will grow back tomorrow morning. They wait on what will be a house when they are dead. Both urgencies live in them at once without contradiction.

### How They Belong

If you stay long enough to be offered soup - peaty water, a broth of smoked pork, the curled fiddleheads of a fern that only grows on that one basalt seep, salt carried up at great price and used sparingly - you start to see the rules not as laws but as manners, as body knowledge.

They do not cut unless they also guide. To take without persuading is rude, and rudeness here is not social, it's practical. A clear cut doesn't stay clear; it rots, slumps, and takes your house with it in the next heavy drip. So they ask. They notch a little, they bind, they wait for the plant to answer. You see a boy talking to a willow. You realize he isn't being poetic. He is checking.

They keep water moving the way other villages keep a hearth lit. A child is given the job of clearing leaf mash from a rill, not as a chore but as a small seriousness, because silence in the water means something is damming and damming means rot up in the roots of your bedroom. Their prayers, if they have them, are not to peaks or sky but to that movement - little stacked cairns where a rill turns, flags above the highest drying racks that tell the wind which way to go, all of it less worship than negotiation.

And they share the vertical without resentment. The idea of owning a plot makes no sense when your floor is someone else's ceiling and your ceiling is a thrush's nesting ground and your wall belongs half to a bromeliad that was there before your grandmother. They have words for *above-my-head* and *below-my-feet* that are familial terms. The family below the big oak-crotch keeps the goats off the granary roots; the family above tends the smoke-hole so it doesn't choke the fledglings in the eaves. A death is not burial in cleared ground - there is no cleared ground - but a wrapping in moss and placement in the peat pool behind the basalt, where the black water preserves and slowly takes, and where frogs will sit on you within a day and not be shooed away.

At night, when the small fires are coaxed alive in stone pits and the cloud comes down so thick you can't see the next roof-meadow, they contract. Hammocks and lofts fill. The dripping gets louder. The frogs get louder. Someone plays a flute made from a hollow reed that has two living leaves still on it, and it sounds thin and reedy and absurdly human against the green roar. Another person answers from another tier and they are not performing; they are just marking where they are, the way the birds do.

That is who lives on the shelf. Not people who conquered a difficult place. People who were allowed to stay because they learned to be as dense, as loud, as wet, as busy, and as patient as the place itself. If you stood still long enough for moss to start up your boots - maybe twenty minutes - they would come and gently, loudly, pull you back into the traffic again, scold you for blocking the way of water and vine and goat and child, and hand you something to do with your hands.
