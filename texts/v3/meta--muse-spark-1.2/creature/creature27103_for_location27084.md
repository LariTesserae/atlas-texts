They do live here. But you have to unlearn what you mean by living first.

At first you think nothing does. That is the intended effect. Whatever lives here has learned to match the interval of the plain, which is: almost nothing happens, almost never. If you are looking for scurry and flutter and breath, you will count this place as dead and be wrong. If you stand still long enough for the thin warm air to stop feeling like weather and start feeling like a medium you are suspended in, you start to see that the stillness is not empty. It is inhabited the way a held breath is inhabited.

The most obvious one is the one you already saw.

Low, flat, about as wide as your chest, it moves out at the edge of the amber spill from an open foundry door where the heat shivers. You thought it was a tortoise. Closer, it is not.

It has no head. No tail. No front. It is a dome of overlapping plates, each plate the color of the salt pan but darker, a rose-grey ceramic that is not ceramic, a chitin that is not chitine. The plates are not quite symmetrical and they do not quite touch; there is a hairline seam of something darker and softer between them that gives when it moves. It does not walk. It flows. Underneath, you glimpse not legs but a continuous, warm underside - a dense fringe of fine, pale filaments, like the cilia of a kiln-brick, that grip the salt and pull without ever seeming to lift. It leaves no track because the filaments do not scrape; they taste and press and release. It is warm to the touch, warmer than the stone, warmer than your hand. If you put your palm near it you feel the heat coming off it before you feel anything else.

It has no eyes because it has no use for the amber light. That light is not for seeing. On its upper surface, in the center of the plates, there is a shallow pit, a dimple filled with a black glass that never reflects. It is a thermal pit. It looks up not at light but at warmth, and it navigates by the map of heat that the plateau exhales. The factories are like suns to it, low and constant. The vents are like stars. It moves toward heat the way a moth moves toward light, but without hurry, without error. A meter an hour. Maybe less. You could watch it for ten minutes and swear it hasn't moved, then look away and look back and its shadow has changed.

It does not eat in any way you recognize. There is no mouth. When it finds a place where the stone is a fraction warmer - over a buried pipe, over a seam where the rock still holds the old, basal heat - it stops. The plates loosen, just a little. The seams widen. The whole animal settles, and if you kneel very close you hear a faint, almost inaudible ticking, like a Geiger counter slowed down a thousandfold. It is precipitating. It is taking the thin, hot brine that never reaches the surface as liquid, the mineral breath that the pipelines carry overhead, the faint iron and salt aerosol that hangs motionless in the air - and it is plating it onto itself. Growing its shell from the air. A life that drinks metal and exhales nothing. When it has had enough it tightens again and resumes its crawl toward the next warm place. It may be ten years old. It may be ten thousand. The edge of one plate is chipped and the chip has not weathered.

That is one kind. There are others, and they are harder to see because they look, at first, like people.

You keep just missing them. That thermos still warm on the railing. The footprints in the fine grit between Building 7 and the brine manifold that start clean and end mid-stride, not faded, just *ended*. The service door that is warm on the inside handle.

They are there. They are sparse on purpose.

When you do see one, it will be at a distance where perspective lies to you, and you will take it for a shadow or a pipe-stand until it moves.

They are tall, but not in the way a person is tall. They are attenuated, stretched by the thin air and the low gravity you felt in your ears when you arrived, though you didn't have a name for it then. Two meters, two and a half, but narrow as a doorframe turned sideways. They wear - or they are wrapped in - something the same color as the concrete that was made from the ground stone, a matte, dusty fabric that is also insulation, also skin. You can't tell where the suit ends and the body begins, and that is not an accident. The green you saw on the pipes, that oxidized copper, is also on them in small patches at the joints: at the elbows, at the throat, at the wrists. Verdigris like lichen that is not lichen.

Their heads are small and smooth. No hair, no mouth you can see. A face that is mostly a broad, amber-tinted plate, like the glass of a furnace mask fused to the flesh. Not goggles. An eye, singular, or rather an organ for seeing heat and vibration. Underneath, where a mouth would be, there is a fine grille, a heat exchanger, that flutters faintly when they breathe - because they do breathe, but not much, and not oxygen. The air here is warm and thin and almost without water, but it carries dissolved heat and the fine mineral smoke that the slow belts grind off the basalt. They breathe that. They take a breath every few minutes, deep and deliberate, and hold it.

They do not hurry. Nothing here has ever hurried. You watch one cross between two factories, a distance you would walk in five minutes, and it takes him thirty. Each step is placed with the care of someone setting down a full cup of liquid metal. The soles of his feet are broad, soft, almost pad-like, made to feel the thrum through the rock. He navigates without looking up. He knows where every pipe is by the way it sings through his soles. The machines are not loud because they are not for him - they are extensions of him. When a pressure valve sighs two kilometers away, he tilts his head a fraction, not in alarm, just in acknowledgment. He will get to it when he gets to it. It will vent again in exactly the same way at exactly the same interval until he does.

There are very few of them. Perhaps a few dozen across a plain you could walk for days without crossing. That is why the city feels empty and not abandoned. It is not built for crowds. It is built for custodians whose metabolisms run at the speed of the rock.

They are not maintaining the factories in the way a mechanic maintains an engine - fixing what breaks. Nothing breaks here, or rather, breaking has been bred out of the system. The rust doesn't flake. The cracks don't widen. What they do is simpler and older: they keep things *even*. They walk the pipelines with a slow hand trailing on the warm iron, feeling for a cold spot that means a flow has thickened. They stand for an hour in front of an open foundry door watching the color of the molten glass inside, adjusting a valve by a quarter-turn that will not make a difference for a week. They sweep - not with brooms, but with those filamented undersides of their hands - a fine layer of undisturbed dust from a horizontal plane, not because the dust matters but because the act of sweeping keeps the dust from learning how to accumulate.

Their time is not our time. Your boot-print will stay crisp for a thousand years, and to them that is a short interval between inspections. The chirp you heard on the darker nights - that single, clear, mineral note every seventeen seconds with no answer - is one of them. Or one of their tools. Or one of their young. It is not a call. There is no one to call to across such distances who would hear in time for it to matter. It is a metronome. It is how they keep from drifting out of phase with the plain. It says: *I am still here. The interval holds.* Then it stops, because holding the interval does not require constant announcement.

They were not always like this. Or maybe they were. The buildings are the same color as the ground because they were made from it, and the people - if they are people - are the same color as the buildings. At some point the distinction between inhabitant and habitat got planed down like the basalt knuckles, until it became a habit rather than a design. You get the sense that if you cut one of them open you would find not organs but small, patient versions of the same things that run outside: a tiny brine loop, a tiny conveyor, a tiny furnace that never goes out, all running slowly and warmly and without urgency. You get the sense that if you cut open a factory you would find something like a heart, beating once every seventeen seconds.

They know you are here. They have known since your ears popped on arrival and your breathing, too fast and too loud, introduced a new, arrhythmic sound into the texture of the quiet. They are not hiding from you. They are just moving at a speed where being seen requires you to be still longer than you have yet been still. If you sit down on the warm salt near a vent, where the heat ripples and the amber light stains the dust motes that hang like glass, and you wait - not for minutes but for an hour, for two - you will see one stop at a distance and turn that smooth amber plate toward you. Not in surprise. In calibration.

You will feel, then, exactly how warm the stone is under your palms, and how thin the air is, and how the whole high, flat world is waiting for you to stop moving so it can be perfectly even again. And you will understand that to live here is not to occupy the city. It is to become one of its intervals. To keep the heat even. To keep the dust from accumulating. To move so slowly that you never appear to arrive or leave.

And when you finally stand and walk away, your footprints will end, too, for a little while, where you stepped onto the soft grit beside a pipeline that hums in the soles of your boots, before the wind that no longer blows remembers, very gently, how to smooth them.
