They live there the way water lives in a sponge.

Not *in* it. *As* it.

At first you think nothing can live there, and you are almost right. That is the trick of the Sink. From the rim you see a solid — a cauldron packed to overflowing with leaf — and your mind supplies what should be inside a solid like that: rustle, hum, chatter, rot. Monkeys. Insects in clouds. Something looking back at you through the leaves.

There is none of that. For a long time you see nothing move at all except the light shafting through the plate-leaves and the slow gather and fall of a single drop of water somewhere three stories down in the green. You begin to understand that to live here you would have to agree to the terms of the place, and the terms are severe.

Nothing grows. Nothing decays. No wind. No sound. A wet heat that never breaks.

Whatever lives there has already agreed.

### The first inhabitant is the place itself.

It is not a jungle. That is the wrong word. A jungle is many plants competing. This is one plant behaving as if it were many.

Or many plants behaving as if they were one body.

There is no real distinction between individuals in the Sink. The plate-leaves the size of doors, the ferns with trunks like houses, the sideways-growing bracing trunks, the hanging roots that never touch ground, the moss-mattress meters deep at the bottom — they are all stitched. Root grafts to root grafts, leaf-fusion to leaf-fusion, vine braided into vine until you cannot pull one free without tearing five others. If you cut a cross-section through the green you would not find soil or air gaps, you would find tissue, moist, pale, fibrous, continuous from rim to rim.

It does not grow, but it is not dead. It holds. It perspires. It beads and drinks its own water back. The whole bowl is a single, continent-sized succulent turned inside out, a lung that has learned to breathe the same hot, stagnant air over and over without needing to exchange it. That is why nothing rots. Rot requires bacteria to do work, requires time to pass. Time does not pass efficiently at the bottom of the Sink. The mass keeps itself in a perfect, sweating stasis — alive the way a seed is alive just before it germinates.

Everything else that lives there lives *inside* that body, the way fish live inside a reef. In its hallways. In its vaults. On its skin.

### The second inhabitants are small, and rare, and wet.

You will not find them by looking for movement. You find them by looking for stillness that is too perfect.

Press your hand to one of the sideways trunks where the hot light falls in a bar and look close. There — a beetle the length of your palm, the color of the waxy leaf beneath it, a pale milky green-white. Its elytra are not hard and glossy but filmed with a microscopic velvet that holds water, beading it like a leaf does. It does not scurry. It inches. One leg, pause. Another leg, long pause. If you watch it for an hour it will have moved the width of your hand. Its antennae do not flick. It breathes so slowly you cannot see it.

Further down, where the air is hottest and the light is softened through three layers of plate, a lizard. Not much bigger than your hand. White-translucent, not white as paint but white as skim milk, with a faint tracery of veins visible through its flank. It is pressed completely flat to the side of a fern trunk, limbs splayed, throat pulsing once, then not again for a long minute. Its eyes are lidded with a clear membrane against the brutal white light. It does not hunt. It waits for condensation to form on its own back and run down the groove of its spine to its mouth. It drinks its own dew.

That is their whole economy. Nothing chases. Nothing flees. There is no need. The prey does not run because the predator does not hurry. They eat, if you can call it eating, the shed waxy cuticle of the plates, the film of algae that grows on nothing, the occasional spore that should have drifted but instead hangs motionless in the still air until something opens a mouth under it. Their metabolism is almost not a metabolism. They are cool to the touch when the air is scorching. They store water in their bodies the way the plants do — in tissue, interstitially — and they can sit for days without losing it because the air never moves to wick it away.

You will go a whole day and see two. A whole week and see five, and realize you have been seeing the same five, because they do not leave their particular chamber in the green architecture. Each has a place — the north side of a certain arch, the lip of a certain leaf-wall — where the angle of the light and the drip-interval is correct, and there it stays.

They do not call. Sound dies in the leaf-mass within a few meters, swallowed by the damp. So they speak, if at all, by contact chemistry: a beetle laying a faint, waxy trail that will persist for months without evaporating; a lizard leaving the salt of its belly on bark that another will tongue weeks later. A very slow mail.

They rarely die. When they do, they do not decay and do not fall. The white lizard that stops breathing stays pressed to its trunk, and over a very long time the vine beside it grows infinitesimally over its tail — not growing, exactly, but *holding* a little tighter — until it is part of the wall.

### The third inhabitants were once people, and are still people, after a fashion.

You find their houses absorbed first and think the place is abandoned. One dry-stone terrace, roots braided through it. One roofless hut of brick, its doorway a living arch. Steps cut into the cliff that end in leaf.

Then you realize the houses are not empty.

Deeper in, where the plate-roof is most complete and the air is a white, bright, windless noon that never ends, the green has made rooms. Perfect, quiet rooms with floors of springy moss and ceilings of interlocked vine. The light comes down in hard pillars and lays itself on the floor and does not move. In those rooms, someone is sitting.

At first you think it is a root. Then you see the shape of shoulders.

They are pale. Paler than the lizards, but not albino — bleached by the reflected light, the color of the underside of the plates. Their skin has taken on the same waxy bloom as the leaves, a fine powder that beads water. Their hair is thin, damp, and does not grow. They wear little, or what they wear has become plant: a woven wrap that is now half living fiber, half cloth. They sit with their backs against the living walls, or lie splayed in the moss as if the moss had grown up around the shape of them.

They are not dead. If you sit near one long enough you will see the chest rise. Once. Then a long time later, again. Their eyes are open, but lidded heavily against the glare, milky with that same clear membrane. They do not look at you when you enter the room. Not at first. Movement is expensive here. To turn the head is to lose water.

These are the ones who stayed. The builders of those terraces and cisterns, or their children, or people like you who came to the rim, saw the bright bowl, and climbed down because they thought shade would be at the bottom. They found there is no shade, only a brighter light, and no thirst-quenching water, only water held in every surface.

To stay alive they learned to do what the beetle does.

They do not farm. There is nothing to farm — the green is not edible in bulk, and it does not produce fruit that falls. They do not hunt. Instead they harvest dew. They hold out cupped hands and the broad leaves above them, angled precisely, will gather condensation and channel it, drop by drop by drop, into the palm. They lift that to the mouth once an hour. They press their foreheads to the cool moss to drink through the skin. They scrape with a fingernail the thin, sweet exudate that forms along the seams where two plate-leaves fuse — the plant's own sweat — and lick it off.

They do not speak. They tried, and the green ate the sound. Now they communicate by leaving a handprint damp on a leaf-wall that will stay dark for half a day before it evaporates, or by simply sitting together in a room for a long time, breathing the same trapped air, shoulder to shoulder.

They do not age, exactly. The stasis that holds the leaf that will not fall holds them too. A cut on the knee stays a clean pink line for years, neither healing nor worsening. Hair does not lengthen. Nails do not need cutting. If you push a vine aside to make space to sit, it will stay pushed for the years you sit there, forming the exact shape of your shoulder.

This is why the place looks finished but new. They are not living *in* the ruin. They have become part of the equilibrium. They are fauna as rare and still as the white lizard, and almost as sessile. A room may hold one, or two pressed together for the faint coolness of contact. A whole kilometer of green vault may hold only three. You could walk for days in the architecture and think you were alone until you realize the pale shape by the far wall has been watching you breathe the whole time.

If you ask them — and you would have to ask without words, by offering water in your cupped hand — why they stay, they will not have an answer that translates to leaving or staying. The Sink does not require a reason. It is bright, hot, damp, still, and complete. It holds everything it needs inside itself, including them.

If you stay long enough on the moss floor, with the white light directly overhead making your shadow a thin blue ring under your feet, with the tap... tap... of a single drop somewhere in the layered canopy marking a time that does not advance, you will feel your own sweat stop evaporating. You will feel your breath become shallow so as not to disturb the air. You will feel time thin out until it is as interstitial as the water — present, never pooling — and you will understand why they do not get up.

And at night, when you climb back to the rim because you cannot sleep in that brightness, and you look down, you will see them as they are: not lights, not fires, but faint, diffuse glows held under the plate-leaves, pale green-white, heat coming back off leaf and skin alike, perfectly still, perfectly spaced through the bowl like dew held on a web — the other droplets in the mat, waiting for the next slow fall that never comes.
