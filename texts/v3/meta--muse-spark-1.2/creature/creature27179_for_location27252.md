Nothing lives here at first.

That is what you think, because you brought an old definition with you. You look for the contract you know - for breath that fogs, for eyes that meet yours, for a track in dust that shows a foot, a paw, a passage. You find none of that, and the emptiness feels absolute. The green that drinks sound and the dim light and the unending chord seem to exclude the very idea of an inhabitant. This place has not finished being a place. How could anything have moved in before the walls were up?

Then you stay long enough for your hearing to break.

Not to fail - to change. After an hour the roar stops being noise and becomes pressure, and pressure becomes a surface. After two hours you can feel shade inside the sound. You notice that where the groves thicken, the chord thins. That where the windbreaks are stacked, there is a pocket of something like quiet. And you realize that's not absence. That's occupation. Someone has been gardening the deafness.

What lives here does not arrive. It accrues. It belongs the way lichen belongs - not as a guest, but as a consequence.

The first to live here are the first to have never needed to arrive at all. The vegetation.

Call it vegetation because you have no other word, but don't think leaf and stem. Think of an animal that decided to be a place, and a plant that decided to listen.

Where it clings to the fresh basalt with those pale acid mouths, it is sessile and patient in the way a barnacle is patient. Where it rises - those leathery kelp-blades, those towers of ruffled felt, those carpets of glass hair that shiver without wind - it is awake. Bring your hand close without touching and you will feel it before you see anything move: a faint lessening of the pressure on your eardrum, a coolness on the skin of your forearm as if sound were being inhaled. The blades are hollow. Not hollow like a reed. Hollow like an ear.

They live by eating the note.

The underside of each blade is bearded with fine cilia, stiff as crystal whiskers, each tuned to a slightly different harmonic of the great chord. They vibrate sympathetically and that vibration is their metabolism. The waxy translucence is not wax at all but a dense lattice of silica and carbon that turns tremor into sugar, if sugar is still the right word for something made of stone-breath and trembling. They take no water because water would damp them. They need the dryness; they need the rock to stay stressed and singing. Their powder that hangs in the air is not pollen. It is ground glass, exfoliated resonators, each grain a potential ear waiting to find a quiet pit on the rock and start humming. It takes ten years to drift ten meters because the air is thin and the sound holds it up. The groves are old the way a coral reef is old - not one thing but a slow agreement to stay and soften the roar together. That's why they are lush where they are lush. They farm silence.

And where there is a farm, there is a farmer.

You don't see them because you keep looking at eye-height. Because you keep looking for a silhouette against the dim. The ones who stacked those windbreaks, who set that loom-weight beside the grove and never came back for it, they learned a long time ago not to stand up.

What lives here now, or what returns here in brief, patient stays, is low and wide. You would not call it human if you met it elsewhere, but you would recognize that it had been human once. Or something like human.

Think of a body revised by this plateau until it fit.

It is still bipedal, after a fashion, but it does not walk the way you walk. Its legs are short, bowed, the feet splayed into broad, callused pads that read the rock through vibration more than pressure. It leaves no print because it rarely lifts its weight; it shuffles, it slides, it keeps three points of contact the way you would on ice - not from fear of falling, but from fear of miscounting distance. When geometry slips, a print that was five meters behind you can be seven when you look back. So they do not trust prints. They do not make them if they can help it.

Its chest is enormous, barrelled, but not with muscle. With resonance. The ribs are not quite bone anymore but a cage of slightly flexible, slightly translucent plates - you can see the dim light through the skin where it stretches thin - that encloses a hollow, dry lung that does not exchange much air at all. It breathes once every few minutes, shallowly, because there is so little to breathe and because breathing is noisy. Instead its skin breathes the mineral exhalation off the young rock, a faint sour-salt vapor you can't smell but they can taste with the whole surface of their back and forearms. Their skin is pale, bloodless, covered in a fine down of the same glass hair as the carpets they tend, each filament trembling. When the chord rises - and it never rises, but they perceive its micro-fluctuations the way you perceive weather - the hairs lean.

Its head is the strangest revision. The face is smooth, almost featureless. The eyes are there, but small and sunken and milky, lidded with a second, clear membrane that dulls the already dim light even further. They do not navigate by sight. Sight lies here. The horizon bowls, the butte jumps forward and back. So their eyes are vestigial, useful only to detect the pooling of light, the difference between bare stone and grove-shadow. Their real eyes are in their jaw and their hands. The lower mandible is broad and flat, set with dense, sensitive teeth that are not for chewing but for listening - pressed to stone, it conducts the deep organ tone directly into the skull. You will find shallow, paired grooves worn into the basalt beside each grove, at exactly the height of a kneeling forehead. Places where someone has rested their chin for a long time to listen whether the grove is hungry.

They have no ears as you understand ears. The roar would have burst any drum. Instead the whole body is an ear. They communicate without voice, because voice is useless. Flat, confiscated, swallowed in the chord. They tap.

One sits beside a felt-tower - and they are always solitary, one per grove, never a village - and presses a palm to the rock, and with the knuckle of the other hand strikes a slow pattern. Not code. Not language. More like grooming. A short double tap that says *I am here, the distance is holding.* A long scrape that says *the powder is drifting north today.* The rock carries it a hundred meters through the stone itself, too low for you to hear, but the glass hairs on another's back prickle. If you put your own hand flat on the stone near one of their old fire pits, you can sometimes feel it still, a faint ticking under the great chord - not the crystal growing, but the memory of their conversation, trapped in the slab.

They build low because a right angle is a promise that this place cannot keep. A knee-high wall will stand for years, flexing slightly as the slab beneath it heaves upward a few millimeters a century. A house would shear. So they make windbreaks and cairns that are meant to fail, and to be rebuilt. The cairn you saw that had been rebuilt many times - that *is* the habitation. The act of rebuilding is the inhabitance. They stack the sharp, unweathered slabs without mortar so the pile can breathe and shift and be restacked when the plateau hiccups and north forgets itself. Inside the windbreak, where the grove has muffled the chord by a few precious decibels, they can finally hear their own heart, and they do their work.

Which is tending.

They do not eat the vegetation. They prune it. With flakes of basalt as sharp as broken glass they shave the dead, mineral-swollen blades that have become too dense with stored vibration and would shatter. They gather the shavings into those shallow pits and coax from them that cool, blue flame - not for heat, you understand, the air is cold but they do not feel cold the way you do - but for light. In the perpetual blue-grey dusk, that low flame is the only true color. They sit by it and let their glass down catch its glow and fluoresce faintly in answer. They harvest the silica powder that hangs in still air by holding out oiled, waxy sheets of their own shed skin stretched on frames - the loom-weight you found - until the sheets are furred with potential groves, which they then press gently into new acid-etched cradles on bare rock, a decade's worth of garden in a day's work.

They are not many. They cannot be. A grove needs a hundred meters of empty stone to gather enough sound to be lush. A tender needs a grove's quiet to think clearly enough to tend. So the distribution is sparse but lush, exactly like the green. You can walk for an hour across deafening, empty basalt and find nothing, and then step around a sharp ridge and find one of them curled in the lee of a windbreak, asleep with its broad jaw pressed to the stone, its back-hairs rippling in unison with the felt-tower above it, both of them purring with the same inaudible frequency. You will think it is dead until you see the powder settling on it and being gently, rhythmically blown off.

They are not dead. They are just on plateau time. Their lives are long because nothing here is fast. They do not age so much as accrete, their down thickening, their finger-pads hardening into those pale gripping pads. When one finally stops - and they do, rarely - it does not rot. There is nothing to rot it. It dries where it kneels, and slowly the glass hair of the grove overgrows it, and after a season you cannot tell which was tender and which was tended. The windbreak beside it remains, and another will eventually find it and rebuild it a little differently, aligning the doorway a few degrees further away from where north forgets itself this year.

If you stay, and you breathe shallowly, and you learn not to look directly at distance, and you press your teeth to the rock until your skull sings with the same note - you will feel them before you see any of them. A lessening. A pocket of mercy in the pressure. A place where the roar has been made, by patient, low, almost invisible hands, just barely habitable.
