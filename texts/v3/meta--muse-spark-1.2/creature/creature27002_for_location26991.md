Who lives here has learned to live *low*.

Not low in status, low in posture, in volume, in expectation. You don't stride in the pit. You wade, even when the ground is dry.

First, you think no one does. From the lip on the plain above, looking down into that hazy bowl of stone and sodium light, it looks uninhabited - too new, too hot, too loud with machines. A quarry with buildings left in it. Then your eyes adjust to the dusk and you see the laundry.

Because people do live here. About four hundred of them, maybe five. Not enough to fill the grid, which is why the grid looks so empty. The town was drawn for two thousand and only the first quarter showed up and then stopped. So everyone lives spaced apart, with a crushed-limestone lot on either side of their house, and everyone knows which light in which window is whose.

They are maintenance people. That is the only real profession the pit allows.

There are the pump men - six of them in rotating shifts, and everyone else knows their names because everyone else listens to their work. The pumps never stop. Thud-thud-thud, day and night, and if the rhythm stutters the whole town hears it before any alarm sounds. The pump men live closest to the lowest terrace in cinderblock houses that have already been re-poured twice because the floor heaved. Their boots never dry. They wear shorts and rubber boots up to the knee, always, even to the shop. Their calves are white-pale and then pruned. They are not big men. No one who stays here gets big - the heat edits you down to lean, corded muscle, a body that can dump heat.

Then the power men, who tend the diesel generators in the concrete bunkers. They are deaf in one ear, all of them, from the thrum that comes up through your feet. They speak a little louder than everyone else, even when the frogs aren't calling.

Then the elevator man and his two sons, who run the rail freight elevator up the wall. Four long grades, clanking slow. They know the delivery schedule the way other towns know the weather. Tuesday cement, Thursday food. The sons are the only ones who go up and down every day and they have tans on their faces and forearms that no one else has. Everyone else is the color of someone who lives under a desk lamp - pallid, with a damp sheen that never quite leaves. Sweat doesn't evaporate here. It stays on you. Children's hair is always stuck to their foreheads. Old men's shirts are always dark at the collar and under the arms by 9 a.m. Skin problems are common - a faint white fringe of fungus at the hairline, at the belt, between the toes - that no one talks about because everyone has it. You learn to powder yourself and live with it.

The rest are the town itself: the woman who runs the shop with the hand-painted COKE sign and keeps canned goods on pallets off the floor so the seep doesn't rust them; the town clerk who works under that crooked row of tamarisks by the town hall - seven thin trees that she waters and defends as if they were children, because they are the only vertical green anyone bothered to plant; the mechanic whose shop door is always open and who does more welding of pump pipe than car repair, because there are only three trucks in the pit and they rarely need to move; the teacher, who holds class in the morning blue gloom before the vertical sun makes the classroom unbearable, and again after it has passed. The children are the most adapted. They don't mind the wet. They play in the permanent sheen of the alleys, in rubber sandals, chasing the hand-sized dragonflies that patrol the settling ponds, cupping tadpoles in their palms, indifferent to mosquitoes. They shout to be heard over the frogs. They have learned not to run at noon. No one runs at noon. At 1 p.m. when the white bar of sun crosses the floor, the whole human town pauses. Shutters close. People sit still in whatever shadow their house throws and wait. It is siesta not by culture but by physics.

The houses show how people live here. Rebar pokes from every roof, capped with plastic bottles to keep the rain - that second, indoor rain of condensation - from rusting it, waiting for a second story that never comes because why build up when the wall already towers over you. Inside, every house has a bare bulb burning even at midday because the pit is dim, and every bulb has a halo in the humid air. Every house has black mold in the corner joints that is scrubbed with bleach and returns by next week. Laundry hung on a line never quite dries; it just goes from wet to warm-damp, and smells faintly of stone. People leave their windows open at night despite the insects, because the weeping wall air is the only cool air there is. They sleep under nets, listening to the chorus.

Because the humans are not the dominant biomass. They are the tenants.

The real inhabitants own the vertical.

High on the raw benches, thousands of swifts and swallows have taken every excavator tooth-mark and made it a nest. They own the dawn sky over the oval mouth. The town wakes not to an alarm but to their screaming wheel overhead, a vortex of wings that eats mosquitoes by the sackful. At dusk they vanish into the stone and the bats pour out of the same cracks in a chittering river, equal in number, equal in noise, taking the night shift over the ponds. No one in town remarks on it anymore; it is just the changing of the guard, as regular as the pump thud.

Lower down, where the wall sweats, a dark fringe of moss and fern holds, and in it live cave crickets — pale, long-antennaed, almost translucent - and the glossy black beetles that run quickly over concrete and disappear into drains. The drains themselves belong to the rats, who here are not skulking. They walk openly at night along the concrete lips, fat and unhurried, because there is no cat that can take this territory from them and the dogs have made a truce.

The marsh-fringe at the foot of the wettest wall is the loudest country. It was not planned. No one laid concrete there because the ground wouldn't take it. So reeds and saltgrass took it, and with them came frogs in numbers that are absurd for a desert. Thousands. After the afternoon condensation rain, or after the rare real rain that falls straight down the shaft, they call in layered sheets of sound - a physical pressure on the ears - that stacks on top of the pump thud and the generator hum until you have to lean in to hear the person next to you at the evening shop queue. Dragonflies the size of sparrows hunt over them. Mosquitoes and midges make a permanent, glittering cloud in any sun shaft, and everyone has learned the slow, constant wave of the hand in front of the face, the way you would breathe.

On the roofs and ledges, pigeons cover every flat surface in a gray-white crust, cooing in the heat. Stray dogs - four or five, ribs showing a little but calm - sleep in the exact spots where the generator vibration comes through the slab, because it keeps the insects off them and warms their old bones. They belong to no one and everyone feeds them scraps, and they never bark; there is too much other noise to compete with.

At night, when the machines are thudding and the frogs are calling and the bats are cutting the air above the amber yards, the town sits outside. Not together - spaced apart, each household in its patch of cooler wall-shadow, on plastic chairs set in puddles that reflect their own porch lights. They talk slowly, loudly enough to be heard, about the pump level, about a pipe that needs welding, about whether the freight elevator cable needs replacing this month. They do not talk about leaving. People who were going to leave already left. The ones who remain have become pit-adapted: patient, humid, a little mold-spotted themselves, moving at millimeters per hour, like the water that beads on the limestone.

They are not strange. There is no strangeness here. If you cut your knee on a piece of rebar, it gets infected quickly in this air. If you drop a stone, it falls. If you don't run the pumps, the water wins. These people have simply understood that a hole cut for rock can, if you stay long enough and don't expect to be dry or quiet or cool, become a place. They keep the water a few centimeters below their floors, the frogs a few meters from their doors, the wall from falling on them for one more season. And in return the pit gives them a kind of membership. They are the sparse, urban, interim-industrial animal that lives in the dusk at midday, between puddles, under the wheel of birds — the one that keeps the lights on.
