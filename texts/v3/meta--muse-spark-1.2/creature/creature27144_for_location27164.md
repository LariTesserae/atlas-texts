They live there the way moss lives on a wall. Not on it. In it.

At first you don't see them at all.

That's the trick of the light and the scale and the way everything rustles. You stand on a mid-terrace in that flat, shadowless grey and you think the place is empty — just a machine left running, just water ticking from leaf to pipe to trough, just vines moving on their own. The motion is so distributed, so quiet, that it doesn't resolve into people until one passes within ten feet of you.

And then you can't stop seeing them. They are everywhere.

They are not tall. They were never selected for height. Five stories of sheds and glasshouses built over a terrace that was never meant to have five stories meant the ceilings came down, the walkways narrowed, the doorways were cut to the size of the beam that was already there. Generation after generation they have ducked a little more, become a little more compact. Not dwarves. Just... economical. Broad through the shoulders and the hips from carrying, short in the leg, long in the arms. Low center of gravity. They move like people who expect the ground to be wet.

You know them by their boots before you know their faces. Everyone wears the same boots, because there is only one kind of boot that works here — soft black rubber, knee-high, split and patched with tar, the soles worn smooth. They make a particular sound on wet concrete, not a splash, not a step — a *suck* and *kiss* as the foot lifts. You hear it constantly now, once your ear tunes: suck-kiss, suck-kiss, in every alley, on every gantry, going up and down the steel stairways that sweat water.

Then the rest of them comes into focus.

They are pale in a way that has nothing to do with sickness. The light never gives them a tan. It never gives them a shadow either. In that pearlescent dim, where you can stare at the sun's smear, melanin has no work to do. Their skin is the color of the underside of a leaf, or of paper left in damp — off-white, a little translucent, with the blue of veins showing at the temple and wrist. Their cheeks are not white, though. The air is always cool, always moving, thin up here where the clouds have to drag. So every adult, every child over six, has the same high, wind-burned flush over that pallor, a red that never fades. Their hair, lank with the damp, is darkened by it. Blonde goes to dishwater, brown goes to black, and it lies flat and straight, cut short with a knife or tied back with twine because otherwise it drips in your eyes while you work.

Their hands give them away as lifers. You can't fake these hands. The fingertips are permanently softened, wrinkled as if they've just been in the bath, because they have been, for years. The nails are short, black at the cuticle not from dirt but from pressed sap and iron stain. The palms are callused but the callus is waterlogged and pale. When they grip a handrail they leave that cold film the description mentioned — not sweat, just the water that never dries on them. Their knuckles are faintly swollen, early, from the cold and the damp. They don't shake hands. They nod.

Their clothes are not clothes so much as another layer of the building. Oilskin and boiled wool, waxed canvas, old electrical rubber cut into aprons. Everything dark — slate, moss-green, rust-brown — because everything pale would be stained in a day. Everything patched. A man's jacket might have a sleeve made from a rice sack, a woman's trousers might have knees from a conveyor belt. They dress in layers that never fully dry, so they steam faintly when they step into the warm pulping sheds on the lower floors and then go cold and clammy again when they step out. Children wear the same, down to the smallest size, miniature oilskins that swish. No one owns an umbrella; it would be useless. You get wet slowly and stay wet.

If you watch one person long enough, you see the rhythm that makes the whole bowl work.

They never hurry. Hurrying on wet iron is how you fall three terraces. They carry. Always carrying. A basket of cut vine tips balanced on the head or shoulder, a dripping burlap sack of roots, a crate of glass panes, a child. They move with a short, sure shuffle, knees slightly bent, weight centered. They don't call out. Sound doesn't carry in a greenhouse full of leaves and fog. Instead they make small, close sounds — a click of the tongue to say *coming through*, a tap on a pipe with a wrench to say *clear below*, a two-note whistle that means *chute is open*. Their voices, when they do speak, are low and a little hoarse. They have what the old miners called air-voice — from breathing cool spore-laden air in glasshouses all their lives. You have to be near to hear them, and then they speak plainly, only as much as needed.

> "Hold that."
> "More to the left."
> "Water's high on four."

There is no shouting foreman. There is no siren. The work enforces itself. If a runnel blocks, the terrace above floods a bed and someone's crop drowns, so someone clears it. If a pane breaks, the vine inside yellows, so someone replaces it. They have perfected a kind of distributed attention. Each terrace is a clan, each block of glasshouses a family ledger, but anyone will stop to re-seat a slipped pipe because the water belongs to no one and to everyone. You learn as a kid to see what needs doing within arm's reach and do it.

That is their psychology. It is the most alien thing about them, more than their skin.

They are not unhappy. They are not waiting to leave. The idea of *leaving* has thinned out up here. Beyond the rim there is only wind and thin grass and cloud and, eventually, the lowlands where the sun is hard and the ground is dry — a story they don't quite believe, or don't want. Why would you want a place where water is scarce and light hurts your eyes? This bowl is the world. It is complete. It doesn't need to grow outward, only upward and downward and denser. They have no frontier myth. They have a maintenance myth.

Time for them is not linear. With no shadows and no real day and night — the cloud lights the bowl the same at noon and at four — they sleep when they are tired, in shifts, in warm lofts above the blowers where the air hums at 50 hertz and lulls you. They wake to the same dim. A day is not a day, it's a cycle: harvest, replant, repair, redirect. A year is not a year, it's when the big cane comes ripe. Children don't ask *when will we be done?* They ask *what needs doing?* Old people, and there are old people — tough, bent, with cataracts that look like the cloud itself — sit on low stools and strip fibers or sort seed all day, because sitting still for a week means moss grows over your feet.

They love the plants the way a good toolmaker loves his tools — intimately, without sentiment. They will stroke a runner to coax it onto wire, they will curse a glorious, beautiful flowering vine while they rip it down because it is choking the oil press. They eat what they grow, mostly. The broad-leafed understory blanched and boiled, the cane pith pressed for sweetness, the beans from the hydroponic racks stewed with salt. Their teeth are stained green-black at the gum. Their breath smells of leaves. The few pigeons and rats are left alone not from kindness but because they have their niche and are not worth chasing.

And they have become, slowly, part plant themselves. Not literally — you won't find chlorophyll in their blood. But look at an old terrace-worker who has spent sixty years in the weep. Her skin at the hairline has a faint green cast that won't wash off, colonies of microscopic algae living in the damp of her skin. The creases of her neck host moss. Small white flowers like those on the retaining walls will sometimes — rarely — root in the felt of her patched shoulder and she will let them there until they seed. She doesn't notice. When she dies they will not bury her in ground, because there is no ground. They will compost her in the warm beds on terrace nine, as they have done for a hundred years, and something will grow faster there next season.

If you stand still long enough among them, with your boots filling slowly with cold water and your hands going soft, you stop feeling like an observer.

Someone will pass and, without breaking stride, tap you lightly on the elbow to move you out of the way of a cart on narrow rails. Not unkindly. Simply: you were in the space that was being used. And you will step aside onto the narrow strip between trough and wall where ferns brush your calf, and you will feel for a second what they feel all the time — that you are not a visitor in a place, you are a temporary eddy in a flow that never stops, never gets louder, never arrives.

The bowl breathes around you. Water condenses. A leaf uncurls. A motor cycles on.

They breathe with it.
