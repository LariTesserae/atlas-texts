You don't see them at first.

That's the point. You stand chest-deep on the stair and you think *nothing lives here* and for a long minute that seems true. The plants are too still to be animals and the machines are too slow to be alive. You watch a bell flower open underwater and cough its pollen-smoke and there is nothing to catch it. You think: a garden with no gardener. A factory with no workers.

Then you realize you've been looking for the wrong kind of movement.

Nothing here darts. Nothing darts because nothing here needs to.

The first one you see has been in front of you the whole time.

Hanging from the underside of the catwalk at eye level, where you thought a curtain of water-velvet had grown especially thick, a frond shifts — not with the current, but *against* it. A slow correction. The green resolves. What looked like a sheet of overlapping leaves is a back.

They are the color of the water when the light is in it. Not green, exactly. Translucent, tea-pale, with the green *inside* them.

When it moves again you see the edges. A broad, flattened body, wider than you are tall, thin as a lily pad but not flat — domed slightly on top like a stingray, or a manta, or a leaf that decided to become muscle. The dorsal side is not skin. It is skin that has become garden. Living moss furs it, water-velvet roots directly into its back, a few tiny white bell-flowers even bloom there, opening and closing with its breathing. It photosynthesizes on its own back. Its ventral side — when it peels itself off the walkway with infinite slowness and turns in the water — is pale and soft and deeply grooved, like the underside of a mushroom, lined with frilled gills that flutter even when the animal is still. Those gills are not separate from the water. They comb it.

It doesn't swim so much as let go.

It releases its grip on the steel — and you see then how it was holding: not with suckers, but with hundreds of fine, root-hair filaments along its rim, each one tucked into a bolt-hole, a pipe seam, a mesh joint, drinking the mineral bleed from the machine. It lets go and hangs in the thermal column, utterly neutrally buoyant. A lily pad that became an animal, or an animal that learned to be a lily pad. It drifts. A turbine ten meters down completes its ten-second revolution and the pressure wave nudges it, and it ripples its whole margin once, a single, slow undulation that moves it three feet to the left, where another patch of walkway is going untended. It reattaches. Resettles. Its moss ripples once more and is still.

There is another. Now that you know the shape, you see three. Four. Maybe more — the basin is too packed to count, and they are too good at being infrastructure.

They are not many. They don't need to be. One can cover a whole tank face. They are distributed like shade.

You watch the nearest one work.

Work is the wrong word. Tend. It extends a limb — if limb is the word. From under its margin, where the gills meet the leaf-rim, a pale arm unfolds. It is not jointed. It is hydrostatic, like an elephant's trunk or a vine's tendril, and it ends not in a hand but in a frayed crown of filaments, each filament tipped with a tiny, dark, almost eye-like bead. It reaches toward a flowering vine that has threaded itself through a turbine grille. The vine has grown too far, its leaves blocking the slow sweep of the blade. The animal doesn't pull. It brushes. The filaments comb the vine, release something — a faint milkiness in the water, an enzyme, a signal — and the vine shivers and goes limp at the contact point and *lets go*. The whole length retracts an inch, curling back on itself, away from the grille. The animal's filaments gather the shed pollen smoke that was hanging there and draw it in toward the gill-grooves. Feeding. Or cleaning. Both.

Everything they do is both.

They are not animals that live in the garden. They are the thing the garden was built to grow. Or to house. The troughs, the ceramic channels, the glass veins — you thought those were for plants. But along the deeper levels, where the light goes green and deep, you can see now that some of the troughs are not growing plants at all. They are cradling things. Pale domes, the size of dinner plates, then serving platters, then tables, nested in warm, circulating water, each one furred with the same moss that will one day be its back. Buds. Young ones, still rooted by a single thick cord into the pipe-work like a fruit. They pulse, very slowly, in time with the heat exchangers.

They breathe the water and they drink the light and they eat the excess growth that would otherwise choke the basin. No grazers, you thought. You were wrong. They graze so slowly you mistook it for growth. They are why there is no rot. A patch of moss that dies browns for half a day and then is gone, combed clean by a margin passing over it, incorporated. They keep the concrete from ever weathering because they keep it covered. They keep the machines from ever silting because they are the silt filter.

And they keep the water in.

When one moves over the vertical sheet — that impossible wall of rain suspended between two plates where ferns grow inside falling water that never falls — it doesn't go around. It goes *through*. It presses its thin body into the sheet and the water parts around it and clings to it, and for a second you see the field that holds the whole basin up: a faint, oily rainbow on its skin, the same tension that holds the meniscus at the rim. The field is not generated by the machines alone. The machines make heat and circulation; the animals make the skin. Their backs tingle with the same faint ozone you tasted in the air. When several of them align along the rim, out of sight above you, the surface holds.

They notice you eventually. Not with alarm.

You have been holding the galvanized rail and your knuckles are white from the cold bite of the steel. The water around you, which has been bath-warm, goes a fraction warmer near the animal that has drifted closest. It does not approach. It just reorients, very slowly, so that one of those dark filament-tips points at you. You feel the pressure change of its slow undulation more than you see it looking.

You realize your breathing — fast, mammalian, bubbling — is the loudest, fastest thing in the basin. You are too quick for this place. You are a flicker.

It extends that pale arm toward you, not quite reaching, and stops a foot away. The crown of filaments opens like an anemone, like a flower. It waits. It is offering to comb you, the same way it combed the vine. To clean the iron and salt from your skin, to take the loose cells, to see if you are growth that needs tending or a structure that needs to be grown over.

If you stay still long enough, the moss on its back will seem to be the same as the moss on the pillar. If you stay another rung lower, the water will close over your mouth, and the air you breathe will be the water they breathe, and you will understand why the basin was built so young and so crisp and so complete: it wasn't built for people to use.

It was built to be a body, and they are how it stays alive.
