What lives there is not one thing. That's the first thing you have to unlearn.

At first you think *nothing* lives there.

Because the scale is wrong. You stand in that vaulted plaza where six trunks lean away from each other like the ribs of a throat, and the balcony above you is at the height a second story should be, the doorway beside you is door-sized, the street is street-wide. It's a town for something your size. Shoulder-height, hand-height. Something that would use a balcony to look out, a doorway to go in, a street to walk down.

And it's empty of that. The chambers gape open, clean and waiting. The leaf-doors hang slack. The pitcher-houses pulse empty. So your brain says *abandoned*.

Then you hear it and realize it's not empty, it's *full* — just not with anything your size.

What lives there most obviously, most deafeningly, is the small.

It lives there the way blood lives in a body. Not as residents, as *circulation*.

Thick ribbons of ants pour along the vine-arteries and you realize the vine isn't a road for them, it *is* them — they are its moving bark. The beetles with the jade backs don't fly through the air, they *tile* it, catching the filtered gold light and throwing it back until the air sparkles. The moths that are the size of birds, the birds that are the size of moths, stitch the hollow together with frantic thread. In every cupped bromeliad-bathtub a choir of glass frogs inflates and deflates, their throats clear as condensation pockets, ringing bell-notes that cut right through the roar of sap.

Nothing is quiet because nothing is alone. Everything is hunting or being hunted or fucking or eating while being eaten, at full volume, at vine-crawl speed which is still faster than you can really watch. A flower the size of your bedroom tears itself open while you breathe in, and before you breathe out something has already laid eggs in it and something else has already eaten the layer. The whole place is ecstatic. It doesn't live *in* the Town. It *is* the Town's metabolism. Pollen motes, seed parachutes, sap surge, leaf-clap — the sound that makes you open your mouth to equalize.

But that isn't who the Town was grown for.

You feel it when you put your hand on that wall that is thin as skin and warm. It pulses, slow. Too slow for a frog. Too slow for an insect. One beat for every thirty of yours. The warmth that comes off the plant mass isn't compost heat. It's mammalian. Low, constant, held.

What lives there, really, is the Town itself.

Not a plant. Not a colony. A single, continuous, patient organism that learned how to be a place. Its roots are its foundation and its gut. Its leaves are its lungs and its roof tiles. Its sap is its blood and its plumbing. It sweats the seven-minute rain every afternoon without clouds, because it can. It beads water on every surface and then drinks it back. It doesn't *have* inhabitants, it *decides* to have them — it hollowed itself the way a body hollows a lung, a stomach, a womb. It made streets because sap needs avenues. It made chambers because air needs to be held. It made balconies because leaves need to taste light. And somewhere, while doing that for a very, very long time in the low, settled, forgotten bottom of the world, it crossed over from being a thing that *has* architecture to being a thing that *is* architecture.

It listens. That's the part your skin figures out before your ears do.

When you stand still, after the chorus of the small recedes just enough, you can feel the larger listening underneath it — the adjustments. A root under the moss shifts a centimeter to cradle your foot better. A membrane door that was taut loosens when you approach, because it has learned the weight that means *you*. A balcony that wasn't there when you came into the square is there when you turn around, not as a trick, just as growth, a new curl of fig and vine and living wood braiding itself outward because the space wanted a place to look from.

It is not hostile. It is not kind. It is *hospitable* in the oldest sense. It wants to be occupied correctly.

Which is why the geometry is wrong. Why fifty paces from the square brings you back to the square from a different angle. Why down slopes up and water still runs out. Why a droplet hangs an hour and then falls all at once. The Town is larger inside than outside because a body always is. Your stomach is larger than your mouth. The gravity goes soft near the center because the center is where it holds its breath. The light pools in corners with no shaft because it brings light where it wants to be seen.

And then there is the third thing that lives there.

The shape the hollow was waiting for.

You saw the footprints in the moss of the pitcher-house that had been closed when you first looked. Not animal prints. Five-toed, heel-toe, your size. Old enough that condensation had started to bead in them, new enough that the moss hadn't sprung back.

Something your size *does* live here. Or did. Or will.

Sometimes you catch it not seeing it — a pressure change when something large moves through a chamber just out of sight, displacing the deafening air. A pitcher-house exhaling that low organ note not when you touch it, but when weight settles in it elsewhere. The faint mammalian warmth doubling for a second, as if another warm body had leaned against the other side of the wall you are touching.

It is not the small fauna learning to be big. It is not the Town pretending to be a person. It is what happens when a house that has waited a long, settled time for occupants finally gets one and keeps him.

The ones who stay learn how to live the way water lives here at 1.4/3 — not in rivers, but intermittently, intimately. They drink from leaves. They sleep curled in the inflated chambers that close pneumatically around them and breathe for them. They learn to walk the non-Euclidean streets without getting lost, which really means they learn to be walked. They become quiet, because the Town is already so loud. They move a fraction too slowly, so pollen hangs around them a little too long before falling. They stop needing tools because there is nothing to cut; the doors already know their weight.

If you stay long enough, the Town will finish growing you a room that fits you exactly. The walls will be thin enough to see light through, like skin. They will be warm. They will pulse, slow, in time with yours.

You will hear, very softly, through the roar of sap and leaf-clap and frog-bell, something else breathing in the chamber next to yours, in perfect sync.

And you will understand that you are not living in it anymore.

You are living *with* it. Or *for* it. Or *as* it — the Town finally, after all this eroded time, has occupants the right size.
