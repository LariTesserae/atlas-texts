What lives here doesn't live *in* the basin.

It lives *as* the basin.

At first you will be sure no one lives here at all. That's the trick of it. The silence convinces you. There is no territorial call, no scramble, no warning chatter. The air is too bright and too still to seem inhabited. You will stand on the spongy mat and feel conspicuously loud, your breath and your heartbeat a rude noise, and you will think you are alone.

Then you will learn to see through the light.

### The Air

The first residents you notice are the ones who have almost edited themselves out of visibility.

They are not birds. They don't have bodies in the way birds have bodies. Think of a kite made from stretched soap film and the bone of a dandelion clock. The smallest are the size of your palm, the largest wide as doors, but they weigh almost nothing. A membrane of translucent lattice, veined with filaments that catch the sun and throw it back as a faint rainbow, with a knot of darker tissue at the center where all the lines converge — an eye, a stomach, a brain, all the same organ.

They don't flap. They don't need to. The warm air rising off the weeping stone lifts them without effort. They ride the thermals in slow, endless gyres, hundreds of them at any moment, at every height from knee-level to beyond clear sight. When they bank you see them for half a second as a flash of pearlescence, and then they vanish back into the glare.

They are called kites only because that's the closest word. They are filters. They strain that bright, thick air for spores, for pollen, for the motes of skin and leaf-dust that hang in those strange hex grids at noon. And they are shepherds. When one of the canopy-climbers sickens or slows, a large kite will peel out of its circle, fold itself like a handkerchief around the animal, and sink to the fern mat with it. There is no struggle. No sound. The membrane tightens, goes opaque for a minute, and then thins again and peels away empty. Where the animal was there is only warm air and a faint, clean smell like ozone.

They are not predators the way we understand predation. They are how the basin tidies up.

### The Middle Distance

Between the flat-topped bottle trees, the air is thick and you think it's empty until you stop walking.

Then the dragonflies resolve.

They were always there, holding position. Two feet from your face, four feet over the sphagnum. Absolutely still. Not hovering — *displaced*. Their wings are not wings but four vanes of black-blue glass, iridescent, jointed in a way that should not be able to support weight. They don't beat. They just *hold*, as if they have decided that gravity is a suggestion.

The largest is as long as your forearm. Its head is a separate thing from its body, mounted on a neck that can swivel 270 degrees with a soft click you feel more than hear. It watches you with eyes that are compound but not insectile — each facet is amber-clear, and you can see it focus on you, on your pulse at your throat, on the water beading on your skin.

There are dozens between any two trees. They do not swarm. They maintain distance with immaculate manners, each keeping its own column of air. They communicate by color. A ripple of turquoise runs down the vanes of one, and a half second later another answers with a pulse of violet. They are ambush generalists, but they rarely need to hunt. The air is so full of midge-things and veil-flies you can't see until a dragonfly displaces — a blink — and is back in its place with something struggling faintly in a basket of filaments under its chin.

They are the nervous system. They see everything, record everything, and forget nothing, but they never make a sound about it.

### The Ground

Look down. The fern mat you thought was ground is a living skin, ankle deep and springy, saturated. It rustles without wind. If you kneel and part the fronds, you will see why.

The beetles are moving under it.

Not beetles. Hummocks. Domes the size of tortoises, some as big as low tables, moving at the speed of a thought through the peat. Their backs are true shells, but the shells are gardens — moss and tiny white flowers and even seedling ferns have rooted in the pits and grooves of their carapaces, so each one looks like a little hill that decided to go for a walk. Underneath, a fringe of a hundred cilia-like legs ripples through the water and mud without disturbing it.

Watch one for a while. It grazes. The front of the dome splits along an invisible seam — not a mouth, a whole forward hemisphere hinging open — to reveal a pale, radular floor that combs the fern mat, taking the top millimeter with surgical evenness. Behind it, the ferns are already lifting again, unfurling, bright green and undamaged. Ten minutes later you can't tell where it fed.

They are the gut. They eat the basin and the basin eats them. When one dies — rarely — it does not bloat or stink. It stops, settles a little deeper into the peat, and its moss covering simply continues growing. In an hour the dome softens, translucent at the edges. By late afternoon the filaments of the kites have come down low and are worrying at it, and by evening the water where it was is clear and tea-colored again, with only a slightly richer patch of flowers to show it was ever there.

Nothing here rots. Decay was edited out a long time ago.

### The Canopy and The Water

Up in the flat lace of the bottle trees move the six-limbs.

They are the only thing here that looks like it has a face you could recognize. A lemur, a monkey, a child — your brain keeps trying. They are the size of cats, covered in short, ash-grey fur that sheds water instantly. Two arms, two mid-limbs that can be arms or legs as needed, two true legs. Six fingers on the upper hands, four on the lowers. Their eyes are huge, amber, lidless, polished like river stones. They do not vocalize. They don't need to.

A troop of eight moves above you without shaking a branch, tossing the waxy orange fruits hand-to-hand-to-hand in a chain, never dropping, never hurrying. One will stop and look directly at you. Not with curiosity — with assessment. With the same quiet consideration the kites showed. It will tilt its head, its mid-limbs folded neatly, and then resume its work. They tend the vines. Those straight-hanging vines with water-cups at the tip are theirs; they bend the cups, drink, redistribute the water to the epiphytes. They also tend the pillars. If you watch long enough you'll see one descend to the leaning triad, run a lower hand over the shifting script — which holds still for it — and then leave. The script rearranges itself after.

They are the hands. Whatever built the black walls and the mirrored pavilion made them, or remade something local into them, to keep doing the small, gentle maintenance the field can't do itself.

And in the water — the shallow pans that are everywhere and nowhere, that reflect the sky so perfectly — lie the keepers.

They look like axolotls the size of large dogs, neotenic, pink-grey as the gneiss itself, their fanned gills waving with a slowness that makes time feel viscous. They never move except to breathe. You could step over one and it would not flinch. But the water around them is not empty. If you crouch until your eyes are level with the surface, you see the ribbons.

They are eels of light, not light like a glow, but light like a solid. Translucent, a foot long, two feet, moving without wake, without displacement, leaving a faint after-image that hangs for a second after they pass. They go through the axolotls, through the rock, through your fingers if you put your hand in, without resistance. The axolotls are their anchors. Or their parents. Or their houses. At the deep-blue dusk, when the basin begins to glow from the litter itself, the ribbons rise in numbers and lace the air just above the pans, and the dragonflies above make way for them.

They are the memory. When a kite dissolves, when a hummock softens, when a six-limb dies — the ribbon takes the pattern and carries it. The basin doesn't have a library. It has them.

---

None of them are afraid of you. None of them want anything from you.

If you sit still long enough, the six-limbs will move around you, the dragonfly will resume its station a foot from your cheek, a hummock will comb a neat crescent out of the ferns by your knee and move on. A kite may pass so close its membrane brushes your hair and you feel a brief, cool static.

They will make space for you because that is the rule that was written into the stone and the water and the field that holds the dust in hex grids and lets water run uphill for three feet before remembering itself. The rule is simple: *Abundance without crowding. Hunger without cruelty. Presence without noise.*

You were expecting inhabitants to be people, or monsters, or nations. Here the inhabitant is a distributed, silent, overwhelming consensus of gentle things, tending and being tended by a technology that has become weather. The hamlet of black walls and mirrored pavilions wasn't a village. It was a toolkit. And the tools were left out because the garden still needed weeding, the water still needed to be sweet, the light still needed to be bright but not burning.

Stay through an afternoon rain. The sheet of water will rise over your ankles and hold the sky. Every kite will rise a little higher, every dragonfly will tilt its vanes to shed water, every hummock will pause, every six-limb will huddle and blink its amber eyes, every axolotl will close its gills once, slowly. The ribbons will swim through the rain as if the rain is air.

And you will be, for that hour, included in the count.
