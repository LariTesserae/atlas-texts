They live far apart from each other.

That is the first thing you understand about them, before you understand anything else about what they are.

You see one first at a distance, because on this plateau you always see everything at a distance. A figure in undyed wool the color of the limestone, moving along one of those too-wide streets. You think she is far away, then you think she is close, because there is nothing to scale her against - no crowd, no traffic, no other bodies. Only stone and sky and the wide interval between houses.

When you get closer, you realize she is not alone in being alone. That is how they all are.

They are human. Entirely human, which is at first a disappointment. You expected something translucent or strange to match the strangeness of the stasis. But they are people with the ruddy, wind-burned cheeks of people who live at altitude, with hands broad and cut at the knuckles from working cold stone. The thin air has made them barrel-chested; their breath is audible, a little deliberate, in through the nose, out through the mouth, even at rest. Their eyes are dark and narrowed - not from suspicion, but from a lifetime of bright, thin light where there is no haze to soften anything. They see very far, and they are used to being seen from very far.

They are sparse the way their city is sparse, not because there was a plague or an exodus. Because they prefer the interval.

A household is one, or two, or three, never more. Houses built for five hold one old man and his loom. A terrace that could feed a family of eight feeds one woman and her beans. Children exist - you will see a boy carrying a bucket from cistern to garden with both hands, with complete seriousness - but rarely. One child per thirty houses. He does not play loudly. He does not need to call for friends. His friends are half a kilometer away and he will visit them tomorrow, walking slowly in the cool that lives in the shade.

When you speak to them, they do not come close. They speak at the distance the street allows, and because sound carries so far in the thin, still air, they never raise their voice. Their language is low, unhurried, consonant-heavy, suited to not disturbing the equilibrium. They do not interrupt. They do not hurry to answer. Long pauses are not awkward. A woman mending a copper pot with a small hammer will look up at you, consider your question, tap once - *tok* - consider again, and then answer as if the answer had been there all along and she was just waiting for the right moment to let it be heard without making a ripple.

> "Has it always been like this?"
> *tok*
> "Yes."

And she means it in both senses. It has been like this since the uplift. It has been like this since this morning.

They are not old and they are not young. That takes longer to notice. Their faces have the smooth, unlined quality of people in their late twenties or early thirties, but their hands and their posture have the patience of people who have done the same task for decades. Their hair does not grey. The boy with the bucket does not get taller between the morning you first see him and the morning you see him again - and the light has not moved, so you cannot tell if that is days or minutes. If you cut yourself on the sharp basalt, they will wrap it in clean linen and give you yarrow, but they will not ask if it is healing tomorrow, because tomorrow will not come. The cut will stay clean and open and not painful, exactly as it is.

Do not mistake this for death. They are not ghosts or after-images. Lift a slate from a roof and you will find the moss underneath damp and alive. Look at the woman's hands and you will see blood under the nails. Offer to help her with the pot and she will let you hold it, and the copper will be warm from her lap. The bean plant by her door holds at once a tight white flower, a fat green pod, and a dry, rattling pod ready to harvest, and if you pick the ripe one she will nod and shell it and eat one raw bean herself - sweet and starchy. She will not plant the rest. There is no need to plant. The garden is already lush. It is already at yield. It will remain so.

They are maintainers, not builders, not growers. Though they built everything by hand - you can see the adze marks on every lintel, the thumb-smear in the lime mortar - they are not building now. The half-finished aqueduct piers at the rim are not unfinished. They are finished in their state of being half-finished. A man will be fitting a stone to a retaining wall when you pass. If you come back after walking the whole basin - an hour, a day, no difference - he will still be fitting that same stone, his cheek against it to test its level. He is not stuck. He is occupied. The task perfectly fills the time that does not pass.

They understand the ecology of spacing implicitly. They do not crowd the hares or the goats or each other. They leave thirty paces between houses because any less would be a pressure. They leave a tussock of grass between garden rows because pulling it would be a change. They keep their herds at two or three, not twenty, because abundance here does not mean multiplication. It means that whatever exists is fully and completely present, and then left alone. 

Their chief art is leaving alone.

The birds understand them. The large hawks circle on thermals that never shift, and the people do not look up with longing or fear. The small brown creepers land on door lintels and are not shooed. The white finches move through the market square in a flock that always has the same number of birds, seventeen, and always crosses from the same roof to the same empty stall at the same angle. The people watch them without counting. You will learn to do this too, if you stay.

At first you think they must be lonely, or sad, or waiting for the sun to move. They are not. Loneliness requires a sense of distance as lack. For them, distance is the medium of life, like water for fish. To have fifty paces of moss and wind between you and your neighbor is not isolation. It is breathing room for the soul. When they do gather - three or four at the cistern at what you think must be noon, though the shadows have not shortened - they do not huddle. They stand at the corners of a wide square and talk quietly, each holding their own vessel, and their sentences have long silences between them where they listen to the thin air.

If you stay long enough, your own breath will slow to match theirs. Your footprints will stay crisp in the moss behind you. You will set down your pack and find it neither warming nor cooling. You will realize you have been mending the same strap for a long time, not out of inefficiency, but because the leather looks rightest just as you are pulling the awl through.

They will not ask you to leave. They will not ask you to stay. They have no future to invite you into and no past to warn you with. But if you stand at an intersection at mid-morning - that eternal mid-morning with its cool shade inside the sun - and look out over the roofs stepping down into the basin, the smoke from a dozen hearths not rising or falling, the pale quick trees not growing or shedding, the goats fixed like statues on the knife ridges, you will feel their deeper nature:

They are not inhabitants of the plateau.

They are part of its stasis. Like the beaded condensation that never drips, like the tarn that reflects a cloud that is no longer there, they are how the plateau keeps itself damp and lush and cool and high and quiet without ever having to change. They are the mechanism by which a city can be vast and empty at the same time, and not be ruined.

You came expecting to find who lives here despite the thin air and the young stone and the still light.

What lives here is a people who learned - or were allowed - to live *as* thin air and young stone and still light live. Present. Abundant. Perfectly spaced. And held.
