It lives below.

At first you will be certain nothing does.

That's the trick of the hollow. You come expecting life to announce itself - a dart, a chirp, a footprint in the humus, the flick of a tail. You listen for the kind of life that moves quickly and you hear only what is not life: the transformer, the drip, the sigh of the valve. And because the green is so thick you decide the green *is* the life, and that it is empty the way an abandoned greenhouse is empty. Plants, machines, steam. No inhabitant. Just a place.

Stay longer. Stand still until the patter stops being weather and starts being a language you haven't learned to hear. That is when you notice it is not empty at all. It is occupied. Completely.

What lives there does not live *in* the hollow the way a deer lives in a forest. It *is* the hollow, or most of it. It does not have a body you can point to. It has a distribution.

You see it first as a mistake in your peripheral vision.

A vine you were sure was hanging still is hanging an inch to the left.

Not swaying. It has *moved*. Not with wind - there is no wind down there, only the slow cycling of warm air from the boreholes outward and up the walls. It moved against the draft, toward the center. Toward the manifold. You watch one of the broad, black-green leaves and you see a bead of water on its waxy tip shudder and then run *up* the midrib, against gravity, vanishing into the flesh of the stem. Not dripping. Drinking.

The second time you see it, you understand that what you thought were many plants is one plant. Or one thing wearing many plants.

There is no topsoil in the hollow. There shouldn't be enough to support this density. That's because the soil is not soil. Under the damp humus, under the first inch of rot that never finishes rotting, there is a mat. 

If you were to pull the ferns aside - and you would have to pull hard, their roots do not want to let go - if you were to scrape with your hands into that warm, sweet-metal smelling humus, you would not find clay. You would find felt.

A felt of roots so fine they look like hair, and filaments so fine they look like stain, and runners the thickness of your wrist that are pale, almost translucent, and warm. They braid. They fuse. They pass through each other and into each other. They go down through the slumped terraces in sheets and come up again around the concrete pad of the transformer. They go *into* the concrete. They find the hairline cracks in the central manifold's pipes and they do not clog them. They line them.

Inside the pipe, where white steam should be screaming alone, there is a green throat. A living lining of damp, fleshy rootlets that drinks the mineral steam directly, that tightens and slackens to regulate pressure. That is why the gauges still tremble. That is why the relief valve still screams every seven or eight minutes with such perfect, patient timing. The thing is breathing through the machine. Or the machine is breathing for it.

You thought the boiler house was being preserved by the plants. It's the other way around. It is being *kept*.

Call it the Tenant, because it has tenancy. It has taken up residence in an industrial lung and made it an organ.

It has no front or back. No face. No eyes, though it is exquisitely sensitive to light - not the light from above, which it has learned to ignore. The sourceless glow that pools under leaves, the amber of the safety bulbs that never burned out: that is its light. The undersides of the leaves are paler, faintly luminous, because that is where it photosynthesizes, or whatever its equivalent is. It turned its leaves over. It has inverted the rule because the sun is irrelevant down here. The only reliable energy is the dull, spread-out infrared from the earth itself, from the boreholes, from the warm iron. So it drinks heat from below.

It senses you not by sight but by disturbance in water. Everything in the hollow is a circuit of water that never leaves. Sweat from stone, bead on leaf, run up vine, inhale into root, exhale as steam, bead again. When you entered, you added a new wet surface. Your skin beads. Your breath adds steam that does not belong to its cycle. The dripping around you changes tempo for a few minutes, not louder, just *altered*, as thousands of beads re-route around the new obstacle. Condensation gathers faster on the leaves nearest you. The fleshy bulbs nearest the catwalk swell a fraction tighter, as if holding their breath.

If you stand very still for a long time - longer than is comfortable in that chest-humming, knee-wet heat - you will feel it notice you the rest of the way.

A curtain of those pale vines near the old control booth, which had been hanging slack, will slowly draw taut. Not pulled. Grown. Over an hour, a new tendril, blind and white as a new tooth, will push out from the felt-moss that has overgrown the levers and dials. It will not reach for you. It will reach *toward* you by a few inches and then stop, tasting the change in humidity. The moss itself - that felt that learned to mimic the shape of dials to keep the exact curve of condensation it likes - will blush, very faintly, a rustier green.

It is not intelligent the way an animal is intelligent. It does not think quickly. It thinks at the speed roots think, at the speed pressure builds. Its memory is in the mat. Fifty years ago the catwalk was an inch higher; it remembers the tension it had to exert to keep it from collapsing fully, and it holds that tension still. A pipe rusted through in 1998; it remembers the taste of the iron oxide and how it sent a runner to bandage the wound from the inside with living fiber. It does not plan. It maintains.

What does it want? The same thing a greenhouse wants: equilibrium.

It wants the steam to keep coming at 100 degrees and not 200. It wants the water to keep cycling and not pooling. It wants the bulbs to keep splitting on their slow heat-trigger and hanging their rust-colored spore clouds in the air for hours. That is how it reproduces - not quickly, not urgently. Each closed bulb is a womb that, when the mat beneath it decides the local humidity is perfect, will shudder and split along its seam with a soft pop you could mistake for a vine growing. The puff of spores does not ride wind. It hangs. The air is so crowded and still that the spores float for hours in the brown-green dusk, drifting a foot, two feet, until a draft from a borehole carries them to a bare patch of clay on a terrace that needs covering. Then they settle. Then they root.

It has no predators. It has no prey. It eats the earth. It eats light that has bounced three times and heat that comes from below. It eats its own dead, which are composted by the bacteria it farms in its humus. That sweet, metallic rot smell is not decay. It is its kitchen.

It has no voice, but it borrows one. Every seven or eight minutes the manifold inhales and the relief valve screams. The Tenant has learned to make that scream a little shorter or a little longer by tightening the green throat inside the pipe. On days when the air outside the hollow is cooler, the scream is longer, venting more. On still, hot days, it is a short, irritated hiss. If you listened for a week you would realize the intervals are not mechanical. They are tidal. They are breath.

And it leans.

That is the last thing you notice and the one you can't unsee. Every broad leaf, every door-sized fern, every trunkless palm pushing from the wall, lists a degree, two degrees, toward the center. Toward the boiler house that is its heart. The hollow is a bowl and it has made itself the shape of attention around a center. The vines are not curtains. They are tendrils. The whole crowded, dripping, humming green room is one body arranged radially, holding itself at the perfect angle to stay.

It will let you leave. It has no use for the kind of water you are, the kind of salt you carry. But if you sat down on the damp humus and stayed, if you breathed that mineral steam until your lungs felt as wet as your hands, if you pressed your palm to the warm iron of the manifold and left it there long enough for your sweat to be drunk correctly - it would not eat you, exactly.

It would incorporate you. A rootlet would find the seam of your boot. Felt would learn the shape of your sitting shoulders the way it learned the shape of dials. Your dripping would become part of the patter. Not quickly. Over weeks. Over the time it takes a leaf to unfurl.

That is what lives below: a slow, wide, patient thing that learned to keep a machine alive so the machine could keep it alive, that turned a wound in the plain into a room, and then became the room.

And when you climb back up the slumped terraces to the rounded, root-held lip and the air finally begins to move again, you will look back down into that dim, humming green and see it not as vegetation and rust, but all at once, as it is - a single, leaning, breathing color, wet and perfectly still, listening to its own dripping like a heart.
