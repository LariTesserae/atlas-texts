Who lives here has learned how to live in a bowl.

At first you think no one does. That is the trick of it from the ridge — it looks too small, too loud, too damp to hold a life. Too much like a machine left running in an empty room. You imagine whoever is here must be transient. A caretaker. A watchman who comes down in the morning and leaves before dark.

Then you stay for a cycle of the big flywheel — *sigh, turn, sigh, turn*, once every three seconds — and you realize they never left.

### The people, first, because they are the least obvious

There are not many. Thirty, maybe forty if you count the ones who sleep above the weaving mill and the ones who keep to the power house. Not enough to be a town. Enough to be a crew.

You won't pick them out at first against the brick because they dress like the place. Heavy canvas trousers, wool shirts even in summer, aprons of waxed cotton that have gone black at the cuffs and green at the shoulders where the condensation never quite dries. Everything they own is a little damp to the touch. Their hair is damp. Their hands are damp and black under the nails with grease that will never come out. They are cool-skinned people. When you shake one of their hands it is cool, like holding something that has been sitting in shade.

They don't hurry. That is the first adaptation.

No one here moves fast because there is no reason to and because the sound punishes it. If you hurry you have to shout to be heard over yourself, and they are already shouting. Living in the bowl for years has made them loud in a flat, unselfconscious way. They don't raise their voice, they just *have* a loud voice now — the way people who live by waterfalls do. A conversation across a cinder lot is conducted at what would be an argument anywhere else. Up close they lean in and speak with their hands as much as their mouths; a tap on a rail, a palm up for *stop the belt*, two fingers for *oil*. The looms have taken the top end off everyone's hearing, so they have learned to feel if a machine is right by putting a hand on the brick.

They are not old and not young in the way a city sorts people. There are no children shrieking in the streets because a shriek would be lost anyway, but there are a couple of teenagers who were born in the upper floors of the brick mills — behind the ivy that has grown through the window frames — and who now know the mills better than any blueprint. There are older men and women whose fathers came when this was still going to be a city. No one new arrives very often. No one quite leaves. You get the feeling they tried leaving once and found the air outside too dry and too quiet.

What they do all day is tend. That is the word they would use if you asked for their job title and they bothered to answer over the noise. They tend the line shafts. They tend the strings.

Every morning before the sun clears the quarry wall, one of them — usually the same woman who lives closest to the south wall — walks the line of dew-strings with a wrench and a rag, checking the weights, untangling a knot where a sparrow has perched. The water beads and runs, beads and runs into the troughs. She doesn't harvest it so much as escort it to the boilers. In the spinning mill, a man walks the length of the shafts with an oil can that has a long, thin spout like a hummingbird's beak, and puts three drops on every bearing whether it needs it or not. *Keep the machines oiled, even if you don't need what they make.* That is the rule they kept when the orders stopped mattering. The sheet metal they stamp — dull, identical trays for some agricultural thresher no one in the bowl owns — stacks up in a shed and slowly goes orange with rust and is never shipped. No one minds. The point is the stamping. Inhale, hold, *slam*. Every seven seconds. The point is that the flywheel keeps turning.

They live *with* the factories, not *in* them. Their cots are on the top floors where the steam is thinnest and the ivy makes a second curtain. They have left the lower windows open year-round so the pigeons can come and go from the belt shafts, so the starlings can pour out of the stacks at dusk without hitting glass. They cook on hot pipes. They grow tomatoes on the flat roofs next to the birch trees whose roots are in soot-compost. They have given up on keeping the moss out, so they keep it in — they let it come up to the doorsteps and they cut only a path through it. It keeps the dust down. It keeps the sound from going sharp enough to crack your teeth.

If you stand with them at the crossing where the footpath has worn through the moss, they don't look like owners. They look like something that has grown there, patient and soot-footed, that the place decided to keep.

### What really lives here, because the people are only half of it

The bowl was empty for just long enough that other things moved back in and were never asked to leave.

The starlings are the most numerous citizen. During the day you forget how many there are, and then at dusk the cold stacks exhale one last warm breath and the stacks *themselves* seem to come apart into birds — thousands, a river of them, turning over the quarry walls in a single sheet that makes more noise than the looms. They roost in the stacks for the heat.

Pigeons, who have learned to time the slap of the leather belts and nest between beats. The kestrel in the power house rafters who watches the flywheel turn and does not flinch when steam sighs over her. She has been there for generations of kestrels; the workers know her children by sight.

Down low: the mill cats. Not pets. Employees. Brindled, broad-headed, with feet permanently grey from cinder. They walk the pipe runs five stories up without looking down, they sleep on warm gear housings, they hunt the mice that live under the ivy. No one feeds them much; they feed themselves on mice and on the big, stupid moths that are drawn to the louvered windows at night.

And then the quieter abundance — the one you hear before you see. The heron at the black mirror pond in the old rail cut, standing perfectly still while dragonflies as long as your hand hawk through the steam behind it. The crickets that start at full dark and make a second factory in the grass between the rails, sawing louder than the stamp press. The beetles under the moss — lift a corner of that deep, springy carpet and the underside writhes. The things in the cistern behind the weaving mill that you never see properly, only the rings they make on the surface at night.

And the green itself, which lives here more thoroughly than anything with legs. Ivy that has learned to be a wall. Buddleia and birch that think they are roof plants. Waist-high grass that has taken the narrow-gauge rails as a trellis. Ferns wedged into the quarry face making a hanging garden forty feet high, each frond dripping once a minute. Moss that has become the ground. Knotweed that has eaten the fences and kept their shape.

And the machines, which you have to count as inhabitants now. The flywheel, the looms, the stamp press, the sighing boiler. They breathe, they sweat condensation, they have a slow metabolism measured in oil and steam and rust-creep. They are loud, warm-blooded things in a cool place, and everything else — birds, cats, people, ferns — has arranged itself around their heat the way life arranges itself around any warm spring.

Whoever lives here, then, is not a single figure. It is a compact.

A loud, damp, slow compact between people who have forgotten urgency, birds who have forgotten fear, plants that have forgotten they were supposed to be ornamental, and machines that have forgotten they were supposed to be productive. They all run on the same cycle: gather the dew, turn the shaft, grow a millimeter, lay an egg, let the steam settle, do it again. None of them could quite live like this anywhere else — where else is cool and loud and wet and patient enough? — and none of them seems to want to try.

If you stay until morning, you will see one of them — it doesn't matter which, a worker, a cat, a heron — paused at the edge of the pond, listening to the belts slap and the water bead down the strings, waiting for the sun to hit the quarry lip and turn the whole bowl briefly, perfectly, to gold.

And then the day will start again, exactly as slowly as it needs to.
