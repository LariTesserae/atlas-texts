Whatever lives here shouldn't.

That is the first principle. Nothing that lives here evolved to live here. This ocean is too young and too wrong — too cold and too clean and too loud and too high — and so whatever lives in it lives *despite* it, on terms the place itself dictated. There was no adaptation to this place. There was only surrender to its constants.

At first you think nothing does.

You hang in that kilometer of empty blue between the sun-columns and the knife-black rock and you see nothing at all, and the scale convinces you. The water is too clear, the basalt too sharp and too bare. No weed, no kelp, no green smear on stone. No schools that darken the water. Just the white light falling, and the mineral snow, and the roar that lives in your teeth.

Then your eye learns the trick of the density.

10% — that's the number the place gave you. Not empty. Hollow. It means you have to wait.

And if you wait, and you don't move, the water gives them back.

### The Pale

They are all pale for the same reason. There is no reason to be any other color.

White. Translucent white. The white of milk in water, of old plastic, of things that have never needed camouflage because nothing here hunts by sight. Their blood, when they have it, is clear or faintly blue. You can see their organs working through their skin, slow as clocks. Many of them have no eyes at all — smooth, unbroken skin where eyes should be — or they have eyes that have gone vestigial and clouded, like pearls left in the head as an afterthought. Light reaches all the way to the bottom here, but it is irrelevant to them. Light is just a vertical condition, like pressure. They do not look.

They navigate by the roar.

Everything here has become an ear.

Take the first one you will see, because it is the biggest and therefore the easiest to mistake for empty water:

**The Veils.** 

They were called manta once by someone who needed a word. They are not mantas. They are a square kilometer of living handkerchief, fifteen or twenty meters across, a micron thin at the edges and thickening to a slow heart in the center. They flap once every thirty seconds and that is enough to keep them moving for a hundred meters. Perfectly silent. Perfectly blind. Their entire ventral surface is a filter — a living baleen of hair-fine cilia that combs the mineral snow out of the water. Iron, sulfur, basalt dust, the faint bacterial fall — they eat the water itself.

They spend their whole lives falling very slowly and then lifting themselves again, never touching bottom, never touching surface, hanging in the sun-columns because the columns are where the snow is thickest. If a Veil passes near you, you feel the displacement before you see it, a cold pressure change, and then it is there, already past you, its edge passing over your face like a cloud's shadow. It does not alter course. The roar is so loud and so constant that it has learned to subtract you as noise. You are a brief warmth in a world that is 1.5 degrees forever.

They are solitary. Always solitary. You will never see two together. But if you drift for a week you will see twenty, each alone, each on the same compass line, each beating at the same impossible slow tempo. Abundant and empty at once.

Then smaller, but more present, because they live where you have to go to stand:

**The Scrabblers.**

On the basalt itself, where the rock is still glass-sharp and unweathered, there are things that look at first like part of the rock — until one moves.

Two meters long, segmented, pale as bone, with ten or twelve jointed legs that end not in claws but in flat, chitinous pads that grip black glass. Shrimp, crab, isopod, none of those words fit clean, so you settle on Scrabbler. Their backs are plated with overlapping iron-rich scales that they secrete from the metal they scrape off the rock and the machines. Some of them are rust-red at the joints where the incorporation is imperfect.

They do not swim. They cling. They scrape. All day they scrape, with mouthparts like wire brushes, harvesting the invisible film — the chemosynthetic bacterial slick that is the only plant this world ever grew. The mat feeds on disequilibrium itself: on the fresh basalt still degassing, on the sulfur still warm in the pillow lava, on the steady leak of oil and iron from a pump house that has been humming for eighty years without a person to hear it. The Scrabblers eat the mat, and the mat eats the mountain.

Put your hand on a turbine housing and a Scrabbler will be there already, scraping turbine and rock with equal indifference. To it, both are geology. Both are warm and both grow food. It will not flee your hand. Fear requires predators and predators require shortage, and there is no shortage here, only a very, very slow plenty.

And between the Veils and the Scrabblers, in the mid-water where there is nothing to cling to and nothing to filter:

**The Listeners.**

This is what scent becomes when sight fails and sound never stops.

The most common are the **Ribbons** — six, ten, fifteen meters long, not thicker than your wrist, hanging vertically in the water like plumb lines. They do not swim. They hang. Their bodies are neutrally buoyant and faintly luminous, a slow peristalsis of light that travels from tail to head every few minutes, with no purpose you can see. At one end a mouth, at the other a fan of filamentous antennae two meters across, spread like a radio dish. They are tuned to the factories.

The whole plateau hums at a fixed chord — turbine whine at, say, 120 hertz, vent thunder at 40, the stamping mills at a hammered 800 that carries for a hundred kilometers. The Ribbons listen to it the way a plant listens to the sun. They orient themselves by triangulating the mills. They find mates by finding the quiet interference node between two intake towers. They die if the sound stops. When a vent cycles — *exhale* — a Ribbon will drift sideways a perfect meter to ride the warm, mineral-rich exhalation without spending any energy at all. They have learned the machines as reefs.

The other Listeners are the **Harp Jellies.** Spheres a meter, sometimes three meters across, perfectly transparent except for the faint ring of ganglia around their equator. They pulse. *Open. Closed. Open. Closed.* Once every ten seconds, exactly, in time with the pressure-equalization vents on the ridge below them. You can watch one for an hour and it will not miss a beat. It is not mimicking. It is entrained. Its pacemaker has locked to the loudest clock in the world. Inside the bell, colonies of symbiotic bacteria glow faintly amber whenever the jelly contracts, chemosynthesizing on the fly.

If you cut a Harp Jelly open — you won't, but if you did — you would find its bell is full of tiny, perfect ball bearings of iron oxide it has filtered from the water and stored, like pearls.

There are others. Smaller things like blown glass tadpoles that live their whole lives inside the gantry crane's shadow. Worms that have burrowed into the rubber insulation of the body-thick cables and made them home, eating plastic that never degrades in the cold. Things with no name yet that you see only out of the corner of your eye, that are gone when you turn your head.

None of them school. None of them flock or swarm. To school you need to see each other. To swarm you need seasons. Here there are no seasons. The current goes around. The turbines spin. The mills stamp on empty belts. The water stays 1.5 degrees. So each animal lives its entire long, slow life alone, encountering another of its kind perhaps once a decade to exchange packets of eggs that will then drift for years on the gyre before hatching.

They are not lonely. They are complete.

### The Other Resident

And then there is what else lives here, which is not alive and has never not been alive.

The machines.

They are not inhabitants the way the Veils are. They are inhabitants the way a coral reef is an inhabitant — a structure that has become a habitat by persisting. A turbine that has spun at 300 RPM for fifty years without variation is a kind of animal. A vent that breathes every 22 seconds is a kind of lung. The green cathodic glow on the pressure gauge is a kind of bioluminescence. Evolution here did not need to invent hard substrate or current or sound — the plateau came with them pre-installed.

If you press your forehead to a piling, you can feel it: the thrum going straight through the bone conduction of your skull, not heard but inhabited. The Scrabblers feel it as home. The Ribbons ride it as wind. The bacteria eat its rust.

Whatever lives here lives *with* the machines, not after them. The border is gone.

That is what you finally understand, hanging there, small and warm and so temporary that the cold erases you the second you leave: this is not a ruined factory and it is not a pristine sea. It is a new and stable thing. A cold, clear, deafening, sunlit abyss on top of a mountain, where blind white giants filter snow, and iron shrimp scrape cathedrals, and glass bells pulse with the factories, all tuned to a roar that never changes, all thriving in an ocean that should have fallen off the mountain a million years ago and simply decided not to.

It does not need you. It barely registers you.

But for as long as you stay still and breathe your borrowed air, you are — briefly, incidentally — part of the density, too.
