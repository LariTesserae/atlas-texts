They live slow.

That is the first thing you have to understand about them, before you can see them at all. If you come down the switchback road at midday, your eyes are still full of the savanna - that flat white glare - and you think the bottom is empty. You think nothing could live in that damp kiln, in that amber dusk under the bulbs. You hear the *thud... thud...* of the low flywheels and you think the place is abandoned and the machines were left running.

Then someone moves, and you realize the whole pit has been full of people the entire time. You just didn't know how to look for people who move that slowly.

They are not a different species. Not yet. They came down on the elevator or the ladders like you did. But the pit finishes them quickly. Within a season, it edits you.

### The Body

The people of the cut are damp people.

Their skin never fully dries. In the 38-degree stillness at the bottom, sweat doesn't evaporate, it just sits — a sheen on forearms, on foreheads, on the backs of necks. So they stopped fighting it. Their clothes are built for it: undyed cotton and linen, cut loose and already softened by rot and repair, the color of wet stone and vine. No one wears anything that clings or anything that would rust against the skin. Feet are bare or in rubber cut from old conveyor belt, because leather molds in a week. Hair is kept short or shaved or tied back wet, because long hair in that heat is a misery.

They are thin, but not starved. Lean the way someone is lean who climbs five stories of rebar ladder ten times a day and never hurries. Shoulders and calves corded, hands and feet larger than you'd expect — broad, calloused, good for gripping wet pipe and vine. Eyes have changed most. They live under sodium and bulb-light, in vertical shadow. Their pupils stay wide. The white of the eye is a little bloodshot always, from the fine coal smoke that never quite clears. They squint painfully during the noon column when the sun hits the cistern, and the children throw tarps over their heads and shriek with laughter at the sudden white pain. The rest of the day they see perfectly in what you would call gloom. They can see the veins on a fern from across the alley.

They speak quietly because you have to. The air is too thick to shout through, the basalt walls swallow high frequencies. Their voices are low, hoarse from damp, a little rusted from breathing wet iron for years. They don't call out; they tap. A wrench on a pipe means *I'm coming up behind you.* Three taps on tin means *water coming through, move your bucket.* Their work songs are not songs, really — hummed, breathy things you can sing without opening your mouth, without losing water.

They sweat, they drip, they breathe shallowly to conserve cool. Old people here have a slow pulse you can see at the throat, 50 beats a minute even awake. Newcomers from the plain come down panting, red-faced, trying to move at savanna speed, and the residents watch them with a kind of patient amusement until they either learn to slow or climb back up the elevator and leave, gasping that the place is unbearable.

Those who stay learn to be still. You will see a weaver sitting at her throttled loom for an hour, feet working the treadle once every four seconds, hands never jerking, eyes never leaving the damp thread. You will see a forge-man leaning against his anvil, watching his metal heat to cherry for a long, long time, feeding the fire one measured scoop of vine-char at a time because too much at once would make the bad air pool. Patience is not a virtue here. It is a climate control system.

### The Work

There is no one who does not work with both plant and machine. You cannot separate them.

Every family tends a vertical plot. Not on the ground — there is no ground — but on the wall that is their wall, the roof that is their roof. A grandmother will spend the morning with a pair of tweezers and a feather, moving pollen from one waxy night-bloom to another inside a vent, because the pale moths are too few and too lazy to do it reliably. A boy will harvest elephant ears from the gutter that feeds his cistern, chop them, fry them on the same stove that powers his mother's soldering iron. The gourd vines are pruned to shade the corrugated tin so the afternoon column doesn't cook the sleeping loft. Bean runners are trained around the flywheel housing to pull heat away. If you neglect your green, your neighbors will come and do it for you without asking, because your hot roof makes their wall hotter.

And everyone minds water. The children learn it before they learn to read. You never, ever block a drip line. You catch what falls on you, but you let what misses you fall further. Every dwelling has a choreography of tarps, gutters made from split pipe, barrels, bottles, leaves curled into funnels. The cistern in the center is communal — not owned, just watched. An old man sits near it all day with a notebook, not guarding, just recording how fast it drips, whose gutter overflowed last night. Disputes are not about who *took* water, but about who *bent* its path. To kink a hose to fill your barrel faster is the closest thing they have to theft, and the punishment is simple: your line gets disconnected for three days and you have to ask, quietly, for water from each neighbor below you, which is a humiliation no one wants twice.

Their machines are alive in the same way the vines are. No one runs anything at its rated speed. A stamping press that could slam once a second is geared down to once every ten seconds — *thud... thud...* — so it never warms the air. The textile looms clack at walking pace. The small foundry pours once a week, at night, when the rim air is cool enough to accept a little smoke. They oil everything constantly, because rust is always forming in the condensation. A mechanic's job is mostly wiping. The engines are throttled, patient, half-asleep, and the people love them the way you love draft animals — they pat hot flywheels as they pass.

### The Temper

They know each other completely. Four hundred meters is not a town, it is a room. From the highest catwalk you can see every laundry line, every roof garden, every face. There are maybe nine hundred of them now, layered three and four stories high, wall to wall. No one is a stranger. Everyone knows whose house has settled two bricks since last summer, whose daughter is coughing from bad air after her husband ran his lathe too fast, whose cats have kittens under the loom.

They are not miserable. That is what the visitor gets wrong. On the plain, people imagine the pit as a prison, a hole of smoke and sweat. Inside, it feels like being held. The basalt warms at night and radiates back their own heat. The vines make the strict rectangle soft, break the industrial geometry into green caves and dripping curtains. The light, for all its dimness, is constant — no blinding savanna glare to squint against. Outside, on the yellow cracked flat, the wind takes everything away. Here, nothing is taken. Even sound stays.

They have made a calendar out of slow things. Time is not measured in hours but in vertical inches: how far the creeping fig has advanced over the foundry door, how deep the black teeth of the rain gullies have cut this year, how many times the cistern has had to be bailed because the moss high up got waterlogged and released all at once. A child born on the second tier will be told, "You were born when the bean vine reached the third-floor walkway." An old woman will say, "My mother helped weld that rebar staircase when the cut was still bright as fresh glass."

At dusk, when the worklamps stutter on and the forges are banked and the air finally, briefly, cools one degree, they come out onto what passes for streets — which is to say, they step out onto each other's roofs. They sit among the gourds and the air plants and the pale night-flowers that are just opening and filling the pit with that sweet rot perfume, and they share water that has been passed through three sets of hands already. They talk low. They listen to drips.

They do not want to leave. The few who go up to trade — riding the shuddering cage elevator with a bundle of recast sheet metal or dried gourd — come back after a day on the plain with headaches from the dry wind and the huge sky. They talk about agoraphobia the way other people talk about claustrophobia. Up there, they say, there is no wall to stop your sight, no ceiling to hold your smoke and your breath close. You feel like you might blow away.

Down here, they are fitted exactly to their wound. Damp, dim, green-stitched, slow-pulsed, quiet. If you live here long enough, you stop noticing the weight of the air and start noticing how thin and mean the air is everywhere else.
