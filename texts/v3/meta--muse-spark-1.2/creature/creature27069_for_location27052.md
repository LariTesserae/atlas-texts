They don't live *in* it.

They *are* the way it stays itself.

At first you think the basin is empty because nothing in it moves the way an animal should. No darting. No calling. No scattering when you arrive. You stand on the worn lip and the whole dish seems to be only stone and water and left-behind iron, ticking over on its seven-second heart.

Then your eyes adjust to the dusk and you realize you are looking at too much life to see any one thing.

The first to resolve are the ones you mistook for part of the stone.

### The Skin

It lives tightest to the rock, so you thought it was a stain. That matte, plum-copper film stretched over the terraces isn't growing *on* the stone anymore than your skin grows on you. It *is* the surface now.

Up close it has texture - not slimy, but fine-grained and leathery, like the dried membrane inside an eggshell left out in the rain. Where water runs thin over it, it tightens and goes darker. Where water pools, it wrinkles and loosens, pulsing once every few seconds, out of sync with the pump. If you watch one wrinkle long enough you see it is not wrinkling at random. It is feeding. It creeps uphill, a millimeter at a time, against the seep, against gravity, into the fresher water coming off the rim. The water beads on it and doesn't roll. It is absorbed.

In the dimmest terraces it lifts off the stone in blistered sheets, making low tents of itself. Underneath, the stone is paler, almost white, etched with a faint tracery of rootless veins where it has been digesting the mineral bloom. Touch it and it doesn't recoil. It goes taut. Colder.

It is not one thing. It is a colony that decided to be a continent. It covers maybe a third of the basin, and everything else lives on the terms it sets for how wet the stone is allowed to be.

### The Lip-Keepers

Then the clicking resolves.

Along every single terrace lip, exactly where the sheet of water breaks and falls a centimeter to the next bowl, there is a line of black beads so dense you thought it was a mineral inlay. They are not beads. They are homes.

Each one is the size of a peppercorn, a smooth, dark, hinged shell, clamped by a pale foot to the polished stone. Hundreds per square foot, shoulder to shoulder, they carpet the brink of every terrace. They live only in that line - not an inch above in the slower water, not an inch below in the splash. Only where the water is thinnest and fastest.

They open together. They close together. Not perfectly, but in a wave. *Click.* A thousand tiny lids snapping shut, filtering. *Click.* Opening again. When they open you see the briefest flash of a soft, orange-pink interior, frilled like a gill, combing the water for whatever the Skin lets through. Their clicking is the sound you thought was dripping. It is the basin's other metronome, set against the pump's thud. If you stir the water above them with your boot, the whole line closes at once and stays closed for a long minute, as if holding its breath.

They don't move. They will live and die on that exact lip. Their dead shells don't wash away. They fill in with silt and become the next millimeter of terrace.

### The Turners

In the center, where the water finally gathers in that flat, black, ankle-deep pan, the ones underneath become visible only when they want to be.

They are the length of your finger, or your whole hand if you count the antennae. Pale as milk-glass, eyeless - the dim never required eyes - with a translucent carapace and a ridiculous number of legs, each leg fringed with fine hairs that never stop beating. They are always on their sides, always half-buried, churning the black silt over and over and over.

There is no rest in that pan. At any moment dozens of them are flicking, burrowing, surfacing with a soft *plip* and vanishing again. They turn the bottom like the Skin turns the stone. If you stand still long enough you see the pattern: they avoid the slicks. Scattered through the pan are pools inside the pool - heavier fluid, rainbowed with oil, that doesn't mix with the water. It sits like poured glass, perfectly flat even when the water around it ripples. The Turners will swim right up to its edge and turn back, antennae twitching violently, as if the slick is too loud to touch.

Sometimes a larger shape moves under them and they all freeze at once, flat in the silt, invisible again. You never see what made them freeze. You only see the silt settle too quickly, as if something below inhaled.

### The Still

Above all of it are the ones in the air, and they are the hardest to accept as alive because they do not behave like anything that flies.

They hang in clouds - not swarms, clouds - each perhaps three feet across, at about chest height, between the catwalks and the pump houses. From a distance you took them for gnats in the mist. Up close each individual is elongated, iridescent grey, with wings you can't see and legs tucked tight. They beat their wings so fast they seem motionless, suspended.

They do not scatter. You can walk straight through a cloud and feel the faintest cool stippling on your face where you pass through them, hear a faint rise in pitch, and then they are behind you, reformed to exactly the shape they were before, hanging in the same inch of air. They never land. They never feed where you can see. They hang where the air is wettest, directly over the places where droplets are doing the wrong thing - where water beads on the underside of a pipe and then, slowly, impossibly, lifts an inch before falling again. The Still gather there. They breathe that reversal. The faint sour steam from the pale salt-spires on the stone seems to tilt toward them.

If the transformer sparks, the nearest cloud contracts, just for a second, every insect turning a fraction toward the ozone.

### The Lanterns

And on the iron itself live the ones that make you think someone is still tending the machines.

They are slow. Slug-like, but not slugs - more like a damp, folded lung, the color of the rust they live on, with a faint light inside them that comes and goes with their breathing. They late along the sweating pipes and the flanges and the bowed catwalks, leaving trails that glow for minutes after they pass, pale amber like the caged bulbs. They are big enough to cup in both hands, heavy, cool. They gather where the metal weeps rusty water and drink it, their bodies pulsing and brightening. Where two pipes meet and the hum is loudest, you will find three or four of them piled together, barely moving, sharing heat.

They are the only ones that seem to notice you with anything like curiosity. One will lift its blind head as you pass, dim, and then settle again, leaving a fresh luminous smear on the bolt-head.

---

None of them is the inhabitant. All of them together are.

There is no hierarchy here. The Skin sets the wetness. The Lip-Keepers strain it. The Turners keep the bottom from sealing over. The Still keep the air from going stagnant. The Lanterns keep the iron from healing shut. And the machines - the pump thudding every seven seconds, the valves turning themselves, the string of amber bulbs - keep the water from ever rising and drowning the Skin, or ever drying and cracking it.

Something lived here before that needed that exact balance - for a hatchery, a mine, a cooling pond - and left its tools running. The basin learned to live in the tools' shadow. Or the tools learned to keep the basin alive.

If you sit on the lowest terrace and go quiet, you can feel it: the whole hamlet-sized room is one shallow, cold, patient organism, made of stone and water and film and shell and silt and hanging air and rust and low voltage. It doesn't need you. It doesn't mind you. It will part its gnat-clouds around you and close its shell-lips under your boots and keep ticking, dripping, clicking, humming, turning itself over, one more time, and again, exactly as wet as it needs to be.
