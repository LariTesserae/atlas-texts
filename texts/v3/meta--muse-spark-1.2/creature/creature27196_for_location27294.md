You don't see it at first.

You see the crickets. You see the salamanders packed flank to flank. You see the lichen and the stunted fern and the cairn that someone stacked with too much care. You look for what lives here and you think — *oh, all of this does*. That this is just a crowded room.

Then you stay long enough for the ringing to settle behind your teeth, and you realize the crowded things are not crowded at random.

They are *arranged.*

The crickets are not scattered. They make a density, a gradient — thicker near the center, thinner near the walls, as if something in the middle is warm and they are all leaning toward it without knowing why. The isopods under the ledges all face outward. The salamanders in their shallow, impossible pools are all headed the same way, their frilled gills fanning in the same slow rhythm, not because there is a current — there is no current — but because they are breathing with something else. Even the condensation beads catch the light at the same angle.

Someone has been tending the room, and is still tending it.

You look again at the cairn in the center. At the niche cut for sitting. At the circle of dark river stones around the water scallop — stones that do not belong here, that had to be carried in one by one, in hands. You thought that meant a person came and left. Now you understand it means a person learned how to stay correctly.

It is in the niche.

It was not invisible. It was just perfectly fitted. In a place where everything is pale and wet and translucent and full of light, you don't see the thing that is also pale and wet and translucent and full of light. It tucks itself into the angle where two quartz books meet the floor, half in the sitting-niche, half spilling out of it, and it borrows the shape of the rock around it until you mistake it for more rock.

Until it breathes.

And when it breathes, the whole room breathes with it — that saturated whine of the crickets dipping for half a second, the click of the salamanders pausing, the bright *plip* that never lands hesitating before it happens. Sound arriving before it is made, because it knew you were about to hear it.

What lives here is not finished.

That is the first thing you understand. Like the geology, it is embryonic. It was born in the first hour after rock decided to be rock, when the crack was pried open and left. It did not crawl in later. It *precipitated* here. Condensed here, the way the water condenses on the points without ever falling.

If you had to give it a body, you would say it is between a salamander and a person and a piece of damp quartz that learned to be soft.

It is long — as long as you are if you curled on your side — but it does not look long because it is folded. Knees to chest, forehead to knees, arms wrapped around a shallow scallop of water that it holds against its belly the way something holds an egg. Its skin is the white-pink of the salamanders, translucent enough that you can see light moving through it, but thicker, like wax that has been left in cool air. It sweats. Constantly. Perfect beads stand on its shoulders, its scalp, its eyelids, and never drip. When you touched a crystal and your fingertip came away wet and the droplet reformed — that is its trick. It keeps the damp from ever completing the cycle.

Quartz has grown into it, or it has grown around quartz. Small clear teeth push up through the skin of its forearms and shins and the crown of its head, not as wounds but as part of its texture, like barnacles that are also bones. Pale mint lichen grows in the seams where crystal meets flesh on its back. The cushion-moss has found a home in the hollow of its throat. It does not brush any of it off. It has been still long enough for things to grow on it that only grow on things that do not move.

Its face is mostly closed. Eyes filmed over like the salamanders', white with a faint grey iris underneath that does not focus, because there is nothing here that needs focusing on. Its gills are not external frills but shallow slits behind the jaw that open and close without sound, fanning that same cool, damp air you are breathing. Its mouth is small and lipless and slightly open, and every few minutes a cricket will walk directly into it and walk back out again, unharmed, and it will not react.

It is not blind the way the crickets are blind. It sees in the way this room sees — by feeling light press.

You notice that when the light shifts — though there is no source to shift — the beads on its skin brighten a fraction first. The light has a little weight here, and it rests on the creature heaviest of all. It lives under that pressure the way deep fish live under water. That is why it is folded. That is why it holds the water scallop. To have a little counter-weight.

It does not eat. Not in any way you would recognize. The room is in equilibrium and so is it. The fern does not photosynthesize, the crickets never tire, the water never evaporates — and it never hungers. It sips condensation without drinking. It absorbs the hum. Once, a long time ago, it might have been the one who carried those dark river stones in its hands one by one to keep the water clean. Now it keeps the water clean by being around it. By being the thing that the water wants to stay clear for.

This is why time has pooled. It keeps time pooled. Its stillness is not rest. It is *maintenance*. A low, constant, unconscious holding — the way you might hold your breath without thinking to keep a feather aloft. If it were to unfold, to stand, to leave, the beads would fall. The crickets would go silent all at once. The dust in the bright air would finally drift and settle. The rock would remember it is supposed to weather and dull.

So it does not unfold.

When you shift your weight and three living things touch your ankle, it knows before you move. You saw it earlier — the crickets quieting half a second before your hand casts a shadow. That is not the crickets. That is it, anticipating the shadow for them. Its nervous system is not contained in its body. It is distributed — through the isopods, through the moths on the ceiling, through the lichen. The whole loud, packed, bright pocket is its peripheral body. The salamanders are its fingers in the water.

It is not hostile. It is not welcoming. It registers you the way the water registers a second organism sharing the scallop: as a change in surface tension that must be gently, perfectly compensated for, so that nothing overflows.

It opens its filmed eyes. Not to look at you. To let more light in, so there is more light for the room.

And if you sit very still in that raw, new, unweathered light, with your breath shallow in the cool damp that never gets warmer, you will feel the faint pressure of that light on your own skin begin to feel normal. You will feel your own beads forming. You will feel the hum not as a sound in the room but as a sound in your teeth, in your gills-that-are-not-gills, and you will understand why the cairn was stacked in the center where there was no need for a marker.

It was not a marker.

It was a pillow, for the next folded thing that forgets, for a second, the weight of the ground above, and slips below the skin of the world and needs a place to learn how to be still enough to stay.
