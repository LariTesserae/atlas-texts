They live low and they live slow.

You don't see them at first. That's part of it. In a place that green and that wet, where every wall weeps and every roof drips, your eye learns to read leaf and brick and rust as one continuous texture, and the people inside it take a minute to separate from it. You hear them — a voice bounced too clearly off damp air, the clank of shears, a child's bare feet slapping on root-street — before the shapes resolve.

Then they do.

Who lives there is, at first glance, just people. Entirely people. No new species, no fantasy. That's what is unsettling and then, after an hour, what is comforting about them. They are humans who have been in that dish long enough that the dish has gotten into them.

### The Body In That Air

They are soft-bodied.

Not weak — you see that immediately wrong assumption corrected when a man lifts a wet timber or a woman hauls a basket of cut vine as thick as your wrist. They are strong in the way of people who do the same slow, heavy work every day. But their bodies have never needed to harden against dryness or cold. The air is 30 degrees and saturated from March to November, and the water table beads out of the walls at knee height. Nothing ever fully dries, including skin.

So their skin stays damp. Perpetually. Children shine. Old people shine. The adults have a slight swollen smoothness to their fingers and faces, a waterlogged fullness, as if their flesh has taken on a little of the dish itself. Lips never chap. Hair never frizzes — it hangs straight and heavy, black or dark brown or the iron-grey of rust, usually cut short because otherwise it would never dry and would hold mildew. They cut it every two weeks, bluntly, with the same shears they use on the wisteria. They smell like the place: sweat that never quite evaporates, a faint green-leaf crush, hot oil from the line shafts.

Their eyes have learned the light. The canopy is pruned into louvers, so the sun falls in hot bars and then in speckled, moving dapples. They squint less than you do. Their pupils seem a little wider, used to the dim under-leaf, used to the warm bare bulbs that burn even at noon. At dusk they don't turn many more lights on — the leaves already glow amber from below and that's enough for them to see the root without tripping.

Their feet tell you the most. No one wears much shoe. Rubber boots for the foundry, otherwise bare feet or thin canvas slips with the toes cut open. The root-streets are ridged and smooth, knotted like old rope, always slick with biofilm. To walk them fast you need a wide, splayed foot that grips. The children have it naturally — they run full tilt down the central canal-street and never fall. The adults have grown into it over decades. Soles are pale, ridged, almost amphibious, callous where they need to be and soft everywhere else. Toenails are kept brutally short. Between the toes, the skin is often a little white and soft from constant damp, and no one thinks anything of it.

Clothing is maintenance, too. Nothing tailored, nothing that would mind getting wet. Indigo work clothes, old mill denim gone almost black with washing, loose shirts that cling when wet and dry slowly on the body. Many shirts are stained green at the collar where vine juice has rubbed off. Many have been patched with the same waxed canvas they use to bind aerial roots to joists. Everything is a little too big, to let air move. Laundry never smells of detergent for long; five minutes after it's taken down from a branch-line, it's damp again.

### The Work That Is The Life

No one lives there without tending.

The hamlet doesn't have jobs and residents as two categories. It has keepers. The equilibrium is too precise to leave alone — if you stopped pruning for three weeks, a fig would throw a limb through a skylight; if you stopped sweeping for two days, the root-streets would be slick with rot; if you let the seep build, the lower brick would bloom with black mold that eats mortar.

So you see them always doing a little work as they move. An old man walking to get rice taps a hanging aerial root back behind its iron band with his knuckle, listening for the hollow thud that says it's tightening. A teenage girl on her way to the mill pauses to pinch off wisteria suckers from around a window frame, flicking them into the canal. A boiler crew — three men stripped to the waist, skin gleaming — shovel husk and pruned wood into the low-pressure engine that thumps and thumps, and while they wait for the pressure to rise they scrape scale off a weeping wall with a flat trowel.

Their hands are the clearest sign of where they belong. Even the children's hands are broad, short-nailed, green under the nails. The adults' hands are nicked and stained, fingertips permanently calloused from line-shaft belts and vine stem. They handle living things and hot metal with the same ungloved familiarity, because in the dish both will burn or bite you slowly if you disrespect them, and neither will if you know the timing.

They talk while they work, but not loudly. The damp air carries voices strangely well, and the engine thump and the thousand drips already make a saturated sound-room. So they speak low and a little slowly, with pauses to let a drip finish. You notice they all hum, almost unconsciously, at the same tempo as the engine house. Not a song — just a low note in the throat while pruning, while sweeping.

### A Generation Deep, Root Deep

They are old there, in the way a town can be old. Not many newcomers; the levee ring makes it clear where the inside ends. People are born in the rooms where fig trunks go up through the floor, and they die in those same rooms. You see it in how the buildings fit them. Doorframes are worn down at shoulder height where five generations of the same shoulder width have brushed past. The iron bands around the root-pillars have been moved outward three, four times — you can see the ghost rings where the previous band sat for twenty years before the root outgrew it.

The elders are not frail in the way elders get outside. The wet warmth is kind to joints. They keep working, just more slowly. The old woman with the hedge shears who trims the wall-vine so it doesn't eat the power line — she does three meters in the morning, then sits on a root-bench and watches the canal, legs dangling in the tea-brown water. Her legs are mottled with age spots and with the faint grey-green lace of surface veins that look, for a second, like lichen. When she sweats, it beads and stays on her skin without evaporating, and she wipes it away with a rag she keeps in a pocket that is always damp.

Children are everywhere but not noisy in a city way. There are no yards, no open ground that isn't canal or root. So they play in the vertical. They climb aerial roots like ladders, they swing from banyan cords that have been left deliberately slack for them, they drop directly from balconies into the canal and thrash around with the heron watching them like a disapproving foreman. They learn early not to pull leaves off — to cut, to guide. A mother will smack a child's hand not for being loud, but for ripping a philodendron runner the wrong way. You learn that the green is not decoration. It's load-bearing.

And for all the density — buildings pressed to the canal, every surface used — they are not crowded. They know how to be alone together. Two men can work on the same loom for an hour without speaking, each occupied with drip and thump and leaf. Privacy is a curtain of hanging roots. Solitude is standing on the levee ring and looking out at the flat rice checks, remembering that the world is dry out there.

### The Other Inhabitant

If you ask them what lives there, they would say the town. Not the people. The town.

Because the other thing that lives there is the composite itself, and they know they are part of it. The figs and the banyans and the wisteria are not plants in a town; they are the town's lungs and walls. The engine is its heart — that patient thump-thump you feel in your molars at night when the bulbs are low and the leaves glow from below. The water is its blood, seeping out of every wall at knee-height, beading on every pipe, cycling from cistern to boiler to steam to drip.

After generations, the line has blurred. There are places in the back of the mill where a beam was replaced fifty years ago by a root that thickened in the right direction, and now the root *is* the beam, banded with iron that it has half-swallowed. No one remembers which came first. There are families where a birthmark looks a little like the moss that grows on their house. That is not mysticism to them. That is just what happens when you let water stay on the surface and you guide the green instead of fighting it, for a hundred years.

They are not trapped. You can see that by how they look beyond the levee. They go out to the rice checks when they need to. They come back because the air out there is too thin and too dry and the light is undivided. Inside the dish the air is heavy enough to lean on. The sound is full enough to hide inside. The boundary is always visible — that low root-mat ring — so you never feel lost. You are inside a single room that breathes slowly, that drips constantly, that is tended every morning with brooms and shears.

What lives there is a people who have become, by choice and by damp, as perennial and as pruned as their walls. Patient. Waterlogged. Warm to the touch. They will be there tomorrow doing the same thing at the same speed, moving a pipe half an inch that a root has pushed, repainting a girder for the fourth time in their life, listening to the engine and the drips match each other until the two are one rhythm.
