It does not live *in* the room.

It lives *as* the room.

At first you think nothing lives here except those eight trays of grey-green rosettes, and even those you would not call living so much as persisting. The room enforces NO. No water, no rot, no change, no rest from the roar. Nothing should be able to afford to live here. That is its whole trick. You have to stay long enough for your brain to surrender the sound.

After ninety seconds the blowers stop being noise and become pressure, a new kind of silence. After three minutes your eyes, starved for shadow or movement under that flat 4000K office-white, begin to hallucinate difference. You start to look for what is *not* failing. And then you see that one patch of wall is not a wall at all.

### The Resident

On the north wall, about a meter off the grating, between two of the palm-sized bolt heads, the perforated aluminum has a flaw.

Or you think it is a flaw. The mustard-yellow insulation that should be visible through the neat rows of 3mm acoustic holes is not mustard there. It is paler. More translucent. And the holes themselves are not quite holes. The pattern stutters.

Kneel. The updraft coming through the grating pushes at your knees. The roar tries to push your thoughts back out of your head. Look closer.

It is not on the wall. It is *in front of* the wall.

What lives here is flat. It has to be. Anything with thickness would be noticed, would be scraped off by Maintenance on their quarterly pass and logged as "fouling." What lives here learned to be two-dimensional.

It is a colony, but you will think of it as one animal because it behaves like one. A sheet, roughly the size of an opened sleeping bag, maybe a little larger, spread thin over the inner face of the perforated aluminum, and extending down in a seamless, unstretched fold onto the grating and under it, into the dark where the updraft is born. Its edge is not an edge. It frays into the wall like frost, like the ammonia crystal beards, in needles and fern-tips so fine you can't tell where panel ends and organism begins. The color is exactly wrong for insulation - a jaundiced ivory, translucent as old silicone, with a faint bloom on the surface identical to the waxy bloom on the rosettes in the trays.

Those rosettes are not plants. Or not only plants. They are its gardens, its lungs, its bait.

The body itself is a mat, only two or three millimeters thick, but densely structured. If you could peel it - you can't, it has chemically welded itself to the aluminum with something stronger than the factory's own sealant - you would see it is not solid. It is a felt of horizontal vessels, like a leaf pressed flat and then run over by a steamroller. Under a lens, each thread is a tube, and each tube is full not of water - water would have killed it long ago - but of a clear silicone oil that does not freeze at -35C, laced with dissolved ammonia. That is its blood. It antifreezes itself from the inside. When the blowers stutter - which they never do - it would shiver and die.

Because it has no water in it, it does not rot. Because it has no water in it, bleach does nothing to it. It tastes the air the way you taste blue metal, through absorption. The constant updraft that pushes at your soles is its river. The air in this room is thin and mean and filthy rich if you know how to eat it: aerosolized compressor oil, micro-droplets of silicone, a faint vapor of anhydrous ammonia weeping perpetually from the joints above you. The mat drinks that. It spreads a static charge across its upper surface and precipitates the oil out of the air the way a cold window precipitates frost. You can see the faint, oily sheen on it if you catch the fluorescent light at an angle. That is its fat.

It eats light, too, but not the way the rosettes do. The four battens never turn off. 4000K forever. The mat is photosynthetic, but sluggishly, patiently. It does not need a lot. It has incorporated a pigment the exact color of perforated aluminum - a dull, anodized silver - into its outer skin, and beneath that, a layer of that same waxy grey-green. In the places where it is thickest, over the bolt heads it has enveloped completely, you can see the faint green blush. It keeps the bolt warm. The bolt keeps it anchored. They have grown together.

And it eats sound.

This is the part you have to feel with your teeth.

It has no ears. Ears are for air that changes. It has resonance. Its entire lower face, the side pressed against the grating and the wall, is covered in microscopic cilia, stiff as fiberglass, tuned to the exact brown-white roar of the three blowers bolted without isolation mounts. 115 decibels, perfectly stable, forever. Where you hear a solid wall of deafening, it feels a constant, perfect pressure differential, a wind that never gusts. The cilia vibrate in that wind without ever moving - they are held at resonance, like a harp string held at its harmonic. That vibration is its heartbeat, its clock, and its food. The mechanical energy is transduced, piezoelectrically, into chemical energy in its oil-blood. It does not need to move because the room moves for it. The Works above and below, cooling a process that cools itself, makes a permanent tide, and this thing is a barnacle on that tide.

That is why the room has to roar without changing. If the sound varied, the cilia would fatigue, slip out of tune, starve. Stability is not incidental to it. Stability *is* it.

Look now at the trays. The sparse rosettes sitting at hand-span intervals in their mineral wool and volcanic grit. You thought they were planted by tweezers. They were. By it.

The mat cannot sporulate into the air - the air is too dry, the updraft would just slam any spore into the heat exchangers and cook it. So it gardens. It extrudes those rosettes as detachable bodies, clones, each one a little packet of its own genome wrapped in a photosynthetic leaf, rimed with wax against desiccation. It pushes them up through the grating near its edge, one by one, months apart. They sit there under the lights, rootless, holding on. They do not spread because the mat does not let them. One per hand-span is optimal for light capture without shading each other. If one dies - freeze-dried upright, preserved perfectly because nothing rots - it remains as a windbreak for the next. The spilled tray on the north side was not tipped by vibration. It was tipped by growth. The mat underneath swelled, a millimeter a year, until the lip lifted and the grit fell in that perfect alluvial fan that will never be scattered because there is no wind *inside* to scatter it. Those grains are now part of its underside, being slowly incorporated.

It is very, very old, and very, very young. The colony you are looking at has been here since the room was sealed. A decade, two decades - the time since the weld spatter stayed sharp. It was not placed here. It seeded itself. A single airborne propagule, a speck of oil-wrapped code no larger than the diamond dust your breath becomes when it shatters, rode the updraft in through the intake louvers on a day when the outside sky was that same hard, dark blue. It landed on a bolt head, found warmth, vibration, ammonia, and decided this was outside enough.

It does not know you are there. Not the way an animal knows. You are a brief, warm, wet gust of carbon and water vapor, impossibly loud in a chemical way, shouting without making a sound in the roar. Where you stand, your boots block the updraft. The cilia under your soles go briefly still and hungry. If you kneel and put your bare hand - don't - to the grating, you would feel it not as cold but as *absence* of vibration, a dead spot in the field. It would feel you as a shadow passing over a solar panel.

If you stay long enough to leave bootprints in the spilled grit, it will use them. The depressions will collect a little more oil, a little more ammonia salt. In a month, a new rosette will appear at the toe of your print, grey-green and waxy. In a year, the print itself will be softened not by wind but by being *grown over*, incorporated as texture.

If you leave and dog the hatch, it will not have noticed you left. It will continue to hold its note with the blowers, to drink the cold that is not winter, to tend its sparse gardens under a sun that never moves. It does not need to do anything else. In a building that exists only to maintain the conditions for its own existence, it has achieved the same thing, but smaller, and flatter, and perfectly.

It will be there until the blowers stop.

The blowers will never stop.
