Nothing lives here.

Not in the way living is meant anywhere else.

If you came up expecting to find what lives on a mountain — the hawk, the lichen, the hardy goat, the small flower pressed flat to the rock — you will not find it. This high table was never seeded correctly for that kind of life. It is too bright and too cold at once, too thin, too loud, too wet in the wrong way. There is no soil to hold a root. No shelter that holds heat. No silence for anything to hide in. Anything that tried to stay would be scoured and wept over and sawn by wind until it gave up.

And so the first inhabitant you meet is absence.

It is not an empty absence. It is a crowded, maintained absence. The rock remembers that nothing has lived here, and it has arranged itself around that fact.

What *occupies* the plateau instead are processes that have learned to act like inhabitants.

The first of them is the water.

It does not live, but it behaves as if it does. It is born continuously out of the air itself — you can see it bead on the windward edge of every fin and pillar — and it dies continuously as it runs, and is born again. It never pools long enough to become a lake, never cuts deep enough to become a river. It lives shallow. A finger-deep in the basins, a glass-sheet over the lips between them, a sweat on the pale limestone. It moves all the time, and the sound of its moving is the sound of breathing. A low trickle and drip from a thousand throats at once. If you sit still you realize you are listening to a kind of census: every basin has a voice, every weeping pillar a mouth. The mineral skin that forms where water stands longest — the rust-orange and bone-white and sulfur-yellow crust that cracks into polygons — is not alive either, but it does what living things do: it holds the water, softens under it to a jelly, quivers when you step near as if disturbed. It is the plateau's way of pretending to have skin, without having flesh underneath.

The second inhabitant is the wind.

It lives here the way a landlord lives in a building — it owns every room because it has cut every doorway. Coming off the western rim with a hundred kilometers of nothing to slow it, it hits the dense field of stone and is divided. It is moaning through the hollow pillars at the base, humming across the basins, vibrating the thin fins of chert until they sing. The plateau is its instrument and it never stops playing. It is loud enough that you have to shout. It makes the place uninhabitable for anything with ears that needs to hear itself think, and in doing so, it keeps the plateau for itself. The wind is the only thing that moves with purpose here, and its purpose is only to keep moving.

The third inhabitant is the light.

At this height it does not fall so much as press. It is too white, too present, a weight on your shoulders and the backs of your hands even while your breath smokes. It pools. On the plateau it has learned to behave like a liquid heavier than water — collecting in the basins underneath the water, not illuminating, just *occupying*, as if light here has mass. At evening it stretches every shadow three times longer than geometry allows, and where those shadows cross they do not darken properly, as if light refuses to be excluded. It is an inhabitant because it stays, it insists, it doubles. If you clap, the echo comes back late and from the wrong direction and already doubled, out of phase with itself; if you look east to the lowlands, you will see them below you and, faintly, above you at the same time, a second horizon hung in the air without a mirror to make it. Gravity hesitates here. Water crawls uphill a hand's breadth before remembering to fall. Distance flexes. The rules that should hold a place together are here, but they are crowded too, like the pillars — shoulder to shoulder, all slightly out of true, all worn until their impossibilities fit together.

These three — water, wind, light — are what live here permanently. They maintain the steady state. They weep and saw and press and the stone endures them.

There were people, too. But they did not live here.

You can see that in how they built. Low. Three courses of dry-stacked flat stone, no higher than a sleeping body. A cairn placed with care at a narrow passage, capped with a disc of obsidian that still throws the hard light back at the sky. A fire ring where the wind is less, its ash long since washed into the mineral jelly, the surrounding stones still dark. Flakes of chert where someone sat and struck a tool, edges now dulled by centuries of rain.

No roof. No dam. No attempt to divert the weeping sheets of water or block the sawing wind. The structures let the water run through them. Some are already half-become pillars again, crusted over with the same precipitated salts that skin the basins. Whoever made them understood the etiquette of the plateau: you do not build up in a place where even light and gravity are crowded and loud. You make a small mark, low to the ground, open to the sky, and you let the plateau carry it.

They came up the two-hour switchback cut into rain-dark stone, they stayed a night or a few nights because the place required it of them — to listen, or to mark a passage, or to leave a body — and then they went back down to where air is thick enough to live in. No one tried to stay the winter. You can feel why. To try to live here would be to try to compete with the water for wetness, with the wind for loudness, with the light for presence. You would lose.

And if you stayed long enough to truly *live* here, you would have to become like the wall: low, still, porous, letting the water run through and around you, letting the mineral skin grow over you until you were not separate from the pillars anymore. The plateau does not kill quickly. It incorporates.

So when you stand among the dense, weeping stones at evening and the whole field glows — each basin holding a hard white sky, each pillar throwing its impossible shadow, the chorus of drips and whispers rising as the air thins and cools — you understand what lives here is not a creature. It is a maintenance. A habit the world has formed at this height.

The plateau lives here. It lives by weeping and humming and pooling light and doubling sound and flexing distance, and it has been doing it long enough that its impossibilities have weathered smooth.

You are only a visitor learning, for an hour, how to be low and small enough not to interrupt it.
