No one lives here the way a person lives in a house. No one lives here the way an animal lives in a swamp. What lives here lives the way water lives in a pot.

At first you think nothing could.

That is the trick of the scale. You step down into the dish and the air hits you — cool, mineral, too thin with light — and the stone gives you back a perfect, empty circle under a low sky that isn't sky. You hear the percussion of water and the hammering of frogs and you think: this is too clean. Too made. Too small. The floor is one floor. The walls are one wall. You can see every stone. There is no cover, no burrow, no deep. How could anything hide here? How could anything be apart enough from anything else to be alive?

And then your eyes adjust to the dim, and you stop looking for something large.

What lives here lives thin and flat and spread out.

It lives as a film before it lives as a body.

The first resident is not an animal at all. It is the green-black felt that lives in the joints. Moss and lichen and algae and bacteria so intergrown you can't tell where one ends and the other begins. It holds the whole room together. It softens the hammer-pecked basalt, it drinks the weep directly from the air, it glows faintly at dusk with that foxfire pallor. It is the pasture. On the north wall it grows thick as felt, on the south wall it is only a stain. It tastes the stone and makes it into sugar, and everything else here eats, directly or indirectly, the stone made edible by that film.

On that film graze the snails. Dozens, hundreds — you didn't see them at first because their shells are the same white as the calcite beards hanging from the joints. Little mineral whorls, the exact color of the water's leavings. They move so slowly they seem part of the wall, a tide of white dots over black-green, rasping. They never leave the wet. If a shell dries it dies.

Below them, in the water itself, is the second layer.

The water here is never more than a hand deep except in the sump, but it is crowded as a sea. Because the floor is not one puddle but a hundred braided runnels, each runnel is a river. Each river has the same inhabitants, repeated like a pattern.

The tadpoles — black, comma-shaped, in such numbers the water looks stippled — keep the runnels clean, eating the film that sloughs off the stone. The transparent shrimp, almost invisible until they flick, eat the tadpole droppings. The newts, slate-colored and broad-headed, as long as your hand and as flat as the flags they lie under, eat the shrimp and the tadpoles both. They move like oil over stone, you only see them when they have already seen you and stopped. And the loach — small, brown, with whiskers and fins that are more like feet — does what nothing else does: it leaves. When a runnel dries for an hour at noon, it walks. It flips and drags itself across damp stone from one water-film to the next, mouth open to the wet. It remembers where water was.

None of them belong in water this shallow, this exposed. In any other place they'd be eaten in a day or dried out by noon. Here they have learned the rule of the room: water never really leaves. It clings. It runs sideways and even uphill along the camber of the flags for a thumb's length before gravity remembers it. It beads and hangs under overhangs for hours before falling. In the central sump — that perfect, mortared navel — you can watch a droplet detach from the surface and rise, wobbling, a hand's breadth into the air before it pops and joins the low mist-ceiling. The animals don't fight this. The newts lay their eggs on the underside of stones where water clings longest. The tadpoles cluster where the film is thickest, not where it is deepest.

Above the water, on each wet flag, sits a frog.

This is who you hear before you see. This is who owns the place, if anyone does.

They are not large. Ochre with a green stripe, or green with ochre mottling, throat sacs like polished amber. Each one sits alone in the middle of its territory — one flagstone, one puddle, one stretch of runnel — far from the next, because the sound needs distance to work. They call and the oval wall throws the call back and the mist-ceiling throws it down again until you cannot tell direction. It is not a chorus, it is a dense, overlapping argument. One inflates, calls, deflates. Another answers from three stones away, but the echo makes it sound like it answered from inside your own chest. They call through the constant percussion of falling drops, they call through the papery rustle of wings, they don't stop. On their backs water beads and then slides off sideways, not down, and they sit with eyes half-closed as if in prayer.

They are the metronome. Everything else is timed to them. When they go quiet — which they rarely do — you hear how loud the water really is.

Above the frogs is the air. 

The air is full of things too small to see until they are caught in a shaft of that diffused, frosted-glass light. Midges and pale moths that hatch from the sump silt and live their entire lives without ever leaving the room, spiraling up into the mist-ceiling where they die and fall like dust. And because there are midges, there are the swifts and the swallows.

They are the only things here that remember an outside. Small, soot-dark swifts with sickle wings and barn swallows with forked tails, hundreds of them. They have made this cistern into a sky. They nested in the chisel-holes left by the builders — every pock-mark that once held a wedge now holds a cup of mud and grass. You can see the mud daubed into the tool scars. They cut the dim air all day in total silence except for the dry rustle of their wings, like shuffling paper, eating the midges. At dusk they vanish into the walls all at once, as if the stones inhaled them. At dawn they explode out again. They never hit the water. They drink by skimming the sump in a single, impossible low pass.

And then, too large for all of this, is the heron.

It doesn't live here. Not really. It visits. It has to duck to get over the low lip, and once inside it looks absurd, a grey-blue stilt-bird standing in a basin that barely covers its toes. But it has learned the room. It stands motionless for an hour in the sump among the three clumps of reeds — the only vertical things on the floor — and the frogs go quiet around it in a spreading circle. Then it strikes, and the splash is obscene in such shallow water, a dropped pot, and it lifts a newt or a loach or a frog and swallows it whole. Then it stands again. If you stay long enough you will see it fold itself and heave itself back over the rim and become a heron again, elsewhere. While it is here, it is a god. When it leaves, the frogs start again as if nothing happened, and fill the space back up with noise.

In the eastern corner, the fig lives alone. One tree, stunted, dusty-leaved, brilliantly green against all that stone-grey. Its roots are not in soil but in the wall, white fingers pried flat between courses, drinking the weep. It fruits, sometimes — tiny, hard figs that the swallows don't eat and the frogs don't eat and that finally fall into the water and rot and feed the tadpoles. It is the only thing here that tries to be tall.

That is the household. Film and snail and shrimp and tadpole and newt and loach and frog and midge and swift and swallow and heron and fig and reed and lichen. No one species is numerous in the way a field is numerous. There's too much space per body. You could lie down and not touch anything alive. But put it all together and the room is loud, frantic, constantly eating and being eaten within arm's reach of where you sit on the worn, trough-worn steps.

And what they all have in common is this: they have learned to live in a place that was built to hold water, not to hold life, and then became both. They don't burrow. They don't hide. They cling, like the water does. They make their homes in tool-marks and runnels and mortar joints, in the exact places where human hands shaped the stone and then left it alone for a thousand years of dripping.

If you sit long enough, until the light thins to that last grey and the mist-ceiling begins to glow and the swifts slot themselves back into the wall, you will feel it — that nothing here enters and nothing here leaves except water, and even the water doesn't leave. It rises, hangs, falls, runs, rests, and rises again. And the things that live here have shaped themselves to that circle so precisely that they have become part of it. They are not inhabitants of the valley-room.

They are how the room keeps itself wet.
