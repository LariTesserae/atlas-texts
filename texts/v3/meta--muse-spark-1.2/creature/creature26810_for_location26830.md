Whatever lives here has learned how not to stand up.

That is the first law of this country. You cannot be tall here. Tall catches sun and wind and dries. Tall needs deep roots and there is no deep to root in — only a hand's breadth of sour peat over iron and bone-stone. Tall needs to fight gravity and this place finished fighting gravity a hundred million years ago. So whatever lives here lives low, or lives wide, or lives thin.

At first you think that means nothing large lives here at all.

If you only glance, that is what you see. The things that declare themselves immediately are small and many and close to the surface film. They are the true citizens. The water itself seems to be alive before anything swims in it.

It lives as vibration. A cloud of midges so dense it bends light, rising and falling over each sedge clump in a single breathing column. The copper dragonflies that hunt through it, each one a hard jewel of chitin and wire, hovering, snapping, perching on a rush stem to flex their wings open and closed. Water-boatmen and backswimmers rowing upside-down under the meniscus, leaving brief, expanding Vs. The frogs — you hear them before you ever separate one from mud — small brown bark-frogs the size of your thumb joint with throats like pale balloons, and the larger green ones sunk to their chins in the warm pan, eyes only, calling in that deep jug-note that makes the water in front of their mouths tremble.

They are not shy. They are the sound of the place, and sound is how you live when you cannot hide behind trees. 

If you stay still long enough for the midges to decide you are another warm stone, larger shapes resolve. They do so slowly, because they move slowly, and because they are the color of waiting.

In the clearer pans, where the silt has settled between two sedge islands, the fish appear. Not as individuals at first — as a ripple of light. A school of silver minnows, each no longer than a finger, turning all at once so their sides catch the white sun. Beneath them, holding position in the tea-colored shadow of a submerged root mat, something that is not moving at all. It is mottled olive and rust-red and black, marbled exactly like the peat and the iron clay, with fleshy, limb-like fins and a broad, flattened head. A lungfish. It does not swim so much as lean forward. It can take air when the water gets too warm to hold oxygen, rising to gulp with a soft, wet pop that you hear across the whole pan. It can bury itself in the mud when the sheet thins in the driest weeks and breathe through a cocoon of its own mucus for months. It has been doing this since before the stone domes were domes. It watches with small, lidless eyes and does not blink.

Higher, on stilt legs, the waders live by the same principle made vertical without breaking the rule. The white ibis and the grey heron and the rose-pink spoonbill are all leg and neck and beak, everything else reduced to keep their bodies out of the sun's reflection while their feet stay in the cool. They stand for an hour without shifting weight, their shadows nailed to the silt. When they strike it is not fast — it is *inevitable*, a slow collapse of the neck that was already aimed. They swallow frogs and lungfish fry and the fat, striped snails that draw bright lines through the mud. They call rarely, but when they do the cry is metallic and carries for a mile through the damp air, and another answers from a pan you can't see, and the distance between them is exact.

At the foot of each weeping dome there are the other old ones. Turtles, broad as serving plates, their carapaces encrusted with the same pale, crusty lichen that grows on the granite itself, and with a thin film of green algae along the seams so that when they tuck in they look like a flake of stone that decided to lie in the shallows. They bask with their necks extended, not moving, letting dragonflies land on their noses. They are not in a hurry to get anywhere because there is nowhere to get to. Their lives are measured in the steam that rises off the stone after drizzle.

But none of these are the animal that *belongs* to this flatness most completely. They visit it. They use it.

There is one that *is* it.

You will not find it until you have sat on the back of a dome for a long time, with your ankles in the seep, and let your eyes learn that not every dome is stone.

It is low. Wider than it is long by almost half, and long by almost the length of a man if a man were to lie down in the pan. Its back is not a shell and not a hide but something between — thick, pebbled skin the color of wet bone and rust, broken by shallow fissures that hold water exactly the way the micro-seams in the granite hold water. In the fissures grows a faint green-brown film, algae and lichen, so that its back weeps too, in sheets no thicker than glass. When it rests half-submerged at the edge of a pan, with its broad snout just breaking the surface and its eyes like two dark, wet pebbles set far apart, you take it for another whale-backed dome. That is its life.

Call it the pan-mother, the dome-salamander. It is not a crocodile — crocodiles need depth to roll — and not a turtle, though it borrows from both. It is an amphibian in the oldest sense: a thing that remembers both water and stone. Its limbs are short, splayed, and massive, more like the buttress roots of the cork-bark trees than like legs, each foot a broad spade that spreads its weight so it never sinks beyond the peat skin. It walks, when it walks, with a slow, alternating heave, belly dragging, leaving a shallow, paired trough in the mud that fills with clear water instantly and persists for days. But it seldom walks. It waits.

It breathes the air like the lungfish does, through lungs and through skin. Its skin never dries because it never leaves the seep. It keeps one flank against the stone's sweat, or settled in a pan where the water is only a few inches deep and as warm as blood. You can see its throat pulse, once, twice a minute. It hears through the water and through the stone, through the vibration of the cicada whine that shivers its back. When a frog swims too close, when a wading bird steps within range, when a lungfish glides overhead — it does not lunge. It opens. The head is absurdly wide, a flat shovel hinged at the back, and the interior is pale. The water rushes in. Then it closes, swallows, and resumes being stone. The whole event takes two seconds and then an hour of stillness to digest.

It does not call like the frogs. At night, when the white sun is gone and the haze holds the day's heat close, it sings low, below the crickets, a single, sustained note you feel more in the soles of your feet than in your ears — a hum conducted through saturated ground from one dome to the next. Another answers, a mile away, then another. They are counting each other.

For breeding it does not need much. In the warmest weeks it scrapes a shallow nest into the black peat between sedge clumps, a circle of cleared mud where the water leans a little deeper, and lays a sheet of eggs like translucent beads, each flecked with iron-red. The male, or sometimes the female — they are barely dimorphic — lies over them, not incubating so much as shading and sweating stone-water over them from its back. The tadpoles, when they hatch, are already flat, already mottled, already expert at holding still. They hang in the algae and look like dead leaves until they are big enough to look like small stones.

It grows with the same patience as the domes. A millimeter of thickened skin a year. A new fissure every wet season. Individuals live for longer than any tree on this plain, for longer than the memory of any bird. When one dies — which is rarely, from age or from a dry year that cracks even the peat — it does not vanish. It settles, flattens further, and its skin sloughs into the organic rot, its broad bones sink and become part of the thin soil over the shield. The pan it kept open remains a little deeper, a little clearer, for years, holding water longer into the dry. In that way the land remembers where its animals lay.

If you place your hand on its back, if you are very still and very respectful and it allows it, you feel what you feel on the stone: not cold, not hot, but exactly the temperature of the world, damp, faintly humming with the insects and the frogs and the slow lean of water toward a sea you will never see. It does not flinch. It has not learned to be afraid of anything that sits still.
