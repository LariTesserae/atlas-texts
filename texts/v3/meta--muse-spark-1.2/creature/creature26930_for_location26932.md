What lives here doesn't live *in* the city. It lives *with* the condition of wetness, the way a fish lives with water. You don't see it at first because there is too much of it to see.

At first, on that broad terrace where the water sheets three inches deep and reflects everything twice, you think the motion is only water. The shallows shiver. The air shimmers with wings. The vertical faces crawl. It takes a few seconds for your eye to subtract the rain and the runoff and understand that most of the motion is bodies, and most of the bodies are trying to be in the same place at the same time.

Then they resolve.

### The People

They are low, as the place is low.

Not short, exactly - they can be tall when they stretch - but low in their center of gravity, in the way they hold themselves. Broad-chested, broad-footed. No one here is built like a reed. The air is heavy and wet and you need a bellows to move it; their ribs are deep, their necks thick. You hear it before you see it, the audible work of breathing in that warm weight, a slight whistle on the inhale that everyone has and no one notices.

They are wet the way stones are wet, which is to say perpetually. They don't try to stay dry. Dryness is a pathology here. A dry person is a sick person, a dusty person, an off-worlder. Skin here never quite dries. It has the sheen of something amphibious, even on the most human of them. In the children it's almost translucent, with a subcutaneous gloss; in the old it's thicker, pebbled, stained at the knees and elbows the color of the biofilm — olive-black, rust-red — because they kneel and wade and sit in the flow so often it has dyed them. Most have very little hair. Hair holds water wrong, stays cold. What they have is cropped to stubble or allowed to mat into felted, water-shedding caps. Eyelashes are long. Many have a second lid, not quite a nictitating membrane, just a pearlescent film that draws across when the hard rain hammers and lets them keep looking while everyone else would be squinting.

Their feet tell you everything. Even those who wear shoes wear shoes that are open, sandals that are more like frames, soles so soft and wide they spread like a duck's. The toes are splayed, the arches flattened from a lifetime of wading. The webbing is not dramatic - no fins - just a little more skin between the first and second joints than you were expecting, enough that when they kick through a flooded plaza they make very little splash. Their hands are the same, clever and webbed, with nails kept brutally short so the film that grows on everything doesn't grow under them.

You can't pick out a uniform. Covering here isn't for warmth or modesty, it's for abrasion and for carrying things. They wear single layers of composite cloth that doesn't absorb, that beads water the way a lotus leaf does, colors gone muted from mineral stain. Pockets everywhere, because you are always carrying something above the waterline. A mother wades by with an infant strapped high on her chest in a sling that keeps its face inches above the sheet-flood, the baby's legs kicking in the warm water, already learning to swim before it learns to walk. An old man sits on a ledge that is also a gutter, his trousers rolled to nothing, letting the current that has decided to run *uphill* for its own reason curl around his ankles. He doesn't correct it.

They don't shout to be heard over the roar, though at first you think they do. They lean. Their language has gone tactile and visual to survive the static. They speak close, jaw to jaw, hand on forearm so the words conduct through bone. When they must speak at distance, they sign - a fast, flicking hand language that has absorbed bird-flock logic, whole sentences made in a single folding gesture - or they flash. Many of them cultivate small colonies of light on their shoulders, wrists, collarbones: engineered yeast that glows a soft amber or blue when fed with skin oil, blinking in patterns. A bargain being struck across a noisy canal is two people blinking at each other in quick amber bursts, hands moving, heads nodding, while the water roars between them. Children learn to make rude light-patterns before they can spell.

They are crowded and they like it that way. No one here has a concept of personal space larger than the puddle they stand in. They bump, they jostle, they share walls because walls are shared. A family will occupy a room that in the morning was a thoroughfare and by evening has grown a wall around them, extruded by the matter-looms overhead in a slow amber scaffold. They don't own the space so much as *borrow* its inclination for a while. They build and unbuild with the same casualness that you might rearrange furniture, because the city heals and flows and they'll get another wall tomorrow. Their belongings are few, sealed, buoyant.

They eat what the place eats. You never see a field. You see them crouched at the edge of a condensation pipe where the white biofilm blooms thickest, scraping it with flat ceramic tools into broth. You see them netting the silver spiraling fry by the thousand in the three-inch plazas, flash-frying them whole on hot stone. You see them cultivating yeasts in shallow trays on every balcony, the trays themselves weeping water that is itself food. Protein is ambient here, falling like pollen - skin, chaff, insect husk, the flecks the cleaners leave behind. They filter it, they culture it, they make it into cakes that steam faintly brine and ozone. No one hunts big things because there are no big things to hunt that aren't also people.

They are not miserable. That's the mistake you make at first, thinking anyone living in a deafening, dripping bowl must be enduring it. They are *tuned*. On the days when - rarely - the rain pauses for an hour and the air tries to clear, they become restless, itchy, over-illuminated. They miss the percussion. They miss the bead of water forming unaided on their forearms. They will stand very still and wait for the next cloud the city exhales for them, and sigh when it finally drops.

### The Swarm That Is Not People But Lives Like People

Around them, *through* them, the rest of the saturation moves and it is not incidental fauna. It is the other citizenry.

The small silver things in the shallows are not ornament. They are pigeons are in other cities. Underfoot, innumerable. A plaza drains and you think it empty and then you see ten thousand fry holding station in the remaining film, each the length of your fingernail, turning as one, living off the biofilm and the city-fat. When you walk, they part and re-form around your ankles.

The air above head-height is seldom empty air. It is infrastructure for the white-and-grey hand-birds that have learned to ride the warm updrafts between towers. They nest in the scaffolds of light, in the rain gutters, in the armpits of bridges, and they move as a single sheet that can decide to fold itself into an alley and become, for thirty seconds, a weather event you have to duck. They scream not to communicate but to be heard at all, and the people have learned to leave intervals for them, the way you leave intervals for thunder.

Lower, the smoke is not smoke. It is the hard-shelled hummers — beetle-things with wings too small, kept aloft by furious beating — that swarm every vent where warm air is exhaled. They are eaten, constantly, by the lizard-runners on the walls. The wall-runners — two long back legs, two short front arms, skin the exact warm basalt color of the composite — never stop. They run in spirals up and down the canyon-streets, tongue-flicking, eating film, eating hummers, eating each other if they can. Behind them come the cleaners, soft, many-legged, blind, the size of your hand or the size of a dog, with frilly gill-plumes they trail in the wet air. They don't eat the city. They *groom* it. They lick the mineral sweat off retaining walls, they polish metal until it beads again, they comb the biofilm and leave a matte, clean stripe that will be re-stained by morning. People step over them without looking, and sometimes absentmindedly feed them scraps.

None of this is wild. It is domestic in the way rats and pigeons and cockroaches are domestic, which is to say co-evolved. The food chain here starts not with sun but with condensation. Light is scattered and everywhere; no plant needs to compete for it. So the base is the film, and the yeast, and the falling skin of so many crowded bodies, and everything ladders up from there.

### The City Itself

And then there is the thing that lives here that is the city.

You feel it when you put your palm on a warm, yielding wall and the wall very, very slightly pushes back, not as resistance but as recognition. The composite — poured and grown and printed at once — is not dead. It is not conscious the way a person is, but it is *alive* the way a reef is alive. It heals. It sweats. It grows scaffolds of light and organizes dust into new balconies because somewhere the water pressure told it to. The field that holds it together thrums in your molars. The people talk to it without talking, by damming a flow here, scraping film there, weighting a terrace. They are its cleaners as much as the many-legged things are.

That is why the water sometimes runs uphill for a few feet before remembering itself, and why light sometimes catches on nothing and throws a shadow anyway. The field has a memory of a wall that was there yesterday, or will be there tomorrow. The puddle is deeper than it should be because the space inside the puddle hasn't decided how big it is yet. The children know this. They have a game where they drop a smooth basalt bead into a puddle that is only an inch deep and watch it fall for much longer than an inch, then fish it out from water that is somehow cold.

No one finds this alarming. When you live in a place that rebuilds itself while you sleep, that weeps water out of solid stone, that is so crowded the air itself is crowded, you develop a tolerance for slight impossibilities. You just make sure your footing is good.

If you stand long enough on that low terrace, the people and the birds and the runners and the cleaners and the city will all flow around you as if you were another stone in the basin, another warm basalt shape for water to bead on. A runner will use your shoe as a bridge. A cleaner will try to polish your ankle. A child will take your hand to show you how to cup water that appeared from the air, and how to drink it before the next hard rain drums down again on ten million roofs, and vanishes into stone that is already weeping it back up.
