Whatever lives here doesn't live here the way an animal lives in a woods. It lives here the way sweat lives on skin.

At first you think nothing does. Or nothing singular. That's the camouflage.

You step down into the interval and your eye says *empty* — too bright, too honest, concrete and cinderblock and a stunted tree. Then you stay still for thirty seconds longer than a person usually stays still in a place that isn't meant to be stayed in, and the whole place starts to move. Not one thing. All of it. The floor shivers.

It starts low.

On the slab itself is the first resident, but it's not one animal, it's a country. A living carpet that pretends to be texture until you shift your weight. Pill isopods the color of wet concrete, each the size of a thumbnail, shoulder to shoulder with smaller, faster black beetles with oil-slick backs, and under and between them a salt of springtails that jump like static when you breathe on them. They keep to the damp. If you watch, you can see the tide line — where the condensation drip has made its dark stain and pothole, the density triples. They don't wander. They farm. They graze the invisible film of algae and industrial yeast that grows where water never quite dries, and they turn the pallet — that black, collapsed sponge of wood at the south end — into soil a millimeter at a time. Lift a corner of it and the underside is white with mycelium and writhing grey. Lay it back down and in five minutes the carpet has re-formed, seamless, as if you never looked. They are deaf, industrious, and they never leave the floor. The floor is their sky.

Then your eye climbs, because the air is warm and the heat draws you up.

The walls are the second resident. From a distance they look dirty. Up close you see the dirt is architecture.

Sheet after sheet of web, not silver but grey-black, overlapping like cheap insulation, like shingles. Each sheet is a little funnel with a narrow throat disappearing into a crack in the cinderblock or the corrugated seam of the dye works wall. The spiders who made them are small, the color of concrete dust, and they do not clean. Wild spiders keep their webs clean. These do not. They let soot and cotton fluff and iron oxide settle until the silk is felted, opaque. It isn't a trap anymore, or not only a trap. It's a shade, a filter, a lung. In the even, doubled light of the pocket, a clean web would be invisible. These are meant to be seen, to cut the glare. At noon when that hard white rectangle crawls across the floor, every spider retreats deeper into its funnel at once, a single flinch. At dusk, when the caged bulb over the welded door clicks on and buzzes, they come forward.

Between and through the webs are the highways.

The ants. You would expect a line. What you get instead is a mine. They have taken the failing pipe insulation — that yellow-pink fiberglass wrapped in cracked plastic along the dye-works wall — and hollowed it into a city. They go in one split and come out another three feet down, carrying nothing a forest ant would carry. No crumbs, no leaf bits. They carry flakes. Rust flakes like tiny, curled potato chips, bright orange, and nuggets of verdigris, that blue-green copper rot from the pipe fittings, and once in a while a perfect bead of condensed water held delicately in their mandibles without breaking surface tension. They store the mineral in chambers. If you tear the insulation open — and the rat has, in one place — you see granaries of sorted color: a pile of orange, a pile of green, a pile of translucent plastic shavings that must have blown over from the bottling plant. They are not food. They are building material, or currency, or offering. No one knows. They work in the heat without hurrying, protected by the constant warmth. In winter, when the whole city freezes, this wall will still be warm to the touch. They will still be working.

The third resident lives in the drain.

You have to crouch to see her properly, because she hates the direct light. The square storm grate overhead dumps its sudden rain, the slab floods, and everything runs for the walls — then the water vanishes down that one choked hole, sucking bottle caps and cotton fluff into a throat that never clears. Just at the lip, where it's always glossy, there's a trembling nap, like ash, like fur. Moth flies. Hundreds of them, each no bigger than a sesame seed, grey and fuzzy with wings held like little tents. They rise in a single soft puff if you exhale on them and settle again immediately, as if they are not flies at all but part of the drain's breath. Their larvae live below, in the black slime you can't see, feeding on whatever the two factories lose — starch, dye, sugar water. They are the pocket's plankton. Everything else eats them in turn. The wasps especially.

The wasps are the only ones who think the pocket is large.

They have built in the abandoned electrical disconnect box, whose metal door hangs open on one hinge. The nest inside is the size of a melon, but wrong. Paper wasps should make paper the color of dry wood. This one is marbled. The workers have chewed the soft fibers of the disintegrating pallet and mixed it with polyethylene shavings and a little rust dust, so the paper has veins of turquoise — that same bright turquoise as the bottling plant's crates — and streaks of white and a faint orange blush. It is beautiful, in the way industrial waste is beautiful when it is made with care. They work on it constantly, that faint ticking you hear. They hunt the moth flies in short, efficient sorties, never going above the wall line, never leaving. Why would they? In here there are no birds to pick them off, no wind to tear the nest. The disconnect box still hums. If you put your hand close you can feel the 60-cycle field raising the hair on your arm. The wasps like it. On cold mornings they cluster on the warm side of the box and vibrate.

Over it all, hardly living at all by animal standards and yet the most stubborn, is the plant life — but it doesn't behave like plant life.

The ailanthus at the north end is not a tree. It's a gesture toward a tree, pruned by starvation into a bonsai that has learned to be concrete. Wrist-thick, six leaves, each leaf coated in a fine grit of soot that it never sheds. It doesn't cast shade. It casts a line. Its roots have found the expansion joint and wedged it wider, hunting the drip. Along its bark, and along the base of both walls, there is a fleshy weed with translucent stems — you can see water moving in them like in a straw — that has no name you know. It doesn't root in soil. It roots in condensation. It drinks the air. And on the cinderblock, in corners that never quite dry, the black mold that was just a stain from far away is, up close, not a stain at all. It has structure. Tiny branching antlers, a millimeter high, that lean. In the morning they lean away from the sun rectangle. At night they lean toward the caged bulb, reaching for that buzzing, dirty light, and by dawn they have pulled back again, as if shy. Water beads on the underside of the cold pipe and on the lip of the grate do not fall when they should. They hang, swollen, for hours, trembling but holding, until a footstep or a compressor kicking on shakes them loose. The mold reaches for them while they hang.

If there is a single *who* here, it is not any one of these. It is the agreement between them.

The pocket is too small for proper ecology. Instead it has made a compact. The drip feeds the mold and the translucent weed and the isopods; the isopods and moth flies feed the beetles and wasps and spiders; the spiders' soot-webs shade the mold; the ants mine the rust that the water frees from the rebar; the wasps build with what the ants discard. The heat from both walls keeps the cycle turning faster than it should — a whole succession from flood to feast to repair in an afternoon, within arm's reach. Nothing leaves. Nothing needs to leave. The rat that watches you from behind the pipe is not a resident the way the others are. He is a visitor who has learned to be still, to drink from the pothole and not disturb the carpet. The pigeons on the roofline peer down and never drop in, because there is not enough sky for them here.

You become part of it while you stand there, whether you want to or not. The air is so damp that sweat gathers on you without exertion — along your hairline, on your forearms — and you realize you have become another sweating pipe, another cold surface for water to bead on. A beetle will cross the toe of your shoe without altering course, because to it you are just a new, warm, vertical wall that has briefly appeared in its world. A spider may test a line to your sleeve. The pocket does not reject you. It incorporates you, damps you, listens with its whole surface to the new temperature you've brought.

Then the white rectangle slides off the slab and up the cinderblock, thin as paper. The caged bulb over the welded door clicks on early, amber under cloud, buzzing louder now. The ants shift their carrying to the other side of the pipe. The wasps tick faster. A drop forms at the pipe's seam, swells to the size of a pea, holds impossibly long, and falls at last into its perfect, patient pothole with a tap you feel through your shoes.

And everything that lives here — which is everything here, except the concrete itself — tightens just slightly, and waits for the next one.
