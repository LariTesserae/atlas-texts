They don't live here the way something lives in a forest or a cave.

They live here the way water lives in a sponge — not everywhere at once, but filling every place that will hold them, and empty everywhere else.

If you stand still long enough in the dim, you start to understand the rule they all obey: nothing here can afford to be alone, and nothing here can afford to be everywhere.

### What holds them together

The first thing you notice, before you can pick out any one animal, is that biomass in this rift does not spread thin. It clumps.

On a hundred meters of clean, sharp basalt you will find nothing but lichen and the tick of water. Then you turn the corner of a tilted block and the stone ends and the animals begin, edge to edge, with no transition. This is not a population. It is a *concentration*.

They are all pale. Not white — the color of wet stone, of mushroom flesh, of wax that has never seen sun. Grey-pink, translucent at the edges, dark only where the gut shows through. They are all damp. Even the ones that look dry bead with condensation if you watch them. In this cool equilibrium, drying out is death, and so every body is a small wet surface, keeping itself close to a seep, a drip, a film of water in a fissure.

And they are all, to varying degrees, blind. Eyes, where they exist, are flat, cloudy, lidded over — light sensors that can tell dim from dimmer and nothing more. In a place with no hard shadows and no direct sun, vision never paid for itself. They navigate by other means.

What lives here is not one species, but a guild that found the same answer at the same time, after the rift cracked open young. Nothing here evolved *for* this place over millions of years. Everything here was already almost ready for it — cool-loving, moisture-loving, stone-loving things from the surface fringe and the shallow underground — that fell or walked or were blown down the fault wall and discovered that the wound suited them better than the world that made them. They have been here a few thousand years, long enough to bleach and thin, not long enough to become something entirely new.

### The floor people

Down low, where the seep-pans and the spongy shattered rock hold water like a cloth, the ground is theirs.

The crickets first — though calling them crickets is a habit of your surface mind. They are orthopterans without song in the way you think of song, flightless, as long as your hand from head to the tip of their antennae, then twice that again if you count the antennae themselves. Their hind legs are not built for great leaps; the sharp, broken footing would snap them. Instead they walk, deliberate, on legs so pale and thin you can see the muscle cord inside like a thread. Their backs are vaulted and beaded with water that never quite rolls off. Their antennae are always moving, tasting the air for humidity. When they gather — and they gather by the hundreds around a single algal pan — they pack flank to flank and work in silence, except for the high, glassy stridulation that comes not from one, but from the whole patch at once. One starts, the next answers, and a wave of chirring rolls down the basin and back as an echo a few seconds later.

Among them, and under them, are the salamanders. Neotenic, blind, slick as wet latex, the size of your forearm, with frilled external gills that wave faintly even when the animal is still. They are the most numerous vertebrate here by far, thousands upon thousands, but you could walk for ten minutes and see none, then find a fissure pool where they lie layered two and three deep, breathing together. You hear them before you see them — a soft, wet lapping when a hundred bodies shift at once to keep their skin in contact with water. They do not croak or call. They exhale through their skin and listen through it.

With them are the beetles — smaller, harder, black as the iron in the shale. They move like scree themselves, a mat that clicks and flows. They are the cleaners. Where droppings whiten the rock below the bird cliffs, the beetles come in a tide and reduce it to mineral crust again within days. Nothing accumulates here.

All of these floor animals share a tempo: slow, cold-blooded, low-metabolism. In this constant cool they do not need to eat much. A mouthful of algal mat, a scrape of lichen, a grub pried from a crack will do for a day. They do not store food. They do not defend territory, because there is no point defending stone that does not change. They simply aggregate where the water is best that week, disperse when it thins, re-aggregate a few meters away when a new seep darkens a wall. Their populations do not boom and crash. The rift holds as many as it can hold, and has held that number since the first century after it opened. Young are few, slow to mature, rarely eaten. Death is mostly slow attrition — an animal that wanders too far from damp and dries where it stands, legs folded neatly beneath it.

### The wall people

Up high, where your neck aches to look, is the other nation. 

They cling to the sharpness. The fault wall is still knapped and flaking, a cliff of fresh teeth and ledges that has never been weathered round. To you it looks hostile. To them it is perfect architecture — a thousand shallow nests that no predator can reach without cutting its feet.

They look like swifts or swallows at first, until you see them up close on a ledge. Smaller, paler, the color of limestone dust. No eyes — just smooth feathered skin where eyes should be. Wings short and rounded, not for long flight; they do not need to migrate because there are no seasons. They fly the way bats fly — quick, fluttering, echolocating with a dry rattle and click that the hard rift walls throw back and forth until the whole valley seems to chatter with them. By day — if you can call this dim a day — they huddle by the thousands on overhangs, bodies pressed together for warmth, guano varnishing the rock beneath them white and, in turn, feeding the beetles and the lichen below. At intervals a few hundred will peel off at once and sift through the dim, hawking the midges and pale flies that breed over the pools, never going far, never rising high enough to find the slot of sky.

Their young are naked, translucent, deaf and blind even beyond the adult condition, and they are fed not with worms from afar but with what the wall itself provides — grubs and mites levered from deep cracks.

Which is where the tool use comes in.

### The hands they have

None of them build. There is nothing to build *with* that would last, and nothing to build *for*. The temperature never drops enough to need shelter beyond the lee of a block. The light never gets bright enough to need shade. The pools never flood.

What they do is pick up the sharpness the rift offers them and use it for five seconds.

You have to watch for a long time to catch it, because it is casual, disposable, almost invisible as behavior. A cricket at the edge of an algal pan will worry at a thin flake of shale with its mandibles until it comes free, then brace the flake with one foreleg and lever up the edge of the mat to get at the softer growth underneath. When it is done, it walks away and leaves the flake where it lies. Another cricket may use the same flake an hour later, or not.

A salamander will nose along a lichen-crusted crevice that its broad head cannot enter, withdraw, pick up a smooth, flat pebble in its mouth, and scrape — *scrape* — sideways along the crust to dislodge a mouthful, then drop the stone. The stone is never carried far. It is never kept.

High on the wall, a cliff bird will land beside a deep, narrow crack, cock its head so its clicks can map the hollow, then hop away to find what fell from above: a twig, a pine needle, a splinter of dry wood blown over the lip from the surface world — the only material in this basin that did not originate here. It will hold it in one precise, zygodactyl foot and probe, fishing for larvae. When the larva is extracted or the twig snaps, the bird discards it over the edge. It flutters down and lands on the basin floor, where a beetle will eventually walk over it and ignore it.

It is not culture. It does not accumulate. There are no caches, no workshops, no favored tools kept and re-used by an individual. It is brief, primitive craft, the kind of intelligence that arises when a place is full of sharp edges and an animal has a problem that a sharp edge can solve. They have no need for more, because the problems never change.

### How the place sounds with them in it

You could think this rift would be silent. Cool, dim, with no wind and no trees to rustle, it looks silent.

It is oppressively loud.

That is them. The tick of a thousand pools becomes one patter. The glassy chirring of one cricket colony lifts, is answered a hundred meters down-basin, lifts again, and the echo returns three seconds later off the opposite wall so that you cannot tell call from reply. The dry rattle of the cliff birds layers over it, and under it the soft lapping shift of salamander bodies. Because the air is still and the stone is hard and the basin is kilometers long with no soft ground to absorb sound, everything carries. A twig dropped on a ledge half a kilometer away ticks as clearly as if it fell at your foot.

And yet — and this is the final quality that defines them — the loudness never changes.

Come back in an hour, it is the same volume, same texture. Come back in a year, in a hundred years, you would find the same lichen map on the same boulder, the same drip hitting the same mineral-ringed pool, the same density of crickets around it, the same bird working the same crack with a new twig. Their world found an equilibrium immediately after the cracking stopped and has held it. No succession is coming. No forest will ever rise here. The stone is too young to have become soil and too cold and dim for soil to matter. The water cycle is closed and perfect. The life that fell into it multiplied until it filled precisely the amount of damp and dim that exists, and then stopped.

They do not struggle. They do not expand. They do not dream of the surface, though the surface is only eighty meters straight up.

They inch and scrape and chirr and probe and huddle and breathe, pale, wet, and blind, in dense islands surrounded by empty stone, in a cool, grey-blue, dripping loudness that has been exactly itself since before any human remembered it was there, and will be exactly itself long after.
