Nothing lives here the way you were taught to recognize living.

At first that is what you believe, and the place encourages the belief. Life, as you know it, is noisy and crowded and wet. It clusters. It calls out. Here there is no cluster and no call. For hours you see only rock and salt and wind-cut ribs, and you think: uninhabited.

Then you notice the scale is wrong for your eyes. Life here does not cluster because the plateau does not allow clustering. It lives as the plateau does — as a single presence with kilometers of emptiness around the next.

The most common resident isn't an animal at all. It's the glass-grass.

You thought it was a plant. Closer, you see it isn't growing so much as *standing*. One hollow filament of silica at a time, sometimes two if the year was good, a meter to a meter-and-a-half tall, transparent to the point of invisibility until it catches the undiluted sun and throws a hard little rainbow on the salt. It has no leaves. No green. It roots without a root — a flared foot fused directly into gneiss, into basalt, sealing itself to stone.

It doesn't drink. There is nothing to drink. In the hour before dawn, when the frost laces the yardang shadows and then sublimates without ever becoming water, the filament is at its coldest. It condenses the air onto itself by being a needle that bleeds heat into the sky. A film of frost forms on its outer wall, then on its inner wall, then vanishes as vapor that is trapped inside the hollow tube. Inside, that vapor meets the faint charge that lives in the rock — the static the Towers farm — and splits. You can hear it if you are close and the air is perfectly still: a faint tick, like a tiny Geiger counter. The grass lives on that. Light, frost that never melts, and electricity. It will stand in the same place for eighty, a hundred years, swaying, humming when the wind finds its resonant frequency. It does not need to reproduce often. Once a decade it shivers and casts a single glass seed — a splinter no longer than your fingernail — that the wind carries for fifty kilometers until it wedges in another crack and seals itself down.

Where the glass-grass feeds on sky, the second resident feeds on stone.

You find it only because your shadow fell across it and it flinched. In the lee of a yardang where the wind has polished the rock amber-smooth, there is a dark stain, wine-red to black, that at first looks like desert varnish. Kneel and it resolves: a crust, many fingernail-sized lobes overlapping like lichen, but thicker, softer. The Lace-Crust. It spreads a millimeter a year. Perhaps less. Its upper surface is pitted with pores that open at night and close at noon. It photosynthesizes, but not eagerly — the sun is too hard, the air too thin. So it eats the rock itself, excreting acid so weak it would take a century to etch a line you could feel. Underneath, it is pale and blind and slightly warm to the touch. If you stay and watch, which you must, because nothing here happens quickly, you see the pores pulse.

It is not one organism. No crust here is. Each lobe is a colony of colonies, and colonies kilometers apart are genetically identical, because the dust carries fragments. You could walk for a week and you would still be walking over the same creature, intermittently expressed whenever the micro-topography is right. It notices light, not you. When your shadow passes, the pores near the edge close. When the sun returns, they open. That is the full extent of its opinion. It has been doing this since the mountains were still mountains. It will be here when the Towers finally stop.

And above them both — rare enough that Seeing one feels like the plateau has decided to acknowledge you — is the animal.

You will not hear it coming. That is the law of this place. Sound 0.1/3 means sound is a waste of energy. Nothing that lives here calls.

High overhead, where the turbulent fields made by the Towers churn silently, something turns without flapping.

The Kite.

From the ground you first take it for ash, for a plastic bag caught in the distortion. Then it banks and you see scale: three, four meters wingtip to wingtip, but so thin as to be almost two-dimensional when edge-on. It is translucent in the same way the glass-grass is translucent, because it is made of the same bargain: silica and chitin and a membrane stretched over hollow spars, like a dragonfly magnified to the size of a small aircraft and then starved of color. Light passes through it. The sun makes its veins glow.

It never lands. It has no legs worth the name, only a folded keel tucked beneath it that could grip rock if it ever needed to, which it never does. To land would be to be trapped in the salt, in the grit, in the stillness. It lives in the turbulence the old machines make.

Watch it long enough and you understand its ecology. The Towers — the Looms — spend all day boiling the air without sound, making and unmaking lattices, vortices, sheets of salt that rise like curtains. Each event pumps the air full of charge, of ionization, of brief, intense electric fields. The Kite drinks that. It circles a Loom at a respectful distance, two or three kilometers out where the air is just starting to misbehave, and tilts its wings into the pressure gradient. The fine hairs on the trailing edges of its wings, each hair itself a glass filament, vibrate and accumulate charge like the grass does. You can see it faintly at dusk: St. Elmo's fire crawling along its wingtips.

It feeds without eating. And when it needs to signal — to another Kite, of which there may be two or three in a territory the size of France — it does not cry. It flexes. A rapid, silent cambering of its wing changes the air pressure across a ten-meter column. Far away, another Kite feels it as a pop in the sinuses, a drop behind the ears, a compass needle trembling. Their conversations are pressure fronts, slow as weather. A single exchange can take an hour.

If you are very lucky, or very still, one will descend. Not to you. To the glass-grass. It will stall above a filament, hover without beating — riding the static the same way it rides the Tower fields — and lower a proboscis as thin and clear as the grass itself, and sip. Not sap. The charge. The faint plasma that lives inside the hollow tube. The grass shivers and dims for a few seconds afterward. The Kite flexes in what you can only read as satisfaction, a low-pressure pulse that makes your ears click, and climbs again, without a sound, back into the blue-violet where the sky wants to become space.

It may be ten years old. It may be fifty. It lays no nest. Once, when the conditions align — a particular storm of distortion, a particular density of dust — it will split along its keel and release a pair of ribbons that ride the wind until they can inflate themselves. Most will desiccate before they ever find a Loom to orbit.

And then there are the Towers themselves, which you have to count as inhabitants even though they were built.

They are not alive, but the plateau has absorbed them into its definition of living because they act more alive than anything else. Two hundred meters tall, matte, light-absorbing, they do not erode. The wind has polished the rock around them but left them untouched. At their tips the air continually fails, rebuilding itself into geometries that have no name — a perfect cube of dust hanging still for three seconds, a spiral of light with no source, a rectangular cloud at knee height that rains light. They are hyperactive in a way no biology here can afford to be. Where every plant and animal and crust survives by being frugal, slow, silent, spread thin, the Looms are profligate, turbulent, endlessly creative, and utterly indifferent.

The animals understand this. The Kites do not nest on the Towers. They orbit them the way seabirds orbit a vent that will never cool.

If there was ever a people here, they left only those needles, and the needles outlived the reason for them. Now the ecology has incorporated the machines as weather — as a second sun that gives not light but invention. The grass grows a little straighter near a dormant Loom. The crust darkens. The Kites gather.

You, standing in your jacket in the cold, hard-edged light with your lungs working a little too fast, are not an inhabitant. You are a visitor the altitude will not keep for long. Your noise — your boots, your breath, your voice — is too loud for here, and the thin air steals it immediately. If you stayed, you would learn to be silent, to drink frost without melting it, to store sun in glass, to speak in pressure.

But you cannot stay on that timescale. So you watch the Kite make one more silent circuit around its invisible fountain, and the glass-grass prism a single hard white spot onto the salt, and the crust close its pores against your shadow, and the Tower boil another brief, beautiful, useless architecture into the middle sky —

and you understand that this place is not empty. It is just inhabited at the density the place allows: one filament per square kilometer, one crust per wind-shadow, one Kite per horizon, one idea per Tower — each alone, each sufficient, each spaced so far apart that loneliness is not a condition but a form.
