They live low.

That is the first thing you understand about them, before you see any of them clearly. Whatever lives here has made a compact with the stone to not rise above it. Even the things that fly do not really rise — they *hold* — and everything else keeps its belly close to the warm rock, as if the thin, warm air might lift them away if they stood too tall.

You don't see them at first because you are looking at the wrong scale. You are looking for a figure against the horizon. They are not figures against the horizon. They are a texture *on* the horizon, until the texture moves.

Then it resolves.

The closest are the smelters.

You thought at first they were a single kind of beetle with hammered-copper backs, and they are, but not the way a beetle is a single animal. They are a city that pretends to be an insect, or an insect that pretends to be an industry.

An individual worker is as long as your hand, low and broad and impossibly heavy for its size. Its back is not a shell but a heat-shield, a dome of chitin laid over with overlapping scales of native copper and iron that it has sweated out of its own body. It moves with a slow, deliberate, overloaded waddle. Its mandibles are blunt, black, made for chewing stone. All day — all night, since there is only night — it wanders between the waxy succulents and the wind-scoured pavements, scraping up dust. Not any dust. It tests. It tastes for iron. When it finds a patch where the magnetite runs rich and red, it will sit and eat the earth itself, grinding basalt and sandstone to powder, mixing it with the bitter sap of the needle-leaf scrub. Inside it, it carries a furnace.

You can see it if one pauses near you in the dark. The plates of its abdomen gape a fraction and a dull cherry light breathes out from within. It smells like hot blood and hot metal. Symbiotic bacteria line its gut like the brick of a kiln, running a chemistry no larger animal should be able to run, reducing ore at a temperature that should cook it alive. It does not cook. It glows. When it has finished, it excretes a single, perfect pellet, still warm enough to steam in the cool dew — a bead of pig iron, or low steel, slag still clinging to it like afterbirth.

It carries this in its jaws to the mound.

The mound is what lives here. The beetles are its hands.

From a distance the mounds look like nothing — another set of rounded, weathered buttes, a little darker, a little more symmetrical than the stone around them. Until you get close enough to feel the heat on your face and realize one of them is exhaling in rhythm. They are everywhere, once you know to look. A field of them across a mesa top, twenty, forty, a hundred, spaced as deliberately as the thorn trees, each commanding its empty circle, each pulsing faintly.

They are foundries.

They are built of mud and chitin and generations of slag, consolidated into something that is not rock and not hive but both. Up close they are porous and intricate. Chimneys pock the slope, sighing metallic steam. Fissures vent heat that makes the air shiver. If you put your hand on the flank you feel a deep, slow throb — not a heartbeat, a bellows. Inside, blind, pale castes work that you never see outside. One caste is all lung, great ribbed bellows-beasts that inflate and compress, pumping air through the chambers. One caste is all jaw, welders that hold larvae in place while a molten bead is fitted around them. They are making more of themselves, but not by laying eggs alone. They are *forging* their young.

The larvae are soft and white and helpless. The workers sheath them while they are still wriggling in casings of still-malleable iron, fitting plates, riveting them with dabs of secreted solder that cools with a *tick*. You can hear it at night if you are near enough: *tick... tick... tick...* as a thousand tiny hulls contract. The young beetles will wear that iron as adults, already rusted to the color of the desert before they take their first step. The mound itself grows this way too, accreting slag year by year, century by century, until it weathers at the same rate as the natural buttes. Some of them must be older than any forest on Earth. They do not think, not the way you think. The mound thinks as a whole, as a distributed, chemical parliament, deciding when to fire and when to cool, when to send out foragers and when to seal its vents against a coming warm rain. It has been on shift for a very long time.

They are not the only ones who live by industry without inventing it.

Further out, on the flat pavements where no mound has claimed the ground, the rock itself is employed.

You hear them before you see them — that engine you mistook for a machine. *Hiss... chug... hiss... chug...* A perfect four-stroke, endlessly patient. A crack in the basalt, a bore-hole polished to a mirror finish by ten million cycles, and inside it a column of stone and superheated water hammering up and down. Geothermal pressure builds in a chamber below, lifts the stone piston, steam screams past it, the piston falls, condenses, builds again. There is no fuel but the earth’s retained heat, no timing but physics. Around these natural engines the stone has been honed until it gleams, and around the gleam a different kind of life has gathered — mats of black, heat-loving bacteria and films of algae that pulse with voltaic light, feeding on the temperature differential. Things like shrimp but blind, with translucent legs, flicker in the splash pools at the base of each piston, never leaving its damp. St. Elmo’s fire crawls along the iron ridges nearby on warm windy nights, blue and aimless, and the insects there have learned to orient by it, antennae lit like filaments.

And above all that, the herds.

They are the reason the plateau can afford foundries. They are the slow, patient conversion of dew and distant scrub into meat and movement.

Far off on a mesa face, under that close, heat-trapping cloud, you see them as a single, flowing stain. The ash-gazelle — though gazelle is too light a word. They are heavier, barrel-chested, ash-gray as the basalt, with wide splayed feet that spread their weight on cracked earth like snowshoes on snow. Their necks are thick, their heads low, their eyes huge and dark to gather the faint glow of the mounds and fungi. They do not call. They do not bleat or bark. Thirty, sixty, a hundred together, they move in a silence that feels intentional, as if silence were their camouflage in a place where sound carries too well and then dies too fast. They drink only at the deep tinajas in the canyon folds, where spring water seeps cold and black and rushes grow in sudden, violent green around it, and where amphibians crowd so thick their translucent throats make the water look like it is boiling with pearls. The herd will stand there for hours, utterly still except for tongues, while those amphibians pulse and the algae slick sighs.

They are watched.

What watches them never flaps. Up in the warm updrafts that peel off the canyon rims, the reapers hang. They are large — the span of a man — but so thin and so dark you only see them when they occlude the faint foxfire on a thorn tree. Leathery wings stretched over elongated finger-bones, not beating, just letting the rising heat hold them. They are utterly silent. Feathers would rustle; membrane does not. They tilt, they circle for hours without a twitch, reading the heat-signatures of the herd below. When one drops, it does not stoop like a falcon. It simply folds and falls like a dropped cloak, and the only sign a kill has happened is a sudden gap in the herd’s formation that closes a moment later as if nothing had happened. No cry from the taken, no triumph from the taker. The plateau absorbs it.

Underfoot, even the plants are inhabitants in the same contract.

The black succulents are not passive. They swell at night, drinking condensation through pores in their waxy skin, and by the small hours they are taut and gleaming, each leaf a canteen. At the driest hour before the nonexistent dawn, they will contract and squeeze a single, fat, perfectly clear drop from a pore at their tip onto the dust below — not for themselves, but for the beetles that will come and taste the salt in it. The thorn tree, lone and flat-topped, is a whole town. Its fissured bark holds gardens of lichen that glow faintly amber, foxfire that does not line up with its shadows. In its branches things click and rustle that are not insects and not birds, things with too many joints that tend the tree’s wounds and carry its hard seeds down to caches in the rock cracks, farming it in return for shelter.

None of them are lonely. None of them are many.

That is the rule of this high continent. Life here has solved abundance not by crowding, but by spacing. By vastness. Each foundry commands its miles of foraging ground, each thorn tree its circle of empty stone, each herd its mesa. There are thousands of herds. There are ten thousand foundries. From where you stand you see three beetles, a patch of glowing lichen, a distant smear of gray that is grazing — and you think it is empty. But if you could rise high enough to see the whole plateau, that lifted world the size of a country, you would see it as it really is: densely, evenly, industriously alive. A factory floor the size of a continent where every station is a mile from the next, all of them quietly working the night shift.

And the shift never ends, because night never ends, and the warmth never quite leaves the stone. They have adapted to the held breath. The beetle does not hurry with its pellet of iron. The piston does not rush its stroke. The gazelle does not bolt. The reaper does not need to dive twice. Everything moves at the speed the warm dark allows, which is the speed of stone cooling, of iron condensing, of dew beading and falling, one drop at a time, for ten thousand years.

If you stood among them long enough, staying still until the tinaja at your feet began to show your reflection again, they would forget you were not part of the spacing. A beetle would veer around your boot. A herd far away would pass without looking. The mound nearest you would exhale, and its warm, metallic breath would wash over your skin, and you would finally understand that you were not hearing an engine at all.

You were hearing a place breathe through all the mouths it has grown for itself.
