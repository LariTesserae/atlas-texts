Nothing lives here at first.

That's your first reading, and the valley lets you keep it for a while. It's what the silence wants you to think. No birdsong, no insect drone, no scurry in the mat. The machines don't clatter. The moths you see don't move when you breathe on them. You can stand on a terrace of basalt with green to your knees and be sure, absolutely sure, that you are alone in an abandoned greenhouse the size of a world-cut.

Then you stay still a little too long.

The first thing that lives here is not an animal at all, or not only an animal. It's the mat itself.

You thought *moss under fern under vine under shrub* was a stack of separate species competing for light. It isn't. Not here. In this cold, bright, intermittent wet, competition is too expensive. What covers every horizontal surface is closer to a single agreement. 

Kneel and put your hand down. The moss does not compress like moss. It gives like flesh. Three, four layers deep, yes, but the layers are stitched. White rhizoids as thick as twine run down through the fern roots and into the vine roots and into the moss again. The waxy alpine trees growing sideways out of cracks are not trees growing *through* the mat. They are the mat, grown woody and upright where it needed to be upright. If you peel back the edge — and it peels, it doesn't tear — you see the underside is pale, damp, veined, and beaded with stored dew in little bladders the size of grapes. The whole green upholstery sweats and holds. It breathes so slowly you can only see it if you fix on one frond and count: on an inhale of about a minute, the dew lenses swell; on an exhale, they release a fine mist that never rises.

It is one thing, distributed. An opportunistic reservoir. It lives on light and on the condensation the plateau sweats. It has no mouth and no need to move. It *is* the place where anything else that lives has to live — on it, in it, under it.

And then, because a place that efficient always grows something to tend it, there is the thing that walks on it.

You don't see it move. You see that it *has* moved.

Look at the three-toed prints again. You found one pressed into the dew early on and thought: bird, maybe. Large bird, lost. But look closer when the light is vertical and hard. The print is not a foot pressing down. It's three points pressing *up* from underneath. The mat grows over everything, but something about three inches down is pushing. The moss there is not crushed; it is parted around three smooth, cool pads, each as big as your palm, each weeping clear water from a pore at its tip. By the time you circle back ten minutes later, the print is gone. The mat has smoothed it over. The dew has refilled.

If you sit — really sit, until the cold of the stone comes through the mat into your bones and your own breathing sounds too loud — and you watch one of the slow machines, you will eventually see its tender.

It was already there.

You thought it was another hummock of moss where the terrace casts that wrong, green-held light. A low dome at the foot of the gantry, by the cistern, where vine has pulled especially thick over a shape. The shape is about as long as a bathtub and half as high. It has no front or back that you can name at first. Its upper surface *is* mat: living moss and fern growing directly from its back, rooted in it. Not camouflage. It grows it. Its skin beneath is waxy and pale as the underside of leaves, translucent enough that you can see shadows of water inside — two or three big, sloshing cisterns that take up most of its body.

When it decides to tend, it does so without what you would call action.

A seam opens in the moss of its back and a limb extends. Not a leg, not an arm. A prop. Tripodal — that's the three toes. Three of them, set out in a wide tripod, jointed backwards in two places, ending in those cool pads. They are not for walking quickly. They are for distributing weight so it never breaks through the mat. It moves the way a glacier moves: by extending a pad a hand's breadth, letting the mat adhere, then drawing its bulk after. A meter in an hour if it is in a hurry. Usually slower. You will not see it walk if you stare. You will look away to check the slow wheel and look back and its dome is a foot to the left.

The head, if you can call it a head, is usually tucked. When it needs to drink, or oil, it lifts. There is no face. There is a shallow, bowl-like depression lined with fine, black cilia that vibrate only when wet. It lowers this bowl into a pothole cistern and the cilia pull dew in a silent, capillary rush; you can see its flank cisterns swell and clear. When it needs to work, it lifts the same bowl to the axle of the wheel, to the gut of the transformer. From a gland in its throat it exudes a dark, mineral oil that smells faintly of basalt and rain, and spreads it with those cilia. That is the maintenance you thought was mysterious. It is not mysterious. It is grooming.

They are the reason the amber bulbs are still on. The bulbs are warm. On cold stone, in thin bright air, warm is rare. The tenders gather under them at night when the dew materializes heaviest, and press their moss-backs to the concrete walls where the heat leaks out, photosynthesizing by day, thermosynthesizing by night.

They do not speak. Sound is waste here — the mat eats it, the air carries it poorly, and vibration shakes dew off leaves before it can be collected. They speak in water and shadow.

When one has drunk enough, it will carry a mouthful in its bowl and cross — slowly, so slowly — to a dry patch of mat ten meters away and pour it, bead by bead, into the root-bladders. When it wants to signal, it will climb onto a gantry crane and hold its back at an angle until its shadow falls across another tender's moss. The shadows here already jitter, as you noticed — the sun flickering a fraction of a degree as if time were breathing unevenly. They use that. A held shadow that holds a half-second too long is a word. A shadow that cuts the green-held light under a terrace is a sentence: *this cistern is empty, this one is full, this drum needs turning*.

This is why the industry is so slow. The factory is not for humans. The conveyor belts and sorting drums and turbines are not making a product for export. They are the tenders' external organs — slow kidneys and lungs built by whoever cut the valley, or perhaps built by the tenders themselves over many patient generations out of riveted steel and poured concrete because steel holds heat and concrete holds water better than basalt. The wheel that turns once every twenty minutes is lifting empty buckets from a cistern that is *now mostly moss* because the moss is the filter. The hopper that never fills is not supposed to fill; it is supposed to aerate. The looms inside the sheds are not weaving cloth. If you press your forehead to the opaque, condensation-wet window, you see the belts moving a thread's width per hour, pulling long fibers of vine through a bath of that viscous, sun-thickened water, coating them, curing them into something like muscle.

That cup you left in the sun — the water that thickened and then the next day was full of dark seedlings. You thought it was strange germination. It was a clutch. They lay like that. Not eggs, exactly. Packets of mat and tender both, in water that has been held and warmed and made viscous with their oil, until they are ready to root. Leave a cup anywhere and one of them will find it and fill it.

There are fifteen or twenty low sheds in the valley, and there are about as many tenders. One per shed, usually, plus a few juveniles that look exactly like mossy stones until you see the dew gather on them faster than on anything else. They are not many because they cannot afford to be many. Everything here conserves — water, motion, sound — and so do they. Their hearts, if you put your ear to the moss of their back, beat about four times a minute. Their lives are long. The green has only been here a short while — this valley is young — but they already look like they have always been here, which is the same thing.

If you walk the length of the valley in an afternoon, you will not be chased or welcomed. If you stand still, one might brush your boot with a pad as it passes, leaving a cool, perfectly clean circle of water on the leather, and keep going. If you stay until dusk, when the cold comes down hard and the dew begins to materialize again on every surface, you will see the undersides of the terraces glow a little brighter green, and you will see, in that glow, all the domes you thought were hummocks slowly, silently uncover their bowls to drink the air as it sweats.

They don't need you to see them. The valley wasn't cut for you. But for as long as you are quiet, and slow, and keep your water to yourself, they will let you share the mat.
