Nothing human lives there.

Nothing *could* live there, if you mean living the way lowland things live. Nothing that needs a drink ever took hold. No person, no mammal, no sod-building grass. The altitude alone would have thinned blood to uselessness, the dryness alone would have mummified any soft thing that tried to wait out a season. What lives there conceded that early and completely, and became something else.

What lives there *is* the place, first.

From far off you think the plain is empty because you are looking at the ground. The ground is empty. It has to be. A mile of polished salt between trunks, then another mile, then another — white as paper, hard as poured concrete, shaved flat by a million years of wind. Nothing crawls across it if it can help it. To cross it is to be exposed under that small, white, pitiless sun, in air too thin to carry a scent, with no shade except the hard, cut-metal shadow you throw yourself. The ground is not habitat. It is quarry.

The inhabitants stand *above* it.

You have to learn to see them not as trees despite their shape, because every word for tree brings water with it. They are columns that learned to stand up for the same reason trees did — to get their working surfaces out of each other's shadow — but they solved it with different physics.

The mature ones are not alive the way a pine is alive. Come closer and you understand they are alive the way a refinery is alive when it's running at full pressure.

Each stands solitary, but each is a crowd. The trunk is not wood. It is rope and stone at once — dense, buff-colored fiber laid down in rings like mother-of-pearl, cellulose braided with silica until it has the flex of cable and the hardness of tooth enamel. You can hear it straining. In the heat of midday the whole mass ticks and pings as it expands. Near the base it is as wide as a low hill and fissured with vertical flutes, like the column of some ruined temple that kept growing after the temple fell. The flutes are chimneys. Put your hand near one and hot air blasts past you reeking of ammonia and hot iron — waste oxygen and volatiles being dumped after the interior chemistry has stripped what it needs.

Higher, twenty, forty, eighty meters up, it stops being a trunk at all and becomes a hydraulic candelabra. The great plates branch off it. That is the wrong word too — they don't branch, they *deploy*. Each is a leaf the size of a warehouse roof, but a leaf that has decided to become an industrial solar panel. Thick at the spine, thin at the trailing edge, corrugated like sheet steel for rigidity, their upper faces are a sooty, matte black-green that drinks light and doesn't give it back. Their undersides are pale and mirrored to throw light back into the interior, and along the corrugations a hundred shuttered mouths open and close.

This is what lives there, and this is what it does all day: breathe.

Not like you breathe. The shutters — stomae the size of your hand — slam open with a clatter you can hear a half mile away, a dry metallic cough as the plate inhales the thin, desiccated wind. Then they snap shut, and inside the veins you can see the sap — not sap, a clear, pressurized oil that is colder than the air — race and pulse under the translucent skin. The light is being eaten audibly. Pressure builds until the rivets of the plant's own making groan. Then the whole plate shudders and vents, a roar down the length of the fluted trunk that flattens the alkaline dust around its foot and leaves a momentary haze of exhaled heat shimmering above the salt.

At the crown, where the sun hits exactest, it is manufacturing.

You can watch it happen, and the speed is what unmakes your idea of a plant. A furled bud, tight as a fist and weeping cold oil, begins to extrude fiber from its tip with a high, screaming whine — cellulose-silicate being laid filament by filament, spinning itself out of air. Carbon pulled straight from the thin wind, nitrogen cracked by the daily lightning that walks the plateau because the forest makes its own weather. It grows in front of you. A plate that was not there at dawn is ratcheting open by noon, creaking and popping, shedding great papery cuticles that rip free and go skittering across the salt in flocks. By dusk it has hardened and joined the slow, tracked turning of the other plates, all angling not *toward* the sun at midday but *away* from it to dump heat, like a field of heliostats running in reverse.

And because nothing here forgives a parasite, a damaged plate does not heal. It detonates. You will be standing in the ringing quiet between inhalations and hear a sharp, concussive CRACK like a rifle, and a whole stadium-roof of leaf shatters into glittering fibrous shrapnel that rains down and is already being stripped by the things that live on the trunk. In the wound a new bud is hissing as it inflates, already pressurizing.

That is the primary life. But the primary life is also the continent for everything else, because the ground has nothing to offer.

Nothing lives *between* the columns. Everything lives *on* and *in* them, in vertical bands as distinct as tide zones.

Low down, where the acid roots have dimpled the salt into a smooth, amber-stained basin and cold oil weeps from cracks at night, the trunk is terraced with dead, shelf-like fiber. There, tight rosettes of silver and rust-colored lichen-analogues clamp flat, needing no rain, pulling their trace water as frost direct from vapor an hour before dawn. Grazers move among them — not insects, not isopods, but something that borrowed from both and then armored itself against light and sound. They are the size of tortoises, low and domed, their shells mirrored so brightly they throw shards of sun as they move, and you hear them before you see them: a faint, constant rasping as their radulas — broad as sandpaper — grind the mineral fiber and the salt itself for nitrates.

Higher, on the flanks where the wind hits, the plates cast hard moving shadows. Gliders live there. They look like the plates that bore them, which is not mimicry so much as shared solution. Translucent bodies light as paper, wingspans three meters and weightless, veins of the same black-green. They launch from a leaf edge and ride not wind but the hot exhalations, the thermal chimneys each column pumps all day, spiraling up in columns of manufactured air. When they shriek — and they shriek constantly, a needle-thin sound that cuts through the bass roar of the plants — they are not communicating so much as echolocating through the deafening thrum. In midday the air above a single crown can hold fifty of them, turning and screaming without ever touching the ground.

And in the crown itself, inside the vent-chambers where the air is briefly warm and ammonia-humid, the air is thick. Clouds of gnat-things — chitin and spun silica, too small to see as individuals until they are on you — breed in a perpetual storm. They lay their pupae directly onto the gill-shutters, trembling there, feeding on the oil mist that coats every interior. For them, the plant *is* the sky and its breath is weather.

They are why the hunters exist. Along the underside of every plate, walking upside down on hooked feet that find purchase in the corrugations, long-jointed predators the length of your arm move so slowly and so exactly the color of the plate that you only see them when they lunge. They pluck gliders from the air with forelimbs that snap forward faster than your eye can track, and comb the gnat clouds with fringed mouths.

Nothing drinks. The gliders land at night to press their pale bellies to the root cracks and soak oil through their skin. The grazers crack salt. The hunters get all the liquid they will ever need from the bodies of the gnats. When anything dies, it does not rot. The air desiccates it in an hour to a light, dry husk, and within a day it has been dismantled — scraped by grazers, dissolved by the faint acid mist the plant sweats to keep its surface sterile, the minerals reclaimed back into fiber.

After dark, the forest does not sleep. It changes shift.

Under the violent, unblinking stars — so many and so sharp through air too thin to soften them — the plain becomes a field of breathing silhouettes. The hydraulic sighing slows but never stops. A low, continuous thrum you felt in your molars at noon becomes audible now as a distant stamping, whole kilometers of columns thumping and ticking as their fibers contract in the cold. Here and there a young column glows from within. Its veins carry a cool, electroluminescent blue, the byproduct of the oil's night chemistry, tracing each vast leaf like a circuit. From a high place, the white void is no longer white. It is a sparse motherboard scattered with dim blue lanterns, each lantern working alone, each lantern exhaling its own small weather into a sky it has made for itself, ringing faintly when the night wind touches its plates like struck bronze.

There are no roads between them. No nests that outlast the fiber they are glued to. No graves. A giant that has stood five hundred years will fail all at once, not with a crash but with a long, shuddering exhalation, its plates folding down like a closing telescope one by one, dumping a final scalding cloud that smells of turpentine and hot copper across the salt, while the grazers that have been waiting weeks at its base already begin to climb.

In the morning, a new bud at the foot of the next column over will have grown a hand's breadth taller while you slept.

That is what lives here: a thing that learned how to make a forest out of light and stone and thin air, and then filled the empty vertical miles it created with tenants that learned how to live without ever touching the ground.
