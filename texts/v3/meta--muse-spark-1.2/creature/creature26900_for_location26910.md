Who lives there doesn't live there the way you live somewhere.

At first you think nothing does. Nothing *could*. The place is too new, too hard, too bright and too wrong. You see the birds and the rat-things and the flat beetle-crabs in their endless lines and you think — okay, vermin. Occupants by accident. The building itself is empty. A factory with no workers, running on its own.

Then you stand still long enough for the brightness to stop hurting, and you realize you have been looking *through* them.

---

You notice it first as an absence of noise where there should be a small noise.

A pigeon-thing takes off from a cable tray and doesn't make a sound, and in the space where its wings should have clapped the air, something else moves without displacing it. A long, thin shape steps *between* the layers.

You try to focus on it and your eyes slide off, because it is the exact color and texture of the place. Not camouflaged. *Made of it.*

They are tall. That is the first true fact that stays. Not tall like a person on stilts — tall like the building decided it needed a way to get from one catwalk to the one eight meters above without using stairs. Two, two and a half meters at the shoulder when they unfold, but most of the height is leg. Legs with an extra joint, reversed at the knee, long shins cased in something that is not skin and not coveralls but both. A pale, matte material the same flat grey-white as poured concrete, scuffed and beaded with condensation. When they stand in the hard, sourceless light their edges go soft and you lose them against a retaining wall until they move.

And they move so slowly you thought they were part of the railing.

This is not stealth. This is their speed. Everything here turns slowly. They turn with it. A single step takes thirty seconds: a careful, silent placement of a broad, soft foot - not a foot, a pad, like the pigeons have, but larger, splayed, with a faint sucker-ring that seals to metal and concrete and peels away without a click — then a long pause while the other three limbs adjust. Four limbs. Sometimes you think you see six, because the arms split partway down. The upper arms are close to the body, tucked. The forearms divide again at the elbow into two thinner forearms, each ending in hands that are not hands. One set is fine and black and many-fingered for picking, for threading wire, for scooping lichen from a seam. The other set is broad and spade-like, for bracing, for wiping condensation into its mouth.

They have no heads the way you expect a head. If you stare — and you have to stare for a long time, because the glare bleaches everything — you see the torso simply narrows into a column of the same pale material and then widens again into a flat, featureless plate, angled slightly down. No eyes. No mouth you can see. The plate is damp. It sweats constantly, like every other underside here. Beads gather on it, run down its front, and are channeled by faint grooves back toward the body. Sometimes, when the light hits it at the right contradictory angle, you see that the plate is translucent. There are shadows moving under it. Dark, slow cusps opening and closing. Gills, maybe. Or shutters.

They drink the building. They eat what grows on it.

You will see one stopped beside a mineral-crusted cistern on a rooftop tray, folded down to half its height, its forelimbs submerged. It is not drinking the water. It is filtering it. The water around its hands goes clear as it draws the pale green biofilm, the dissolved concrete dust, the thin soup of midge larvae that live in every standing pool, through frills you can't see. You will see another working its many-fingers along an expansion joint, harvesting the wiry moss and the black-green lichen in a slow, even strip, leaving the concrete paler behind it, and then bending — impossibly, joints bending the wrong way — to press the mass to the underside of its head-plate where it dissolves.

They don't hunt the birds. They don't need to. The birds are abundant and the birds ignore them completely, which tells you the birds know them. Once, a flock of those too-jointed swifts poured down a factory canyon and broke around a standing one like water around a piling, reforming on the other side without a single bird touching it. Not afraid. *Accustomed.*

There are more of them than you think, because you only see them when they are still. When they walk, the wrongness of the place helps them. You tried to map the building and found that walking down six flights can bring you up. They don't have that problem. They know the seams. You watch one approach a corner where dust is falling sideways and water is running a few inches uphill, and it doesn't hesitate. It places a foot *into* the seam and is suddenly twenty meters further along the catwalk, limbs not having appeared to move between. The lenticular stutter you saw in the far layers — that shutter-reset where a whole vista rearranges — happens *around* them without disturbing them. They are in continuity with the error. The building was made wrong on purpose to accommodate them, or they were made after to fit the wrongness.

Do they live together? Not socially, not as you use the word. They don't gather. They don't call. The air is too thin for calls and they have nothing to say that would carry. You find one per vista, sometimes two, never close. They maintain distance the way pigeons on a wire do, evenly spaced across an expanse too large to see across. If two come near each other on the same gantry, one will stop and fold, and wait — for minutes, for an hour — until the other has passed with that patient, peeling-footstep gait. No acknowledgement.

But they are tending. That is the word that finally sticks as you watch long enough to see purpose.

Each one has a circuit. Not a territory. A circuit. This one walks the same loop of catwalks, stairs, and vertical shafts for what must be days, wiping the same handrails free of condensation, clearing the same drains of moss, tightening the same loose bolts on the same humming electrical cabinets with those delicate black fingers. The building sweats and sprouts and tries to rust and they polish it back. They are not building it. They are *keeping* it new. That is why the weld beads are still crisp. That is why no step is worn concave.

They were not here before the building. They emerged with it. Exuded. You get the feeling if you pried open one of those humming cabinets you'd find not wires but a warm, damp hollow full of folded, half-formed ones, waiting to be unfolded.

And they are not afraid of you. When you finally stand close enough that your own too-loud footsteps have stopped echoing, one will be beside you on the same narrow walkway, and you will have no memory of it approaching. It will be still, its damp head-plate angled not at you but slightly past you, beading in the bright, cool light. You will smell nothing — the air is too thin, too metal — but you will feel the cool coming off it, the same cool that lives in the girders. You will see your own distorted reflection in its plate, thinned and stretched.

If you move, it will wait. If you touch it — and you will want to, because everything else here is hard — the material gives slightly, warm underneath. Like skin over concrete. It will not react. It will wait until you step away and then it will place its spade-hand where your hand was, and wipe away the oils and the salt you left, and polish the rail until it gleams again, slowly, silently, with infinite patience.

It lives here because here is the only place its shape makes sense. It could not live under a sky that is truly a sky, or on a ground that is truly ground. It needs the thin light, the beading water, the wrong geometry to step through. It needs a mountain made of building to be its mountain.

You hear your own breath again, visible and quick in the cool. It does not breathe at all.
