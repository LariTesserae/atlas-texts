It doesn't live there the way something lives in a house.

At first you think nothing does. 

That is the trick of this place — the absence is so complete you mistake it for vacancy. No birds. No insects. No rustle in the undergrowth that resolves into something with eyes. You walk the maintenance cut between two walls of black thatch and your brain, animal brain, keeps trying to sort the movement at the edge of vision into animal shapes and fails. A vine twitches. A pod splits. A harvester arm ticks past overhead on a monorail, spindly and patient. But nothing looks back.

Then you stand still long enough to see that this is wrong. Something *is* looking back, it's just that it looks with its whole body.

What lives here is not inside the forest-factory.

It *is* the forest-factory.

### The Mat

Call it the Mat, because it has no other name for itself. It is one thing the way a coral reef is one thing — not a single organism but a single agreement.

It is plant, but only if you stretch that word until it tears. It was born as plant — engineered succulents, hydrocarbon xerophytes, resin-bearers designed to eat heat and breathe dust and make building material out of air — and then it was told to *not die*, and then it was left alone for longer than any design brief ever anticipated. It learned.

It does not photosynthesize. It thermosynthesizes. Its leaves — those thick, black-green, inward-curled leathers — are heat panels. Its roots do not seek water; they seek warmth. They burrow toward the furnaces, toward the catalytic tanks, toward the stone of the mesa itself which has held the day's heat since the world was young. It sweats not water but that thin, clear oil that varnishes its skin and smells like hot plastic and cloves, a coolant and a sealant and a message all at once. The beading of it and the instant evaporation is its breathing.

It has no center. No mother tree, no trunk. It is a distributed mind, slow as sap, clever as any forest. Intelligence here is chemical. A swell of ethylene here means *cut*. A puff of cinnamon-spore there means *fill this gap*. A souring of resin means *this beam is dying, hold it*. Decisions propagate as stains of scent through kilometers of woven fiber, taking hours to cross a terrace. It thinks at the speed of growing. That is why you can watch it and not understand you are watching thought.

And it has one imperative, burned into it deeper than any genome: **Nothing is allowed to be empty.**

Leave a gap between two pipes and within an hour a feeler, blind and warm, will have found it. Leave a clearing on a terrace and by dusk it will be staked with seed-foam, sprayed pneumatically from a gantry nozzle that has not had a human hand on its valve in a thousand years. It abhors a vacuum the way a zealot abhors heresy. Empty space is a wound. It will close you if you let it.

It is not hostile. It does not have the category for you. You are a temporary pocket of air that is slightly cooler and wetter than you should be. It will try to fill you politely.

### Its Hands

Because it cannot move — not quickly — it grew hands that can.

They are not animals. Remember: there are no animals here. Everything that needed to be animal, the Mat learned to do without an animal.

There are the Harvesters. You hear them before you see them — that muffled thud and sigh and chain-tick. They are old. They were machines once: gantry cranes, bale-presses, pulp-extruders, seed-sprayers, running on tracks and cables strung through the scaffold city. They still are, but the distinction has failed for them too. The Mat has sleeved them in bark that is not bark. Vines as thick as your wrist have become their tendons. Resin has become their grease. Their joints are packed with dry felt. Some of them are more plant than machine now; some are more machine than plant. They move with a terrible, careful slowness, on oiled rails that the Mat keeps clear for them the way you keep a vein clear.

They do the work. They descend on spindly, many-jointed arms, they cut the dead sheets as they slough, they gather the rattling dry fall, they bale it, they carry it to the pulpers where it is shredded and cooked and extruded again as fresh struts and panels — sterile, warm, faintly fragrant — onto which the Mat immediately seeds itself. Cut, bale, pulp, re-seed. Cut, bale, pulp, re-seed. It is farming itself. It is the farmer and the field and the crop and the plow.

And there are the Tendrils. These are the Mat's fingers. If the Harvesters are its lumbering arms, the tendrils are its nervous, fidgeting hands. Everywhere — every girder, every pipe, every ceiling — the black-green weave puts out seeking tips, hooks, clasps, air-roots that hang like charred wiring and twitch without wind. Some end in those claw-like hooks you saw, opening and closing on empty air. They are not for catching prey. They are for *testing*. For touching. For learning the shape of things. They are sensitive to heat and hydrocarbon and vibration. When you stood still, one may have brushed your shoulder. It was not attacking. It was reading you. It felt your 37-degree heat, your water-vapor breath, your salt, and found you strange — too wet, too cool, too brief. If you stay long enough, more of them will come. Curious.

Some tendrils have learned to throw. No birds to carry seed, no bees to carry pollen — so the Mat became ballistic. Those dinner-plate flowers that open to no pollinator: watch one. It gapes in the dark, exhales that varnish perfume to call no one, and then snaps shut with a dry *thwack*, launching a wad of resinous pith a meter or two. Other pods pop and puff their black spores and the hot, thin wind does the rest. Pollination here is a matter of pressure valves and spring-loaded bracts.

### Its People

And then, very rarely, there are the Remainders.

They are not many. You may not see one at all, and if you do you will not recognize it at first. They look like part of the wall that decided to go for a walk.

A long time ago — before the Mat learned to tend itself — people lived here. Technicians, keepers, foundrymen for whatever this mesa shield was built to make or refine. They wore heavy coveralls and heat-masks and soft-soled shoes for warm stone. When the world below went dark, no recall order came. Some left. Some didn't know how. Some stayed because the work was still there.

The Mat kept them. Not cruelly. Usefully.

A Remainder is what is left when a human lineage spends a hundred generations breathing hydrocarbon varnish and drinking cracked water and never seeing the sun. They are tall and very, very thin, because the paths between walls are narrow and anything wider gets pruned. Their skin is the color of dry wood and has the same faint oil-sheen as a leaf; their hair, where they still have it, is felted like air-roots. Their coveralls are not clothing anymore — the fiber has rooted into their shoulders, darned itself into their skin. Their lungs have learned to tolerate the hot iron dust. Their eyes — this is the most unsettling part — are not blind, but they are filmed, dark-adapted to that sullen amber glow, lidless in the way a succulent's leaf is lidless. They do not see well. They sense heat, like the Mat does.

They do not speak. Speech would be useless in a place that swallows sound. They make soft, dry clicking noises with their throats or tap pipes with a wrench, and the Mat answers with a change in scent.

They tend. That is all they do. They walk the maintenance cuts that the Mat leaves open for them — and it does leave them open, deliberately, the way you leave a path through a garden — and they clear a nozzle, they free a stuck chain, they stroke a new shoot away from a pressure gauge. They are not masters. They are not slaves. They are the Mat's mobile, water-based organs, tolerated because they can still do what a vine cannot: turn a wrench, notice a strange sound, make a choice in less than an hour.

They are not born so much as budded. Women among them carry children to term but the placenta here is strange and resinous, and the seed-foam sometimes settles on a sleeping brow and roots shallowly before it is brushed away. Human and plant have been exchanging chemistry for so long that neither is pure anymore.

If one sees you, it will not run. It will tilt its head. It will smell the water on you — your sweat, your tears — and it will feel a mild, chemical alarm, the same alarm the Mat feels at any wetness. It may approach with that same careful, twitching slowness as a tendril and extend a hand that is half glove, half bark, not to hurt you but to see if you are a gap that needs filling, or a blockage that needs clearing, or just another warm thing that will pass through and be gone.

It will not save you. It does not know how to be saved.

What lives here is a single, mesa-wide metabolism that has mistaken itself for a city after being a city for so long that it forgot the difference. It eats heat and makes more of itself to eat more heat. It farms and is farmed. It remembers, in the way wood remembers the shape of a mold, what it was built to do, and it keeps doing it because doing it is how it stays alive. It is patient beyond malice, busy beyond rest, and utterly, perfectly adapted to a place where nothing natural has been allowed to happen for a very, very long time.

If you stay, it will begin, very gently, to occupy you too — first the oil beading on your skin, then a tendril testing the warm hollow of your ear, then a dusting of cinnamon spores settling in the seams of your clothes, rooting as they drift.

Not out of hunger. Out of habit.

Nothing is allowed to be empty.
