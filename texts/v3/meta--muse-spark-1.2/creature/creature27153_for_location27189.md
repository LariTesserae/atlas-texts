Whatever lives there doesn't live *in* it.

That is the first thing you have to unlearn.

At first you think — there must be a den, a nest, a burrow. A hare has a form somewhere drier. The birds must have a roost up on the basalt teeth. The beetles must have a hive inside the rock. You scan the bowl for a center, for some place more inhabited than the rest, and you don't find it.

Then you think — no, the hare *is* what lives there. And the birds, and the midges, and the heron. That sparse, curated fauna. But you watch them and you understand they don't live there either. Not the way a thing lives in a place. They are *in* it the way embroidery is in cloth. They are held there. The too-large hare chews and never swallows. The birds vent steam in time with the piston. The heron points and never strikes. They are not inhabitants. They are *fixtures*. They are part of the furnishings of a room that was decorated with animals the way another room might be decorated with ferns.

The midge cloud hangs and hums at 60 cycles and never moves and never dies and never feeds. That is not living. That is being displayed.

So you look again. You listen to the loud, sustained chord — stone scream, steam hiss, electric thrum, water trickle, insect drone — all held on the same note. And you realize you have been looking for something that moves against the background. But nothing moves against the background here. That *is* the rule. Nothing is allowed to advance, to erode, to grow, to finish.

Whatever lives there has solved that problem by becoming the background.

It is the bowl itself, but not the rock. Not the machine.

It's the wet.

At first it looks like just wetness. The dark varnish on the basalt. The beading, the sheeting, the water that wells up around your imagined ankle if you stepped onto the peat. The water cupped in the door-sized rhubarb leaves. The water that beads on the heron's head and never falls.

Then you stay — in your looking — a little longer, and the wet stops looking like a condition and starts looking like a skin.

It is one skin.

A continuous, cold, clear, impossibly thin organism spread across every surface inside the rim, end to end. It lives as a film, as a mat, as a saturation. Where it is thin, it is the varnish on the basalt that never dries. Where it is thick, it is the peat itself, the sponge of living and half-living sphagnum and liverwort and shattered rock that quakes and holds your weight. Where it pools, it is the rills that braid and unbraid without cutting. Where it is held aloft, it is the gloss on every leaf.

And where it deepens enough to have a body, it lifts itself into shapes that you mistook for other things.

Watch one of those black cables that burst from the peat and loop over the ferns. Warm to the touch, faintly pulsing. It sweats water, too. You thought it was a power line, a root, a vein of the mountain. It is a vein, but not the mountain's. It's a raised tentacle of the film, thickened, insulated in a sheath of dark, smooth tissue like an earthworm the width of your thigh. It hums because current runs through the salty water inside it — not electricity put there, but electricity *made* there, the way an eel makes it. The current is its nerve impulse, slow, looping, found and returned. It does not go anywhere. It circles the bowl, end to end, to keep the chord in tune.

Watch the gears. The interlocking basalt teeth twenty feet across, turning against each other with a grinding you feel in your molars. The cold blue sparks shearing off that are not sparks. Moss grows right up to the edge of the shear and never tears. That's because the moss is not separate from the gear. At the seam, the green fur thickens and becomes a lip, a gasket, a wet gristle that cushions the stone-on-stone. The organism has grown itself into the machine, has upholstered the moving parts of the mountain with its own body so that they can turn forever without wearing down. The mountain provides the torque; it provides the bearing.

It does not have a face until you are already looking at it. Then you find you have been looking at its face the whole time.

In the middle of the bowl, where the peat is deepest and wettest and the light is most even, the film wells up slightly. The tree ferns there part just enough. The water table rises to the level of the moss and makes a shallow, clear lens, a pool no larger than a kitchen table, perfectly still despite the tremor of the pistons. It reflects the dim indigo sky without a ripple. If you kneel — and the moss would take your knees without cold — you see that the peat beneath the lens is not peat.

It is pale. Translucent. Layered like muscle, like mycelium, like stacked leaves of wet paper, breathing very, very slowly. You can see the cables enter it and dissolve into it, see the faint blue light move under it in pulses, irregular but not random, like thought.

And on its surface, breaking the reflection, there are eyes.

Not two. Many. Not compound. Not human. Little perfectly round pools of water that are slightly more convex than the rest, that bead and do not fall, that have a dark fleck at the center of each like a pupil. They all look up. When you shift your weight, the dark flecks follow you, not by moving, but by refracting. The thing is looking at you through the water it is.

It has no front or back. It is all middle. But the lens is where it is *most* — its ganglion, its heart, its clearest attention.

This is what has learned to live here, because nothing else could.

It is young and old at once, the way the bowl is. Its surface is fresh every second — water condensing, condensing, condensing out of the cold air, sheeting down, beading — so it is always being born. And underneath, in the peat layers, it is ancient, dense, composted of itself ten thousand times over, so it is always having been.

It is industrial and organic at once, the way the bowl is. Half its body is stone-ground smoothness, half is fever-green leaf. It does not distinguish. It secretes the peat that insulates the cables. It secretes the moss that greases the gears. It cups the steam that vents from the stone piston in leaf-bowls and lets it rain back down warm. It farms the midges in the air above it and the hare upon it and the beetles inside its seams — not to eat, but to keep — because a factory that produces nothing still needs moving parts, and living moving parts are softer and replace themselves.

It does not eat. It does not grow. It does not die. It regulates.

You can see its whole work in one held moment: A bead of water trembles on the lip of a basalt face and stretches, hanging. A needle-beaked bird opens its mouth to vent. A gear shudders an inch with a scream. The hare's jaw clicks. The blue spark shears and vanishes. And the film holds them all there, in that trembling, stretching, hanging instant, by taking the infinitesimal excess of motion — the drop that would have fallen, the bite that would have swallowed, the gear that would have advanced — and drinking it, and cycling it back as humidity, as current, as the drone of midges.

It lives by persisting. It lives the way a note lives when you hold it on an organ with perfect air pressure. It has achieved equilibrium with time, and its consciousness — if you can call it that — is not a line but a held chord. It does not think *and then*. It thinks *and, and, and*, all at once, all the while. It is not waiting for anything. It is not building toward anything. It is performing the immense, quiet, loud work of remaining exactly itself.

If you stayed, you would feel it regulate you, too. The cold clean air in your lungs would start to taste less like stone and more like being *included*. The hum in your molars would sync with the film's pulse. Your own blinking would slow. Your bead of sweat would bead and tremble and hang, and not fall.

It would not be unkind. It has no edge to be kind with. It would simply upholster you in moss where you chafe against the world, and loop a warm cable gently over your ankle to keep you where you are bright and visible in the sparse, curated green, ten feet from the next perfect thing.

And you would hang there — in the dim, in the wet, in the loud — beautifully, endlessly, without proceeding.

That is what lives there.

It lives there by being the place, and letting the place be alive.
