What lives here doesn't live here the way a deer lives in a valley or a hawk lives on a mountain.

It lives here the way water lives in a pipe.

At first you think nothing does. You listen through the hammer-scream of the motors and the hiss of the valves and the endless *plok* of ceiling-water into basin-water and you think: nothing could. No bird would nest in a throat that never stops swallowing. No rat would den where there is nowhere dry to sleep. No person would stay where you can't hear your own name if you shouted it. And you're right, for a second.

Then your eyes adjust to the sodium dim and you see that of course something lives here. It has to. A room this wet, this loud, this perfectly enclosed, doesn't stay empty. It grows something to fit it exactly.

It is sparse. Like everything else here.

If you wait through two pump cycles - through the overfill and the gulp and the clunk that shudders the grating under your boots - you will see him. Or it. The distinction has gotten thin.

He was a man once, unambiguously. Now he is the Keeper.

He is not tall. He has made himself compact to fit the scale of the room, which is only fifteen meters long. He wears what anyone would wear here and never takes it off: black rubber boots to the knee, always full of an inch of water that never drains, thick canvas pants gone dark with damp, a wool sweater under a slick yellow rain shell that never dries. The shell beads like the walls do. Condensation forms on his shoulders the same way it forms on the I-beams. When he exhales, his breath fogs and vanishes, same as yours, only his does not surprise him anymore.

His hair is cut short, practical, the color of wet concrete. His skin has gone the same - pale in a way that has nothing to do with not seeing sun and everything to do with being washed continuously in cold, mineral water. It is pruned permanently at the fingertips, cracked at the knuckles where the cold has gotten in. His face is lined not with age but with water-lines; faint horizontal traces where the spray has run down it year after year, following the same paths the water follows on the poured walls.

He is deaf in the way the room requires. Not fully - that would be dangerous - but selectively. The whine of the green pumps at that tooth-aching pitch, the thunder of the spillway, the roar that has no source. He doesn't hear it anymore. Or rather, he hears it with his body. He stands with one hand always lightly on something that vibrates - the railing, the housing of Pump One, the steel grating - and he reads the room through the soles of his boots and the bones of his hand. When the pressure switch is about to clunk, his fingers twitch a half-second before it does. When the overflow gutter chokes on a leaf of scum, he is already moving toward it, not because he heard it, but because the floor's shudder changed by a fraction.

He moves rarely and efficiently. There is so much empty space between the two pumps, between the two caged lights, between the intake slit and the drain, that to move without purpose would be obscene. He checks the gauges - their needles trembling violently behind glass - with a rag that never gets dry. He scrapes the brown-green algae slick from the waterline with a flat shovel, not to clean it, but to keep a foothold. He watches the dark-green film of moss in the corner where the grey light falls. He does not encourage it. He does not remove it. He lets it be, because it is the only other thing here that has decided to stay without being bolted down.

He sleeps here, after a fashion. Not in a bed - there is no room for a bed in a single pocket - but in a steel chair bolted near the door, with his boots still on, his head tipped back where the ceiling weeps least. He sleeps between cycles. Ninety seconds of true quiet when both pumps idle and only the dripping and the sheeting and the cloud pressing at the slit remain. Then the clunk, the scream, the surge, and he is awake again without ever having seemed to wake. He eats cold food from a tin he keeps on top of the warmer pump housing, so the food is never cold, never hot.

He is young, like the room is young. The anchor bolts still have clean threads. The corners of the spillway are still ninety degrees and have not yet been rounded by years of water. So too the Keeper's joints are still sharp, still unweathered. He hasn't yet learned to be worn down. But he is learning. You can see where his shoulders have begun to stoop to the exact height of the low ceiling, where his gait has become the slow slosh that doesn't try to beat the sound.

He does not welcome you when you open the steel door. He doesn't notice you the way a host does. He looks at you the way the basin looks at incoming water: you are more volume to be moved through. He will wait for you to leave, because everyone leaves. The alternative is to go down with the water, vertical through the penstock into the mountain's black throat to turn a turbine you will never hear, and he knows you won't do that.

There are other residents, even sparser and more patient, that he tends without thinking of them as alive.

The Twins - the two green pumps - are the lungs. They are hot to the touch despite the cold, sweating oil and water, screaming whenever they breathe. They live on pressure and hate stillness. If they stop, the basin overfills, the room drowns, the mountain forgets how to exhale. They are the noisiest living things here and the most needy.

And the Film - the moss, the lichen, the algae. A hand-sized smear of dark green, a few pale threads, a treacherous slick. It does not hear, does not need to hear. It drinks only mist. It asks for nothing but the dimmest light and the coldest seep, and it will be here long after the Keeper's threads have rusted and his yellow shell has finally rotted through, still patiently clinging to the concrete grain left by the formwork.

That is the ecology of a room that thinks it is the outside. One wet man becoming concrete, two iron lungs that think they are hearts, and a scattering of green that never needed to be anything else. All economical. All contained. All breathing together while the cloud presses sideways against the slit and the water keeps coming and never stops.
