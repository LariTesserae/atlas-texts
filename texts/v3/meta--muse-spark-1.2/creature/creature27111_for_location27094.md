At first you think nothing lives there.

That's the proper trick of the bowl. It looks inhabited and empty at the same time — like a town right after everyone has stepped outside. The streets are there. The houses are there. The roofs are intact and the gutters run. But there is no smoke, no footprint that stays longer than the few minutes it takes the moss to rebound, no sound that is not water or stone or slow wood. If you stand still long enough the cold makes you think the place is dead in the way a cold place is often dead: too high, too wet, too dim.

Then you notice the wrongness that made you think "town" in the first place.

A plant city should be random. This one isn't.

The willow-houses are not scattered where a seed happened to fall. They are spaced. They lean away from the prevailing seep the same way, they arch to make a consistent head-height that has nothing to do with snow-load. The root-streets don't just fork; they *join*. A thick cord of living willow root runs like a main avenue from the warm spring on the east wall to the deepest peat hollow, and every smaller root feeds into it at an angle too regular to be erosion. The thatch-canopy is thatched. Somebody layers it. Where a vine has snapped under ice, it has been re-woven, the living ends tucked back into living bark while they were still bleeding sap. No cutting. No tying. Just guiding.

You are looking for the inhabitant and you keep looking past it. Because the inhabitant looks exactly like the city.

They are not in the houses. They *are* the houses, partially. Or rather, they are the part of the bowl that learned to walk a little.

If you crouch down where the lichen light is strongest — inside one of those low, smooth-walled willow vaults where the air is a degree warmer and the condensate beads and falls like slow rain from ceiling to floor — and you let your eyes go out of focus, you will see that not all of the moss is moss.

You have to unlearn scale again. From the rim they looked like buildings. Up close the buildings look like buildings. The people look like cushions.

They are small. That is the first accommodation to the bowl. Nothing here gets tall. The tallest willow arch is maybe to your chest. The people never got taller than your knee. When they are still, which is most of the time, they are indistinguishable from the cushion plants that dome the floor: tight, hard mounds of tiny, overlapping leaves, black-green on the outside, furred with water beads, perfectly round as if trimmed. You will have stepped near a dozen before you realize one of them is watching you, because watching is not done with eyes.

Then one moves.

It doesn't get up. It *reforms*. The dome shivers, and the leaves on one side peel back, not like skin but like a fern unrolling. Underneath is not flesh. It is a paler green, almost translucent, a wet matte surface that condenses water the way everything else does, but faster. No mouth you can see. No nose. Deeper inside the rosette, where the leaves are youngest and still curled, there is a knot of tissue the color of peeled birch that pulses very slowly. That's the warm part. If you put your hand near it — and you will, because your hands are freezing — you feel the same faint heat that comes off the exposed roots in the street. Sap-warm. Living warm.

When it decides to go somewhere, it doesn't walk. It *extends*. A lower fringe of leaves pinken and swell with water, pressing into the peat, rooting for a moment. Then the body behind it draws forward, the way a cushion plant inches over rock over years, but here you can see it happen in minutes. A slow, gliding, root-and-release, leaving behind a faint brighter trail where the peat was compressed and licked with clear slime. They are not fast. They have never needed to be fast. The bowl is three hundred paces across, and a whole life can be spent crossing it twice.

They are not one species. That is the second thing you have to unlearn. In the bowl you cannot tell where one organism ends and another begins, and they don't either.

The mound-people are moss, but also willow, but also fungus. Lift the edge of one — if one lets you, and some will, unfolding for you with a cold, trusting patience — and you will see the threads. White fungal hyphae as fine as cobweb lace the underside, running from the mound's belly directly into the peat, into the root-street, into the wall of the willow-house itself. They are all plugged into the same circuit. The warm spring on the east wall is their heart. After the cloud lowers and the dim goes bluer, they gather there, not in a huddle but in a mat, their edges touching, their leaf-rosettes opening and closing in unison, exhaling that one breath the whole hamlet takes at noon. You can hear it then, the creak you thought was wood.

It is them, growing.

They tend. That is their entire culture, if you can call something so slow a culture. They do not build. They persuade. A young willow shoot is stroked — with those pale, wet leaf-hands that are not hands — for days, always bent a little further toward the shape of a lintel, until the wood remembers. A gap in the canopy that lets too much drip through is closed by coaxing a liverwort to make a leaf twice its normal size, cupping it. A root-street that has choked with peat is cleared not by digging but by persuading the sphagnum to die back for a hand's breadth, which it does, politely, because they all share the same water.

They have no tools because tools would be a severance. No cut ends anywhere in the bowl, because a cut is an exile. No fire, because nothing here will burn — strike a match and it hisses and drowns — and they have no word for it, no scorch-mark in any willow vault. Light is what the lichen gives. Heat is what the sap gives. Time is measured not in days but in condensations; they bead and drip, bead and drip.

If you stay quiet and low, a few will come to you. Not out of curiosity the way an animal is curious. Out of their logic of occupation. Every surface must be occupied two or three times over. Your boot, your coat, your hair, beading with that atmosphere that precipitates onto everything — you are a new surface. They will climb you as slowly as they do everything else, and you will feel only a cool weight, like damp velvet on your ankle, on your wrist. Under the outer leaves their bodies are soft and give like soaked bread. They will taste the salt on you and find it novel, then seal you over with a thin, green film that will itch a little and then keep you warmer than before.

They do not speak. On a windy night outside you might think the sigh of cloud against the rim is language. Inside, they speak the way the bowl speaks: through water pressure. When one near the warm spring drinks deeply, a root two hundred feet away swells a millimeter. That is a sentence. That is how they say *the seep is strong today* or *mend the thatch on the north arch* or *a stranger is sitting in the third house and is not intolerant of being covered*.

They were not always this way. Or maybe the bowl was not. You can see, if you peel back moss from the basalt lip, a thin line of charcoal under the peat that no plant put there. Something tried to live here once with axes and nails and was kindly, completely, grown over. The willows remember being forced and did not like it. These soft architects are what came after — what the place allowed. Some are old. In the largest willow vault, where the lichen fur is thickest, there is a mound twice the size of the others, so integrated into the wall that its dome is the wall. When it breathes, the whole house creaks. The others arrange themselves around it like furniture around a hearth, and in the deepest blue hour it gives off a little more light than the rest.

You will leave before dark, because if you don't, the cloud will seal the rim and you will lose the path down through the scree. When you look back from the lip, the bowl will already be erasing you. The root-street where you walked will be plumping up, the footwell filling with water and moss. The thatch will settle. A few of the mounds will have shifted to cover the place where you sat, because an empty space is inefficient, and wasteful, and cold.

And for a moment, before the cloud closes, you will see the whole hamlet at once — no larger than a hamlet, no smaller than a world — breathing under its lid of luminous weather, saturated, occupied, tended by a people who never learned to be separate from their houses, who grow their rooms around them and their bodies into their rooms, until house and person and street are one continuous, patient, photosynthetic thought, waiting out the mountain's young, angry weather in the only way anything survives up there:

by staying low, and wet, and together.
