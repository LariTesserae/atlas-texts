They do not live *on* it.

That is the first thing to understand. Nothing here lives on the plain the way a deer lives on a meadow or a weed lives on a lot. Everything here *is* the plain, or a fold of it, or a function of it. The distinction between rock and animal has worn as thin as the rock itself.

But if you need to separate them — if you need to give them names to hold them still — this is what you'll find:

### 1. The Pavement

They are the country.

At first you think there are millions. When your eyes adjust you understand there is one thing with millions of edges. The dominant animal is not an animal at all in the way you mean. Call them **Lithomorphs**.

Each unit is from a fingernail to a dinner plate across, low and domed, like a river stone that decided to be soft on one side. On top: a carapace that isn't grown but *made*. They gather the flour-fine dust — which is itself half rock and half the powdered frass and shed cement of their ancestors — and bind it with a protein mucilage they exude from their mantle. It sets like weak concrete. Pitted, matte, the exact bone-sand color of the domes, with a faint rust-mottling that is not decoration but colonies of iron-oxidizing bacteria they farm in the pores. The edge of each carapace is fringed with a circlet of glassy cilia, colorless, that interlock with the cilia of its neighbors. That's why they tessellate so perfectly. They are holding hands.

Underneath, they have no legs. They have a foot — a single, broad, pale muscle like a limpet's — and around the foot, a frill. A wet, cream-colored veil of folded tissue that looks like a lung turned inside out. Every twenty to forty seconds, each fringe dilates, kisses the stone or the damp sand, and contracts. *Ah.* You heard it. That is not breathing. It is drinking. The frill is hydrophilic to an absurd degree. In the cold hour before dawn, when every dome sweats and the swales darken, the frill condenses water straight out of the air and laps it, with the bacterial film and the dissolved silica and the microscopic spores that have settled overnight. By noon, when the seep has retreated, they have closed. The fringe tucks under the carapace, the cilia seal, and they become stone again to wait.

They move, but you will accuse yourself of imagining it. A Lithomorph never lifts its foot. It oozes, a millimeter an hour, toward a humidity gradient only it can feel. The pavement as a whole flows like a very slow glacier toward wherever the morning lace of water was darkest. That's why the pattern of seams is different each afternoon. They are not individuals navigating; they are a sheet responding to thirst. Where two domes lean together and a basin holds water until ten a.m., they pile three deep, shingled like scales, the ones on top filtering first, the ones below filtering what drips through.

They do not eat. They filter and they photosynthesize a little, or rather their tenants do. The pitting on their backs is a garden of black lichen and cyanobacteria. In the brutal vertical light, that garden makes sugar. At night, the Lithomorph reabsorbs some of it.

They do not reproduce often. Once, maybe twice a year when a real rain comes — a sudden, brief hammering that for five minutes turns the swales into true shallow rivers — the pavement shudders. Every carapace lifts a fraction and releases a puff of rust-colored spores from under its fringe. The water carries them a few inches, sometimes a few feet. Most land on another carapace and die. A few land in a bare seam and cement their first grain.

When they die, they don't fall over. They just stop opening. The cilia go stiff. Wind and the feet of their neighbors slowly abrade the carapace back into dust, which the neighbors then pick up and use again. Nothing is wasted here because there has never been anything to waste.

If you lie down and put your palm flat on them, they will not flee. After a minute, the warmth of your hand will make the frills beneath it open, tasting your sweat.

### 2. The Ones Who Walk Without Touching

You have to be careful to call them predators, because they so rarely kill.

**Stiltwalkers** — the locals, if you had a local, would not have a name for them because naming is a kind of sharpness and nothing sharp lasts here. They are pale, the color of the underside of a Lithomorph, which is not a coincidence. Four, sometimes six, impossible legs like dry grass stems, jointed in reverse, holding a body the size of a terrier a foot above the pavement. The body is a knot of chitin and folded membrane, almost nothing to it. A head that is only a needle.

They walk with a deliberation that looks like reverence. Each foot is a splayed pad the size of a coin that fits exactly into the seams between Lithomorphs. They never step on a carapace. Not out of kindness; a Lithomorph carapace can't hold their weight without cracking, and the crack would leak precious moisture. So they have learned to step only in the cracks, picking their way for hours across a single swale.

The needle probes. Not to spear the Lithomorph — their fringe would seal and the needle would blunt. It probes to steal the thieves. In the damp seam-humus live true mites, springtails, nematodes, tiny white things that graze the bacterial film before the Lithomorph can filter it. The Stiltwalker sips them with a tongue of negative pressure. Occasionally it will delicately pluck a juvenile Lithomorph the size of a lentil, still soft before it has made its first cement, and dissolve it. Otherwise it is a gleaner, a shepherd that keeps the pavement clean in exchange for taking a tithe.

They make no sound because they have no stridulation, no hard parts that tap. When two meet, they touch needles and exchange a droplet of water mouth to mouth.

### 3. The Ones Who Polish

On the bald backs of the domes where the pavement thins — too dry, too exposed even for Lithomorphs — you find the gloss.

It is a trail, six inches wide, that catches the sun. Following it, you find the maker: a **Glider**, the length of your forearm, the shape of a slug that forgot to be a slug. No shell, no face. Just a low, muscular loaf, translucent amber, with a foot that secretes not slime but a thin oil that does not evaporate. It moves over naked stone, lapping the black crust lichen with a broad, radular mouth on its belly. Where it has passed, the stone is faintly brighter, polished, and for an hour the trail is damp enough that a Lithomorph at the edge will creep toward it to drink the oil before it dries.

The Gliders are the only thing here that leaves a mark that is not immediately reused. But even their bodies are mostly water. If you touch one — and you shouldn't — it feels cool, tighter than you'd expect, like a water balloon filled with warm honey. They do not flee either. They simply stop, and wait for your shadow to pass. They have no eyes. They navigate by feeling the stone's stored heat.

At midday they wedge themselves into north-facing cracks and seal their breathing pore with a wax plug.

### 4. The Ones Who Are Almost Not Animals

Two or three meters up, where the air should be empty, there are **Veils**.

You thought they were plastic bags at first, or thistledown. They are bladders, a foot to three feet across, ribbed like a jellyfish, transparent except for a faint iridescence. They do not flap or buzz. They float, because they are filled with hydrogen — made in a gut chamber by symbiotic bacteria that split water — and warmed a degree above ambient by the same bacterial garden that colors the Lithomorphs. In air this thin, that is enough.

From their bellies hang five or six threads, twenty feet long, almost invisible, beaded with adhesive droplets. They drift on the sigh-wind that never gusts, threads brushing the pavement as lightly as spider silk. The droplets collect the airborne spores, the mites that get caught in updrafts, the dust of dead Lithomorphs. Then, slowly, a thread reels itself up, hand over hand without hands, and wipes its catch across a slit mouth.

They are completely silent because silence is buoyancy. To beat a wing would be to spend water.

At dusk you will see them sink a little, closer to the stone, to conserve heat. At dawn they rise. They never go higher than ten feet. Above that, the wind would take them off the table and they would never find their way back to dew.

### 5. The Spirals

And then there are the things that remember how to make a shape.

On the driest dome-backs, above the reach of even the morning seep, are the low spirals. They are not made by Stiltwalkers or Gliders or Veils. They are made by the Pavement itself, when a patch of Lithomorphs decides to stop being pavement for a while.

No one sees them build. You leave a dome in the evening bare, you return two mornings later and there is a new spiral, a meter across, a single whorl of agglutinated dust the height of your ankle, walls paper-thin but hard as fired clay. The opening is at the center, a fist-wide shaft that goes straight down into cooler earth, into a chamber that stays damp all day.

Inside is air that is ten degrees cooler and smells of moss. Inside is alive, but not with a creature you can see. The walls are lined with living fringe — the same cream-colored veil as under a Lithomorph, but grown huge and colony-thick, respiring slowly. The spiral is a lung, a condenser. Its shape forces the sigh-wind to spiral down and cool, dropping its water on the chamber walls. It is a well that makes its own water without rain.

And in the chamber, in the damp dark, the pavement reproduces in a different way. It buds. The communal veil grows little nodules that detach and creep out at night as soft, shell-less crawlers, no bigger than woodlice, to find a new seam and begin cementing their first grain.

The empty spirals are not empty. Put your ear to the opening and you will hear that same *ah* — but deeper, slower, once a minute — as the whole structure breathes. Put your hand near it on a hot afternoon and it will exhale coolness onto your palm. The pavement flows around the spirals because the spirals are the only place where the pavement is allowed to be vertical, to be architecture instead of skin.

Some spirals are old, their walls collapsed, their chamber filled with dust. Even those breathe a little, as the dust holds moisture longer than bare rock. The plain is dotted with them to the haze, a quiet city with no streets, where every house is a throat.

---

If you sit until the light goes short and the domes sweat again, you will feel what lives here most of all.

It is not a species. It is a behavior: *to stay.* To not evaporate. To take the thinnest possible film of water and hold it, filter it, use it twice, and return it to the air a little cooler. The Lithomorphs and the Stiltwalkers and the Gliders and the Veils and the Spirals are not five kinds of animal. They are five organs of a single, table-wide body that learned, when the mountains wore down, that the only way to keep living high and warm and dry was to become a second geology — slow, silent, tessellated, and breathing together, once every half minute, across a country that has forgotten how to do anything quickly.

Stay still long enough and your boots will be part of the seam.
