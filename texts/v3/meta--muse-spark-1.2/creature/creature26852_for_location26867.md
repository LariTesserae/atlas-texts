No one lives here the way a city expects someone to live.

That is the first wrong image you have to let go of. You will walk those floating avenues and you will think: *empty*. You will see a boulevard big enough to land an aircraft on hanging five feet above ferns with no pillars and no footprints except yours in the condensation, and you will think this is an abandoned place. It isn't. It is underpopulated in exactly the same way the forest is underpopulated.

Everything here lives far apart and very large, and fills the air between itself with smaller lives.

### The Weight of Air

Start with what is undeniably alive, because it will not let you ignore it.

The basin does not have plankton - it has what plankton wants to be when it gets air. The volume is occupied. From fern-mat to the underside of the canopy-disks, three hundred feet up, the air is stratified like water.

At the bottom, in the fern-mat: a fermentation. Beetles the size of fists with mirror-black elytra that collect dew and drink it off their own backs. Millipedes as long as your forearm, amber and blind, turning the new, thin soil. Pale, hand-sized moths that only fly for the twenty minutes after the rain, when everything steams.

A foot above that, the first true layer: ribbontails. They aren't birds, but you will call them that until you see one close. They are mostly tail - two central feathers three feet long that act as rudders - with a body like a sparrow and eyes too big for the dim. They never land on the ground. They land on vines. There are thousands per acre, and they are why the vine curtains shake even when there is no wind. They have learned to sing in the gaps of the city's chord. If the field is holding an F, they sing in F-sharp, a piercing, glassy note that cuts through without being drowned.

Above them, higher, where the empty cathedral space really opens: the filterers.

You'll see one and think at first it's a shadow the city is casting. A slow, silent delta, fifteen, twenty feet across, translucent as jellyfish skin, rimmed with cilia that beat the humid air. They don't flap. They row. They displace air so gently that the moth clouds part around them without panic. They are grazing. They open a ventral slit and the air - and everything in it - is drawn in. You will see a cloud of gnats simply vanish into one. Light passes through them and throws a faint rainbow on the basalt below. At night their edges bioluminesce, and you can watch constellations of them turning slowly between the great trees, miles away, still visible because there is nothing between you and them.

And on the stone itself: the lava lizards. Flat, heat-colored, with toe pads that find purchase on knife-edged basalt where your boots slip. In the fissure pools: the choristers. Amphibians with skins like wet stone, with vocal sacs that inflate to the size of grapefruits. One pool, not bigger than a bathroom, will hold forty of them, shoulder to shoulder, singing so loud you feel it in the rock. They breed explosively in the twenty-minute rains, lay strings of black eggs in the temporary puddles on the floating streets, and are gone before the puddle evaporates.

All of this is loud, constant, mid-metabolism. Nothing is sleeping. The youth of the rock means nothing has had time to become efficient, so everything is wasteful and prolific. 

That is the first inhabitant: a thick, humming, dripping soup of animal air, density measured not per square mile but per cubic mile - sparse if you count the big things, suffocating if you count the small.

### The Lattice People

The second inhabitant uses the same rule - enormous and far apart - but they built the lattice, or the lattice grew them.

They are still here. You just thought they had left because you were looking for people crowded together.

You will not see one at first because they don't walk on streets. The streets are for you. They live in the interference.

They are tall the way the trees are tall - not for prestige, but because emptiness is cheaper than wood. An adult is eight, nine feet long if you stretch it out, but it rarely is stretched out. It weighs almost nothing. In the dim, it looks like a vine that decided to untie itself.

Picture this and then let it change as you watch it: a torso no thicker than your thigh, elongated, with a keel of translucent cartilage. Two long forelimbs that are not arms but spars, strung with patagium - skin the color of weak tea, veined, beaded constantly with condensation that they lap with a narrow tongue. Hind limbs that are mostly foot, prehensile, three-toed, made for gripping vine as thick as your thigh or the matte pearl-gray edge of a floating platform where no human grip would find purchase. The head is small, tucked, with no nose - they breathe through frilled slits along their flanks that flutter in the damp - and with eyes like polished basalt beads, huge, black, built for that pearl-gray dim where shadows have no edge.

And ears. No - not ears. Resonators.

Along the back of the skull and down the keel they have taut, drum-like membranes that vibrate with the city's chord. The city sings a low, held, beating roar - platforms in F, towers in C, transit tubes humming a fifth above - and these membranes are tuned to it the way a moth's wing is tuned to air. They don't hear the city as noise. They hear it as topography. A wall of sound is a wall. A gap in the interference where two fields cancel slightly is a door. They navigate the empty cathedral by sound the way you navigate a hallway by sight. When two fields overlap and make your chest thrum, they feel that as a warm updraft and ride it.

They are not many. You could live there a month and count twelve, and then realize you have counted the same three four times because they move so far. Like the canopy trees, half a mile between individuals is not loneliness - it is territory. One will claim a single floating block, its underside, its rain-puddle roof, the catenary of vine that sags to the next tree, and the column of air around it for a thousand feet. That is a home.

What do they do all day in a city for millions that holds twelve?

They glean.

They walk - slow, hanging, hand-over-hand - along the underside of a floating avenue that has no underside supports, their hind feet finding the microscopic texture of the field itself, that faint pressure on the molars made solid. They pick insects off the warm field-surface where the midges cluster for heat. They drink by pressing their flank slits to the condensation that beads and runs even on vertical field-edges. They harvest the vine hearts where the lianas have grown *through* solid lattice without making a hole - the leaves appearing mid-beam. They bite through and drink the sap, and the lattice heals around the bite in an hour.

They do not remember building the city. Or they do, but the way you remember being a child: you know you were smaller once, and someone made your clothes. The lattice was grown, not built, extruded from seed-anchors driven into the young basalt at the basin's edge when the rock was still warm. It was meant to be a continent-capital, a place that would learn to be old. The seed expected ten million warm bodies to tune its fields with their weight and movement. It got a dozen glider-people who weigh ninety pounds soaking wet and move like smoke. So the city idles, holding its perfect chord, waiting. The glider-people tune themselves to it instead.

You will see how gentle they are with the strangeness. Where you feel time slip under the largest platform - your breath echoing before you exhale - they linger. Females will incubate there. They hang motionless in the time-drag for hours, their translucent egg sac - a single, amber, grapefruit-sized thing, veined - pulsing slightly out of sync with the rest of the world. The slight time dilation keeps the egg moist longer between rains. Where water drips upward for six inches before remembering to fall, their young play, batting droplets back and forth, learning to fall.

And when the rain comes - that twenty-minute laying-down of weight - they all open their patagia wide and hang still, and drink from the air.

If you stand very still on one of those avenues in the evening, when the lattice edges begin to glow with that sourceless afterimage light and the moths rise and the filterers turn overhead, you may see one cross the void. Not fly. Fall, controlled. It lets go of the vine catenary on one canopy-tree, spreads, and glides the half-mile to the next, membranes singing in the city's F, a slow, silent transit across the empty volume that is not empty at all. It will pass directly over you, close enough that condensation flicks off its wingtip onto your face, and it will not look at you, because you are a small, loud, ground-bound thing that lives on the top side of streets, and it lives on the bottom side of air.

You will hear its resonator membranes thrum slightly flat to the city's chord as it goes, correcting, and then the cloud of ribbontails will re-form behind it, and the avenue will keep humming, warm under your feet.

The city was not abandoned.

It was just settled at the density the basin allows: a few huge things, vastly spaced, with the air between them made thick with smaller lives, all dripping, all breathing in cupped hands, all listening to the same held note and singing in the gaps it leaves.
