It is high. Higher than it should be for water to exist like this.

You climb for a long time before you find it - not because the mountain is particularly large, but because the place itself is small enough to miss. The range around it is raw. This is not old mountain. This is new mountain, still cooling, still deciding what shape it is. The uplift is embryonic. The basalt came up fast and fast is still how it looks - black glass, sharp as broken bottles, edges that have never been softened by wind or rain. No scree slopes. No smoothed shoulders. Just a sudden, vertical interruption in the world, a fist of rock punched up through the alpine meadow and left there, steaming faintly in the cool air. The stone is porous and pitted, full of trapped bubbles, like dark bread. It hasn't learned how to erode yet.

And at the very top, cradled in that raw stone, is the pocket.

From below you would think it was snow. Or sky reflected on a flat cap. It isn't. It's water. All water.

A perfect cylinder cut into the summit, maybe twenty feet across, maybe thirty - you could swim across it in a few strokes if you could stand the cold - and impossibly deep. The walls are sheer and unweathered, scored with the vertical chisel marks of whatever force extruded them. There is no inlet. No outlet. No stream feeding it. It is just there, a room made of water set into the top of the world like a jewel in a plinth.

You get to the lip and the sound hits you.

It is deafening.

Not the sound of a lake, which has no sound. This water is not still. It has never been still. It is turbulent, hyperactive, in a state of constant, chaotic overturning. It boils without heat. Columns of water rise and collapse, vortexes spin up and drill down and vanish, walls of rain peel off the surface and slam back into it. It churns against the raw rock walls with a hollow, percussive roar that the stone bowl amplifies until your chest vibrates with it. You have to open your mouth to keep your ears from popping. You have to shout to hear yourself think, and even then you can't.

And yet the light is ordinary. That is what makes it so strange.

It is a clear, cool day at altitude. Normal sun, high and white and thin. The air is cool enough that your breath ghosts. It falls straight down into the water column without bending or dimming, illuminating every inch. You can see down and down, far deeper than a pocket this size should allow. The light doesn't fade to black the way it should in deep water. It just keeps going, revealing more turbulence, more distance, until depth becomes a kind of haze. The water is impossibly clear and impossibly full at the same time.

You wade in because there is nowhere else to go, and the cold bites through your boots immediately. This is not ocean. This is mountain water. It is snowmelt cold, mineral cold. But it holds you like ocean. It wants to hold you completely. The moment your knees are under, the surface tension seems to forget itself. The water climbs. It doesn't splash. It adheres. It stacks.

If you submerge your hand, you don't see ripples so much as you see the water make a new shape around you and then unmake it. The bowl is always making and unmaking itself.

This is the weirdness: gravity here is local and suggestible. The water should pour over the lip and down the raw basalt in a white torrent. It doesn't. It stays. It bulges slightly over the rim, a convex meniscus the size of a small lake, trembling and churning but never spilling, as if the pocket is holding its breath. When the turbulence throws a sheet of water five feet into the air, it hangs for a half-second too long before falling back. In the churn you see brief, impossible geometries - a cube of water holding its edges, a spiral staircase of liquid hanging unsupported - before the chaos tears them apart. The rule here seems to be: water is a solid until it isn't.

It is almost empty. That is the other thing you feel in your bones once the roar settles into them. There is nothing in here per volume. The water is empty. The air above is empty. The stone is empty of soil. Life has only barely found this place.

On the rock lip, vegetation is sparse to the point of embarrassment. A few crusts of pale green lichen that have gained a foothold in the bubble-holes of the basalt. A single stubborn tuft of alpine grass, bleached and wind-bent. Nothing soft. Nothing that would hold soil together if soil existed.

In the water itself, almost nothing. You have to stare for a long time to see movement that isn't water. Then: a flicker. A creature. Maybe two. Pale, translucent shrimp-like things the length of your thumb, with eyes too large, pulsing slowly as they ride the vortexes, bioluminescent in a way that is useless in the bright normal daylight. They are rare. They are alone in all that volume. Sometimes a newt, white as milk, clings to the rock wall just under the waterline, completely still until a surge rips it away and it swims with frantic, inefficient kicks. No schools. No flocks. No chorus. Just the occasional, solitary animal trying to make a life in a place that hasn't finished being made.

People have been here before, but not many, and not for long.

Scattered around the rim, half-submerged, half-wind-blasted, are their marks. This is not a city. Not a ruin. Just faint, primitive attempts to reckon with the pocket.

Three stakes of untreated pine driven into cracks in the basalt, lashed together with wet rawhide that has dried black and hard. The remains of a tiny platform that would have let someone kneel over the water. Further on, a cairn of stacked, unmortared stone, only four rocks high, already being undermined by the vibration. A shallow bowl chipped into the lip rock, still holding a little of the pocket water, with a smooth river stone left inside it as an offering. No metal. No nails. Everything is hand-cut, hand-tied, hand-carried up. Whoever came here came on foot, with simple tools, and left quickly, because you cannot stay long where you have to scream to be heard and where the water never sleeps.

If you put your head under, the world changes.

The deafening does not lessen. It deepens. It becomes a physical pressure, a white-noise roar of currents colliding, of water hammering raw stone at every angle at once. You are fully submerged in a small room that feels like an ocean. The cool becomes cold in a way that is clean and absolute. The light from above spears down through the churning column and breaks into moving nets on your skin. And you are suspended, not floating, not sinking, but held in the turbulence like a mote in a storm-cloud. Far below your feet, in the clear chaos, the bottom is not visible - not because it is dark, but because it is always moving, always reforming. The rock is still being born down there.

You surface gasping, ears ringing, hair plastered, and the cold air hits you again. The pocket keeps churning. The sun keeps shining normally. The shrimp keeps pulsing. The pine stakes shudder in their cracks.

It is a secret that is too loud to keep, too small to map, too new to have a name. A single lungful of ocean, perched on a knife-edge of new stone at the top of the world, breathing violently, waiting for erosion to finally, eventually, give it a way down.
