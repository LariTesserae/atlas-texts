High up, where the air goes thin and mean.

You don't find it by climbing toward a summit. You find it by noticing the mountain has a seam in it.

The main range here is a black, wind-scoured spine of basalt and frost-shattered granite, an extreme elevation where even lichen gives up. The light is poor and comes sideways. The sun never clears the ridge properly, just drags itself along the rim far above, and the sky is a deep, bruised violet, too close. Breathing is work. Sound carries too far. Everything outside this place is stripped and eroded to its essentials - broad, weathered shoulders of rock that have long since settled into the shape they intend to keep. No new slides, no fresh scree. The wind has polished the stone to a soft, pitted leather. It is a landscape that has finished becoming.

And then the seam.

A crevasse, no wider than a doorway, that you could walk past a hundred times and think is just shadow. The cold pours out of it, not as wind but as a stillness. You have to turn sideways to slip in. Inside, the rock opens just enough to make a room.

That's the scale of it. Not a valley. Not a forest. A single hidden pocket, perhaps fifteen meters long and six across, walled on all sides by sheer, vertical stone that goes up thirty meters to a slit of sky. A place embedded inside the mountain, contained. From the outside it doesn't exist. From inside, the outside barely exists.

The air in the pocket is cool, perpetually cool, the kind of cool that lives in deep stone even in summer. Your breath makes a brief ghost and vanishes. There is no damp to it. No water at all. The stone is absolutely dry, a desert dryness despite the cold. No seep, no drip, no ice. The floor is a fine, compacted dust of basalt flour and salt, pale as ash, that doesn't hold a footprint for long because nothing moves it. If you kneel and press your palm to it, it is cool and utterly arid, sucking the oil from your skin.

And yet it is packed, choked, saturated with vegetation.

Not the green you expect. This is a lushness that has solved the problem of water by refusing it.

The walls and floor and even the ceiling of the crevice are covered in a dense, impenetrable thatch of growth. It leaves no room for anything else. Fronds of black iron-fern unroll from cracks, their leaves not leaf at all but overlapping scales of dark, fibrous mineral, thin as paper, dry as rust. From them hangs a moss, or what looks like moss - a deep, dusty emerald and copper wool that grows in matted bolsters a foot thick, swallowing the stone. Thicker trunks, if you can call them that, rise like charcoal reeds, segmented, bristling with needle-leaves of tarnished brass that chime faintly when they touch. Every surface is occupied. There is no bare rock left to see. The plants grow into each other, through each other, frond piercing frond, rootless, pressing tight. You cannot push between them without bending that dry, crackling mass. It yields and springs back.

They are dry to the touch. They do not wilt. They do not drink. When you break a stem - and it snaps with a clean, metallic *tick* - the inside is not wet or sappy, but a dense felt of grey fibers, faintly warm, smelling of ozone and hot dust. This is a flora that feeds on air and stone and static. In the cool, thin atmosphere, fine mineral dust hangs perpetually suspended and the plants comb it from the air. Condensation of industrial origin does the rest. There are no animals here to carry pollen, to chew leaves, to rot fallen matter. Nothing has ever eaten here. And so nothing has ever needed to regrow. Fallen fronds simply do not fall. Dead growth remains standing, interlocked with living growth, until you cannot tell which is which. It is a forest without birth or death, a packed, held breath of vegetation.

Which makes sense when you hear it.

The pocket is loud. Unreasonably loud for a still place.

Set into the stone at intervals, half-swallowed by the copper moss, are machines. Scattered, not as a complex, just as isolated artifacts left where they were bolted. A pump housing the size of a chest, its gauge glass blind with age. A gantry of riveted steel that spans from wall to wall, its catwalk now a trellis for the iron ferns. A transformer box, humming. A low, broad engine block seated in the dust at the far end, with a flywheel that turns and turns and turns.

They are all running. They have always been running.

There is no fuel line you can see. No exhaust. Just a deep, even, industrial roar that fills the tiny room and makes the brass leaves shiver. The flywheel turns without wavering. The pump thuds at a constant 40 beats per minute. The transformer hums a perfect, unwavering low C that you feel in your molars. Electrified air tastes faintly metallic on your tongue. A string of amber work-lights, caged in wire, runs along the gantry and glows dimly, not flickering, not bright enough to throw sharp shadows. Just enough to make the metallic leaves glint dully.

And none of it ever changes.

That is the rule of this place. It is in equilibrium. The sound never gets louder or softer. The lights never buzz or dim. The flywheel has no variation. The plants do not sway in a breeze because there is no breeze; the air pressure holds perfectly steady. The temperature never drops at night, never rises at noon. The dim light from the slit far above never shifts as the sun moves; it is always the same late-afternoon gloom, as if time outside has been paused and only this pocket's own time keeps going, looping on a single, perfect second.

Erosion here finished a long time ago. The rock is weathered smooth under the moss. The machines are rusted to the exact point where rust stops progressing. Even the dust on the floor does not settle differently. You can stand for an hour and realize you have not heard a single irregular noise, not a tick, not a pop. No animal calls, no insect whine - there is no fauna, not even a microbe you can sense. The absence is absolute. No birds would dare the thin air to reach this slit, and even if they did, there is no water to drink, no soft leaf to eat.

This lushness is strange because it is not alive the way lushness should be. It is a mineral lushness, a still-life that grew until it filled every cubic centimeter of available space and then stopped, in perfect balance with the machines that feed it charge and vibration. The ferns lean *toward* the transformer hum, not the light. The moss grows thickest around the warmest conduit. The whole pocket feels less like a garden and more like a single, packed organism that is also a factory - vegetation as insulation, machine as heart, rock as shell.

If you stay too long, the loud constancy becomes a kind of silence. The cool presses in. You become aware that you are the only thing here that is still subject to change - your heart beating irregularly, your lungs working the thin air, your need for water that this place cannot and will never provide. The pocket does not need you to witness it. It will be exactly this, dim and loud and cool and impossibly full, with its scattered, humming engines and its dry, crowded forest of iron leaves, long after you have to turn sideways again and slip back out into the thin, empty wind of the high world.
