It is not a desert that goes on forever.

That is the first thing you have to unlearn. From a distance, in the thin air, you expect the high country to open out — a plateau, a plain. But this place has edges. It is a room.

You come to it over a long, established fall of rock. Not jagged. Not new. This is not a fresh break in the earth. The wind has had time here. The heat has had time. The basalt of the mesa has been worked smooth at its shoulders, rounded at its lips, the color gone out of it to a matte, dusty black-red that holds the day's heat long after the light is gone. At 2,000 meters or more, the air should be cold and sharp. It isn't. It is close and dry and still hot, as if the stone itself is exhaling.

You crest the rim and the ground drops away in a single, clean scoop — an old vent, a collapsed blister, a crater that never quite learned to be a volcano. Maybe three hundred meters across, maybe less. Cathedral-scale. Hamlet-scale. You can see the whole of it from one standing place, rim to rim, wall to far wall, and that is what makes it feel strangely interior, like a bowl left out for something.

No water has been here for a long time. Not none at all — there is a memory of it bound up in the salt. The floor of the bowl is not sand but a pan of fractured mineral, pale pinks and yellows of evaporite, alkali crust that pops and sighs under the heat. It is dry in a way that feels chemical, not seasonal. The air takes moisture from your mouth before you can swallow it. Whatever water comes, comes bound inside other things and leaves again without ever pooling, without ever reflecting.

There are no plants. Not even the stubborn ones. No lichen on the basalt, no thorn scrub in the cracks. The heat and the dry and the darkness have edited that whole kingdom out. This is a mineral world, pre- or post-vegetal. The only verticals are stone and salt and the faint, scattered built things: low cairns near the rim, not houses, not ruins exactly. Three or four of them, shoulder-high, made of stacked, wind-scoured slab without mortar. No tools left, no wire, no glass. Someone piled them and left, or something did. They are weathered into the landscape now, the same color as the rim, and you only notice them because their shadows are too regular.

The darkness is the strangest part, because it should be bright. High places are bright. But the bowl is in deep, deep shade. The sun has gone, or the walls are high enough to keep it out for most of the day, or the air above the mesa holds a thin, permanent haze of fine ash that dims everything to a bruised twilight. It is not pitch black — you can see — but it is a darkness that has weight. The sky above the rim is a flat, starless indigo, too thin to scatter light properly. Heat without light. You feel it on your skin before you see where it comes from: the floor itself is warm. Not burning, but well above blood temperature. You could lie down on the salt pan and feel it through your back. In places the cracks breathe a hotter air, a shimmer you can see in the dark.

And the floor moves.

That is the second unlearning. At first you think the salt pan is textured — pebbled, scaled. Then you realize the texture is not salt at all.

It is packed.

From rim to rim, wall to wall, the floor of the bowl is covered. There is no room between presences. A living carpet, a thatch, a shingle of bodies so dense the ground beneath them is only guessed at. This is the fauna, and the word fauna is too familiar for them.

They are not insects, not quite. They are not reptiles. They are about the length of your hand, low and flat, each one a matte-black oval of overlapping plates edged in a dull, hot amber, like cooling slag. No eyes that you can see. Along their undersides, a fringe of fine, pale legs moves constantly, not quite in unison. Thousands. Tens of thousands. The number fails because you cannot count edges. They overlap, they climb over one another in slow, endless circulation, like a single organism that has decided to be many.

They make almost no sound. That is what holds the whole place in its quiet. With that many bodies you expect a roar, a chittering, a rustle like rain. There is only a faint, dry susurrus, the brushing of plate on plate, the soft tick of a leg finding purchase on salt. Mostly quiet, so quiet that when one of them flips — a sudden, quick arch of the body — you hear the tiny *clink* of its carapace and it carries across the whole bowl.

They are not eating plants, because there are none. They are not drinking water, because there is none to drink. The rules here are different. The heat is the food. You watch long enough and you see how it works: the ones near the warmest cracks go still and flatten themselves, their amber edges paling as they absorb. The ones that have fed get restless and circulate outward, toward the cooler walls. And then the hunters move.

Because it is active. This is not a dormant aggregation. It is a whole economy in motion.

Among the flat black plates are other forms, fewer, longer, the same mineral palette but with a raised, glassy ridge down their backs. They move faster, threading through the pack without disturbing it, until they stop, poise, and drive that ridge down like a wedge. The plates scatter — not in panic, but in a fluid parting — and when the long ones lift again they have a soft, pale underside impaled, legs still flexing. No cry. No struggle that makes noise. The rest close over the spot within seconds and the floor is continuous again.

Elsewhere, the flat ones are molting. They shudder and split along a seam you did not know was there, and a slightly larger, slightly softer version pulls itself out, leaving the empty husk behind. The husk is not wasted. Within a minute, two or three neighbors have nosed it and begun to dismantle it, edge by edge, with mouths too small to see. Nothing accumulates. Nothing is wasted in a place with no water to wash it away.

If you stand at the rim long enough, you see the pattern is not random. The whole mass slowly turns. The outer edge creeps clockwise along the bowl walls, the center moves counter, a vast, silent convection cell driven by the heat rising through the salt. The packed density makes them behave like a liquid, like a slow tide of chitin and mineral. The cairns on the rim are above it for a reason. Nothing built on the floor would remain unburied for an hour.

The air smells of hot stone and faint iron, and of something else — a dry, ozone tang, as if the chemistry here runs on something other than water. When you breathe, the back of your throat tastes faintly metallic. The wind, when it does move, is hot and thin and does not stir dust, because there is no dust left to stir; everything loose has already been incorporated, shell or salt.

You understand, after a while, why the light is low and why the place is small and why it can be so crowded without collapse. It doesn't need the sun. It doesn't need rain. It needs the deep heat leaking up through the old vent, and the mineral, and the dark. It is a sealed room that feeds itself. The strange quality is not that it is alien — it is that it is so complete. It has settled into its own state and will remain, established, weathered and in balance, whether anyone looks at it or not. The cairns mean someone once looked, and decided not to stay.

If you throw a pebble down onto the floor — and you will want to, just to test the quiet — it does not clatter. It lands with a soft *thunk* on the living thatch, and the thatch ripples. A perfect circle of disturbance runs out from the impact point and then smooths, as the bodies resettle, shoulder to shoulder, plate to plate, packed again so tight you cannot see the salt between them.

And then, as if offended by the interruption, the whole bowl gives off a slow, collective sigh of heat, and continues turning in the dark.
