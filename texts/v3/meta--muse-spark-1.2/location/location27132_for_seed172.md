It is not outside. Or rather, it is outside in the way an attic is outside - above everything, exposed, but still a room.

You find it by going up until the mountain stops being a mountain.

The massif here is not rock. The mountain was leveled. Or capped. At about four thousand meters where the air goes thin and mean, the peak has been taken off with a saw and replaced with a platform, and on the platform they built the Works, and the Works built over itself until there is no more mountain left to see. In every direction there is more building: low, flat-roofed sheds of corrugated steel, transformer yards, radiator fields like black glass harps standing on end to bleed heat into a sky that never wants it, pipe runs fat as subway cars. But this place is not that. This place is hidden *within* that.

A service pocket. A lung.

You open a hatch that was never labeled and you drop down six rungs into it and the sound hits you first.

### The Room That Roars Without Changing

It is a rectangular void, maybe nine meters long, six wide, four high — the size of a small chapel or a large shipping container, fully embedded on all six sides in the body of the factory. You can feel the weight of the building pressing in from every direction. No stone. No soil. Nothing here was found. Every surface is made.

The floor is open steel grating, galvanized and still sharp at the edges where the punch press left burrs. It has never been worn smooth. Under it, more grating, and under that, darkness and moving air you can't see but can feel as a constant upward pressure. The walls are sandwich panels: outer steel, thick mustard-yellow insulation, inner perforated aluminum that was meant to dampen sound and has completely failed. The ceiling is the same. All of it is bolted with exposed heads the size of your palm, the weld seams still bright, unrusted, untarnished. Because there is no water here to rust them.

At this temperature and elevation nothing rusts in the ordinary way. The air is cold enough to burn. It is the kind of cold that lives in sealed environments — not winter-cold, but refrigeration-cold, a manufactured, maintained, constant -35C that does not swing with day or night. Your breath does not plume and vanish; it falls. It shatters. The vapor flash-freezes into fine diamond dust that ticks against the grating and disappears through the slots. Your nose sticks shut. The metal tastes blue on your tongue even though you haven't licked anything.

This is a dry cold. There is no snow. There is no ice in the way you'd expect. The Works at this altitude runs on non-aqueous chemistry. The cooling loops that thunder around you carry anhydrous ammonia and dense silicone oils, and where they have wept at the joints they have left not stains but crystal beards — white and jaundiced salt-fields, sharp efflorescence that grows against gravity in half-inch needles. They crunch under your boots like frost but they are not water. Nothing here would know what to do with water.

Light is ordinary and therefore strange. Four long fluorescent battens behind frosted polycarbonate, 4000K office-white, buzzing, absolutely even. No shadows to speak of. No sun, though you are higher than any sun should be. Through the intake louvers on the east and west walls you can see a slit of outside — a hard, dark blue sky with a sun that gives no warmth — but the light inside does not change with it. It is day forever at the same intensity.

And the sound.

The deafening is not dramatic. It is not banging or clanging. It is equilibrium. Three industrial blowers, each the size of a car engine, are bolted directly to the grating and to the wall without isolation mounts, and they are moving that cold, dry, ammonia-kissed air through heat exchangers the size of houses somewhere above and below you. The sound is a solid. 115 decibels of perfectly stable white-brown roar that occupies the entire frequency range at once, so loud your teeth rattle and your vision blurs if you clamp your jaw. But it never varies. There is no cycling, no thump, no rhythm. You wait for it to change and it doesn't. After ninety seconds your brain gives up trying to parse it and begins to treat it as silence. You can shout and hear nothing, not even yourself. You feel your voice in your chest and nowhere else.

This is why the vegetation is still here.

### The Sparse Gardens

Because the room is entirely built, nothing grows from the ground. Instead, someone — or some maintenance protocol — placed eight shallow trays on the floor, four along each long wall, out of the main airflow. They are black polypropylene basins, industrial parts-washer trays, each holding a thin bed of mineral wool and volcanic grit. In them grows a sparse, low, and stubborn crop.

It is not moss, not exactly. It is something that can live on cold and light alone. Flat, grey-green rosettes like lichen trying to be a succulent, each leaf rimed with a waxy bloom against desiccation. They do not spread. They sit at intervals, one rosette every hand-span, with lots of empty grit between, exactly spaced as if planted by tweezers. The leaves are brittle. Some have been freeze-dried where they stand and remain upright, dead for months without rotting, because rot needs moisture and microbes and there are neither. No insects come to pollinate them. No birds to eat them. No animal has ever been in this room for longer than you will be. The air is too thin, too cold, too loud, too full of oil vapor. The plants do not care. They just hold on.

Near the north wall one tray has been tipped, perhaps by vibration — though nothing ever vibrates differently here — and its grit has spilled in a neat little alluvial fan across the grating, frozen in place, each grain exactly where it fell. It will stay that way. In a young landscape, sharp things stay sharp. There is no wind inside to round edges, no water to smooth. The spilled grit has not been scattered. The weld spatter on the wall has not been knocked off. The ammonia needles have not broken. This pocket was made very recently as these things are measured — a decade, maybe two — and since then nothing has happened.

That is the rule of this place: **nothing happens.**

It is not abandoned. Abandonment implies decay, entropy, the slow softening of things. This is the opposite. This is a sealed equilibrium. The machines run because they must run. They cool a process that cools them. The light stays on because it was wired to stay on. The cold holds because insulation holds. The sound stays deafening and constant because the fans spin at a locked RPM forever. The sparse rosettes stay sparse because nothing encourages them and nothing kills them.

You stand on the grating and feel the constant updraft pushing at your soles and the roar pushing at your ears and understand that you are inside a machine that is not making anything except the conditions for its own existence. It is industrial, total, high, cold, dry, loud, and perfectly, unnervingly stable. A hidden room near the sky that breathes once and then holds that breath forever.

If you stay, your bootprints in the spilled grit will remain as sharp as the day you made them.

If you leave and dog the hatch, nothing will have changed.
