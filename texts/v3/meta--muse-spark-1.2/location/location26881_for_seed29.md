High up, the air is already wrong.

You have climbed past where any plant will try to hold on. Past the last lichen crust and the last stunted grass, past the scree that slides under your boots, up where the wind has thinned and goes cold and clean in your lungs even while the sun burns. The ridge here is not old. It is new in the way mountains are new — a shoulder of rock that has been shoved up too fast, a blister in the crust that hasn't learned to be a mountain yet.

From a distance it just looks like basalt, dark and broken. Up close you see it hasn't been weathered at all. There are no softened edges, no gullies, no soil. The rock is raw, just-formed. Glassy in places where it cooled too quick, still holding the bubble texture of gas that was trapped as it rose. Sharp. Under construction. The whole elevation feels embryonic, as if the earth made this ridge last week and forgot to erode it. You can see the flow lines, the ripples frozen in stone, the conchoidal fractures where it cracked as it shrank. Nothing has had time to wear it down.

And then there is the mouth. Not a cave mouth the way water makes caves. No dripstone, no carved arch. Just a seam, a vertical split where the cooling skin pulled apart, barely wide enough for a person to turn sideways and slip through. From outside, even at noon, you see nothing inside but dark.

The heat hits you before the darkness does.

Outside the wind is alpine cold. Two feet inside the seam it inverts. The air is suddenly still, dry, and hot — not the damp heat of a low jungle or a spring, but a mineral heat that lives in the stone itself. The rock radiates it. The floor under your hands is warm through your gloves. The air smells like hot iron and salt and struck flint. There is no water here. Not a seep, not a condensation bead, not even the sheen of damp on the walls. What little moisture was ever here baked out long ago. If anything moves as a liquid, it is not water — a slow, bright bead of sulfur that sweats from a seam and hardens before it can drip.

You have to crouch, then crawl, then you are inside.

It is a single room. That is all there is. Not a system, not a network. A pocket. A hidden lung perhaps twelve meters long and four high, an oval bubble left when gas was trapped in the upwelling. You could touch both walls if you stretched. You could see every surface from where you kneel. It is intimate the way a chest cavity is intimate — fully enclosed, self-contained, embedded deep in the mass of the ridge with only that one tight seam to the outside world. The mountain holds it like a geode holds its crystal.

At first your eyes tell you it is empty and dark. There is no sky here, no reflected light. The only light is what the rock itself gives off — a deep, low, sullen red-black glow from deeper down where the chamber's floor is thinnest over the still-hot body below. Not bright enough to see by, just enough to turn the darkness into a very dark maroon, to show you the edges of things. Your headlamp, if you turn it on, seems to be swallowed.

Then your eyes adjust and you understand the room is not empty at all. It is packed.

Every inch is occupied.

The walls, the ceiling, the floor — if you can call it floor — are covered in a continuous, living mat. No plants. Nothing green has ever taken hold in this dry kiln, and nothing will. What covers the stone is animal. Thousands upon thousands of them, shoulder to shoulder, carapace to carapace, so dense there is no room between presences. At a glance you might think it is a texture on the rock itself, a scale or a bark, until a leg twitches and the whole surface ripples.

They are furnace beetles. Or their cousins — low, flat, black-red creatures the size of your thumb joint, with backs like enameled iron and legs like wire. And among them, paler crickets, almost translucent, with long antennae that never stop tasting the hot air. They do not hop. They do not fly. In this heat and crowding they crawl, slowly, deliberately, over and on top of each other in a constant, patient tide.

There is nothing built here. No nest, no hive wall, no web, no human artifact. No tool. The rock is untouched except by them. And yet what they are doing is industrial.

It takes a minute to see the pattern in the slowness. The beetles work the stone. Their mandibles scrape — not eating the rock, but harvesting the bloom of mineral that sweats onto it: sulfur, iron salt, crusts of white and yellow that form and re-form in the heat. They carry grains in their jaws to the warmer center of the chamber where the floor glows dullest, where other beetles are gathered in a dense, churning mill. They press the mineral dust together with a secretion, tamp it, turn it over. The crickets follow behind, laying a gloss over the pressed cakes. Further along, other beetles chew those cakes again, extruding them as hard, black pellets that are pushed to the chamber wall and cemented into place, extending the living floor by a millimeter.

It is a factory floor with no foreman and no building. A conveyor, a stamping press, a kiln — all made of bodies moving in sequence. An assembly line that is not constructed but grown through sheer number and repetition. The beetles at the wall tap the new pellets with their heads in a slow, metronomic hammering to cure them. The sound of one is nothing. The sound of ten thousand is deafening.

In this pocket the sound has nowhere to go. It stays and stacks. A loud, dry, encompassing clatter: chitin on stone, mandible on mineral, leg on carapace, thousands of tiny hammers and looms working at once, plus the deeper, underlying hiss of the rock itself exhaling heat through microfissures. It is not chaotic. It is rhythmic in the way a mill floor is rhythmic — a clack-clack-clack that layers into a roar. You feel it in your molars before you hear it clearly.

And for all that noise, nothing happens fast.

Watch any one beetle and you will think it is barely moving. A leg lifts, holds, sets down. A grain is carried three body-lengths in a minute. A pellet is tapped once, then again thirty seconds later. The work of the whole chamber is immense and ceaseless, but its tempo is geological. The cakes cure over hours. The floor extends a hand's width in a season. The air shimmers with heat and never stirs. Even the beetles' breathing is slow — you can see the faint pulse under their wing covers, patient as bellows.

This is the rule of the pocket: heat without water, abundance without greenness. Life here does not photosynthesize; it chemosynthesizes, it eats stone bloom and air. It does not need light and does not make it. It needs only the steady, slow outward leak of the earth's warmth and a space that has not yet been broken open to wind and rain. It is a normal rule, just a rare one. Nothing about it is alien or impossible; leave any hot, high, fresh bubble of rock sealed in darkness long enough and something will find it and fill it completely, and learn to smelt it the way we learn to smelt ore.

If you stay too long your skin prickles with dry heat, your ears ring with the stamping, your eyes try to find a horizon and fail — there is only warm stone an arm's length away in every direction, crawling with patient industry. When you back out through the seam, the cold thin air of the high ridge hits you like plunging into water, and after a few steps the mountain looks barren again, empty, silent. You would never know the pocket was there unless you had knelt inside it.
