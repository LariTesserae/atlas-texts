It is a bowl at the top of the world that is full to the brim.

You don't expect water at this elevation. The air up here should be thin and shearing, the rock should be wind-scoured and dry. Instead the mountain keeps a sea in its cupped hands.

The cirque is small. You could walk its rim in ten minutes if you could walk on water. From any point in the middle you can see every wall at once — a hamlet-sized amphitheater of stone, close enough that its edges feel like a room. The walls are young. They have not had time to soften. Glacial quarrying has only just left them, and they are still sharp: black basalt and pale granite cleaved into stair-steps and vertical flutes, knife-edged arêtes, columns that broke yesterday in geological time and have not yet shed their splinters. There is no talus slope, no beach, no smoothing. The water meets the rock in a clean, hard line, and where it touches, the stone is still glassy where it fractured.

The water is everything. It is not a lake in the mountain, it *is* the mountain's weather. The cirque is completely submerged — not flooded, not marshy, but drowned under a depth that shouldn't be possible at 3,000 meters. The water is cold, not freezing, but the kind of cool that makes your hands ache after a minute and makes light go straight and clean. And light does come. This is not the dark of the deep sea. The sun is high and normal and white, and it pours through ten meters of utterly clear water to the bottom without dimming. You can see every grain on the floor, every joint in the wall, in noon-bright detail, but refracted, trembling. The surface, far above your head if you sink, is not a surface at all — it is a hanging thickness, a low sky of water that never breaks. Rain does not fall *into* it; it *is* the rain, suspended. The world here ended its dry age a little while ago and hasn't drained.

There are no plants. Not a blade, not a kelp, not a lichen large enough to call green. The rock is bare mineral — iron-black, feldspar-white, sulfur-yellow where it sweats. If there is color it comes from bacterial varnish, a slick of rust-red and manganese-black that paints the sharp edges, not living so much as staining. This is a pre-green world, or a post-green one, where food does not come from leaves.

Because the bowl is dense with other things. The water looks empty from the rim until you get close, and then you realize it is crowded to opacity. Not crowded like a reef — crowded like a held breath. The water column is thick with fauna, layer after layer, near-touching, never still.

They are not fish as you know them. At first they look like mechanisms that have grown themselves.

Closest to the rock, the vent fields do the work of a factory with no factory. On the bowl's floor, along a fresh fissure that still ticks as it cools, white smokers rise — natural chimneys of precipitated silica and anhydrite, each one a smokestack with no one to build it. They jet cool water and hot water in alternating pulses, and the pulse is industrial. You hear it before you see it: a deep, lung-filling **THUMP - hiss - THUMP - hiss**, slow as a sleeping giant's heart, about once every twenty seconds. The whole bowl resonates with it. The chimneys are pistons. The fissure breathes. When the pressure peaks, a sheet of mineral bubbles bursts from every crack at once and the entire volume fizzes white, then clears.

And the animals have fit themselves to that engine.

There are bivalves the size of dinner plates with shells like boiler iron, dark and riveted, hinged with what looks exactly like a weld seam. They clamp to the sharp basalt steps in dense banks, hundreds per square meter, and they open and close in phase with the vent pulse — not quickly, but with that slow, immense industrial patience. *Clack.* All at once. *Clack.* Filtering. Their inner mantles are crimson and fringed with cilia that comb the water, and the synchronized clacking is loud enough to feel in your molars.

Between them run the conveyor animals. They are colonial — each one a chain of translucent, barrel-shaped zooids linked end to end like a living timing belt, three or four meters long, looping continuously over the rock. The loop moves. Slowly, inexorably, maybe a centimeter a minute. The top side carries nodules of manganese and sulfur up toward the rim, the bottom side brings clean grit back down. There are dozens of these loops, side by side, buzzing faintly with the static of their cilia, like electrified lines. Nothing built them. They grew into the only shape that works here: a natural assembly line.

Above them, in the open water, the pelagic fauna is a snow of bells and screws. Medusae with stiff, coppery bells that contract with a hydraulic sigh. Ctenophores that have evolved perfect propeller fins, spinning rather than swimming, their combs flashing as they turn. Things like shrimp but with bodies made of interlocking plates that click and chime as they flick. All of them are faintly luminous even in the bright day, not with bioluminescence but with structural color — interference patterns that throw back the sun in oily rainbows. The water is so full you cannot extend an arm without brushing something cool and smooth and faintly vibrating.

It is very loud. For a place with no wind and no leaves to rustle, it is deafening. The vent thumps, the bivalves clack in chorus, the zooid belts hum, the water itself creaks against the young sharp rock as the whole mass shifts minutely with barometric pressure. It is the sound of a vast, empty mill operating itself. But nothing happens quickly. A belt takes an hour to make its circuit. A smoker chimney grows a millimeter a day. A bivalve might live a hundred years opening and closing to the same beat. The bowl is a machine that has chosen a cool, slow metabolism. Change comes, but on the scale of seasons, of the rock cooling one more degree.

The rules here are simple and strange:

1.  Water wants to stay up. Gravity is normal, but the basin holds. No spillway has yet cut. Until erosion finally saws a notch in that sharp rim — and it will, in a few thousand years — the sea remains perched.

2.  Life copies industry without invention. No one designed the pistons and belts; physics designed them, and life, finding the pistons and belts already implied by pressure and chemistry, grew into them. Form follows flow.

3.  Everything eats the stone's breath. There is no photosynthesis to base a food web on. The vent's chemistry — dissolved iron, hydrogen, sulfur — is the sun. Bacteria eat it, bivalves farm the bacteria, belts carry the waste, jellies eat what sloughs off the belts. The mountain is feeding itself to its animals.

If you float in the middle, suspended, you are at the exact center of a cold, bright, crowded room that has no doors and no builders, only the ticking of its own natural engine. The rim holds the sky-water in. The walls, still fresh from their making, keep their edge. And all around you, the dense, strange, abundant crowd opens and closes, churns and carries, loud and patient, waiting for the day the bowl finally breaks and pours its little ocean down the side of the world.
