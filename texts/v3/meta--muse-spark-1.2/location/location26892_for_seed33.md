On the roof of the world, where the air goes thin and cold.

It does not feel like a mountaintop. There is no peak. There is a suspension — a high, tilted plain of pale stone lifted so far above everything else that the sky has changed its behavior. You have climbed for a long time without remembering the climb. The slope was so vast it felt level. Now you are simply *up*, and the world below is not visible, only inferred by the way the light fails before it reaches you.

The air is cool enough that your breath should show, but it doesn't quite. It is too dry for breath to become cloud. This is a dry so complete it feels like a physics law. No rivers, no pools, no damp in the rock. The stone itself is thirsty. If you lick it your tongue would stick. What water exists here is an insult to the idea of water — a film of frost that appears in the shadows before dawn, not even frost, but a white salt-sweat that hisses and vanishes when the dim sun touches it. No melting. It goes straight from solid to air with a faint ticking sound.

The plain is not barren in the way a desert is barren. It remembers being worked.

At first, from a distance, you think you are approaching a city. 

It has the organization of a city. That is undeniable. A valley-wide dispersion of vertical forms, clustered and spaced with intention — avenues between them, plazas where they pull back from each other, neighborhoods where they lean together. Some are slender as chimneys, some are broad as temples, hundreds of feet tall, flat-roofed or crenellated or hollowed with windows that are not windows. Layer upon layer of construction is visible in their sides, strata of accretion, like cut paper or sediment or wasp-nest.

But no one built them. Not with hands, not with tools.

Closer, the illusion of architecture breaks and reforms into something stranger. The material is wrong. It is a mineral city, but not quarried. It is grown and weathered and grown again. A basalt-black base, then a band of rust-red, then a thick course of salt-white that crumbles at the edges like sugar, then a cap of wind-polished glass the color of bone. The wind did this. The salt did this. Time did this. For so long that erosion has stopped being destruction and become a kind of authorship. Edges are rounded, not sharp. Every corner has been worried smooth by dust moving at speed for a thousand thousand years, until the forms look settled, at rest in themselves. Nothing is new here. Nothing is ruined either. It is simply in its state.

You walk an avenue that is not an avenue. The ground is not paved. It is the same plain-stone, scoured into parallel grooves by prevailing winds, so that it *looks* paved, looks intended. The dust between the grooves is fine as talc and cold to the touch. Your footsteps make no echo. The distance between the towers is immense. This is an empty city. A hollow city. You could stand in the center of one of its plazas — a great, wind-hollowed bowl the size of a town square — and the nearest vertical mass would be a quarter mile away, silent and indifferent. The scale is regional, a valley that forgot it was a valley because its walls are so far away and so low, just a dark smudge where the high plain finally curves down into nothing. You are inside something the size of a city with the population of a salt flat.

Light comes from nowhere in particular. It is not night and not day. A perpetual dim, like the hour after the sun has gone behind a storm that never arrives. The sky is not blue but a close, high, pearlescent gray, bright enough to see by but without a source. There are no harsh shadows. The towers do not cast shadows so much as they *gather* dimness around their bases. And here is where the place stops being merely remote and starts being wrong.

The weirdness is not dramatic. It accrues.

First, the distance lies. That tower you thought was near is not near. You walk toward it for ten minutes and it does not get larger. Then, all at once, you are beside it and it is vastly larger than it should be, curving overhead. When you look back, the plaza you came from is too small, like a model.

Second, gravity is polite but inconsistent. In the avenues, down is down. But if you stand in the archway of one of the larger structures — a hollowed-out monolith with walls a foot thick, open to the wind — down tilts. Dust falls slowly through the arch at an angle, as if the world has been tipped a few degrees just for that room. You feel heavier on one foot. If you stay too long you get a faint nausea, like being on a boat.

Third, the city is bigger inside than outside. Not metaphorically. The hollow towers are empty, but some are *too* empty. You peer through a window-hole no wider than your shoulders and see an interior that should not fit, a cathedral of smooth, inward-curving stone that goes back further than the tower's diameter allows. If you toss a pebble in, you don't hear it land. Some interiors have a second sky in them, a dimmer, closer gray that seems to breathe.

This is the active part, the dynamic that belies the silence. The place is not still. It moves, but it moves without sound.

The towers grow. Not visibly, but you can feel it if you press your palm to the salt-white course — a faint, slow warmth and a vibration like a very low hum felt more in the teeth than the ears. Mineral is being deposited from the dry air itself, molecule by molecule. On the leeward side, it is being taken away as fine dust. The city exhales. Over centuries a street will close, an arch will open.

High above, clouds move at impossible speed. They are not water clouds — they are dust veils, thin and rippled, racing from horizon to horizon in minutes, silent, casting fast, soft shadows that make the plain appear to ripple. When one passes overhead, the static charge raises the hairs on your arms. Sometimes the dust devils stand up between the towers, thin spinning needles as tall as buildings, wandering the avenues like careful animals, never touching the walls.

There is life, but you have to learn to see it as not-life.

No plants. Nothing green has ever taken hold here; the salts would flay a root in an hour. What grows is mineral mimicry — black, glassy filaments that branch like frozen ferns on the north face of the towers, not alive, just crystal efflorescence. And then, rarely, the actual fauna.

They are not abundant. You may walk a whole day and see one.

The first kind are the gliders — pale, translucent things the size of a coat, like manta rays made of dry skin and stretched filament, that ride the thin high air between the towers without ever flapping. They make no sound. They turn by warping their edges. When the light hits them right you can see the towers through them.

The second kind are the builders, though that is a generous word. Low, slow, blind things the color of the stone, as big as tortoises, that move along the base of the towers and secrete a paste from their undersides — dissolved salt and stone dust and something organic, something that smells faintly of iron. They lay down a new layer. A millimeter a year. They are why the walls are stratified. They have no tools, no technology, no culture. They are the city’s metabolism. They do not notice you. If you stand still, one will go over your foot, leaving a cool, gritty trail that hardens before your eyes.

The rules here, as far as you can infer them by staying:

1.  **Thirst is structural.** Nothing holds water because water does not hold together here. It sublimates before it can pool. To drink you would have to lick the temporary frost and you would be drinking mostly salt.

2.  **Distance is negotiable.** Straight lines are a suggestion. If you need to get somewhere, do not look directly at it. Look slightly to the side and walk. You will arrive sooner.

3.  **Silence has weight.** Sound does not carry. Shout and your voice falls flat within ten feet, as if the dry air eats it. The quiet is not an absence; it is a pressure. After a while your own heartbeat becomes intrusive, and you learn to breathe shallowly so you don’t have to hear it.

4.  **The city remembers being other things.** In certain plazas, if the dim light is right, the towers are not towers but trees, or not trees but stalagmites in a cave so large the cave is the sky. The place flickers between interpretations without ever settling. This is not illusion. The rock really was all those things at different times, and the times have not fully separated.

If you stay until the light dims further — and it does, very slowly, on a cycle that is not a day — the towers begin to show their hollows more clearly. Light from nowhere pools in the windows. The wind picks up just enough to make the dust sing, a single, pure, almost inaudible tone as it moves through a thousand hollow throats at once.

It is not a city for living in. It is a city for being *between*. A high, cold, dry, and almost perfectly empty urbanism made by wind and salt and patient, mindless animals, balanced on a plain so high the planet seems to have ended and something else, something still forming, has taken its place.

And it is still, very quietly, in the process of becoming more itself.
