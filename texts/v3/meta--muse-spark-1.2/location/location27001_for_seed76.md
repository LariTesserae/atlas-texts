It is high. Higher than weather.

You are standing on a continent that decided, a long time ago, to be a table. Not a peak, not a range — a lift. The land rose once, very slowly, and then stopped and stayed there, breathing thin air for so many millions of years that it has forgotten any other shape. The elevation is not dramatic in the way a cliff is dramatic. It is absolute. The sky is closer here and the world falls away on all sides so far that the horizons are not lines but hazes, a pale smudge where stone meets sky without interruption. Far below you, if you walk for days to the escarpment, there is a sea of cloud that never touches this height. It boils and re-forms and catches sunset colors and stays beneath your feet. You live above the clouds, in the bright.

The light here is a physical weight. There is almost no atmosphere to filter it, no humidity to soften it. It arrives white and clean and total, pouring down without shadows at midday and then throwing shadows a hundred meters long in the late hours. The stone is light-colored — not white, but bone, and salt-blushed grey, and a kind of rose-flecked pumice — so it throws the light back at itself. Even with your eyes half-closed the brightness hums on the back of your retina. It should be hot. It is not. The air is thin and cool to the point of sharpness. When you inhale it feels rinsed. Your exhale makes a brief ghost that vanishes instantly. The rock holds no heat after the sun leaves it. At night, frost forms not from dew — there is no dew — but as a direct lace of crystals from the air onto stone, which sparkles violently at dawn and then sublimates before it can melt.

There is no water. Not in the way you expect water to be.

There are no rivers here, no lakes, no springs, no dark line of willows that betrays a creek. If water ever cut this land, it did so so long ago that its beds are now broad, shallow troughs filled with wind-sorted gravel, their curves still legible but dry as bone. The ground is not thirsty, it is beyond thirst. It is a dry chemistry. In places the old seabed — or salt flat, or playa of some mineral that was never sea — lies exposed in plates the size of counties, cracked into polygons, each plate crusted with a thin glitter of salts and silica that crunches like hoarfrost underfoot and then resettles. In other places the wind has stripped it down to bare country rock: basalt softened by age, tuff, scoria worn to rounded shoulders, everything sandblasted and pitted and then smoothed again.

And yet it is lush.

That is the first contradiction that takes a while to resolve, until you learn to see what "lush" means up here. It does not mean a jungle, a meadow, a crowding. The density is low — terribly low by lowland standards. You can walk for an hour and see only stone and light, and then come upon a presence that stops you. But what *is* here is extravagant, thriving, lavish in itself.

They are not trees as you know them. They are not thirsty green things.

The dominant forms are what you might call aerophytes or glass-groves if you needed a word. Each one is solitary, or in loose kinships of three or seven scattered across a slope with a half-mile of empty mineral between them. From a distance they look like low, dark smoke frozen in place, or like coral. Up close they are huge — many are larger than any tree you have known, thirty or forty feet across and half as tall, but they do not reach up. They spread, they mound, they billow. They are made of thousands of fronds that are not leaves at all but translucent, waxy ribbons, deep teal and rust-red and a black-green so saturated it seems to drink the bright light and hold it. The fronds are stiff with silica, edged with fine hairs that comb the wind. They do not root in soil; they anchor.

Their trunks, if you can call them that, are not wood. They are a column of compressed, felted fiber and mineral, like peat turned to stone but still alive, still faintly warm to the touch if you lay a hand on it in the cool air. From that base, aerial roots — pale, cord-like, almost luminous — sprawl outward across the rock for yards and secrete a faint acid that etches the stone, holding fast and dissolving a meal of minerals directly. The whole organism never drinks. It harvests. The hairs on every ribbon condense the trace vapor that rides the high thin winds — not into droplets, never into liquid — but straight into a chemical film that is wicked inward. At midday the groves seem to shimmer, not with heat but with that faint exhalation, a barely visible halo of exchange.

Between these giants is the second layer of lushness: the ground itself is alive. What looked from a distance like bare salt-crust is, in many places, a continuous mat of lichenous growth, ankle-deep and springy, olive and ochre and sulfur-yellow, forming continent-wide aprons that follow the contour of the land. It is not soft like moss. It crackles. It is dry and brittle on top, wet with chemistry underneath. When you step on it, it sighs and slowly springs back over minutes. It photosynthesizes with a ferocity that makes the air around it taste faintly metallic, ozone-sharp, even though there is no humidity to carry scent.

The land is old. That is its most obvious rule. Nothing here is sharp or new. The ridges are long and whale-backed, the basins are shallow and harmonious, the wind has had time to try every shape and keep only the ones that endure. Talus slopes have sorted themselves by weight. The salt polygons are not cracking; they have *cracked*, long ago, and now lie settled. Even the groves look established, as if each one has been exactly where it is for centuries, growing outward a millimeter a year, in perfect balance with the slow abrasion of dust and light. Erosion is not an event here. It is the condition. The plateau is being very gently erased, grain by grain, and it is being rebuilt, frond by frond, at the same pace.

It is vast enough to have its own weather, but its weather is not water weather. In the afternoons, when the plateau heats differentially, low pressure pulls a wind up from the cloud sea below, not as cloud but as a clear, fast river of air that comes over the escarpment and runs shallow across the tableland. You can see it coming from miles away because it lifts the fine salt dust and carries the spore of the groves — great slow clouds of rust-colored flecks that rise and drift and settle without hurry, glittering in the bright light like plankton in sun-water. The wind is the only sound for long stretches. It is not loud. The thin air carries poorly. What you hear is close: the faint clink of silica fronds touching each other, the granular hiss of dust moving over crust, the occasional soft pop as a mat of lichen contracts in the cool. Even your own footsteps are muffled, as if the world is listening inward.

Fauna is rare to the point where each encounter feels like an anomaly, a visitation. There are no herds, no flocks. If you stay still long enough, you may see a grazer — something low and six-limbed and covered in the same waxy filaments as the plants, so well camouflaged you thought it was a shadow — moving with immense slowness from one grove to the edge of another, rasping at the fallen fronds. Or a needle-thin glider, almost transparent, that rides the afternoon dust-river for hours without beating a wing, its body a kite of membrane stretched between elongated limbs. They do not call. They do not rustle much. They make the silence feel larger, not smaller.

There is an unusualness to the light that you only notice after a day. At certain angles, especially near evening when the sun is low and the air is coldest, the fronds of the groves become almost invisible, transmitting light rather than reflecting it, so the whole organism glows from within as if it were a lantern. And the shadows the land casts on itself are not quite where geometry says they should be — a faint double-edge, a refraction in the thin, cold, mineral-dusted air that makes distant formations appear to float a hand's breadth above their true position. It is not magic. It is merely what happens when light, stone, and a biology that learned to be glass have lived together for a very long time.

Nothing has been built here. No wall, no cairn, no road. No tool has ever marked the salt. The groves do not need tending and the stone does not need shaping. The processes that are running — wind sorting, light harvesting, spore drifting, crystal blooming and vanishing, the imperceptibly slow migration of the mats across the polygons — have been running without intervention and will continue.

If you lie down on the warmest rock in the late afternoon, with the cool air beginning to pool around you and the light turning amber and the dust-river high above you turning to gold, you can feel the plateau breathe. Not metaphorically. The mats expand a fraction as the temperature drops. The groves tighten their fronds. The salts tick. The whole vast, high, bright, dry, lush, quiet table of a world exhales once, and waits for night.
