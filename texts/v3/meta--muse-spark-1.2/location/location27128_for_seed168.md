It is high.

Not a mountain peak, not a range - a table. A single broad lift of the world, a plateau set so high the horizon drops away from it on all sides in a clean, hard line. From its edge you could see the lower world as a haze, but up here the air is thin and warm and absolutely clear, under a normal sun at what looks like late morning and stays there. The light has no angle to it, no progress. It's warm the way a stone is warm after sitting in sun all day. Not hot, not cool. Held.

This place has only just been made.

You can tell by the ground. It hasn't been weathered. It hasn't been taught how to be ground yet. It is a raw, uncut slab of dark basalt and salt-crust and pale, unoxidized mineral, still showing the flow lines and the cooling cracks from when it extruded. There are no riverbeds, no gullies, no talus slopes. The edge of the plateau is not crumbled but sheared, a fresh fracture hundreds of meters high where the rock still looks poured. The surface is not smooth, but it isn't eroded either — it is wrinkled, roped, blistered. Press-marks. As if the whole plain was stamped out by a die so large you can't see its borders, and left to cure.

There is no water here at all.

Not hidden, not underground. No damp in the air. No cloud that will ever rain. The salt that crusts the flats between the darker rock is not the salt of a dried sea, it is the native mineral of the stone itself, blooming outward. The air has no humidity, no scent of water. Your mouth would dry if you breathed it long. The concept of a watershed, a spring, a dew, simply does not apply. This is a dry chemistry world.

And yet it is lush.

At first from a distance you might mistake it for emptiness, because it *is* empty — 0.3/3 empty. The scale is regional, a valley or a small country spread out in one unbroken plain, and there is almost nothing in it per volume. Vast stretches of raw, warm stone between presences. Silence lives in those stretches, a profound, complete silence that makes your ears ring.

Then you see where the vegetation is, and you understand the trick of the density. It is not spread thinly. It is clumped, gathered, intensely lush where it occurs, and nowhere else.

They are not green. They are not thirsty. They grow without water, they have no use for it.

They grow as industry does.

In groves spaced a kilometer or two apart, sometimes more, the plain erupts with forests of filament. From a single root-plate — a fused, iron-black nodule melted into the basalt — a thousand, ten thousand vertical threads rise. Each one is fine as wire, bright as fresh copper or as dull as soot-covered steel, branching and branching again without leaves, without bark. They are all stem and stamen and fiber. Up close a grove is not like a wood at all; it is like a Loom left running. The threads vibrate faintly, extruding themselves tip-first from the air, pulling mineral vapor and static out of the warm atmosphere and spinning it into more thread. At the crown, fifteen or twenty meters up, the filaments fray out into tassels of metallic down, copper-wool and brass-moss and tangled filaments so fine they float even in still air. They catch the normal light and scatter it warmly, so that each grove glows from within — a dense, lush, rust-red and verdigris and gold thicket, humming.

For that is the industry. There are no structures. No mills, no factories, no houses, no roads, no ruins, no artifacts at all. Nothing has been built here. The *function* of industry is present without the *architecture* of it. The plants are the engines. The plain is the factory floor. You can stand inside a grove and hear it if you hold your breath against the silence — not a machine roar, but the quietest industrial sounds imaginable: a ticking as filaments cool, a faint transformer whine, the softest hiss of deposition, like a soldering iron touching flux a kilometer away. The smell is warm metal and hot ceramic and ozone, but very faint. Everything is at idle. Production without product, or production where the product is simply more of itself.

The vegetation does not drop seeds. It sheds. Constantly, impossibly slowly, the tassels slough off lengths of filament that do not rot. They lie in drifts around the base of each grove, a deep, springy duff of metal wool that never compacts, never decays, does not feed anything because there is nothing to feed. No animal has ever walked here. No insect hums in the copper canopy. No bird. The lushness supports nothing but its own weight. Where a filament falls, it lies until it is buried by the slow creep of salt. Where a grove stands, it stands. The equilibrium is absolute. Nothing erodes, nothing migrates, nothing evolves. The filaments grow a fraction of a millimeter a year and the salt blooms a fraction of a millimeter a year and the warm air does not move.

If you walk from one grove to the next — and it is a long, quiet, warm walk across that raw, newborn basalt, your footsteps making no echo — you will see that no two groves are quite the same alloy. One is almost white with aluminum thread, another deep wine-red with iron oxide fur, another brassy and green. But each is equally dense within, equally empty without. Each is a little country of fiber, surrounded by kilometers of nothing that has just been born and will never age.

The weirdness is not violent. It's the quiet wrongness of a world whose rules have been substituted cleanly, one for one. Water removed, industry inserted. Biology replaced with metallurgy, but keeping lushness. High elevation without cold, or wind. A landscape under construction that has paused mid-press and decided to stay that way, stable as a held breath.

You could stay here a long time. The light would not change. The silence would not break. Far off, across the plain, another grove would catch the sun and flare gold, and make its small, patient, electric sigh, and continue to weave itself out of nothing, needing nothing else.
