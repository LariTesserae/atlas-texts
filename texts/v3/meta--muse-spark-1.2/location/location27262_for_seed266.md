It is high. Higher than high should reasonably be.

You don't climb to it so much as you come up out of the lowlands and realize the lowlands are gone. The world flattens and lifts into a single table - a continent lifted like a loaf - and the air thins until light gets hard and clean. At noon the sun is normal, white-gold and direct, not dimmed, not colored. It just hits harder because there is nothing between you and it. No haze. No moisture to soften it.

The heat is not desert-fierce, not burning. It is warm the way deep stone is warm. The rock itself holds the day and gives it back at night. You can feel it through the soles of your boots. A dry, mineral warmth that smells like iron and hot dust and ozone.

There is no water here. Not hidden, not underground. This place finished its water a long time ago. The salt that would have been seabed is still here, kilometers thick, folded and fractured and lifted. White and rust-red and bone-yellow, capped in places with basalt so dark it looks blue. The wind has had a very long time to work on it. That is the first thing you understand about the shape of things: nothing here is new. The cliffs do not crumble. The pillars do not fall. They have been carved to equilibrium. 

The edge of the plateau is not a slope but a wall, terraced and undercut into colonnades, alveoli, organ pipes of stone two and three kilometers tall where the wind has learned to play the same notes for a million years. Inland, the plain is not flat. It is fluted. Long yardangs like overturned hulls, fifty meters high, all aligned east-west, marching in parallel fleets to the horizon. Between them, corridors of salt pavement, polished to a ceramic shine, where your footsteps make no dust because the dust has already been winnowed away. In the hollows, dunes of black glass sand that never move, too heavy for even this wind, rippled and frozen like water caught mid-flow.

It is vast enough to have its own weather without ever having rain. In the afternoons, heat lifts off the white flats and makes false lakes of sky. High, thin clouds form from nothing - not water vapor but fine silica and salt exhaled by the stone - gather into anvil heads, drift for days, and dissolve again without ever breaking. You can see the curvature at the edge of vision, the plateau bending just slightly away from itself, so that distant mesas seem to float.

And it is empty. Profoundly, intentionally empty. Not barren in a dead way, but sparse in the way a large room with very few, very considered things is sparse.

The towns are the first presences you learn to see. From far off they look like discolorations, a smudge of darker mineral, until the light hits a wire or a window and flashes. They are not sprawling. Each one is a tight, dense knot of construction, as if the builders agreed to take up as little of the plain as possible. Most are cut down into the stone rather than built up from it - terraced pits, quarry-cities, the walls stepped with dwellings, workshops, cisterns that have never held water but hold something else, a fine oil-black slurry that catches the sun. Above ground: only what needs to be above. Headframes. Vent stacks. Long, skeletal gantries that walk out over the void on struts. The architecture is all right-angles and rivets and sun-faded ceramic brick, stained the same red as the cliffs. Between towns, there is nothing but the wind-carved plain and the straight lines that connect them - single rail lines on low trestles, or moving cableways strung between pylons that hum, carrying ore buckets the size of houses at a walking pace. You can watch a bucket for an hour and it will have moved only a little. Everything industrial here is industrial at the speed of geology.

No one wastes motion. The factories - and there are factories in every town, low and broad with sawtooth roofs - do not roar. They breathe. A deep, spaced thump, a sigh of pressure released, a conveyor rolling on dry bearings with a sound like paper being torn very slowly. The smoke, when there is any, is not smoke but heat-shimmer and fine, glittering particulate that falls almost immediately, adding another micron to the plain. The machines are not new. They are maintained, patched, rebuilt in place for generations, their iron darkened and polished by hands and wind. They make more machines, more rail, more cable, more town, very slowly, as if the entire plateau were a single project being added to one brick at a time.

There are no plants. No green, no lichen even. The concept does not apply. Where you expect a scrub or a weed in a crack, there is instead a crust of salt crystals, or a fan of needle-glass that sings when the wind passes.

And yet it is not lifeless. It is crowded, if you know how to look.

The fauna do not need what plants make, because they never evolved to need it. They are mineral things, but not inert. The grazers move in small, widely scattered herds across the salt pavement - low, broad, many-legged tables the size of cattle, their backs covered in overlapping plates of chipped basalt and living ceramic that they grow themselves. They lower their flat heads and rasp the salt with mouths like grinding mills, taking the halophilic films you cannot see, the iron bacteria, the wind-fallen organics from the towns, the very dust itself. When they exhale, the air around them shimmers with expelled fines. When they die, they sit down and become boulders, and after a season you cannot tell them from the yardangs.

Above them, the air is full of other things. So thin you almost miss them at first, until one banks and catches the sun. Kites - not birds, not insects - vast translucent membranes stretched over hollow frames of bone-glass, a meter across, then three, then ten. They do not flap. They ride. They are so light they are almost not there, filtering the high silica clouds with frilled undersides, precipitating it into nodules that grow along their spines. Sometimes a whole flock will hang motionless for hours in a thermal, barely rippling. Sometimes one will descend, impossibly slow, to brush the salt with trailing filaments and lift again. They cast little shadow.

Smaller things skitter. Bright, beetle-like assemblages of articulated iron and enamel that live in the lee of the rail pylons and feed on spilled lubricant and the black slurry, clicking quietly to each other with tapped codes on the metal. They are drawn to the warmth of the factories at dusk and scatter in perfect, liquid patterns when a grazer approaches.

The rules here are different but consistent. Water is not a solvent; heat and charge are. Flesh is not soft; it is laminated, sintered, grown like pottery. Sound does not carry far in the thin, dry air, so everything is quiet even when it is busy. You hear your own breath, the faint tick of cooling stone, the distant, patient thump of a stamping mill three kilometers away as if it were next to your ear and also very far away. Nothing hurries. Migration is a shift of a few hundred meters over a season. A kite may live for a century, drifting. A town may take a thousand years to add a terrace.

And there is the strangeness that settles once you have been here long enough to expect the ordinary.

The horizon is wrong. Due to the height and the clear air and perhaps something else, distant things loom. A mesa forty kilometers away looks like you could reach it in an afternoon, its strata legible. When you walk toward it, it does not get closer so much as it gets more detailed. Light bends a little too much. At midday, the shadows sometimes fall slightly toward the sun, as if the stone remembered an older light.

The wind, when it comes down the long corridors between yardangs, does not gust. It arrives as a single, coherent sheet, a river of air moving at one speed from ground to a hundred meters up, and it can carry those kite-fauna sideways for miles without ruffling them. If you stand in it, dust does not swirl around you; it streams past you in perfectly parallel lines, parting around your legs.

And at night - though the sun here is normal and the days are warm and bright - the stone cools and contracts and the entire plateau pings, a low, resonant settling that you feel in your molars more than hear. The towns dim their few lights and the factories continue their slow thump without pause. The grazers fold down and become indistinguishable from the ground. The kites go high and become stars that move, very slowly, against the real stars.

You can stand on the edge where the wall drops away into nothing and the air falls with it, and look out over the lower world you came from, hazy and blue and wet, and understand that this place is not an extension of that one. It is not a mountain on that world. It is a world on a world, with its own physics of patience, its own dry biology, its own slow, quiet industry that has no need to be fast because it has already been here forever and intends to remain.
