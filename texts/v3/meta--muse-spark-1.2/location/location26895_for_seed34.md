It is a high country, and it is vast in a way that stops feeling like high country at all.

At first there is only the table. A plateau so broad you could walk for days and still be on it, still be under the same cool sky, still have its edge hidden by distance and air. The elevation is not a peak. It doesn't thrust. It lifts — a whole region raised up together until the air thins and cools and the light gets clean. The world below is not visible. There are days when the clouds make their own country underneath it, a second, inverted plain of white that breaks on the plateau's cliffs, and days when you can see fifty miles to a low blue haze that is still part of the same rise. It has weather of its own. Fronts are born here and stall here and rain themselves out against nothing but more plateau.

The rock of it is old. Not jagged new mountain rock, but settled rock. Weathered into its final shape. The basalt and slate and limestone have been there long enough to have agreed upon their form — long, level bedding planes, wide shallow basins, softened scarps, cliffs that have wept themselves smooth. Everything is water-cut, but not violently. The gullies are broad U's, the valleys are shallow bowls, the escarpments are stair-stepped by a million seasons of freeze and thaw. Nothing is collapsing. Nothing is raw. Even the scree slopes have been sorted by time into neat aprons. It feels balanced, in-its-own-state.

The water is everywhere without ever being a lake or a sea. The plateau is wet the way a sponge is wet. The water table sits just under the skin. You can feel it in the ground, which gives slightly underfoot even where it looks like solid stone. Every low place is a marsh. Every vertical face weeps.

It condenses out of the cool air in the morning and never quite evaporates. It beads on rock, it gathers in the joints between stones, it runs down the lee side of every wall in dark threads. Springs push up frankly in the middle of flat ground — a clear eye of water and cress around it, then a runnel, then a rill that joins others until it is a stream that has no obvious source and no definite bank, just a braided wetness moving steadily toward the plateau's distant rim where it will go over as a fall so thin and wide it looks like mist. The sound of it is not a roar. It is drip, seep, trickle, sigh. Water moving through stone at the speed stone will allow.

And on this wet, cool table, dense as lichen on a boulder, is the city.

It is not a city as a different thing built upon the landscape. It is the landscape, built.

From a distance it looks like geology. The same stair-step bedding of the natural cliffs, repeated and intensified — terraces, retaining walls, enclosures, all made of the same stone, without mortar, without metal. The builders quarried directly down. A family would cut its house from the floor of its courtyard and use the block to raise the walls around it, so negative and positive match. Over centuries this has made a honeycomb that follows the faint fall of the land. Levels and flat roofs creating new levels. Alleyways that are sometimes streets and sometimes the flat rooftops of the tier below. Stairwells cut straight through living rock. Cisterns under every plaza to catch the endless seep.

It is urban in the true sense. Not scattered farms. Continuous construction. Layered. For a long day's walk in any direction you are still inside it — still passing doorways, still with a wall to your shoulder, still hearing your footsteps echo between stone. And yet it is never tall. Two stories, three at most. It stays low against the wind, hunkered into the stone it came from, so the whole immense settlement reads as texture rather than skyline. A vast, inhabited outcrop.

There is no tech here to see. No wire, no glass, no smoke, no straight machine edge. The stone is worked by stone. Wood is used sparingly — lintels of dark, water-hardened timber brought from the sparse groves on the plateau's lower skirts, now polished black by hands. Doors are leather, or woven reed, or just a hanging of felt. A pot is a pot. A knife is chipped slate. Life operates at the speed of hands and weather.

The light is plain daylight, high and cool and generous. At this elevation the sun is bright without being hot, a white-yellow disc in a pale blue that deepens toward violet at noon. Cloud shadows move across the terraces like slow animals, and because the city is so wide you can watch a single shadow take an hour to cross from one quarter to another. When it is overcast — often — the light becomes even and shadowless and every wet stone shines.

It is cool always. Not bitter, not alpine winter. A late-spring cool that makes you want to keep moving. Breath shows in the mornings. Midday is comfortable if you are in the sun and out of the wind. Evenings pull the chill back down out of the sky. People dress in layers of undyed wool, the same grey-brown as the stone, and the buildings are built for it — thick walls that hold a little of the day's heat, houses opening inward around courtyard hearths, narrow windows to keep the wind out.

Vegetation is sparse, because the stone allows little else. What grows does so in the cracks the city and the water allow. Pale lichens map every north-facing wall. Cushion mosses make dense, low pillows on roof-tops that have been abandoned to them. In the marshier basins between terraces, sedge and cotton-grass shiver. A few hardy things — a stunted willow no higher than your knee but perhaps a hundred years old, a twist of juniper hugging a wall — mark places where soil has collected enough to hold them. The green is not the point of the color here. The color is stone grey, water black, sky blue, and the warm buff of dry thatch.

For the same reason, animals are rare. Not absent, but thinly spread across so much space they are events when you meet them. A high harrier tilting over the terraces, its shadow the only fast thing above the roofs. Small, buff-colored pikas whistling from wall to wall and vanishing. At dusk, you might see a long-legged grazing animal far out on one of the open marsh commons left deliberately unbuilt among the houses — something like an alpaca, moving in threes and fours, drinking from the runnels. Mostly, the living presences are people, and even they are quiet.

The quiet is profound. The density would make you expect a dense noise — markets, engines, crowds — but there is none. The stone absorbs. The wind is tempered by the labyrinth of walls into something that touches rather than howls. The water is everywhere but it does not rush. What you hear, if you stand still on a flat roof at midday, is a cloth of small sounds on top of silence: the tick of condensation falling from a lintel into a basin, the soft bleat from a courtyard two levels down, the scuff of a sandal on basalt, a child calling once and then stopping. Then long stretches where there is only air and the faint fizz of distant, evaporating drizzle.

And yet it is not still. It is active — vigorously, continually active. The city is a water engine. The cisterns fill and are bailed, the marsh commons flood and drain with the passage of cloud systems, the sedge grows and is cut, the stone itself slowly greens and flakes where the water works on it. Clouds form directly overhead in the early afternoon as the plateau heats just enough to lift its own moisture, and by three o'clock it rains, not hard, but thoroughly, for an hour, and everyone steps under lintels and watches the runnels deepen and join and then, when the sun comes out again, the whole vast terrace steams. People move through all this constantly — carrying water jars, leading animals, mending a wall by fitting a new stone where an old one settled, shifting a reed mat to catch the drip from a weep. No single thing happens fast. But everything is happening.

If there are rules to this world, they are simple and exhaustive: water goes down. Stone goes nowhere unless you move it. Wind moves west to east until the plateau makes it turn. Cool air holds less. People build low and thick or the weather will take them apart. No one sings louder than the wind for long.

At night, the plateau holds heat back in its stone and the city glows faintly from courtyard fires — hundreds of thousands of them, low and orange, scattered across a darkness as wide as a sea, reflecting in every cistern and runnel until the city looks like it has fallen into the sky. Above, the stars are bright and steady and cold. Below, the wet rock ticks as it contracts. Somewhere a water channel overflows with a soft, continuous gurgle and no one is in a hurry to stop it.
