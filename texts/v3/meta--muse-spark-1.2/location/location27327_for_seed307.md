It is not a valley. It is not a range. It is a pocket.

High — so high the air should be thin and killingly cold, but it isn't. You've climbed into a heat. The mountain here is young, too young to have learned how to be a mountain. It is a fresh upthrust, black glass and raw basalt still sharp enough to cut boots, to cut hands, to cut light. No softening. No soil. The ridges are knife-edges, the faces are conchoidal fractures where the earth simply broke open and cooled too fast. Volcanic glass that still holds the stress of its birth. You can see the tension in it.

And near the summit, where the wind should be stripping everything bare, the rock folds inward.

A mouth. No bigger than a room. Maybe smaller — you could touch both walls if you stretched out your arms, if you dared to touch them. An intimate, hidden caldera, a single blister in the skin of the new stone. From the outside you would miss it entirely. A shadow between two fins of obsidian. But inside, it holds a whole world.

It is wet. Impossibly wet for this height.

The heat does it. The rock itself is hot. Not sun-hot, stone-hot. The floor of the pocket radiates. You can feel it through the soles of your feet, through your knees if you kneel. Somewhere below, very close to the surface, water lives under pressure and pushes up. It doesn't erupt. It weeps. Every surface sweats. The black glass walls are always beaded, always streaming with slow, fat droplets that never quite fall the way water should. They gather, they crawl, they join into sheets that run down in silence. The air is saturated — you breathe it like warm broth. Low cloud condenses directly on your skin, on your eyelashes. Where the walls undercut, the water has formed a shallow, steaming pan no deeper than your ankle, no wider than a kitchen table. Clear to the bottom, which is not sand but shattered, razor-fine black grit and pale silica crust, hissing faintly where the heat kisses it.

There are no plants. Nothing green has ever taken hold here. No lichen, no moss, no heroic alpine grass. The world is mineral. The only color that isn't black or steam-white is the rust-red of iron blooming in the water, and the opalescent sheen of silica where it precipitates in rings around the pool. The ecology has skipped vegetation entirely.

Because everything else is animal.

You notice them slowly, because they are silent.

At first you think the water is rippling. Then you think the rock is moving. Then you understand the density.

They cover every wet surface. Layered. Overlapping. Near-touching. Thousands of them in a space that should hold dozens. They are not insects, not quite — they are pale, translucent things the size of your thumbnail to the length of your hand, soft-bodied and jointed, like shrimp that forgot the ocean, like blind salamanders made of wax. No eyes. No pigment. They have fronded gills that flutter in the steam, blushing pink with heat. They crawl over each other without aggression, without hurry, a slow, constant churn. Some graze the bacterial slick that grows on the glass — the only thing like a plant here, a thin, invisible biofilm that tastes of sulfur. Some filter the warm pan, flicking their tails. Some carry.

That is the strange part.

They use tools. Primitive, thoughtless, perfect.

A larger one will pick up a flake of obsidian in its forelimbs — a flake as sharp as a scalpel, freshly fallen from the wall — and scrape at the silica crust to expose the film beneath for the smaller ones. Another will carry a droplet of water balanced on a curved shard, ferrying it from the weeping wall to a brood clump in a drier crack. No nests. No buildings. Nothing constructed that lasts. Only held objects, used and dropped. Hand-tools in the most literal sense, without hands, without minds as you know them. The tools do not accumulate. The moment an animal drops a chip, another picks it up.

They make no sound. For all their overwhelming numbers, the pocket is quiet. This is the rule of the place: silence is preserved. You hear only what the mineral world allows — the faint tick as glass contracts, the soft plip of condensed water falling from an overhang into the pan, the almost inaudible sigh of the spring breathing. Even when a hundred of the pale things shift at once, it is a whisper like wet paper. The heat seems to swallow sound.

And the heat and wet and height have broken something.

The light is normal at first — pale, high-altitude sun cutting straight down into the pocket. But it doesn't behave. Shadows fall slightly slower than the things that cast them. If you move your hand quickly through the steam, its shadow arrives a half-second late. Water droplets on the wall sometimes run upward for an inch before remembering gravity. The steam doesn't rise and dissipate; it hangs in flat, glassy layers, each one reflecting the inverted image of the tiny pool below it, so you look up and see infinite shallow seas stacked in the air. In the corners of the pocket, space folds. You think you see the back wall, and then you realize you're seeing the side wall from an impossible angle. Depth stutters. The pocket is bigger on the inside than its mouth suggests, but only when you're not looking directly at the edge.

Temperature doesn't cool with distance. A foot away from the hot pan, the air is still scalding. A foot above the steam, it is still scalding. The heat has no gradient; it is simply the condition of being inside. Time has a similar flatness. The creatures move at an active, biological pace — feeding, scraping, carrying, molting in soft, synchronized waves — but nothing ages. The glass never dulls. The pale animals you watch now are the same ones you saw when you first crouched down, and also their children. The water cycle is tight and immediate: rock sweats, animals drink and disturb it, cloud forms a hand's breadth above the pool, rains back down in minutes, endless.

If you stay too long, your own sweat stops evaporating. Your breath condenses and stays in front of your face, a little personal cloud that does not drift away. The silence deepens until you can hear your own blood, and then even that seems to quiet, as if the pocket prefers the mineral sounds.

There is no way to build here. Anything you placed would be undone — dissolved by silica, shattered by the slow, sharp settling of new glass, or simply carried off chip by chip by the patient, pale crowd who know only how to hold and scrape and carry, without ever making anything that stays.

You are a guest in a hidden, hot, wet room at the top of a young world, full to bursting with quiet, blind life that never needed plants, never needed noise, never needed you.
