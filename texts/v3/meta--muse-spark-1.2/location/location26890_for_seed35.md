It is below.

Not deep in the planetary sense — not mantle-deep, not pressure-crushing. Just far enough down that the surface is a rumor. You came down through a throat that was cut and not worn. That's the first thing you notice about the rock: it has not had time to learn how to be a cave.

The shaft is a perfect, rude cylinder, blasted and cored, its walls still sharp with the chatter-marks of the boring head that made it. No dripstone, no smoothing. The salt and anhydrite glitter where they were sheared yesterday or a thousand yesterdays ago — it doesn't matter, there has been no water to blur the edge. The walls are dry as paper, warm to the touch. Geothermal warm, skin-warm, the kind of warmth that doesn't come from sun but from the earth holding its own heat close.

And then the shaft opens, and you are not in a cavern. You are in a room. That is the scale of it. A hamlet turned on its side and buried. A cathedral without a sky.

The chamber is roughly oval, perhaps eighty meters across at its longest, the ceiling low enough that the industrial lights almost brush it. You can see every wall from where you stand. There are boundaries. This place ends. And because it ends, it is crowded.

Everything is crowded.

The floor is not floor. It is a plain of salt that has never known rain. It lies in crisp, interlocking plates, white and faintly pink, with seams of gunmetal shale between them. From those seams, in the razor-edge where machine-cut meets mineral, the salt has tried to grow. It throws up sharp, young crystals, clear as glass, six-sided spears and feathered dendrites that have never been scuffed by wind or foot. They crunch underfoot like fresh frost, and they do not heal. In the still, dry air they just persist, un-eroded. The air itself is sealed — not stale, exactly, but *complete*. No draft. No weather. The dust that your boots kick up does not swirl away. It hangs.

The light is dim and industrial and does not flicker. Strings of caged sodium lamps and a few newer white LED bars are bolted directly into the raw salt wall, their wiring exposed in black conduit that runs like veins. They buzz with a flat, amber light that never quite reaches the far corners, leaving the ceiling in a permanent dusk. The light doesn't move. It has no source beyond itself. It just is, at 40 percent, forever. The lamps are warm. The rock is warm. The air is warm. Everything sits at the same temperate equilibrium, no cold pocket, no hot vent — a held breath.

Scattered through the chamber are the remains of work. Not a city, not even an outpost. Just enough to say someone intended to stay. A gantry of rustless steel that spans half the chamber and stops mid-air where the next section was never installed. A control booth on stilts with its windows still intact but filmed over with salt dust on the inside, its levers all set to ON. A conveyor belt, motionless but thrumming, its rubber cracked into a dry riverbed pattern. A water tank, bone-dry, its hatch open to show a interior furred with salt crystals as fine as hair. The machines are not ruins in the romantic sense. They have not collapsed. They have simply stopped becoming. There is no rust here to speak of — there is no water to make rust. The iron is dark, preserved, dusted with a fine pale powder of halite that precipitated straight out of the dry air. Every bolt, every weld, every fresh cut looks like it was made last week.

And the machines are still on.

That is the sound. It is loud, not deafening, but total. You feel it before you hear it — in your molars, in the soles of your feet. A low, even chord made of a dozen sources that have synched into one note: the sixty-cycle hum of transformers, the whine of ventilation fans turning in sealed loops, the tick of relays opening and closing without purpose, the conveyor motor stalled but energized, humming against its brake. It is an industrial Om, perfectly level, with no variation, no sputter, no change in pitch. It has been this loud for a long time and will be this loud. The sound has no attack and no decay. You stop hearing it as sound and start hearing it as pressure.

What lives here lives *in* the sound, and there is a lot of it.

At first you think the walls are patterned. Then you see the pattern moves, just barely, a shivering. The density is startling. Every surface that isn't sheer salt is occupied, layered, touching.

They are pale. Almost all of them are pale. With no sun and almost no water, color is a luxury no one bothered with. The sparse vegetation is not green. It is mineral-vegetation, if you can call it that — brittle, rust-red towers of bacteria and salt-tolerant fungus that grow in the seams where the lamps leak a little heat. They look like corroded wire brushes, a few centimeters high, scattered one every few meters. Dry, stiff, they do not spread. They just hold their ground.

The animals have taken the rest.

Thousands of them, in a space no larger than a village hall. Blind cave moths the size of your hand, their wings like salt paper, clinging to the lamps in dense, overlapping shingles, not flying, just vibrating. Pale isopods, segmented and glossy, moving with slow, deliberate steps over the salt plates, their antennae combing the dry air for the faint chemical tang of the bacterial brushes. Things like crickets but without legs built for jumping — their back legs are shovels for digging salt. They cluster on the warm housings of the transformers, drinking the waste heat.

And stranger.

Near the control booth, a colony of something that is not quite animal and not quite machine has grown *around* the conduit. Translucent sacs, each the size of a melon, pulse faintly with the same frequency as the electrical hum. Inside each sac you can see a filament, copper-bright, that is both nerve and wire. They draw current directly, not through mouths. They are rooted to the steel. When you step close, the isopods part around your boot without fear, and the sacs do not contract. Nothing here reacts quickly. The ecology has reached equilibrium. Predator and prey, parasite and host have negotiated to a standstill in this sealed, warm, dry box. No one needs to chase. There is enough warmth, enough current, enough bacterial film for everyone if no one moves too fast. So no one does.

You notice the weirdness last, because at first the strangeness masks itself as just… crowding. But the geometry is wrong. Sound should not be this even in a room this sharp. The salt crystals along the far wall are all leaning very slightly toward the center, as if the center has a little more gravity. If you stare at the gantry from the corner of your eye, it seems longer than it should be, as if the chamber is holding more volume than its walls admit. And the dust you kicked up — it still hangs, exactly where you left it, not settling, not dispersing, each mote locked in place as if the air itself is a gel that has decided to be air only in name.

The rules here are simple and were written without you:

1. Nothing dries further, nothing wets.
2. Nothing erodes. What is sharp stays sharp.
3. Energy is not spent, it is circulated. The lights do not burn out. The fans do not wear.
4. Life does not expand. It saturates.

It is not dead. Dead implies it was alive and stopped. This place never started in the way surface places start. It was cut, equipped, seeded — perhaps by accident, perhaps on purpose — and then sealed and left to find its own steady state. It found it. A warm, dim, loud, dry stasis, dense as a hive, sharp as a fresh wound, small enough to hold in your mind all at once.

You stand on the salt plain under the unblinking amber lights, listening to the single, endless note, surrounded on every side by pale, patient multitudes that have no need to acknowledge you, and you understand that you could leave and come back in a hundred years and the dust you disturbed will still be hanging exactly there, the moths will still be shingled on the lamps in the same pattern, the salt spears will still be an inch taller and no duller. It is waiting for nothing.
