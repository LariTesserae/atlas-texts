This is a high place.

Not a peak — a lifted country. A plateau that was shoved up into the thin air all at once and hasn't had time to learn how to be a mountain yet. You can feel it in your lungs first. The air is bright and cool, maybe 8 or 9 degrees in the full sun, the kind of cool that makes the light feel sharper. At this elevation the sky is a pale, hard blue, close and scrubbed clean, and the sun is not warm so much as *present* — a white, high-pressure glare that flattens shadows and makes every wet surface throw it back at you. You squint even with cloud. The light gets into everything. It bleaches the greens and makes the water shine like tin.

The geology is raw. That is the first thing you notice after the light. This plateau is young, embryonic. The basalt came up dark and blocky and it hasn't been worn down. There are no deep valleys, no softened ridges, no river canyons. Instead the ground is a vast, uneven table of fresh rock — stepped terraces, sharp jointed pavements, low blistered domes where the lava cooled — and it is all still under construction. Erosion hasn't really started. Water doesn't cut here; it sheets. It spills in broad, shallow fans over the lip of every rock step in a skin a few millimeters thick, finding no channel because no channel has had time to form.

And because it hasn't formed a channel, the water stays.

Everything is wet. The atmosphere itself is wet. Clouds don't so much pass over as condense directly out of the air — you walk through air that beads on your sleeves, on your eyebrows, on the hairs of your arms. The rock weeps. Every vertical face has a dark beard of seepage that never dries, feeding the ground below. The ground itself isn't really ground. It's plant.

Vegetation here is not a covering. It is the substrate. A total, uninterrupted living mass, meters thick in places. Sphagnum and peat moss and cushion plants and woolly alpine hair-grass have colonized the raw basalt completely, building their own soil out of themselves. You don't step on rock, you step on a deep, saturated mattress that sighs and takes your boot to the ankle and then slowly returns. The peat holds water like a sponge wrung just to dripping — every footfall releases it, every depression is a black mirror pool. In the flatter reaches the bog becomes a mosaic of small, bright tarns, each one sky-filled, each one fringed with cotton grass that shivers even when there is no wind. On the slightly higher, drier steps, the moss gives way to low, dense thickets of dwarf willow and juniper, only knee-high but so tightly interwoven you couldn't push through them if you tried. They grow sideways, mat-like, hugging the rock. From a distance the whole plateau looks like it has been upholstered in green velvet that has been left out in the rain.

It is an open country, valley-sized. You can see a long way because the plants are low and the air is clear. The plateau stretches for kilometers before it simply ends — a clean, uneroded scarp dropping off into hazy lower air you never quite see. Within that openness, things are sparse. The vegetation is everywhere, but everything else is scattered and far apart. One stunted rowan, then nothing for three hundred meters. One cairn of piled basalt. One pool with a single dragonfly, then no animal again for a long time. The emptiness is part of its character — a lot of breathing room between presences. You are never crowded here. You are never even accompanied.

What presences there are, are industrial.

They are not towns. They are not ruins, exactly. They are working, or they were working very recently. Scattered across the bog at intervals of a half kilometer or more: a pump house of riveted steel and concrete, its paint flaked to orange primer by the bright sun and the wet. A low, long processing shed with a corrugated roof that drums in the wind. A substation humming behind a chainlink fence that has been swallowed on two sides by moss, the moss climbing the diamonds. Narrow-gauge rails, not for transport but for peat cutting, running out onto the bog and disappearing into a cutting face where the black peat has been sliced vertically like cake, exposing a wall ten feet high that glistens and drips. And the wires — black power lines marching on concrete poles across the green, straight as a rule, carrying electricity away toward that scarp edge.

None of these structures are large enough to dominate the landscape. Each one is an island in the living mass. But they are definitely industrial. Electrified. Mechanized. You see the scaled production logic: the uniform cuts, the prefabricated parts, the generators. Someone decided this high, wet, bright bog was a resource and brought engines up here to harvest it.

The engines are why you can't hear yourself think.

The sound here is deafening, and it is not biological. Fauna is rare — once in a while a pipit cuts across the moss, a few hardy midges that die quickly in the cool air — but they make no chorus. The roar comes from two things that have become one thing: water and machines. Because the plateau is so flat and the erosion is so young, water doesn't drain quietly. It moves everywhere at once, a million shallow braids of runoff hissing over rock, pattering through grass, pouring over every low ledge in a white, glassy curtain. And that water has been put to work. At the edge of each industrial cut is a sluice, a spillway, a turbine house. Water is taken, dropped, run through a turbine, returned. The combined sound is a constant, low-frequency thunder that vibrates in your sternum, topped with a high, ceaseless static of falling water and the industrial drone of dynamos and pumps under load. The wind, when it comes — and it always comes at elevation — combs through the cotton grass and makes the power lines sing, a harsh Aeolian wire-note that carries for miles in the thin air. You have to raise your voice to be heard by someone a meter away. After an hour you stop trying to talk.

And yet, for all that noise and wet and industry, almost nothing changes quickly here.

The dynamic is slow. Patient to the point of stillness. The peat grows a millimeter a year. The rock weathers by frost-shattering a flake at a time. The turbines turn at the same RPM all day and all night. The pools reflect the same blinding sky hour after hour. The only fast motion is water endlessly renewing itself on its short journey across the rock before it goes over the scarp. Everything else — the moss closing over a fence post, the rust creeping across a pump house roof, the bog slowly swallowing a disused rail siding — happens on a scale of years and decades. It is a loud, bright, wet, living place that is also profoundly still. You feel that if you left for twenty years and came back, the same pump would be humming, the same sheet of water would be sliding over the same basalt step, the moss would just be a little thicker, the water a little more present.

The rule of this world is simple: life arrived before the land was ready for it, and industry arrived before life could finish softening the stone. So now they exist together, layered. Raw rock, total plant, scattered steel, all under that huge, cool, merciless light, all saturated, all roaring quietly and slowly becoming something that has not yet decided what it will be.
