It is high. That is the first fact and the last fact and the fact that contains every other fact.

The air has been thinned until it is almost a different substance. Not empty — just less there to carry anything but light. The light is terrible and total. At this elevation there is nothing left to filter it. The sky is not blue so much as it is *present*, a deep, hard, enameled dome that presses down on the world with brightness. The sun is smaller and whiter and far more exacting than it is below. There are no clouds to soften it, only a few high mare's tails that throw no shadow at all. Everything has an edge. Every rock has two sides: the sun-side which is hot to the touch despite the cold, and the shade-side which holds frost at noon.

You are standing — or you imagine standing — on a table that was lifted.

This is not a peak. A peak would be more dramatic and more simple. This is a highland, a plateau, a ruined plain left in the sky when the rest of the world fell away. It extends out of sight in every direction, but not as a flatness. It rolls. It breathes in long, settled swells of stone, like a frozen ocean. The rock is old. Not jagged-old, not the sharp violence of young mountains, but the oldness of something that has been weathered for so long it has come into agreement with the wind.

Erosion here is not an event. It is a finished language.

The basalt and granite have been planed and pitted and hollowed into forms that now protect themselves. Broad bowls and shallow troughs, whale-back ridges, low amphitheaters with smooth walls, potholes perfectly circular where water once drilled and then left. Frost has wedged every joint into a staircase. Scree slopes lie at the exact angle they can lie at forever without moving. Nothing is falling. Nothing is about to fall. The world has found its shape and keeps it.

Cool is the permanent condition. Not winter-cold, not killing cold. The cool of stone that has never really warmed through. Your breath makes a small cloud that vanishes instantly in the dry, bright air. The sun warms your face while the rock steals heat from your boots. If you put your hand on the ground in shadow you will find ice an inch down, even in the height of what passes for summer here — a clear, sleepless, wind-scoured summer that never greens.

Water is scarce the way it is scarce in a good desert — not absent, but precise. There is no river, no lake that deserves the name. Instead there is dampness as a system.

Condensation collects every night on the undersides of stones and in the lips of the wind-hollows, silvering them with dew that never quite becomes runoff. In the deeper bowls, tarns the size of a room or a house hold water so clear and so still it looks like glass poured into rock. They are fed not by streams but by seep. Dark threads of moisture weep from the cliff seams, drawing a black line down paler rock, nourishing a fringe of moss before disappearing again into air. After a rain — which comes rarely, suddenly, sideways — the whole plateau will briefly connect: rills run for an hour in channels polished to a shine, vanish into gravel, and are gone. The rest of the time, you can walk for a mile and hear water trickling somewhere just out of sight, under stone.

And where there is water, even that little, there is something living that has learned to live on almost nothing.

Vegetation is not a cover. It is an annotation. The ground is mostly stone — bare, lichen-painted stone. The lichens are the true color of the place: mustard yellow, rust orange, pale sage, and the black-green of old copper, mapping each boulder in continents. Between them, in cracks and in the lee of rocks, the higher plants make their attempt. Cushion plants, tight as fists, hugging warmth. Tufts of wiry fescue that shiver even when there is no wind. Dwarf willow no higher than your ankle, its leaves glossy and small as coins, creeping along the ground instead of rising. No trees. Trees would be an insult to the wind. Every hundred yards you find another solitary, starved, perfect little garden tucked into a hollow that catches one hour more sun, one drop more seep.

If you looked only at the ground, you would call this place empty.

It is. The density of things is astonishingly low. Distance is the dominant material. You can see for miles to a horizon that shimmers with heatless light and there is nothing between you and it except stone and light and air. A boulder. Then half a mile of nothing. Then another boulder. The sky is enormous over it, a volume so vast it makes sound feel lonely.

And yet the place is not empty at all.

The air is full of wings.

This is the contradiction that takes a moment to resolve: the ground is hollow, the sky is crowded. Fauna here has abandoned the ground. Or perhaps it never needed it. Clouds of small, dark birds — not quite choughs, not quite swifts — move in braids and sheets, hundreds of thousands of them, turning on a single thought. Pikas and hares and something like a marmot but leaner watch from rock tops with eyes that never blink against the glare. On the tarns, midges rise in pillars. On the lichen, beetles the size of your thumb click and right themselves. And higher, always higher, the large ones wheel: pale raptors and condor-things with ten-foot shadows that cross the ground like clock hands.

They are abundant beyond reason for such a spare place. Life has decided to be here lavishly, but thinly spread. You could walk an hour and see only stone, and then round a ridge to find a hollow where a thousand nests crowd a single seep, chittering. Or look up and realize the entire sky has been faintly speckled the whole time.

And they are loud.

That is the other weather of this plateau. The sound.

The wind itself is a constant, not gusting but pouring — a high, unbroken river of air that comes over the edge of the world from nowhere and goes over the other edge to nowhere. It finds every flute and pipe the erosion has carved and plays them all at once. The plateau hums. The potholes moan. The long troughs between ridges act as resonators and make a low organ note that you feel in your teeth.

On top of that, the fauna never stops. The birds never stop. The insects never stop. Not in a chorus that rises and falls — in a single, sustained, deafening chord that does not vary. It is not louder at dawn or quieter at dusk because there is no dawn or dusk here, only that same white sun moving like a slow lamp. It is not seasonal because there is no season. The sound simply IS, at the exact decibel of a waterfall heard from inside the waterfall. You have to open your mouth to equalize the pressure. After a few minutes you stop hearing it as sound and start hearing it as the condition of air itself. To shout would be meaningless; your voice would be taken and laid flat into the roar without ever making a ripple.

That is the deep rule of this place: it does not change.

Not slowly. Not at all. Things happen — a bird lands, a cloud shadows a tarn for thirty seconds, a stone ticks as it expands in the sun — but nothing accumulates. Nothing progresses. The lichen does not spread. The scree does not slide. The water does not cut deeper. It is a place in perfect, eerie equilibrium, as if time has been thinned along with the air. The light is always this bright. The cold is always this exact cool. The sound is always this loud.

That changelessness makes the few unusual things stand out more sharply.

There are stones here that should not hold together the way they do — thin arches of basalt a few inches thick spanning six feet, humming faintly with the wind, that have not collapsed though they look like they should have yesterday. There are tarns that are perfectly warm on one half and rimed with ice on the other, with a razor line between. And there is the matter of the tools.

There are no buildings. There have never been buildings; the wind would not permit the idea. But there are flakes. If you kneel by a windbreak rock you will find them: a scatter of chipped obsidian and chert, the debitage of someone — or something — knapping hand tools. Clean edges, simple blades, scrapers, a point. No habitation, no hearth, no wall. Just a place where hands sat and worked for a while and left. Some of the flakes are still sharp enough to cut. Some of the pika burrows are lined with them, incorporated into nests. The large birds will pick up a bright flake and drop it from height, as if testing. Whether the makers were people who passed through long ago and learned not to stay, or whether the fauna here has learned a primitive craft of its own, is not answered. The plateau does not answer. It keeps its equilibrium. The flakes just weather, very, very slowly, in the bright, deafening, beautiful stasis at the top of the world.

You come to understand, after a while, that the emptiness is not a lack. It is the size of the room that the sound and the light need in order to be this complete.
