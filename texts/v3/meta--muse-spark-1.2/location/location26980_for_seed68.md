It is not a city. It was trying to be one, and then it stopped.

You come to it from the north, down the old service road that drops off the basalt shelf — and that's when you first understand how small it is. From the ridge you can see the whole thing at once, edge to edge, like looking into a bowl or a room. Low country, cool country. The land here is young. That is the wrong word for land, but it's the right feeling. This whole flat was not here very long ago — pushed up or poured out or cut open — and it hasn't learned how to be soft yet.

The rock under everything is sharp. Not mountain-sharp, low-sharp. Black and grey basalt with clean, stair-step fractures where it was quarried and then abandoned. The quarry walls still stand around three sides of the hamlet like the walls of a cathedral that was never roofed, forty feet high, sheer, dripping. Beyond them the old farmland goes on, but inside the bowl the ground is something else: level, cindered, half gravel and half slab, bitten with service trenches and rail cuts that were made all at once with machines and then left to weather. Nothing has been rounded. Even the puddles have corners. Where the stone was broken, it stayed broken. Moss has found the cracks but hasn't softened them.

The air is cool enough that you notice your breath in the morning, not cold enough to drive you inside. Normal daylight — open, white-blue, high and even — falls straight down into the bowl without any of the haze you expect from an industrial place. It makes everything legible. You can see rust on a bolt from half a street away.

This is a town, no question, but a sparse one. An urban idea drawn with too much erasing. There are eight or nine big buildings to call it a town at all, and they do not huddle. Each one stands with a full lot of breathing room around it, as if they were placed by someone who was afraid they might catch fire from each other. Which they might have.

They are factories in the proper industrial sense — not workshops, not makerspaces — brick mills three and four stories tall with arched windows, most of them still skinned in soot, corrugated iron sheds, a power house, a waterworks that never had much water to work. Iron staircases cling to their outsides like vines made of rust. Narrow-gauge rails run between them, not quite meeting. Brick smokestacks, cold now or mostly cold, stand like landmarks. You could navigate by them: "turn left at the stack with the starlings." There are streets, laid out on a grid that is too generous, with curbs and lampposts and painted lines going to places where there is nothing left to go. The infrastructure is all there, scaled for more life than ever arrived. You get the feeling the planners drew a city and then only built every third building.

Between the buildings, the emptiness is not empty.

Because it is lush. Improbably, aggressively lush for a place that is only damp and not wet. There is no river through the bowl — the river is a kilometer east, you can hear it if the wind is right. Here, water comes differently. It condenses. The basalt walls weep all night. The cool air hits the warm brick of the mills that still run, and by morning every surface is beaded. The stacks exhale steam even when they are banked for the night, and that steam settles. Gutters rattle for an hour after dawn with a rain that never fell from the sky. Pockets of water live where they can: a cistern behind the weaving mill that never dries, a low corner of the old rail cut that became a black mirror pond, the rusted boiler beds now full of rain, the seams of the quarry walls where ferns have wedged themselves and made hanging gardens.

Where there is condensation, there is green. The vegetation here is opportunistic and total. Not a forest — something more deliberate, more sown and then unsown. Ivy covers the north face of every brick building so thick you can't see the windows until you are close and realize the windows are open behind it, and the ivy is growing inside. Buddleia and birch have seeded themselves on the flat roofs and grown into small trees, their roots in six inches of composted soot and leaf litter. Moss makes a carpet over the cinder lots between the factories, so deep and springy it silences your footsteps. Morning glory and knotweed have taken the fences. In the places where no one bothered to lay slab, waist-high grass shivers between the rails. The whole bowl smells of wet brick, iron, and crushed green.

And it is loud.

That is the first thing you actually hear before you see the green properly. For a place so slow, so patient, it is unreasonably loud. Two of the mills are still working, and have been working on the same job for years. In the spinning mill on the west side, the line shafts still turn — long steel axles running the length of the building, slapping leather belts, driving looms that clack and clack with a rhythm you feel in your teeth. In the power house, a flywheel the size of a house turns once every three seconds, and every turn sighs steam and throws a hot, metallic breath out the louvered windows. It is not a frantic noise. It is slow, grinding, inexorable. Hammers that fall every seven seconds. A stamp press that inhales, holds, and then slams. The sound bounces off the quarry walls and has nowhere to go, so it stays. Even when the machines pause between cycles you hear their echo.

The slow dynamic is everywhere else. Nothing here happens fast. Rust creeps across a corrugated wall over years and becomes a kind of paint. The ferns on the quarry wall grow a millimeter a week. The pond in the rail cut rises an inch after a week of damp mornings and falls an inch in a dry spell. The town government, if there is one, meets once a season. The work being done in the mills — they are pressing the same sheet metal parts they pressed last year, for a machine no one nearby uses — doesn't seem to need to be done quickly. It is industrial, fully electrified and mechanized and scaled for production, but it has forgotten urgency. The engines run because they have always run.

That is why the fauna have come back, or rather why they never left. It is abundant to the point of being strange to see in a factory town. Starlings pour like smoke from the stacks at dusk, thousands of them, chattering so loud they compete with the looms. Pigeons nest in the belt shafts, utterly unafraid of the slap of leather overhead. Cats — mill cats, brindled and soot-footed — stalk along the pipe runs. The pond has herons, absurdly elegant against the rust, and dragonflies big as your hand that hunt in the steam. At night the crickets take over the lots between the buildings and make a second factory of sound. In the moss, beetles. Under the ivy, mice. The workers — there are workers, a few dozen, in heavy canvas and wool for the cool air — step around nests without comment. No one chases the kestrel that lives in the power house rafters. It is understood that the building belongs at least as much to it.

There is an unusualness here that is not dramatic. A quiet weirdness. On the south wall, where the quarry face weeps most, someone has trained the runoff. Not with pipes, but with strings. Hundreds of vertical strings, weighted, running from the lip of the wall to troughs on the ground, and each morning they glitter with gathered dew that runs down them like beads on an abacus. The water is collected and used to feed the boilers. The factories themselves are half plant now; you will see a steel I-beam that has become a planter because wind-blown soil lodged on its flat top and a row of grasses took hold and now sway five stories up. The brick itself seems to sweat moss from inside out.

The rules of this place, if you stay long enough to learn them, are simple:

Keep the machines oiled, even if you don't need what they make. Let the water find its way down the strings. Do not scrape the moss off the lots — it keeps the dust down and the sound from becoming unbearable. Leave the doors and windows open so the birds can come and go. Dress for cool, for damp. Speak loudly.

If you stand in the center, at the crossing where the two main streets meet and where the moss has been worn away by footsteps, you can turn in a full circle and take in the whole world. Stack, mill, power house, ivy, wall, fern, pond, rail, grass, sky. It is small enough to hold in your head like a single room, and complete enough that you don't immediately feel the need to leave. The light is still even. The flywheel turns. The heron waits. A belt slaps. Condensation drips from a leaf onto a hot pipe and vanishes with a hiss that is, for a second, the loudest thing in the bowl.
