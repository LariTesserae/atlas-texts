It is a high place first, before it is anything else.

The light hits you like a physical weight. Not hot — cool, thin, bright to the point of bleaching. The sky over the plateau is a deep, gashed violet-blue that never quite becomes the soft blue of lowlands, because there isn't enough air to soften it. The sun is small and white and exact. At midday it throws shadows that are hard-edged as cut metal, and even in full light the air holds a coolness on your skin, a constant high-altitude chill that lives in the stone. You breathe and feel you are not getting quite enough, each inhalation clean and sharp and a little insufficient.

You are standing on weather that has had a very long time to make up its mind.

This is not a mountain range. It is a continent lifted. A shield of old mineral, a tableland so vast its far edge bows down with the curvature of the planet and vanishes into haze. Once, this was seabed or lowland, but it has been uplifted and planed and left alone for so long that its shape feels settled, inevitable. The ground is not jagged or raw. It is broad, undulant, eroded into forms that have stopped arguing with wind. Low domes, shallow basins a mile across, long wind-combed ripples in stone that look like frozen dunes, all of it scoured and polished. The substrate is pale — salt, chalk, lithium white, gypsum, ash-grey basalt showing through like bone where the salt skin has cracked. You can walk for hours and the polish does not change, except where it is broken by a darker stain, a radial bloom of lichen-iron. There is no soil. There is no water.

That is the first rule here. There is no water to be had.

Not in the way lowlanders understand it. No rivers, no streams, no pools. No damp under rocks. The air is desiccated, wrung out by the altitude and the cold, and what little moisture circulates does so as a brief, hard frost that sublimates before noon. The salt flats exhale a faint alkaline tang when the sun hits them. The world is anhydrous, and everything that lives here has conceded that fact entirely. This is not a desert waiting for rain. This is a chemistry that has moved on without it.

And yet it is green. Or not green. It is *vegetal* beyond any temperate comprehension.

From a distance, you misread them. You think they are rock formations, or ruins, because nothing that sparse could be alive at such scale. They stand apart from one another across the white plain — a forest that refuses to be a forest. Each individual is enormous and solitary, with a mile or more of empty salt between it and its neighbor. But each is also a continent of itself, and together they fill the sky.

They are not trees. They are something that solved the same problem — how to stand tall and eat light — with different materials and no interest in compromise.

The dominant form is columnar-broad, a fluted trunk the diameter of a hill, made not of wood but of layered, fibrous mineral, a cellulose-silicate that grows like nacre. It is pale at the base where it roots, darkening upward to a sooty black-green, then branching, not into boughs but into vast, sail-like plates. Each plate is a leaf the size of a stadium roof, but thick and rigid, corrugated for strength, angled to the sun and translucent at the edges where light passes through as through frosted glass. They do not flutter. They turn. You can see them move.

They have no need for water because they do not use it. Their sap is not sap — it is a clear, volatile oil, ammonia-bright and cold to the touch, that circulates in pressurized veins you can see pulsing under the translucent skin of the younger plates. They pull what they need directly from the air: carbon from the thin wind, nitrogen fixed by lightning, minerals mined by roots that exude acid and dissolve the salt plain beneath them. Where they root, the white ground dimples into a shallow, smooth basin of their own making, a chemical quarry. Around old individuals, the salt is stained amber and green with leached salts.

This is why, despite having no buildings at all, no walls, no roads, no ruins, no sign that any hand ever stacked a stone on another, the place feels industrial.

There is no built thing here. If you cut one of the great forms open you find no workshop, no engine. But it *is* the engine.

Stand near one and you hear it. Or rather, you feel it before you hear it, because the sound is so large it stops being sound.

They work.

The plates inhale with a hydraulic sigh that can be heard a kilometer away — a vast, slow gasp as they draw in cold air through stomae that open like shutters along their corrugated faces, slamming shut with a clatter like sheet metal. Inside, the oil ticks and percusses. Pressure builds, valves knock. When the sun is high, the whole organism goes into a kind of frenzied manufacture. Excess oxygen and waste gases are vented in abrupt, deafening exhalations that roar down the fluted trunk and blast the salt with hot, metallic-smelling wind. Fibers are extruded at the crown, where new plates are being spun out of the air itself with a high, screaming whine of cellulose being laid down filament by filament. Growth is not slow here. The turbulence is biological. A plate that was furled tight as a fist at dawn will have unfurled and hardened by dusk, creaking and popping the whole time, shedding papery cuticles that skitter across the plain in flocks like dead insects.

You thought from far away that the plain was empty and quiet. Up close it is a factory floor with no roof. Everything is clanking, hissing, sighing, groaning under tension. The oldest, largest individuals — whose crowns are a hundred meters above the salt, whose leaves cast shadows you can live in for an afternoon — produce a low, continuous thrum that you feel in your molars. It is not wind through branches. It is the sound of pressure vessels working, of a living refinery stamping out more of itself from sunlight and stone. When a storm moves across the plateau, and it does, weather made by the forest itself — great convection cells rising from the heat of all that vented gas, condensing into virgas that never rain — the wind sets all the plates ringing. Not rustling. Ringing, like struck bronze. The sound is literally deafening at close range. You need to open your mouth to equalize it. Birds, or what pass for birds here, do.

And there are birds, in abundance. Life has not filled the spaces between the giants, it has filled the giants themselves and the air above them. This is the contradiction that resolves when you see it: the ground is sparse, but the living volume is vast. Between trunks there is nothing but white mineral and wind and your own small shadow. *On* the trunks, there is a vertical continent.

The flanks of each column are terraced with hard shelves of dead fiber where colonies of things live. Bright, dry analogues of lichens and mosses cling in tight rosettes, silver and rust-red, needing no rain. Armored grazers with mirror-bright shells, like oversized woodlice, rasp at the fiber. Gliders with wingspans of three meters but bodies light as paper launch from leaf-edges, riding the thermal columns that the plants themselves exhale. They are translucent, veined like the leaves they mimic, and they scream as they go — a thin, needle-like sound that cuts through the industrial bass. In the crown, in the narrow humid exhalant chambers where the plant vents its waste air, swarms of smaller things breed in a constant, storm-like cloud: gnats of chitin and silica whose larvae spin their pupae directly onto the vent-gills, trembling. Predators come for them, long-jointed hunters that walk the undersides of leaves on hooked feet, upside down to gravity, their carapaces the same sooty black-green as their host so that they disappear until they move.

Nothing here drinks. Everything condenses or extracts. The gliders land and press their bellies to the salt to collect the cold oil that weeps from root-cracks during the night. The grazers crack the salt itself for its trapped nitrates. Death is quick and desiccated; a corpse mummifies in an hour and is stripped to its mineral in a day, not by rot but by the dry, efficient scraping of a hundred small mouths and the plant's own acid mist.

Weirdness is not spectacle here, it is the quiet wrongness of a system that works without what you thought was necessary. Leaves that face *away* from the sun at noon, to dump heat instead of gathering it. Roots that grow *up* during the night to mine dew from the air that never actually forms. A plant that, when a plate is damaged, does not heal it but detonates it — a sharp crack and the whole plate shatters into fibrous shrapnel to prevent parasitic colonization, and a new bud is already inflating from the scar with a high-pressure hiss. The rules are familiar — light in, mass out, competition, predation — but the solvent has changed.

And it is all in motion. This is not a stable climax forest holding a pose. It is turbulent, brawling, hyperactive. The established, weathered geometry of the land is the only thing that is stable. On top of it, biology is in a constant state of construction and demolition. A young column grows thirty centimeters a day during the bright season, visibly taller by evening, its crown swaying dangerously until its neighbors' wind-shadow allows it to thicken. A senescent giant, its internal pressures finally failing after centuries, will collapse not with a crash but with a long, shuddering sigh, its plates folding down like a closing telescope, exhaling a last, scalding cloud of gas that smells of hot iron and turpentine, feeding a dozen opportunists that have been waiting for weeks on its lower flanks, already chewing at the base.

By night the sound does not stop. Under the crush of stars, which are merciless and unblinking through the thin air, the plains are a field of huge, breathing silhouettes. You hear distant hydraulic thumps as if some unseen stamping mill were at work over the horizon, and nearer, the ceaseless tick and ping of fibers contracting in the cold. The light is gone but the work continues, slower now, a night shift of chemical assembly lit from within: some of the younger plates bioluminesce faintly along their veins, a cool electroluminescent blue, tracing oil as it moves. From a high ridge the plain looks like a circuit board breathing, a sparse scattering of dim blue lanterns across a white void, each one industrious and alone, together making their own weather, their own noise, their own sky.

There is no one here to tend it. No one needs to.
