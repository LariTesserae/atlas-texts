It is high.

Not a peak - a broad, lifted place. A table of stone that was pushed up not long ago, geologically speaking, and hasn't had time to be softened. The air finds you thin and cool, the kind of cool that lives in shade even when you stand in the sun. Breath comes a little short, sound carries a little far.

The plateau is young rock, dark basalt and pale limestone still in sharp contrast, still in sharp edges. The uplift left it fractured in clean, vertical breaks. Cliffs fall away from the rim in stair-steps, unweathered, without talus aprons at their feet yet - as if the mountain forgot to crumble. The top itself is not flat, but broken into shallow basins and low hogbacks, a fresh topography of knife ridges and blocky tores that still show the chisel marks of tectonics. There has been no time for rivers to carve valleys. Water hasn't learned the routes yet.

And yet there is a city here.

From a distance you would think it natural - the same grey and buff stone as the plateau itself, low and spread wide. It does not huddle. That is the first strangeness. It is urban in extent and ambition, a town that could hold thousands, but it is sparse in the way it occupies itself.

Streets are laid out with intention, stone-paved by hand, with gutters and low walls and terraces, but they are too wide for the people in them. Houses stand with generous emptiness between them - thirty, fifty paces of common ground, or garden, or simply weathered rock left alone. There is infrastructure everywhere - cisterns, granaries cut into the hillside, retaining walls wrapped around every contour, a ring of aqueduct piers that is somehow both monumental and unfinished-looking - but it serves a population that has thinned itself out to the point of quiet. You can stand at an intersection and see the whole valley of the city: roofs stepping down into a basin, smoke from a dozen scattered hearths, and between them, more empty space than building.

The building is all handwork. No sawn steel, no glass in great sheets. Fieldstone split with wedges and set dry or with lime, roofs of heavy sod and slate, timber where timber will grow - and timber does grow, lushly, despite the cold. Posts and lintels are adzed, not milled. Door hinges are hammered copper, green-black. Looms and ladders and plows are wood and stone and simple iron. You can see the tool marks on everything, the slight waver of a hand-cut beam, the repetition of a pattern learned by muscle rather than machine. And because the city is young in its stone, so are its repairs - no rounding of corners from centuries of hands, no deep ruts in the thresholds. Everything looks placed, recently, and then... left to stay as it is.

The vegetation should not be this abundant at this altitude and this coolness, but it is. It is not forest - there aren't enough centuries of soil for forest. It is something fresher, more immediate. Moss and lichen in thick, saturated mats over every north-facing stone. Grasses in tussocks, dense and emerald, filling the spaces between houses. Low, wind-trained shrubs with small, waxy leaves, and stands of a pale-barked, quick-growing tree that the inhabitants have half-domesticated - coppiced for poles and firewood, but always allowed to spring up again in the gaps. In the sheltered basins where cold air pools, there are sudden gardens, intensely green: tubers, brassicas, beans running on twine lattices made of withies. The plants are lush, but they are not crowding. Like the houses, they have room. Each garden is an island, each thicket a deliberate pocket between pavement and rock.

Water is here, but not as a system. The plateau is fundamentally dry - too high and too young to hold aquifers, too cool for heavy rain. The dampness is interstitial. It arrives as condensation. As the sun warms the stone - a normal, clear, high-altitude light, bright without being harsh, the kind that makes edges perfectly visible - the cold rock sweats. Morning after morning you wake to find every surface beaded, the moss dripping, the slate roofs dark with it. In the hollows, small tarns appear and remain, perfectly still, reflecting the sky without a ripple. In the cisterns, water holds at the same level always. It is also the mist that forms at the rim where valley air hits the plateau edge and climbs, then vanishes by midday. There are no rivers here, only this daily breath in and out, a dampness that sustains the lushness without ever flooding it.

And the fauna - there is so much of it, so quietly.

Herds would be the wrong word, because that would imply density. Instead, life is everywhere but thinly distributed, as if the world decided abundance did not require crowding. The short grasses rustle with hares the color of stone. High-altitude goats with slate-grey coats pick along the sharp ridges in pairs, not herds. Birds - many, many birds - are the chief citizens: small brown creepers and alpine choughs with red beaks, large, slow hawks that circle without ever needing to flap, flocks of white finches that move between the gardens. Insects are thick in the sun - bumblebees heavy and slow in the cool air, moths that rest on the warm stones with wings open. On the edge of each tarn, amphibians with translucent skin blink and wait. Nothing is rare. Everything is just... spaced. You hear a call here, and the answer comes from half a kilometer away. You see tracks crossing the wide street, but not the animal that made them.

You hear so little overall.

For a place with a city and with all this life, it is mostly quiet. Wind moves, but without dust to carry. The stone does not shift. The birds call infrequently, and when they do, the notes are short and die quickly in the thin air. There is no clamor of market or industry, though you can see a market square with stone stalls. People move deliberately, speak low, work with hand tools that thud and scrape rather than ring. Even the water is silent - the tarns do not overflow, the cisterns do not trickle. Sound doesn't accumulate here.

That is the rule beneath everything else, the one you feel before you understand it.

Nothing is changing.

Not in the sense of being preserved or abandoned. In the sense that change has been taken out of the physics of the place. The sharp escarpments do not weather. Lichen does not overgrow the adzed beams. The gardens are lush and at full yield and also not growing. A bean plant holds ripe pods and new flowers at once and does neither wither nor sprout further. A hare's footprint in the moss stays crisp, not filling in. The sun - though the light is perfectly normal daylight, mid-morning, clear and shadow-casting - does not move. Shadows fall at the same angle for hours. Condensation beads, but does not drip. The city's stone does not stain. The goats do not age.

It is not frozen dead. It is frozen in equilibrium. It breathes without progressing. You can walk, you can pick a bean, you can talk to a woman mending a copper pot - and the pot will be mending forever, if you step away and return. Life persists, abundance persists, craft persists, all held at the precise moment where damp and lush and cool and high and sharp and open and sparse and built and quiet are in perfect balance.

It is unusual only slightly - not a spectacle, just an afterthought that nags. You will notice that the tarn reflects a cloud that is no longer in the sky. That the same finch seems to cross the square every time you glance up. That anything you set down stays exactly as warm as when you left it.

The city will continue as a city without ever becoming a ruin or a metropolis. The plateau will remain young without ever getting old.
