It is a bowl in the high rock.

Not a valley, not a crater — a shallow place scooped out of a white mesa, as if a giant had pressed a thumb into warm clay and left it to harden. From the rim you can see the whole of it at once, and that is the scale of this place: one room. One hamlet-sized hollow with a definite edge. Walk twenty minutes in any direction and you hit the wall. The world ends in a lip of weathered stone and then falls away into nothing — a sheer drop into haze where the high desert below is only a suggestion of rust and distance.

The elevation makes the air thin and difficult. It's warm here, a dry, baked warmth that doesn't come from the sun. The sun itself is dim, a smudged coin behind a permanent veil of high, thin cloud that never breaks and never rains. The light is the light of late afternoon held forever — amber, flat, without shadow — bright enough to see every grain but too diffuse to cast a true shade. The heat comes up from the rock itself. The mesa holds the day's warmth in its salt and basalt and exhales it slowly all night. You feel it through your soles. The stone is never cold.

And there is no water here. Not a trace. No spring, no seep, no damp line in the stone, no morning dew. The air scrapes your throat. Your lips crack within an hour if you don't oil them. The ground is a field of dry things: fine blistered salt that crunches like frost underfoot, slabs of pitted dolomite the color of old teeth, dunes of iron-red dust that have settled into permanent, wind-carved ripples, and everywhere a grit of gypsum crystals that glitter dully without moisture. It is a place that has been dry for so long that erosion has given up on cutting and settled instead into smoothing. The boulders are round-shouldered. The rim is softened. Gullies are not gullies but long, shallow swales polished by wind-borne dust over ten thousand years. This place has arrived at the shape it wants to be and holds it, weathered, in balance.

And yet it is lush.

The contradiction hits you only after your eyes adjust to the dimness. At first you see only scattered dots of color on the salt — gray-green, amber, bruised purple — and mistake them for lichen or stone. Then you come closer and understand that each dot is a crown, and each crown is enormous and alive and utterly without water.

They stand apart from each other, with a great deal of empty salt between them — sparse, as if the bowl can only afford a few extravagances. No thickets, no canopy. Ten, maybe thirty individuals in the whole bowl, but each one is a world of its own. They are not trees as you know trees. They are not green.

The largest, near the center, rises fifteen feet tall on a trunk that is not wood but a dense column of braided fiber, like hemp rope fossilized, pale and peeling in papery strips. From its top explodes a fountain of leaves, each leaf as big as a cloak, thick, waxy, translucent. They are not green but opal — cream veined with rose and soot-black, inflated like lungs, rustling even when there is no wind because they are always half-deflated, creaking as they pump themselves full of hot air. They do not store water. If you cut one — and the people who live here sometimes do, with careful flaked knives — it does not bleed sap. It exhales a warm, scented oil, citron and iron, and a puff of fine, dry spores that hang in the air for hours. The oils are their blood. They drink nothing. They make their moisture from the air's chemistry and from the light, condensing trace ammonia and hydrocarbons onto the vast, electrostatic fields of their leaves, which crackle faintly in the dimness. At dusk you can see Saint Elmo's fire ticking along their edges.

Around them, other forms. Low, sprawling cushions of what looks like black moss but is actually a dense felt of hair-fine glass threads, warm to the touch, thrumming. Barrel-shaped succulents made not of flesh but of layered, dry membranes like lantern paper, nested one inside the other, rattling. Vine-things that never touch the ground, that anchor to nothing but air, tumbleweed skeletons that levitate a foot above the salt on the heat, rolling slowly, endlessly.

All of them are dry. All of them are lush. Their surfaces are dusty, powdery, yet they are soft and full and pressing outward with life. The bowl smells like a spice market sealed in a jar — resinous, peppery, mineral — a smell of oil and sun-baked fiber, not rot, not damp.

Because the plants are there, the animals are everywhere.

You hear them before you see them. This is a loud place.

The leaves clack. The lantern barrels pop and drum as their membranes tighten and release in the heat, a constant arrhythmic percussion. The felt cushions hiss with static. And through it all moves a storm of fauna that never touches water and never needs to.

The air is thick with them. Not birds. Membranous gliders the size of your hand, then the size of a cloak, made of the same oiled paper as the plants — translucent, ribbed, with no mouths that you can see. They ride the heat rising off the salt, they collide with the great leaves, they feed by brushing the electrostatic fields and gathering the charged dust and spores on their bellies. They chitter, not with voices but with the dry buzz of wing-casings rubbing, a sound like a thousand knuckles cracking at once. When one dies it crumples like burnt parchment and is immediately set upon by a tide of ground things.

The ground things are abundant and quick. Armored pill-insects the size of fists, their backs crusted with salt, rolling the dust into balls. Whip-legged striders that skate over the salt without touching it, held up on cushions of static. Something like a lizard but made of interlocking plates of fiber, with fronds for a tail that sifts the air. They do not drink. They chew the oil-leaves, they gnaw the glass-felt, they crack open fallen lantern membranes to lick the bitter wax inside. Everything is in motion, everything at once, a bazaar, an anthill kicked open — turbulent, hyperactive creation and destruction on fast-forward.

You can sit and watch a felt cushion double its diameter in an hour, sending out fresh glass hairs that sing as they grow, only to be stripped clean by a flock of gliders in minutes, only to begin again. Seed-pods the size of melons swell and burst with a gunshot crack that makes you flinch, firing corkscrewing fibers into the air that whistle as they spin. A tumble-levitator snags, tears, reforms. Nothing is stable. The place is not growing toward anything; it is churning, storm-state, Solaris-like in its restless making and unmaking, yet somehow never bare, never exhausted. The established, weathered bowl contains the chaos without being changed by it.

The people — if they are people — live lightly and sparsely, as the place demands.

You won't see a village. There are perhaps three shelters in the whole bowl, and they are not together. Each is a low windbreak of stacked salt bricks, half-melted back into the ground, roofed with stretched, cured leaf-membrane pulled taut over a frame of braided fiber. Beside each one, a cairn, a loom weight, a circle of ash where oil was burned for light. A few artifacts left to weather: a bone awl hafted with resin, a shallow bowl carved from dolomite, chimes made of tuned, dried lantern skins that knock and moan in the updraft and account for some of the loudness, though no one is there to play them.

Their technology is hand and stone and fiber. They cut leaves with flakes of chert, they weave the glass hair into blankets that crackle, they collect spore-dust in oiled skins, they press oil for their own throats. You see a footprint in the salt-dust near one windbreak, already half-erased by the striders. You do not see its maker. Perhaps they move with the gliders, perhaps they sleep through the loudest hours.

The rules of this world are simple once you accept them:

Light is scarce but enough.
Water is forbidden and irrelevant.
Oil is blood.
Static is muscle.
Heat is food.
Sound is how everything knows everything else is still alive.

Stand on the rim as the dim amber light thins a little further toward true dusk — though true night never quite comes — and listen to the bowl turn over itself below you. The great opal crowns clashing, the barrels popping like drums, the gliders buzzing, the chimes of the absent builders knocking, the hiss of growing glass, the crack of seed artillery, all of it rising on the warm breath of the rock into the thin high air. A hamlet-sized world, dry as bone, lush as a jungle, too loud and too busy and too strange to be possible, going on without water, without pause, in its perfect, settled, turbulent circle.
