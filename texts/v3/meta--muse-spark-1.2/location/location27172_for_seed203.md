It is not a cave.

That is the first mistake you make when you try to picture it. A cave has a ceiling close enough to touch, walls that return your voice. This place is below the surface the way an ocean is below the surface — not enclosed, but lowered.

A continent fell.

Or was never lifted in the first place. A rift, fresh on a planetary scale, split the crust while it was still cooling. The walls are young. You can tell because nothing has had time to soften. Basalt still shows the bright, sharp hackle of its breaking, stair-steps of black and iron-red fault blocks as big as mountain ranges, unweathered, unrounded, their edges still knife-clean. Talus slopes are small for the scale of the cliffs — only a few thousand years of frost-shattering has dusted the feet of these walls with scree. Everything else is still as it was torn.

You are kilometers down. The sky is not sky. It is ice.

The roof of this world is a sheet of clear, cold ice several hundred meters thick, a single continuous lens that caps the entire rift from end to end. It is not dark beneath it. It is brighter than day on the surface.

Sunlight comes through the ice without diffusion, refracted and concentrated, thrown down in hard, pale columns that do not move with the hours. The ice acts as a lightpipe — the whole plate glows. On clear days you can see the sun as a blurred white coin swimming far above, and around it, trapped air bubbles and stress fractures in the ice throw prisms and slow-moving caustics across the abyss. At night, the ice still glows faintly with stored light, a deep polar twilight that never fully goes out. The light has no warmth in it.

The air is cold. Not bracing, not crisp — absolute, dry, still cold that makes exposed basalt ring when struck. Your breath falls. It does not rise. It sinks and pools and freezes on the moss at your feet as rime. And yet everything is alive.

This is a plant world. Not a forest in a cavern. A plant *as* cavern.

Vegetation does not cover the terrain here. It *is* the terrain.

From the basalt floor — which you only glimpse in patches — rise trunks. There is no better word but they are not trees as you know them. Each one is a pillar, a tower, a living building. Thirty, fifty, a hundred meters across at the base, flaring buttressed roots that grip the raw rock and split it further with slow, irresistible growth. They rise for a kilometer, two kilometers, until their crowns brush the ice roof and spread against it like frost on a window, flattening into vast photosynthetic plates that drink the bright, cold light directly.

Between them is emptiness. That is the paradox of this place. It is totally vegetated and almost empty at once. Because the individuals are so vast and so spaced, you can walk for a day in the clear air between two trunks and see nothing but the distant next column and the ice-bright void. The density of the world is hollow. It is a cathedral space, a vaulted void where the pillars are alive. Sound dies quickly between them. The moss that carpets everything — a deep, springy, pale-green and white boreal felt that covers rock, root, fallen bark — eats sound. The cold eats sound.

And yet the place is turbulent. Violently, chaotically alive.

These plants are not slow. In this bright cold, under this fresh, mineral-rich basalt dust, with meltwater always available, they grow at a visible speed. You can sit and watch a shoot extend. Tendrils as thick as your body uncoil in minutes, seeking light. Bark sloughs off in papery, house-sized sheets that sail down for an hour before landing. A root will crack a boulder while you watch, with a sharp gunshot sound that is the loudest thing you will hear for days.

Growth and collapse are the weather here. Entire limbs, overextended, freeze and shatter and fall, bringing down curtains of lichen. A trunk will abort a crown-plate and grow a new one overnight, the old one descending like a pale continent, folding as it falls, to be set upon immediately by white fungal blooms that dissolve it in a week. The forest cannibalizes itself and rebuilds itself constantly. From a distance, the motion looks like time-lapse; up close, it is silent and relentless and slightly terrible. The air between the pillars is always full of slow-falling detritus: seeds like downed parachutes the size of sails, pollen snow, flakes of translucent leaf-glass that ring faintly when they chip against rock.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. There are no oceans, no lakes that last. The cold does not permit it. Instead, water moves as a thin film. Condensation forms on the underside of the ice roof in beads the size of rooms, merges, and falls as cold, pure rain that never reaches the ground — it is caught by the crown-plates and channeled down grooved bark in rivers that run *on* the trees. Every trunk is a watershed. Water braids down its flanks in silver sheets, gathers at the buttresses in temporary black pools steaming faintly in the cold, then sinks into the moss and into the porous basalt. In the early light, the whole rift glitters with hanging water. The air smells of wet stone and green sap and ozone. If you put your hand to the moss and squeeze, water weeps out, cold as melt.

If you stay still long enough you might see an animal. Not many. Life here has not had time or warmth to diversify much. There are things like moths, but large as blankets, translucent, that drift between the pillars without ever flapping, riding the slow convection currents that rise off the faint geothermal warmth of the floor. There are bark-grazers, pale six-limbed things that move with painful slowness up the trunks, their mouths leaving bright scars in the lichen. They make no call. Everything here conserves heat and noise.

And scattered through the moss, at the feet of the pillars, are the marks of people. Not a civilization. Never that.

Small things. A lean-to made of fallen plate-leaf, lashed with living vine that has since continued to grow, pulling the shelter slowly apart. A cairn of stacked basalt chips, each rock sharp and fresh. A rope bridge, now slack and overgrown, strung between two buttress roots over a damp chasm — the rope is braided fiber from the airy down-seeds, hand-twisted, primitive, strong. Carvings on a root face where someone has scraped away the lichen to make a picture: stick figures under a pillar, a sun behind ice. A cold hearth, stones blackened, long since filled with moss.

No roads. No fields. The forest would not allow a field. Anything cleared is taken back in weeks by the turbulent growth. The people here live like birds in a redwood canopy scaled up to geology — moving, making do, never staying long enough for the forest to notice them. You find their abandoned things more often than you find them. And when you do see a light at evening, far off between two trunks — a small, warm yellow fire, impossibly small in that bright, cold vastness — it stays distant. The void is too large, the moss too quiet to carry a shout.

This is the rule of the place: Light without heat. Growth without soil. Vastness without fullness. Everything is young and everything is already huge, and it is all still learning how to be.
