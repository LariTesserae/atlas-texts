It is not a valley. It is not a cave. It is the place where the surface failed to close all the way.

From above you would walk over it without knowing - flat, wind-scoured basalt plain, thin lichen, thin air. And then your boot finds no ground. A soft edge, a lip of stone that was never sharpened by weather, and you look down.

The Drop is about the size of a house turned on its side and sunk. Not deep - twelve, maybe fifteen meters - but deep enough that the temperature changes when you step over the lip. Cool. Not cold. The kind of cool that lives in stone that has never been warmed properly. Your breath doesn't quite fog but the air takes the heat off your skin immediately.

The walls are wrong in the way new things are wrong. They haven't learned how to be walls yet.

Emerging geology, raw and unsmoothed. This is not a canyon carved by a river over an age. This is a bubble that rose in the cooling rock and popped, or a place where the earth inhaled and forgot to exhale. The basalt is still vaguely soft-looking, folded like cooling wax, rippled, unscarred. No talus slope, no scree, no river-cut. The edges are round-shouldered and blistered with vesicles the size of fists and heads, like the stone was poured yesterday. In a few places the wall bulges inward as if it is still growing. If you put your hand to it, it is dry and faintly warm from some deeper heat, and it sweats.

Because for all its dryness, it is damp.

Not wet. There is no standing water, no pool, no stream. The dampness lives as behavior. Condensation beads on every vesicle and runs in slow, deliberate lines down the raw folds, gathering in shallow, dark hollows at the base where the stone has dimpled to hold it. For an hour the air will be perfectly dry and then, without rain, without clouds - there is no sky here except the ragged circle of it far above - the walls will weep. A fine mist exhales from the stone itself, hangs for twenty minutes in the cool, and vanishes. The floor has dark patches and light patches that migrate through the day. Where the water collects, a thin skin of silt has formed, black and smell-less, and nothing else.

Light should not reach here like this.

You are below the surface, walled in, but the place is bright. Painfully bright at noon. The light comes straight down the throat of the opening, of course, but it does not act like sunlight should. It pools. It gathers. In the upper third of the pit the air is clear and ordinary, but near the floor the light thickens and becomes almost particulate, like dust motes made of light itself, hanging motionless. It collects in the vesicles and makes them glow. It puddles in the damp hollows and makes the water shine white even in shadow. Shadows themselves are hesitant - they fall a few degrees off from where they should, and they are pale at the edges, as if reluctant to commit. If you stand very still at the bottom and look up, the circle of sky looks closer than the rim should allow. If you climb halfway up and look down, the floor looks farther away than it did. The space is not the same size twice.

Vegetation is sparse to the point of apology. There is no soil to hold it. A few things have insisted.

In the damp hollows, a low mat of something that is not quite moss - gray-green, translucent, more like frost on stone - spreads in coins the size of your palm, widely separated. Three or four wiry stems, jointed and leafless, rise from cracks near the light-pool, pale as etiolated sprouts, trembling though there is no wind. They never quite grow longer, never die. Near the very center where light puddles deepest, a single, stunted tree-thing has tried to happen - a trunk no thicker than your wrist, bark like folded paper, maybe seven feet tall, with a flat top of feathery, lichen-like leaves that turn themselves not toward the sun but toward the brightest patch of wall. Nothing else germinates here. The stone is too new to have been forgiven by roots.

And yet it crawls.

Fauna - abundant, impossible abundant for such a small, spare pocket. But sparse in the way the parameters demand: never crowded, never swarming. Each creature has its own vast allotment of empty air and empty stone, as if the place assigns solitude politely.

They are quiet. They have to be; sound doesn't carry right here. A chirp or a click dies a foot from its maker, muffled by the cool, damp air and the strange geometry.

On the walls, colonies of glassy isopods the size of your thumb work the condensation lines, each alone on its own trickle, their backs reflecting the pooled light like little lenses. Higher up, things like dragonflies but with no wings - they swim through the thick light without beating anything, flickering from vesicle to vesicle, pausing long enough to drink light and then gone, each on its own looping, unhurried patrol.

On the floor, long-legged pale amphibians - almost translucent, you can see the dim glow of their stomachs - move with such slowness you think they are statues until you look away and find they have crossed three feet. They do not croak. They touch the damp silt with a tongue like a damp leaf and leave a perfect, brief print that dries in minutes.

Something that could be mistaken for a bird, if birds were made of folded stone and breath, nests in the blisters of the wall. You only ever see one at a time, and it never makes a nest out of anything - it cups the bare stone with its body and the stone warms under it. When it lifts, there is a faint, perfect impression of feathers left in warm rock for an hour before it fades.

Everything is active. Everything is hunting or drinking or growing in its isolated, careful way, but nothing hurries, nothing makes noise enough to echo. It's an ecosystem running at low volume, like it's being careful not to wake the rock.

The built things are not built by you.

Scattered - three, maybe four if you count the one that might just be a shadow. Near the base of the paper-bark tree, a ring of small, upright stones, none higher than your knee, set in a circle too precise to be tumble. No mortar, no tool marks. The stones are the same raw basalt as the walls, but they have been licked smooth on one side, as if tongued for generations. Inside the ring, the silt is always damp, even when everywhere else has dried.

Halfway up the eastern bulge, a ledge holds a single structure - a hollow, woven dome, about the size of a beehive, made from those pale jointed stems, now dead and bleached. Something made it, lives in it or left it. The weave is tight and clever, with a single round doorway that faces the light-pool. Nothing moves inside, but at certain angles the interior is darker than shadow has any right to be.

And on the north wall, perhaps the strangest artifact: a handprint. Not carved, not painted. Pressed, as if someone pushed their palm into the stone while it was still warm wax, and the stone remembered. The fingers are too long by one joint. The palm holds a permanent bead of condensation that never falls.

There is no tech here. Nothing has ever needed it. The rules are different enough that tools would be rude.

The rules, as you learn them by standing there:

1.  Light has weight and sometimes rests.
2.  Distance is negotiable, but only when you are not measuring it.
3.  Water does not need a source to arrive.
4.  Stone can be new. Stone can be unfinished, and you can watch it try to decide what shape it wants to be.
5.  Abundance does not mean crowding. You can have a hundred lives in a single room and still feel utterly alone with each one.

If you stay past the hour when the mist exhales, you will notice your own breathing has gone quiet to match. You will notice the pale amphibians have all turned, separately, to look at you, without moving their heads. You will notice the light at your feet is a little brighter than it should be, as if it has pooled around your boots because you are also, briefly, a feature of this pocket.

You will climb out eventually, because below-surface places that are this bright and this cool and this new do not want you to stay long. The lip will feel a little higher on the way out than on the way in.

From the plain above, there is no sign again. Just flat basalt, just wind. Just a cool exhale from a crack in the earth that a moment ago was not there, and then, when you look back, is.
