It is a town in a hole.

Not a valley, not a basin made by a river - a cut. A perfect, steep-sided rectangular pit dropped forty or fifty meters straight down into the flat land, as if a city block had been pressed down into soft earth and left there. The land around it is ordinary and level, field and road, but here the ground simply stops at a lip of weathered limestone and then falls. From the fields you would almost walk past it if not for the sound.

Standing on the rim you look down and you don't see a pit. You see a roof.

The entire floor and walls of the excavation are filled - not covered, *filled* - with growth and building at such density there is no empty volume left. It is a hamlet the size of a quarry, a single dense room with walls of rock for its boundaries, and everything inside has grown or been built to occupy every inch of air.

The air itself is the first thing. It is warm. A trapped, greenhouse warmth that has nothing to do with season. The sunken walls shelter it from wind, the mass of living leaves respires, the machines below exhale heat, and the wet stone sweats. It is the warmth of a bathhouse, of soil under glass. You can see it - the atmosphere condenses. Even on a clear day, the air inside the pit hangs a little white, a low cloud that never quite becomes fog, beading on every surface.

Because it is always wet.

Water is not a river here, not a lake. It is everywhere in small amounts. It weeps from the quarry walls in dark vertical streaks where the limestone has been cut, seeping from bedding planes and running down in threads that never dry. It stands shallow on every flat surface and drips from every leaf tip. The pit has no drainage except what people have pumped, so the water table is right there, just under the floor. You hear it before you see it - a constant trickling, pattering, plinking against metal and leaf. Walk the iron stair that switchbacks down the wall and your handrail is wet. The steps are wet. The rock is black-wet and soft with moss that has padded itself into the chisel marks left a hundred years ago, until the tool marks are just a texture under green felt.

And the plants. There is not a surface that is not a plant, and not a plant that is not part of the structure.

This was built as an industrial quarter, layered and urban and deliberate - brick sheds and concrete machine halls, narrow row houses three and four stories tall pressed shoulder to shoulder, alleys, boiler rooms, catwalks, corrugated roofs, water towers stitched together with pipes and cables. That skeleton is still there, but it is no longer the primary material. The vegetation has not *overgrown* it so much as *become* it.

Root and vine are the architecture now. The faces of the buildings are lattices of creeper and fig, their leaves tiled so thick the brick is only a suggestion underneath. Flat roofs have become shallow meadows of fern and sedge, their soil held by the roof itself, their edges spilling over in green curtains. The ground level has no ground - there is no dirt to see, only a dense mat of moss, liverwort, and low creeping tradescantia so saturated it squelches underfoot, pierced by the trunks of fast, pale trees that have shot up in any crack that would hold them, their canopies closing overhead into a single, continuous thatch about ten meters below the rim.

That thatch is why the light is dim. Daylight reaches the pit, but it has to filter through that living ceiling. What arrives at the floor is green-filtered and diffuse, the light of a deep forest floor or a cathedral with stained glass - bright shafts where a gap opens around a pipe or a chimney, then long, cool penumbral gloom. The electric light takes over without ever fully replacing the day. Bare bulbs and long fluorescent tubes burn always, even at noon, hung under walkways, screwed to walls, strung on wires through the leaves, humming amber and white. They catch the beads of water on spider webs - there are no spiders - on wires and filaments of aerial root, and make everything glitter.

It is loud. That is the strangest thing for how small the place is. You can see from one wall to the other in a single glance, a space no larger than a village square folded up vertically on terraces - and yet it has the roar of a factory district.

The sound never stops because the machines never stop. Deep pumps thud and chug to keep the floor from becoming a pond, lifting seep-water up the walls in black iron pipes that shudder and sweat. Ventilation fans as big as doors turn slowly overhead, trying to move the heavy air, their blades slapping. Presses and lathes and textile looms work somewhere in the green warren - you rarely see them directly, only hear their stutter and whine through the leaves, and see the tremor of a catwalk as something heavy stamps below. The foliage itself makes noise: the ceaseless drip, and the rustle of thousands of leaves in the fan-wind. It adds up to a dense, enveloping industrial hush that is not quiet at all - you have to raise your voice to be heard three feet away, but after a minute your ear accepts it as the equivalent of silence.

What you do not hear is anything alive beyond plant and man.

There are no birds in the canopy. No insects ticking against the bulbs. No rats in the alleys. No echo of pigeons on the rim. The absence is not remarked upon by the people who live here - they are used to it - but for a visitor it is uncanny. The town is totally, intensely alive with green, with humid growth that should be riotous with fauna, and it is empty of them. Leaves are perfect, unchewed. The air is clean of flies. Food is grown, pollination is done by hand or by wind in glass houses tucked into the walls. The ecosystem is pure plant and human and machine. If a bird strays over the edge, it does not linger in the dim, loud, wet heat.

Everything here changes slowly.

Rust advances by millimeters a year on the pipes and is painted over. Vines advance by centimeters a day and are pruned back. The limestone walls, cut squared and blasted, have had time to settle. Their edges are no longer sharp. The corners have been rounded by a century of seeping water, by frost no longer reaching here, by roots prying. The terraces - because the floor is not flat, but stepped in three broad industrial tiers following the old quarry cuts, each holding its own row of buildings and its own flooded gutter - have found their balance. Nothing collapses. Nothing erupts. Growth and decay have reached an agreement. A beam will sag and be jacked up. A roof will leak and be patched with sheet metal and then be covered again in moss within a month. The people have learned the pace of the pit. You work steadily, you keep the pumps running, you cut back the creeper where it gums the fans, you do not fight the water or the green, you live inside them.

From the rim at dusk the place looks like a single organism breathing. The warm air lifts in a visible sigh, the lights below glow amber through the leaves like embers in a basket, the fans turn, the water runs, the walls weep, and the whole small, packed, sunken city just continues, saturated and settled and humming to itself in its hole in the ground.
