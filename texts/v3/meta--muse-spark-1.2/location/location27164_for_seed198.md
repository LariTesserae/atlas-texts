It is not a valley that was built in. It is a valley that was built.

High up where the air goes thin and cool, a broad mountain bowl opens between two long shoulders of rock — not high enough for snow to hold, but high enough that clouds snag and have to drag themselves over. They do it all day. The light is the light that makes it through them: flat, dim, pearlescent grey, without shadows. Even at noon it feels like early morning just before the sun should have risen. You can look directly at where the sun ought to be and see only a brighter smear in the cloud.

There is no ground here anymore. Not in the way ground means dirt and stone that was there before anyone came.

The whole bowl has been terraced. Not cut — made. Retaining walls twenty, thirty feet high, poured concrete and stacked basalt block and riveted iron plate from different generations of building, all weathered to the same dark, wet color. They step down from the rim to the flat of the old lakebed in broad, irregular stairs, each terrace fifty to a hundred yards deep, each one absolutely occupied. And on every terrace, another level has been built on top: sheds and mills and tenements and glasshouses and tanks and gantries, three, four, five stories high, leaning into each other, bridging the alleys with pipes and walkways and gutters, until there is no bare face left. Even the vertical walls are not bare. They weep.

Water is everywhere but never in one place. It doesn't have a river. It has a thousand small decisions. The atmosphere condenses on every cold surface — on the iron, on the glass, on the leaves — and beads and runs. Springs that were capped a century ago still push up through joints in the concrete and are led away in open stone runnels. The retaining walls sweat continuously; dark stains spread down them, green-black with algae and mineral. On the flat roofs, tar-paper sags under shallow, permanent pools. In the terraces themselves, the growing beds are always damp, the air always just past rain. Your boots are never quite dry. The stones are never quite dry. The handrails leave a cold film on your palm.

And it is green. Impossibly, chokingly green for something so completely made.

That was the purpose of the making. This is not a factory that happens to have weeds. This is a factory for growing. Every terrace is a level of cultivation, and every building is part of it. The lower floors are dark and warm from machinery — pulping sheds, fiber mills, oil presses, drying lofts where warm air is pushed through slatted floors by electric blowers. The upper floors and the roofs are glass and plastic, long production houses where vines and cane and broad-leafed understory crops grow in shallow hydroponic troughs stacked ten high on iron racks. And between — in every crack that was not designed for growing — the wild has taken the invitation. Moss thick as carpet over concrete. Ferns thrusting from rust-split I-beams. Morning glory and kudzu-type runners thrown over power lines and left to run because it's easier to harvest them there than to fight them. Even the walls that weep have been colonized, hanging gardens of liverwort and small white flowers that root in the lime. There is no empty surface to see whether it is natural or artificial anymore. The built and the lush have packed into each other until you cannot put a hand between them. If you pull a vine you find wire behind it. If you open a panel you find roots.

It is established. Nothing here is new or raw. The concrete is pitted by decades of acid water and softened at the edges. Iron has gone to a stable, deep rust that no longer flakes, just stains everything below it orange. Wood is black and swollen. The whole system has settled. Terraces have slumped a few inches here, bowed there, and been shored up with a buttress that itself now has ferns growing from its top. It doesn't look ruined. It looks balanced, like an old boulder field — everything has found the angle it will hold for a long time, while still slowly moving.

The scale is difficult to hold in your head until you walk it. From the rim it looks like a single town, but once you are down in it you realize each terrace is a neighborhood, each block of glasshouses a field you could spend a day harvesting. The bowl is perhaps three miles across. You can see the other side through the mist, lights on in the workshops even during the day because the dim never lifts, and you can hear — if you listen — the faint, dispersed activity of thousands of other people doing the same work you are.

It is not loud. That is the strange thing, for an industrial place.

There are engines — big, slow electric motors turning augers and conveyors, waterwheels still turning under the runnels because the drop from one terrace to the next is free power, transformers humming behind caged doors at 50 hertz. But everything is muffled by water and leaves and fog. Movement is constant and turbulent, but it is a quiet turbulence. An anthill turbulence. No sirens, no stamping presses; just a perpetual shunting and shifting. A cart rattles past on narrow rails. A crew drags a dripping tarp full of cut stalks to a chute. Someone overhead on a gantry is replacing panes, tapping glass. Water ticks from level to level, from leaf to pipe to trough. Vines visibly grow — you can almost watch a runner find a new hold over the course of an afternoon. The whole bowl rustles and drips and hums, and because it is so densely packed, the sound doesn't carry. It stays local, close, like the sound inside a greenhouse.

There are almost no animals. Too occupied, too managed, too cool and damp for much to hide. A few pigeons in the rafters, rats along the warm pipes, small pale moths that live their whole lives inside the glasshouses and die on the glass. You see their bodies in the gutters. Mostly it's people and plants and machines, the plants filling the niche that animals would take.

The rule here is simple and unspoken and enforced by the place itself rather than by anyone in charge: everything that can be used must be used. No surface left fallow, no drop of water left to run without turning something or watering something first. Space is saturated. If you stop tending a corner for a week, something — vine, rust, pool — will occupy it. If you need a new walkway you build it over the old growing bed and put a new growing bed on top of the walkway. Up or down, never out, because there is no out. The rim is the limit. Beyond it is just cold wind and thin grass and empty cloud. Inside, everything is made and everything is alive and everything is in motion, not toward any completion but just — maintenance. Harvest and replant and repair and redirect the water that never stops weeping from the rocks that are no longer visible.

If you stand still long enough on one of the mid-level terraces, with the cool damp air in your lungs and the dim light making colors soft, you can feel the place breathing around you — water condensing, leaves uncurling, motors cycling on and off, people passing with loads on their shoulders, always passing, never stopping, never loud enough to break the hushing veil of the fog that holds it all together.
