It's low country.

Not low in the way a valley is low, with hills to frame it — low like a plate left out on a table. Flat enough that you can see weather coming from a long way off, even when the air is too thick to see far. The land is only a few meters above sea level and in places not even that; it remembers being sea floor. The ground is not ground the way inland ground is. It's fill and silt and concrete poured over silt, clay that never quite dries, aggregate pressed down and then pressed down again. Everything built here had to be driven deep on piles to stay put, and still it settles. Not catastrophic, just established — the long accommodation between weight and mud. Curbs have bellied outwards, their corners rounded soft as river stones. Asphalt has slumped into shallow troughs where the trucks used to run heaviest. Concrete has gone pitted and scabbed, showing the gravel inside it. Steel has scaled over in rust the color of dried blood, then rusted again under that. Even the brick has gone porous.

There are no plants. That is the first thing wrong, or would be wrong somewhere else. Here it just is. No weeds forcing through cracks, no trees along the avenues, no grass in the medians. The heat and the water and the chemistry conspired against it a long time ago. The soil is too saline, too slicked with oil and mineral, too hot. What grows is not green — a dark bacterial film on standing water, a pale tartar of mineral on pipes, a rust-colored slick where water weeps continuously from a retaining wall. Otherwise, the world is mineral. Stone, and what humans made from stone, and metal.

It is hot in a way that has weight. Not desert hot and clean, but low, wet heat that sits on your shoulders. The air is dim even at midday. A permanent low overcast the color of wet cement holds the sun somewhere behind it, and underneath that the city holds its own haze — steam and heat-shimmer and the thin smoke of low-grade fuel. Light comes flat and diffuse, without shadows. On the brightest days the sun is just a hotter spot in the gray. Everything sweats. Metal sweats. Concrete sweats. The air itself condenses on every underside and drips.

And it is wet. Not in the way of a river running through, but in the way of water that will not leave. The water table is just under the surface; you can see it in every excavation and trench, a dark mirror a foot down. Rain comes often — short, heavy, steaming showers that drum for twenty minutes and then stop, leaving everything drenched. Between rains, water finds its way out anyway. It weeps from the bases of walls, from the joints of the levees, from the limestone rip-rap along the canal. It condenses on the undersides of pipes and falls in a constant, scattered ticking. The canals that grid the town are full to their lips, brown and warm, moving sluggishly toward the sea. The streets have gutters that never go dry, and low lots between buildings have become shallow pans, a few inches of tea-colored water over silt and trash, reflecting the gray sky. The air smells of hot water on hot stone, of iron, of brine.

The town is there, and it is unmistakably a town, but it is not full. That is the second wrong thing that becomes normal if you stand in it long enough. This was built to be dense — blocks of warehouses and machine shops, long sheds with sawtooth roofs, water towers on spindly legs, cinderblock offices with faded painted signs, a whole grid of roads wide enough for trailers to turn around. Power lines on massive steel lattice towers march in straight lines out toward the horizon. Pipes run overhead in bundles, big enough to walk through, lagged and dripping. Factories with corrugated walls, now closed, now running at half, now closed again. It is urban in its bones. But it is hollow now. A third of the lots are empty, scraped to gravel and puddles, with only the ghosts of foundations showing as rectangles where weeds never came to cover them. A half of the buildings are empty, doors left ajar, windows dark. The distances between occupied places are vast. You can stand at an intersection and see four blocks in each direction with nothing moving, the street stretching out, heat wavering just above the asphalt. The scale is open — you can take in the whole district at once, the repeating forms going soft with haze before they end. It feels regional, not intimate; not a neighborhood but a whole industrial flatland.

The sound matches the emptiness. For an industrial town it should roar. It doesn't. Most of the engines are off, or idled down to a low idle you feel more than hear. Somewhere far off a pump house chugs, steady as a heartbeat, pushing water from one canal to another where it doesn't particularly want to go. A transformer hums behind a chainlink fence. The wind moves through the lattice of a water tower and makes a faint, mournful tone. Otherwise it is mostly quiet, and because it is quiet you hear the small sounds: water dripping from a pipe joint onto a puddle, the tick of cooling sheet metal, the slither of water in a gutter. Human sounds are rare and carry too far — a door closing half a mile away.

Because the quiet does not belong to emptiness. It belongs to the animals.

They are everywhere, once you adjust your eyes. This is their city now.

Without plants, they have made their economy out of each other and out of what humans left and out of the water. The shallow pans and canals are thick with life. Small silver fish and shrimp flick in the warm brown water in schools so dense they wrinkle the surface. Blue crabs the size of dinner plates pick their way across parking lots from one puddle to the next, their shells caked with silt, moving with sideways, deliberate clicks. Gulls and terns and herons stand along every rooftop and pipe-rack, so many that at first you take them for some kind of roofing material, white and gray against the rust. They are still for long stretches, watching the water. Then one drops, then another.

Rats move in lines along the bases of walls, not furtive but numerous, indifferent. Feral dogs in thin packs trot down the center of roads, tongues out in the heat, not barking. Pigeons pour from the broken windows of a warehouse in a sudden sheet, wheel once in the dim air, and settle again. On the concrete walls, cockroaches and isopods and beetles cover every shaded vertical by the thousands, a living texture that only moves if you stare. At night, and even in the dim day, the air is stippled with dragonflies and flies as big as bees, and with the swallows that feed on them. Snakes — water snakes, rat snakes — lie thick as cables in the warm culverts, and you see the bulges in their bodies where they have eaten something else.

Nothing is loud about it. There are no choruses, no howling. It is an overwhelming, *quiet* abundance, as if the sheer number of bodies has agreed to keep the noise down. The predation is constant and close and almost silent: a heron spearing a fish, a crab snapping a smaller crab, a gull taking a chick. The factories drip, the water moves an inch an hour toward the sea, the heat presses down, and the animals trade places in an endless, patient cycle.

The rules here are simple and non-negotiable. Water goes where it wants, which is everywhere, and you build around it or on stilts over it or you pump it forever. Heat sets the schedule; everything strenuous happens early or under cloud, midday is for stillness. No green will hold, so you do not wait for shade — you make it from sheet metal and concrete. The city will not be full again, not with people, because there is no reason for it to be, but it will not empty either, because the water and the warmth and the structures make a perfect low, wet, mineral reef, and life does not need permission or plants to fill a reef. It just needs water, and heat, and places to perch and hide.

If you stay still long enough on a street corner, the town stops being abandoned and starts being inhabited — just not by you. A crab crosses the road in front of you, unhurried. A heron watches you from a pipe with an eye the size of a marble. Somewhere behind the haze, rain starts to fall again, hissing as it hits hot metal roofs, and the gutters all begin to run, and the whole flat, rusted, dripping plain seems to exhale steam into the dim light.
