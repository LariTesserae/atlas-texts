It is high, and it is under water.

Not high in the way a mountain is high above water — high the way a table is high above a floor. A mesa standing alone in the abyss. If you could drain the ocean, you would see it as a vast plain of midnight drop-offs on every side, a pillar of rock rising two kilometers from the true bottom, its top flattened as if struck with a plane. But the ocean is not drained. It has never been drained here.

The water over the mesa is not shallow. It is a weight. Three hundred meters of warm, black water press down on the roofs, and that is what makes it dark. Sunlight does not reach here as light. At noon, far above, the surface glints white, but by the time its rumors filter down they are nothing. A deep indigo that is almost black, a darkness that is not absolute — your eyes adjust and find it is full of a kind of dusk that comes from nowhere and everywhere.

The warmth is the first unusual thing. Deep water should be cold. This water is warm as blood, as a held breath. 28, 30 degrees, bathwater that never cools. It comes up from the stone itself. The mesa leaks heat. In the darkness you feel it before you understand it: no chill on skin, no need to shiver, the water holding you like an amniotic close.

There is no plant here. Not a frond, not a blade, not a filament of green. That absence is total. In other seas you would see weeds and kelp forests, algae dusting every surface. Here there is only mineral and animal. The stone is bare, or rather, it is covered, but not with plant.

For everything here is built.

The top of the mesa is not rock. Or it is rock, but none of it is unshaped. Somewhere unimaginably long ago, hands squared it. The entire summit — perhaps four hundred meters across, a hamlet's worth of ground, a cathedral's worth if you count volume — is paved and walled and terraced and roofed. There is no shore, no beach, no natural edge where the made world gives way. The makers tiled the mesa lip to lip with flat basalt slabs, and then built up.

It was a city for air-breathers. You can tell. Stairs that were meant to be climbed with feet and lungs. Doorways. Courtyards open to a sky that no longer exists. Low houses of fitted stone without mortar, domes, and narrow alleys that turn at right angles. A plaza with a raised dais. A cistern — though now the whole city is a cistern. Everywhere the chisel marks of primitive tools: the scalloped bite of stone hammers, the long scrape where a bronze edge slipped. No glass, no steel, no joint more complex than a peg or a lashing, now long rotted away. Only stone on stone, and the occasional green-black nodule of corroded copper where a door once hung.

And then water came, or the land sank, or both — so slowly that the erosion has had time to finish its work. This place is ancient in the way a river pebble is ancient. All the sharpness has been taken off. The corners of every block have been rounded to a soft radius. The stairs are dished in the center from feet and then from currents, then from feet again that were not feet. The carvings on the plaza walls — figures with raised arms — have been worn until they are only suggestion, foreheads and bellies smoothed down to the same plane. The stone itself has a polish to it, a deep-sea patina, dark grey and pitted with the dissolving kiss of warm water over centuries beyond counting. Nothing is collapsing. It has settled into its final form. The city will not fall further; it has learned how to be underwater.

In this small, bounded world, life is dense.

There is no green to feed it, but it does not need green. The water itself feeds it. The warm stone sweats minerals, and on every surface grows a skin — not plant, not fungus as you know it, but a bacterial felt, pale as bone, cream and sulfur-yellow, velvet-thick on the undersides of lintels and in the gutters between paving stones. It is the meadow. And the fauna graze it.

They are everywhere, and they are quiet.

Pale blind fish the length of your hand move in slow shoals through the alleys, not swimming so much as drifting in formation, fin-flicks barely disturbing the water. They do not dart; the water is too warm, too heavy, and they have no predators that chase. White shrimp, translucent as glass, pick over the bacterial felt in crowds so dense their legs make the stone seem to crawl. Isopods the size of loaves huddle under eaves, antennae waving with infinite patience. Simple things with shells spiral on the walls, laying down calcium in water that wants to dissolve it again. Clouds of tiny blind things — copepods, larvae — hang in the water column like dust in still air, so thick in places the darkness appears to glitter.

And something larger, too. Eels or salamanders, you cannot tell, as long as your arm, the color of the stone, moving with a slow undulation from house to house. They go from doorway to doorway, nosing inside. They live inside the houses now. You can see their tails disappear where families once slept. They are not afraid. Nothing here moves fast enough to be afraid.

Because nothing is fast here. The dynamic of this place is patience. The water moves, but only as a breath moves — a creeping of warm upwellings that stirs the dust-clouds an inch per hour. The fish turn together over the course of minutes. The shrimp graze a single slab for a whole day before advancing to the next. The stone itself still erodes, grain by grain, and the bacterial mat grows a millimeter per season. Sound, what little there is, travels slow and soft in the warm thickness.

It is quiet the way deep water is quiet, which is not absolute silence but the removal of almost all sound. No surf, no wind, no birds. What you hear when you hold still is the faintest suite of small noises magnified by the stillness: the click of a shrimp's leg on stone, the sigh of water exhaling through a doorway, the occasional soft *tok* as a shell falls from a wall to the pavement below and lands without a splash. If you stay long enough you hear your own blood, and you realize it sounds like the place.

The overall rule of this world is simple: weight without pressure, enclosure without air. You are held. You cannot fall, only sink slowly. You cannot run, only push gently against the warm dark and be moved a little less gently back. The built world holds its shape against the water, and the water holds everything else.

At the edge of the city, the pavement ends. Not in ruin, but in intention. A low parapet, knee-high, runs the entire perimeter of the mesa, still perfect after eons, its top rounded as a loaf. Beyond it, there is nothing but water going straight down into a black that is not water but absence. The fauna do not cross it. The shrimp turn back. The fish shoal along it as if it were a shore. It is the boundary of the small world, and it is as definite as a wall in a room.

If you stand at that parapet — and you can stand, the water lets you stand, though your weight is almost nothing — and look out, you see the darkness pressing on the hamlet of stone, the warm water diffusing the faint lights of the animals themselves. The shrimp carry a dim chemiluminescence on their backs, not bright enough to be called light, more a rumor of edges. The roofs below hold pools of this faint glow in their courtyards. The whole submerged hilltop hamlet glows faintly, like a memory of a city seen through rain, high up in the night sea, alone, finished, and inhabited to bursting by things that never knew it was built to be dry.

It does not need to be remembered to continue.
