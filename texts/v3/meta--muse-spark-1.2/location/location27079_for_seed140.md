On the top of the world, there is a bowl.

Not a peak. That is the first thing you have to unlearn. You climb for a long time to get here - up through thinning air and switchback stone - expecting a summit, a point, and instead the mountain ends in a flatness. A table. A palm held open to the night.

It is high. High enough that your lungs work a little harder and your ears pop as you crest the last lip, high enough that by all rights the air should be cold and sharp. It isn't. It's warm. A low, mammalian warmth that hangs in the dark without a source, as if the rock itself is breathing out the heat it saved from some older sun.

The darkness here is not the darkness of night. Night still has stars, a moon, a faint gradient. This is a thicker thing. The sky above the bowl is low and completely closed - not clouds, exactly, but a condensing atmosphere that never breaks. A warm, black vault that drinks all light. You can open your eyes wide until they ache and see nothing. Not blackness as a color, but the absence that was there before eyes evolved.

And yet the bowl is wet. And it is alive beyond reason.

### The Stone

The rock tells you how old this place is. This highland has not been thrust up recently. It has been up here forever. Erosion has had its entire lifetime to work on it, patient as prayer.

There are no sharp edges left. The basalt and sandstone rimming the bowl are rounded down to soft shoulders, slopes that look poured rather than broken. The boulders half-sunk into the peat are smooth as river stones the size of houses. You put your hand on one and your palm finds no corner, no fracture - just a continuous, cool curve worn by ten million years of rain that came and went. The ground underfoot is not soil, but the final product of soil: a deep, black, greasy humus, inches thick with decomposition, so aged it has lost all grit. When you step, it gives silently and springs back minutes later.

Water moves here, but not in rivers. There are no channels left to cut. Instead the stone weeps. The entire rim sweats. Warm condensation beads on every surface, gathers, and runs down in dark films and curtains. It drips from the lips of rocks with a slow, metronomic patience. The center of the bowl is a shallow, peaty marsh - not open water, but ground that is more water than ground. If you kneel, water wells up around your fingers without you having to dig. The air itself is near-precipitation. Each breath is damp. Each exhale disappears into the same warm wet that inhaled you.

The scale of this is what disorients you. The bowl is small. You could walk its diameter in five minutes if you could see. Perhaps three hundred meters across, a hamlet-sized world with a clear edge all around. A grove. A room. And yet inside that smallness, the emptiness is immense.

### The Hollow Grove

Density is the wrong word for this place. It is not dense. It is hollow.

The vegetation is lush to the point of excess - walls of growth on every side - but it grows spaced out, as if each plant was given a reverent distance. Great broad-leafed things with black, rubbery leaves the size of doors rise on trunks like pillar roots. Ferns larger than you uncurl slowly from the peat. Vines as thick as your wrist hang motionless in the dark, beaded with water that never falls until it is too heavy. Fungi - shelves and brackets and fingers - bloom from every stone, pale in a way you can feel more than see.

None of it is green. Green is a color that needs light. These leaves are black-purple, black-red, a deep bruised color that would look like nothing in daylight, built not for photosynthesis but for drinking heat and water straight from the air. They are warm to the touch. They feel like skin. The canopy, if there is one, is not overhead but all around you at chest height and above, a cathedral with no roof, vast space between trunks where the warm, empty air hangs still. You can stand between two plants and feel you are alone in a hall, even though life is crowding the edges of your perception.

It should be silent in a place this empty. It is not.

### The Chorus

It is loud. Not with wind - there is no wind in the bowl. The rim shelters it completely. It is loud with animals.

You cannot see them, but they are everywhere. Abundant. The air thrums with them. A layered, continuous chorus that seems to come from the peat itself.

Low down, a hundred unseen throats pulse: amphibians with sacs full of warm air, calling in slow, overlapping booms that you feel in your sternum more than hear. *Whump...... whump...... whump......* Each call takes a long time to swell and fade. Higher, insects - or things like insects - stitch a searing, metallic drone that never stops, only shifts in pitch as one group hands off to another. Something with wings the size of your hands moves through the hollow spaces between trunks with a soft, leathery *whuff*, displacing the damp air. Things rustle in the peat, things slip into the weeping pools with a quiet *plok*.

You realize after a few minutes that nothing is moving quickly. That is the rule here. The dynamic is slow. A leaf falls over the course of an hour, turning as it drops. The water beads swell and hang for minutes before falling. Even the animals call slowly, as if time has been stretched. The warmth and the darkness have made everything patient. There is no urgency. Growth happens, death happens, but on a calendar where a season is a long, slow breath.

And threaded through all of it is another sound.

### The Scattered Work

There are structures here. Not many. Scattered.

At first you think they are stones, because they have been here long enough to acquire the same water-sheened smoothness as the boulders. But your hand finds right angles where no right angle should exist. A low wall, waist high, perfectly level, made of something that is not stone - too light, faintly warm, with a grain like frozen smoke. A pylon leaning at fifteen degrees, half-swallowed by a fig, humming so low you feel it in your molars before your ears. A circle of pillars no taller than a child, set precisely around the marsh center, each one weeping water that runs *uphill* along its side before atomizing.

This is not human work. Not old human, not new human. The engineering is wrong. The materials hold themselves together without joints or mortar, as if they were grown in place. One ruined arch near the eastern rim still holds a faint field inside it - you pass your hand through and the air there is cooler, and for a half-second your fingers feel stretched, as if the space inside is larger than the arch should contain. The field makes a sound, too: a clear, patient tone like a struck bowl, that rings for minutes after you disturb it and slowly fades back into the amphibian chorus.

Whoever built them did not build a city. They placed a few things, like instruments, and left. Or perhaps these are the last teeth of something larger that erosion has already taken, ground down to these few stubborn remnants that physics cannot quite erase. Lichen - or something using the same niche - grows over them, but cannot quite grip them. The water beads differently on their surfaces.

Weirdness here is not spectacular. It is quiet and near-normal, until you notice the air above the marsh is a degree warmer than it should be with no source, or that the dripping water from the rim falls slightly slower than gravity would dictate, or that the calls of the animals occasionally harmonize with the hum of the pylons without either changing to meet the other, as if both are tuned to the same absent note.

You stand in the warm, wet dark, in a flat place on top of a worn-down world, in a grove the size of a house that feels as large as a country because of how empty it is, and you listen. The water keeps weeping from stone. The great black leaves keep slowly drinking the dark. The animals keep calling, loud and slow, filling the hollow with life you can't see. And the old, inexplicable machines keep their vigil, holding this small, ancient, impossible garden in place against the night that never ends.

You could stay here a long time. The place would not mind. It changes so slowly it would not notice you leaving, or notice you staying.
