There is no ground here. Not anymore.

What was once earth has been replaced so completely and for so long that the idea of "ground" has become an abstraction. This is a low country, a basin so broad and so shallow you only know you're in it because the horizon never quite resolves — it just blurs into wet air a few miles out in every direction. The elevation never changes more than a few feet. This is not flatness from youth, from a floodplain newly laid down. This is the flatness of age. Of everything tall having been worn down, everything sharp having been rubbed smooth, everything loose having been washed away until only the most stubborn, settled form remains.

You are standing on The Floor.

The Floor is everything. Poured concrete, iron plate, brick, aggregate, ceramic tile, asphalt patch, steel grating - all of it laid, relaid, patched, poured over, bolted down, and then left to weather for a time span that feels geological. You can see the layers at the rare places where it has cracked. A cross-section like tree rings: black tar over grey concrete over red brick over riveted iron over something pale and pitted that might have been the first pour, a thousand years ago. The surface itself is no longer hard-edged. Millennia of water and wind and vibration have eroded the built world into something almost natural. Corners are round as river stones. Seams have widened into shallow gullies. Bolt heads are smooth domes you can barely feel under your boot. The whole plain dips and swells in broad, exhausted undulations - the sag of a continent-sized structure settling into its final shape.

It is cool here. Always cool. The air holds a damp chill that gets into the seams of your clothes and never leaves. Your breath makes a faint ghost even in what passes for summer. And it is wet. Not flooded, not drowned - wet in the way an old basement is wet, or a cave mouth. Water has no deep earth to sink into, nowhere to go. It lives on the surface.

It weeps up from below. Through every expansion joint, every rusted grate, every hairline crack in the Floor, water pushes up under faint pressure - clear, cold, mineral-tasting. It sheets. It pools in the shallow, palm-deep depressions that pock the plain for miles, black mirrors that ripple without wind. It runs in slow, braided channels that follow the ancient sag of the Floor, not because anyone designed them to, but because water has carved its own meandering rivers into solid concrete over centuries. In the darker hours - which is most hours - you hear it dripping. From overhead.

Because there is an overhead.

Not a sky. A Roof would be too simple a word. A superstructure, a lattice, a second Floor above the first. Far above - high enough that you lose it in the dark - is a tangle of pipes and catwalks and trusses and gantries and the vast, vaulted underside of whatever continent-machine this is. You can see its shape only where the work-lights catch it: the belly of ducts big enough to walk through, the slow turning of ventilator fans the size of houses, the occasional flare of a furnace or a forge far off. Most of it is lost in darkness and steam. The air never really clears. It is a hanging, condensing atmosphere. The heat of industry below meets the cool air of this low basin and the entire volume sweats. Droplets form on every girder, on every cable, on the underside of every plate, grow heavy, and fall as a perpetual, cold rain. Not weather. Condensation. It patters on the Floor, on the pools, on the metal, on your shoulders. In places it falls heavy enough to be called rain. In others it is only a mist that beads on every surface and makes the iron shine black.

And it is deafening.

At first you think it is the rain. Then you realize the rain is the quietest part. The sound is the Floor itself, working. This is still an industrial place, not a ruin. It has not stopped. Pumps throb underfoot, a deep, chest-rattling *thud-thud-thud* that you feel through your soles. Somewhere beyond sight, stamping presses fall with a percussive *BANG* that rolls across the plain like thunder and never quite fades before the next one comes. Water is being moved - always - through culverts and sluices with a roar of white noise. Steam vents scream. Conveyors rattle on sagging rollers, carrying nothing or carrying slag, it hardly matters. Chains clank. The Roof creaks as it expands and contracts. There is no single source, no direction to turn away from. The sound is the air. You have to lean close and shout to be heard, and even then your words are taken and flattened by the wet, humming dark.

Light is scarce and low and artificial. There is no sun here, or if there is, its light never penetrates the Roof and the vapor. What illumination exists is made. Sparse pools of it - a sodium lamp buzzing orange over a pump house a half-mile away, a string of bare white bulbs marking a walkway that leads nowhere, the dull red glow of a smelter vent, the occasional cold blue-white flicker of an arc welder left running on automatic. Between those islands, long stretches of near-dark, lit only by the faint reflection of water on water. Shadows are deep and soft-edged in the mist. Distance becomes guesswork. You can see maybe three or four structures at a time before darkness and spray swallow the rest.

The structures themselves are sparse. That is the strangest thing about a place that is totally built - how empty it feels. The density is low. For miles, there may be only the Floor, the pools, the rain, and the sound. Then, a thing: a pump house like a small chapel, its brick walls bloated and rounded by erosion, its windows long since gone, its interior still churning. A cooling tower, squat and immensely wide, its concrete flaking into lace, doing nothing but breathing steam. A field of iron pillars, each as thick as an old tree, holding up nothing you can see anymore. A row of tanks, lids rusted open, filled with dark water. All of them far apart, as if the builders understood they needed to leave room for the age to come. There is so much space between presences that the emptiness feels intentional. Balanced.

Nothing lives here that you would call an animal. No birds cross the dark air. No insects skim the pools. Even the water, for all its abundance, holds nothing that swims. The only life is what can root in a crack without soil. And even that is sparse.

Lichen - grey-green and black, crusting the leeward sides of concrete swells like frost. Mats of moss or something like moss, dark and sodden, thriving in the eternal drip-line under pipes, spongy underfoot. In the shallowest pools, a thin, rust-colored algal skin that breaks when the rain hits it and reforms. A single, stunted thing that might once have been a reed, growing alone from a seam where dust has collected for a century, its few leaves trembling in the ventilator wind. Vegetation exists only as an afterthought, a stain on the machine. It does not soften the place. It emphasizes how long the machine has been running without tending.

The weirdness is not dramatic. It is in the rules.

Water does not behave quite right. It wells *up* and flows *outward* toward the shallow rims of the depressions, as if the Floor is very slightly breathing, exhaling the water table through its pores. In some places, the condensation falls upward - droplets torn from the Floor by a draft and carried up to join the mist on the Roof, a reversed rain. The erosion has exposed things that should not be exposed: rebar that has grown together like roots, ceramic insulators fused into stalactites, layers of paint hundreds thick peeling away in sheets that hang like shale.

And the machine does not stop. No one is here to run it - or if there ever was, they are gone - but the processes continue, active, cycling. Pumps pump water that leaks and is pumped again. Forges heat and cool with no ingot inside them. Conveyors carry their emptiness in loops. The whole low, dark, wet, ancient, made-world is in motion, an ecosystem without biology. Water evaporates from the warm pools, condenses on the cold Roof, rains down, pools, is pumped, leaks, evaporates again. Rust blooms and flakes and is washed into cracks where it settles like sediment. Moss grows a millimeter a year on a surface that was smoothed over centuries.

You understand, standing on the humming Floor with cold water seeping around your boots and the deafening air pressing on your ears, that this place has reached its oldest stable state. It will not collapse. It will not be reclaimed. It has been industrial for so long that industry has become geology. It has been built for so long that the built has become natural. It is not waiting for anything. It is simply being, wet and dark and loud and flat and empty, endlessly and actively persisting in its cool, dripping dusk.
