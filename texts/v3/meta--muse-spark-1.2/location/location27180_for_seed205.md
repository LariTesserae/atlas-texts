This is not a forest.

A forest implies ground, and then trees on it. Here there is no ground in the way you remember it. There is only plant, and then more plant, comprehending the world.

You are high up. Very high up - not a peak, a *plateau*, a continent lifted. If you could peel the green away you would find basalt and sandstone a mile beneath you, old, red, weathered into mesa and butte and deep, soft bowl-valleys that have not changed shape in a hundred thousand years. But you cannot peel it away. That stone has been settled, established, made peace with wind and heat a long time ago, and then it was buried.

What grows over it is not covering it. It *is* the terrain now.

### The Architecture

At first you think you are looking at trees. The scale is wrong for that.

From where you stand — on a limb that is as wide as a highway, its bark furrowed into canyons deep enough to lose a house in — you see others. One. Then another, kilometers away through the hot, hazy air. Then another, and another. They are not crowded. That is the disorientation. For something that is so totally, absolutely vegetative, there is so much *emptiness* between presences. A tropical jungle would pack a thousand trunks into a hectare. Here there are perhaps three individuals in a whole valley.

Because an individual is not a tree. An individual is a place.

The mature ones are called, by the people who live in them, Holdfasts. Each grows a single trunk two hundred meters across at the base, flaring into buttresses that become ridges and ravines of living wood. The trunk rises a kilometer, maybe more, and then explodes. Not branches - architectures. Boughs thick as freight trains cantilever out for half a mile, then fork, then fork again, then flatten into photosynthetic plates the size of fields, overlapping, tilting to the sun. Their undersides are pale and fleshy, veined like skin. Their tops are dark waxen green, covered in a dust of hot pollen that yellows your hands.

These plates do not form a continuous canopy. They form an archipelago of roofs in the sky. Between any two Holdfasts there is a gulf of air, bright and hot and full of wind. You can see straight through to the far horizon where the same forms repeat until they blue out with distance. The whole continent is like this. It has weather of its own. Cloud streets form in the corridors between the Holdfasts and dissipate again. Thunderheads snag on the heat rising off a single crown and are forced to rain.

There is no soil to speak of. What soil there was is now held in the trees themselves - in the crotches where centuries of fallen leaf-plates have composted into hanging meadows, in the grooves of the bark where black, peaty earth has collected, in woven nests of aerial roots as thick as your body that hang down hundreds of meters and never quite touch the buried stone below.

You walk on living matter. Everything you touch is living or was living a week ago.

### Heat and Water and Light

The light is ordinary. That is almost the strangest thing. No perpetual twilight, no alien magenta sun. A yellow-white sun, high and honest at midday, rendering greens green, browns brown. But it is filtered, dappled, then suddenly undappled as you step from the shadow of a leaf the size of a house into a gap where the sun hits you with full, high-altitude intensity.

And it is hot. Dry, high, settling heat, the kind that comes from being close to the sun and far from the sea. 38C in the shade, hotter on the bark. The air shivers over the leaf-plates. Thermals rip upward between the Holdfasts with enough force to keep birds and much larger things aloft without a single wingbeat. You sweat constantly. The sweat does not pool. It evaporates, and then it returns.

Because there is no ocean here. There are no lakes. Water exists at 1.1 - just enough to be a verb, not a feature.

Each morning before the heat crests, the entire continent exhales. The Holdfasts transpire a volume of water that would be a sea if it ever collected. It comes out as condensation. Cold air drains off the high leaf-plates at night and kisses the hot bark, and by dawn every vertical surface is streaming. Beads gather, join, run in temporary rivers down the furrows of the trunk, pool in the elbow-joints of boughs where hanging meadows act as sponges, then vanish by 10am as sun steam-rips it back into the sky.

Occasionally a Holdfast sweats too hard and births a cloud that does not evaporate. It breaks as a brief, violent, local rain — ten minutes of fat warm drops hammering a single square kilometer and nowhere else, cutting gullies in the living soil, filling bark-canyons that become nurseries for tadpole-things for a day before they dry to cracked mud again.

In the lowest places, where the buried stone dips, there are true rivers — not flowing *over* the land, but *through* it. A buttress root will split and rejoin and in its hollow runs a slow, tea-brown river, roofed in wood, lit by slit windows. Things drink there, in line.

### The Sound

You hear it before you see anything else.

It is not a jungle hum. It is a physical pressure.

The fauna here are not inhabitants of the plant. They are an equal mass. For every ton of Holdfast, there is a ton of things that eat it, bore it, fly from it, mimic it. And almost all of them communicate by stridulation, by drum, by air-sac.

The base note is the Holdfasts themselves: their leaves shiver in the thermal gaps and buzz like vast sheets of paper. Then the insects - not swarms, but individuals the size of dogs, the size of doors, spaced sparse but each monstrously loud. A single amber-backed chord beetle on the bough opposite you draws its serrated hind legs across its abdomen and the whole valley rings like a struck gong for five seconds. Billions of glasswings, each nearly invisible, beat in phase and make a wind-chime rain that never stops. In the hanging meadows, throats inflate.

And the larger things: gliders with fifty-foot membranes that stretch from forelimb to tail, that ride the thermals between Holdfasts, screaming — not call-and-response but a constant, overlapping Doppler chorus as they circle, hunt, evade each other. You feel sound in your sternum. If you shout, you cannot hear yourself. The people who live here go partially deaf by thirty.

Yet it is active, not chaotic. This is not frenzy. This is a settled, old, predator-and-prey economy that has been balanced for eons. You can watch a camouflaged slag-mantis, bark-textured and fifteen feet long, remain perfectly still for an hour on a vertical trunk as beetles crawl over it, until a glider dips too low and it takes it out of the air with a foreleg faster than you can blink.

Nothing is in plague. Nothing is collapsing. It has eroded into equilibrium.

### The Marks

There are people. Not many. The density of anything intelligent here is low, as it is for everything larger than a beetle. You have to look for them.

On a horizontal bough where the bark has weathered into a slope, you find a village. Not built - *carved* and *tied*. Hollows are scooped into the living wood — the Holdfast heals around them in a raised lip of resin, sealing the wound. Over the hollow, a roof of cured leaf-plate, stitched with vine. Rope bridges of braided aerial root stretch across the terrifying gap to the next bough, swaying hundreds of meters above nothing. Smoke from a cook fire is instantly shredded by wind.

Their tech is hands and fire and stone. Polished chert knives, leaf-fiber cordage, fire-hardened spears for prying grubs out of bark, baskets woven from pulped fiber so tight they can carry water. They do not fell the Holdfast. To cut a living bough would be to cut the continent. Instead they prune, they tap. They collect the morning condensation in leaf-cups. They harvest the stray rain pools. They farm the hanging meadows, planting parasitic orchids that swell into tubers.

You see their marks elsewhere too, if you know what to look for: a series of handholds chipped into the bark-canyon leading down to the river-in-wood, worn smooth by generations. A petroglyph of a glider, pecked into lignin, already half-healed over. A solitary watch-tower of lashed root-pole on the very edge of a leaf-plate, where a boy with a bone horn watches for storms.

They are scattered, and they intend to be. No road could exist here. To be dense would be to be heard by what hunts in the gaps.

### The Rule

The weirdness does not announce itself. It accumulates.

First: the Holdfasts are not rooted where they began. If you watch long enough — and the old people do — you see that over years, the great trunks shift. Not grow, *migrate*. Aerial roots on the leading edge thicken into new pseudo-trunks, the trailing buttresses die and slough away. A Holdfast can walk, very slowly, a few meters a year, following the sun and the condensation gradients, leaving behind a deep scar of enriched, crumbling bark-soil where its old footprint was. The map of the continent is not fixed. Valleys between Holdfasts widen, close.

Second: the inside of a Holdfast is hollow in places, and it breathes. At noon in the heat, fissures in the bark exhale a hot, sweet, spore-laden wind that smells like cinnamon and rot. At night, they inhale, a slow sigh you can feel if you press your palm to the wood. If you stand too close, your ears pop. The hanging rivers sometimes run *uphill* for minutes when a great inhalation pulls.

No one thinks this is magic. It is just how the world works when plant is substrate, when plant is world, when the plant has been alone and dominant so long it has learned to make its own weather, its own geography, its own lungs.

You sit on the highway-wide limb as the afternoon firestorm of sound reaches its peak, the light going gold and horizontal through the gaps, the heat finally starting to break, the Holdfast beneath you faintly expanding with its daily breath. Far off, across a mile of empty, humming air, another Holdfast blurs in the heat, and another, and another, until the vast repetition becomes indistinguishable from a mountain range — which, in a way, it is.

Somewhere below, deep under bark and meadow and moving root, the old stone waits, patient, eroded, and entirely unnecessary.
