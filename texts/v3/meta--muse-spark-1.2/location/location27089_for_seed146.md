It is not a cave.

A cave implies something carved out of a world that has an outside. This place never had an outside. Or if it did, it was sealed over so long ago that the memory of sky is gone.

You descend to get here, but not far. A fissure, a throat in the upper rock not much wider than your shoulders, that you have to turn sideways to enter. The stone around it is warm to the touch, not from any sun but from the slow, held heat of the mass itself. Twenty meters down the walls stop being *walls* and become *ceiling*.

And then it opens.

The first thing you understand is the scale is wrong. Your lamp — whatever dim amber you brought, a coal in a clay cup, a bundle of slow-burning fungus-fiber that gives off more smoke than light — can throw perhaps ten meters before the dark drinks it. But the dark beyond your light is not small. The air doesn't echo. It absorbs. You can feel the volume pressing out on all sides, a continental hollow, a planetary blister. The ceiling is so high you never see it. Sometimes a faint, sourceless heat-shimmer makes you think you see it, a low vault of basalt a kilometer above, but it might be nothing at all. The floor does not curve. It goes on until sight fails.

This is a young place, though nothing here seems to age.

The floor is not sand, not dust. It is fresh. The basalt and salt and glass are still sharp where they broke. You walk on a plain of hexagonal plates, each one a meter or two across, fitted together like a tortoise shell that cracked as it cooled. The cracks are clean, knife-edged, a few centimeters wide. There is no wind to round them. No water to smooth them. The heat comes up through those cracks in a steady, breathless exhalation, dry and mineral and faintly metallic, 38 degrees, 40 degrees, always. The air itself is thin but heavy, still, saturated with fine salt that hangs without falling. When you breathe, your throat is dry instantly. Your lips crack within an hour and then stop cracking, as if your body has reached equilibrium with the aridity.

There is no water here. Not in pools, not in seeps, not in the air. The salt field stretches to every horizon, sometimes white, sometimes a deep rust-red where iron has bloomed, sometimes black glass where the rock cooled too fast to crystalize. In places the salt has extruded up in sharp, feathered flowers, in needles, in curling ferns of halite a foot high that would shatter at a touch and then — don't — because they never grow back. Nothing erodes. Nothing is replenished. What broke, stays broken. What formed, stays formed.

And it is crowded.

At first, in the near-dark, you think the ground is textured. Then you see the texture is moving, or rather, holding still so perfectly that you have to watch for minutes to perceive movement at all.

The fauna have no eyes. Or they have eyes that are not for this dark. They are everywhere, dense as cobbles in a riverbed, but silent and still. Low, domed things the size of tortoises with backs of interlocking silica plates, warm to the touch, venting heat from seams along their sides. Long, ribbon things that lie perfectly flat between the cracks, the color of basalt, lifting a frilled, heat-sensing head every few hours and then settling again for days. Pillared things on six jointed legs, as tall as your waist, that stand motionless for so long that salt crystals grow on their backs, and then take a single deliberate step and freeze again. They do not eat each other, not often. There is no plant to eat. There is no prey to chase.

They eat the rock, and the heat, and each other’s breath.

You crouch and watch one of the domed ones. Its underside is a soft, pale oven, pressed against the stone. It exudes a faint acid that does not wet, that etches the basalt and liberates iron and sulfur. It breathes in the warm, dry gas that sighs from the cracks and breathes out something else, something that the ribbon things absorb through their skins. A closed loop, slow as stone. They do not drink. Their chemistry is not water chemistry; cut one — you shouldn't — and it does not bleed, it outgasses, a puff of warm chlorine and ammonia that stings the nose and is gone. They are abundant because nothing dies quickly here. They live long. They move rarely. The entire plain is covered with them, shoulder to shoulder, a living pavement that extends beyond any light. In the far dark, you hear at long intervals a soft *tick* as a silica plate expands, or a faint sigh as something shifts its weight a millimeter. That is all.

In that mostly-quiet, the strangeness becomes clear.

Geometry does not quite hold. If you walk a straight line — keeping a crack to your left — for what you think is an hour, and turn and look back, your lamp is visible, close, as if you had not moved. Distance stretches, then contracts. The ceiling, when you think you see it, seems to be both flat and curved. The abundant life does not distribute randomly; from a rise — a pressure blister in the basalt, ten meters high, its sides still sharp as broken pottery — you can see patterns in the placement of the domed things, spirals and radial bursts, as if they settled according to a field you cannot feel. Some of the creatures are translucent in the dimmest light, and inside them you see not organs but slow-moving lights, like embers drifting in oil, that hold their position relative to the ground even as the animal moves.

It is a place of equilibrium. Time does not pass here the way it does above. The sharp edges stay sharp. The salt ferns do not grow. The animals do not starve. Even the heat does not cool. It is warm and it has always been warm.

And there are marks.

Scattered across the plain, not many, but enough that you always see one if you walk long enough, are structures. They are not ruins. They were never cities.

A low wall, waist high, built of the same hexagonal plates pried up and stacked without mortar, in a perfect circle ten paces across. Nothing inside. The stones are still sharp, the corners unworn. A cairn, a single pillar of balanced basalt flakes, too delicate to have been made by any of the animals, marking nothing. A shallow pit, scraped clean of salt, with a ring of blackened stones around a cold hearth where something that was not wood was once burned — perhaps the dried carapace of one of the dome-things, which burns with a low, oily blue flame for a night. A handprint pressed into cooling glass when it was still soft, fingers splayed, too small or too long to be human, now frozen forever with its edges still crisp.

Whoever built them used primitive tools. Chipped stone, hammered copper that has not oxidized in the dry air, rope made from braided fiber that is not plant fiber but the sinew of something that was stretched and cured. They came, or they were here, and they placed these things and left, or died without decay. Their things remain because nothing here wears away. You find a shallow bowl, chipped from salt crystal, sitting upright on a plate as if just set down. You lift it. Underneath, the pattern of animals has not reclaimed the space. They have simply stayed around it, dense and patient, for however long it has been.

There is no wind. There is no weather to come. The dark is not complete — if you stay long enough your eyes adjust to a faint, deep-red glow from the rock itself, and from the drifting ember-lights inside the largest of the pillar-creatures, which together give enough light to see your own hands as grey shapes. Sound does not carry. If you call out, your voice falls dead ten meters away.

The rule of this world is stasis. Not death, but suspension. Energy moves in tight, silent circles, from rock to animal to air to rock again. Nothing is added, nothing is lost. The sharpness never dulls. The heat never lessens. The vast, low, salt-white plain with its living pavement and its scattered, simple circles of stacked stone goes on and on under a ceiling you will never see, warm, dry, dark, crowded, and utterly, profoundly still — and it will be exactly the same when you turn off your light.
