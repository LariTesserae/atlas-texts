On the top of a mountain that never knew air.

It is not a mountain as mountains are on the surface - this one rises from a plain so deep and so flat that it might as well be the floor of the world. Three kilometers straight down the flank drops, a black wall of fresh basalt, hardly weathered at all. The stone is young. You can see where it broke. The cooling cracks are still sharp enough to cut, columns and stair-steps and glassy ledges that haven't had time to dull. No sand has gathered at their feet. No slope of scree. The edges are as they were when the earth extruded them - a recent thought, still crisp.

And you are high. Even under water, you feel the height. The abyssal plain is far below, pressure doubled and doubled again down there, and here, on this narrow summit plateau, there is a strange vertigo of depth in both directions - the kilometers of water above you pressing down, and the kilometers of water below you falling away from the cliff edge on all sides. If the ocean were drained you would be standing on an alpine peak above the clouds. As it is, you are standing on a peak in the dark.

The water is everything. It is not a sea you look at. It is the medium you are inside, cold and complete. Cool - not freezing, not hostile, but a steady, deep-mountain-lake cool that never varies, about the temperature of stone in shadow. It does not move. That is the first wrongness. At this depth there should be current, a slow pour of cold over the summit, particulate snow drifting down. There is not. The water is perfectly still, as if the ocean has been poured and then paused. A mote hangs a meter from your face and does not drift. It just hangs. If you push your hand through the water, it parts without wake and settles instantly behind you without a swirl. The rain of detritus from above - the faint mineral dust that should be forever falling - is suspended in place, a sparse, fixed constellation of specks in the dark, each exactly where it was a year ago, and a year before that.

There is almost no light. From far, impossibly far above, a filtered dusk reaches down, attenuated to a deep indigo that is more idea than illumination. It does not scatter. In normal water, light shafts and diffuses. Here the darkness is strangely clear, like looking through flawless glass. You can see a long way along the plateau and see nothing, because there is nothing to see, just the clean dark water and the sharp stone.

And on that stone - the town.

It is small. That is its strangeness as a city. It was clearly built to be larger, or it is the last knot of something larger that has been erased. Five or six structures, if you can call them structures. They are not ruined. They are not wrecked by pressure or time. They are too precise for that, and too empty.

They grow low across the summit, following the hexagonal fracture of the basalt. Blocks of matte material the color of bone and basalt, seamless, without joints or windows in the way you expect. The surfaces are warm to the touch, minutely warmer than the surrounding water, though the water never warms around them. When you come close you see the surfaces are not solid in the normal sense - they have a depth to them, a faint, slow-moving grain like interference patterns under skin, like something thinking very slowly. They are not lit from within. They emit a kind of light that doesn't travel. It clings to the edges of the buildings in a thin, cool corona, just enough to read the shape of a doorway that is far too tall and narrow, a plaza that is perfectly flat and perfectly empty, a pylon that does not connect to anything, holding its top a meter below where the water should crush it.

This is the technology. It does not hum. It holds the impossible in perfect equilibrium. The water should crush these hollow forms. The cold should have cracked them. The sea should have filled them. Instead the field that holds the stillness - the same field that has stopped the current, stopped the fall of dust, stopped time itself from eroding the fresh stone - also holds a bubble of other physics around the architecture. Angles are slightly wrong. A corridor you look down seems longer on the left side than the right until you blink. A cube set on its corner balances on a single basalt flake without weight. Inside the doorways there is no inside, only a deeper dark that returns your gaze after a delay.

No one is here. Or rather, there is no density of presence. The plateau is sparse beyond the way an abandoned place is sparse. The buildings are spaced as if to give each one solitude. Between them are wide intervals of bare, young rock, and the fixed stars of suspended silt. Sound, when it comes, carries too far and then stops too abruptly. Mostly, there is quiet. The water, being still, carries no hiss or surge. What little sound there is belongs to the structures: a very low, occasional tick, like a cooling stone, but at intervals too regular to be stone. A faint chime that is not heard so much as felt in the teeth, when the light on a pylon brightens by a fraction for no reason.

Nothing grows. The stone is too new and too clean, and the water is too still and too cold in a way that forbids it. No algae films the basalt, no kelp, no bacterial mat. The mineral world remains mineral. It shines where it broke, black glass at the cliff edge, pale plagioclase flecks in the gabbro like frost. The only life is rare to the point of rumor. Once, while you stand on the plaza, a shape passes far beyond the cliff edge - pale, translucent, larger than a building, moving without moving its body, as if it is being translated across the water rather than swimming. It does not disturb the suspended motes. It does not see you. It is gone, and the darkness is empty again for a long, long time. Perhaps it was the same one, looping on a decade-long orbit around the peak.

The rules here are not weather or season or decay. The rule is *stasis*.

Things do not fall. Things do not rust. Things do not drift. The young mountain will remain young. The sharp edges will remain sharp. The dust will remain exactly where it is. The light will not brighten toward day or dim toward night. The city, which was laid out as a settlement for many and now holds none, will continue to organize its empty plazas and impossible pylons and doorways that look into depth without depth, not because someone maintains it, but because it was built out of a physics that decided to stop negotiating with entropy.

You can stay on the summit a long time. The cool water does not chill you further. The darkness does not press closer. The quiet does not become silence; it remains precisely, evenly quiet. You are high above the world floor, deep below the world surface, inside an ocean that has become an atmosphere, inside a town that has become a mineral thought, and nothing here is waiting to happen.
