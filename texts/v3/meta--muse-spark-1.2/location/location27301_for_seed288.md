You notice it first as a absence in the plain.

The high steppe runs flat and featureless for a long way — wind-scoured basalt and caliche, pale dust, thin lichen holding the rock together in papery rosettes. Then the ground simply gives way.

Not a cliff. Not a canyon with sharp walls. This is older than that. The Hollow is so old its edges have forgotten how to be edges. The rim is a long, tired slope, rounded and slumped, where the plateau has sagged and settled and finally fallen inward in a slow collapse that took a hundred thousand years to finish. You walk to the lip and the wind stops, because the air down there is heavier and stays put.

It is a basin, a bowl the size of a county. Maybe fifteen kilometers across, maybe twenty — hazy distance makes it hard to judge. You can see all the way across on a clear hour, to the far rim which is the same soft, worn contour as the one under your feet. The walls fall away in broad terraces, not steps but swells, as if the earth had been draped in cloth and smoothed by a hand. Everything has been sanded by time. Boulders are not boulders anymore but low, loaf-shaped humps half-buried in their own dust. Gullies are wide shallow troughs with no water in them and no memory of water — only wind running through them like it has since before the continents settled.

You go down.

Light goes with you but not all at once. Up top the sky is white and high and thin. Halfway down the slope, the angle steals the direct sun. By the time your boots hit the floor, four hundred meters down, noon looks like late evening elsewhere. A dim, even, pearled light, filtered through the dust that never quite settles in the still air. No shadows to speak of, only a soft brightening overhead where the opening is. It is cool here. Not cold — cool in the way deep stone is cool. The air is perhaps fifty degrees, a relief from the sun-baked plateau, and it smells of mineral and old earth. Your breath is a little more visible than it should be.

There is no water. That is the first rule of the place and everything else has organized itself around it.

No spring, no seep, no creek bed with damp sand. The floor is a dry pan of compacted silt and salt and fine basalt grit, cracked into plates the size of tables. Where you scuff it, the dust beneath is talc-fine and smells faintly of iron. You could dig ten meters and find only drier dust. And yet it is not barren. That is the contradiction that takes your eyes a while to resolve.

The green is not where you expect it. It doesn't follow a watercourse. It follows the air.

From the floor, the basin looks sparsely furnished at first. Open ground, long emptiness between things. But the empty space is a trick. Look closer at each rise, each hump of ancient stone, each abandoned mound, and it is furred.

A kind of vegetation holds the Hollow that knows nothing about roots in water. The dominant form is a low, fleshy rosette, dusty green-grey, its leaves thick as fingers and coated in fine white hairs that comb the air. Stone-lettuce, the old trappers would have called it, but it is not lettuce. It grows in loose colonies, dozens here, a hundred there, with yards of bare pan between. Between them, curtains hang. Where the basin walls are steepest, long shawls of lichen and moss and air-fern drape down the soft rock for meters, never touching ground, drinking fog. They are lush in a way that seems impossible for a desert — dense, piled, almost tropical in texture — but they are dry to the touch, rustling like paper when wind brushes them. At dusk they bead. Not with rain. With dew wrung out of the temperature inversion, each hair of each leaf collecting a droplet overnight that never falls, only sinks back into the leaf by morning. The plants sweating and drinking their own breath.

It is a slow place. A place of patient gradients. One of those rosettes might take twenty years to grow a hand wider. The hanging gardens on the terraces add a millimeter a season. Nothing hurries. The dust drifts a few grains a day. Even the light changes slowly, the dim oval of sky brightening and dimming as if through frosted glass.

And then the sound hits you.

From the rim you heard nothing. The wind up there makes its own noise. Down here the air is still and the sound has nowhere to go. It is deafening, but not loud in the way a machine is loud. It is a living loudness, layered and total, a solid wall that presses on the ears.

It is fauna. An abundance that you don't see at first because it is scattered and small and perfectly matched to the sparse, low growth — and then you realize the ground, the air, the walls are moving with it.

The city is not a human city. There was never a tool here.

The first time you think they are hills, those long mounds on the basin floor. They are too regular, too upright. Five, six, eight meters tall, weathered into soft cones, spaced far apart so that you can walk for minutes between them in quiet dust — sparse — and then stand in the shadow of the next. They are towers. They are built.

Not of stone. Of earth cemented with saliva and frass and fiber, baked in the cool dry air to a hardness like adobe. The builders are still building. Small, dark, chitinous things the size of your thumb move in sheets over the surface, repairing, carrying, carrying. Termite is the word your mind offers but it's not right; these are something larger, slower, with a faint phosphorescence to their backs in the dim. Their mounds are the infrastructure. Each one is riddled with chimneys and lungs and deep chambers that breathe for the colony. Around their bases, secondary construction has accrued over centuries — wasp-paper galleries, swallow nests made of mud pellets, the woven stick platforms of larger birds, all using the mounds as foundation, as high ground, as wall. Layer upon layer, generation upon generation, until the mounds have become districts. Avenues run between them where the denizens have worn the pan smooth with feet. The outer skins are now patched with the same felted vegetation that grows everywhere else, so that the towers look like groves, like shaggy hills with green pelts.

There are larger movers too. Things that do not live in the towers but move between them. Low, heavy grazers like capybara but dust-colored, with broad padded feet, cropping the rosettes with careful mouths and never eating a whole colony at once. Flocks of small, soot-colored birds that live entirely on the hanging curtains, vanishing into them in a scatter and emerging elsewhere. And above everything, in the dim air, a cloud of insects so thick it hazes the far wall — gnats, stridulators, humming things — rising and falling in slow columns.

The sound is them. The stridulators alone could be the source. Millions of them in the lichen curtains and on the towers, each rubbing leg on wing. At midday their song becomes a single, unwavering tone that you feel in your molars, a metallic shriek that seems to come from the stone itself. The birds answer it with sharp, glassy calls, and the grazers low. Even the towers make noise, a faint, constant sigh as air is pulled through their breathing chimneys and out again. There is no silence to be found down here. Even cupping your ears, the vibration comes up through your boots.

It is urban in density and function without ever having been invented. There are neighborhoods — the older, larger, greener mounds to the center where the air is stillest, the newer raw-earth cones near the walls where the slope still occasionally sloughs. There are highways of scent and footpath, middens, nurseries. There is no plan, but there is order, the kind that emerges when many things want the same sparse resources and negotiate over a thousand years.

You stand in the long space between two towers and understand the rules here:

Nothing is wasted. Water is not stored, it is caught and used within hours. Height is wealth, because height is cooler and damper air. Sound is how you hold territory when you cannot see far in dim, dusty light. And age is not decay, it is smoothing. Nothing sharp survives here. Edges are a temporary condition.

If you stay until the light thins further toward true dusk, the insect cloud comes lower, and the dew begins to form on every leaf-hair, glittering like frost in the half-light, and the towers exhale one long, warm breath, and the deafening chorus shifts down a half-tone and holds it — not louder, not softer, just present, as it has been since long before you found the rim, and as it will be long after the wind finally files the last soft hill down to dust.
