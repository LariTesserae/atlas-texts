On the high ground, there is a sea.

Not a lake that looks like a sea. A sea that ended up on high ground, and stayed.

You come up the long, weathered approach first — the old mountain itself. It is not sharp. At this elevation the world should be thin and wind-scoured and bare, but this one is round-shouldered and settled into itself. The stone has been there so long it has forgotten how to be craggy. It slopes up in broad, cool gradients, its basalt and limestone softened by lichen the color of old iron, by the patient work of roots. The path is not a path, exactly, but a worn groove where feet and water have agreed for generations to go the same way. The air thins and cools as you climb. It smells like stone and coming rain even when the sky above is clear.

And then the rim.

The caldera is small. You could walk its circumference before midday if you were not stopping every few steps to look. Maybe a hamlet could fit inside it, with room to spare. That's exactly what did.

The crater holds water to the lip. No — it holds *more* than to the lip. The water stands proud of the rim by twenty, thirty feet, a smooth, vertical wall of dark water holding its shape against all reason, like a glass with no glass. You can see the tension on its surface, the way the wind makes it shiver but never spill. From down the mountain you thought the light on top was cloud. Up close you understand: it is sun through water.

It is submerged on the summit. A hanging ocean, cool enough to ache your teeth, deep enough that the bottom is a rumor. Normal daylight comes down through it and changes as it goes — at the top, white-gold and rippled; a few fathoms down, green and columned; deeper still, a slow blue dusk that never quite becomes night. Dust motes and pollen and tiny silver fish hang in those columns of light like notes on a staff.

And it is not empty water. It is choked — no, *made* — of plant.

Vegetation did not colonize this place. It *is* this place. There is no substrate that is not living.

From the rim you can see the forest that grew to hold the sea in place. Great kelp-analogues, but not kelp; mountain plants that learned to live drowned. Their trunks are thicker than houses, olive-black and deeply furrowed, rising from the crater floor far below and breaching the surface of the hanging sea, their crowns spreading open above water like lily pads the size of courtyards. Below water, between those trunks, is a lattice so dense it is architecture. Interwoven stems as wide as streets, aerial roots as taut as cables, bladders the size of barrels holding air, mats of leaf and moss so thick you could walk on them — and people do. The whole volume from stone to surface is green-black living mass, layered and hollowed and braided. Light filters between leaves bigger than doors.

And inside that mass — the town.

It is urban in the way a beaver dam is urban, or a coral reef. Not sprawling, but intentional, dense with purpose, scaffolded. This is not a few huts. This is a settlement scaled to the basin, a vertical hamlet that fills the green volume without filling it up.

There is a lot of emptiness on purpose. The built density is sparse, scattered — you notice the silences between structures more than the structures themselves. A dwelling here, empty water and drifting fronds for thirty feet, then another. They did not pack together for warmth; in this cool water, warmth is not the problem. They spaced themselves for light, for growth, for the slow breathing of the plants they live inside.

Each dwelling is grown more than made. At primitive tech, with nothing but stone adzes, bone knives, cordage, and time. They do not cut the great trunks down. They coax them. A family will tend a young column for a decade, guiding its shoots, binding them with woven fiber, weighting them with stones until they arch into a dome. They carve hollows into the living wood while the sap still runs, and the wood heals around the doorway. Walls are living pith, floors are braided rhizome, roofs are overlapping leaves stitched with root-thread that continue to photosynthesize overhead, mottling the interior with green light. Between houses, bridges are not built but persuaded — living runners trained across the gap, thickening year by year until they can bear weight. The oldest bridges are so weathered and thick with epiphytes you can't tell where bridge stops and tree begins. Everything is established, in balance. Nothing looks new. Even the newest house looks like it has been there for a lifetime, because it has — its lifetime and its maker's.

It is quiet in the way old things are quiet, but it is very loud.

Sound travels too well in this cold, dense water and in the wood that carries vibration like a drum. The place is never silent. You hear it before you see it from the rim: a low, constant creak and groan as the great plants sway against their moorings, as water presses and releases. The hollow booms of bladder-pods imploding and refilling. The bright, metallic chorus of millions of small fauna — shrimp and glassfish and water beetles the size of your hand clicking, chirping, scraping, singing inside the stalks. Larger things move slowly and loudly too: pale, blind newts as long as canoes that graze on the leaves, whistling through blowholes as they pass between houses; schools of soot-black carp that turn as one and make the water thrum. And the people — their voices carry, their tools knock. A stone hammer on living wood underwater makes a sound like a bell. Children shout and the sound stays.

There are no streets. There are currents. Cool water moves with a patience that borders on stillness — the dynamic here is slow, almost geological. Leaves turn to follow the sun over weeks, not hours. Houses grow a hand's breadth a year. The sea itself inside its wall barely circulates; you can see the faint, cold drift of particulate spiraling down for days. But for all its slowness, the place is alive and abundant. Every surface that is not wood is animal. Anemone-like filterers bloom on every stalk, barnacles with feathery tongues, snails whose shells are gardens for smaller snails. Eels knot in the root tangles. Birds — ordinary, air-breathing mountain birds — dive straight through the vertical wall of water without breaking it, as if the water were air to them, and spear fish among the rooftops, and burst out the top again, shaking droplets that never fall.

The rules here are simple and slightly unusual, and you learn them quickly because the place enforces them gently:

One, water stays where the plants tell it to. The wall holds because the outer ring of crown-leaves transpires in unison, pulling a skin of surface tension tight around the whole basin. Tear a leaf, and the wall shivers. Tend the ring, and it holds.

Two, nothing dead is wasted. When a stem dies it is not cleared. It is hollowed, inhabited, eaten from within by fish and insects and finally by the same fungi that soften the mountain stone. A dead house becomes a nursery, then a reef, then a bridge.

Three, you move slowly. The water is cool — not freezing, but cool enough that quick movement chills the blood. Everyone swims as if in thought. Even the fish are unhurried. Hurry is a kind of rudeness here, and also useless. The plants will not hurry for you.

At noon, when the sun is directly overhead, the entire submerged town lights up at once. Every leaf, every translucent wall, every drifting shoal catches the light and throws it. From the rim it looks like a city made of lanterns under green glass — scattered constellations of warm light in cool water, connected by living thread, bounded neatly by the dark stone circle that holds the sea up in the sky. Small enough to hold in your eye at once. Deep enough to get lost in for a lifetime.

You slip over the rim and into the wall — it gives like cool silk — and suddenly you are inside, breathing water that the plants have made breathable, hearing the town's loud, slow creaking all around you, suspended among leaves bigger than houses in a mountain-top ocean that should not be there, and is.
