It is high enough that air has weight and thinness at the same time.

You are in a bowl at extreme elevation, not the peak itself but what the peak holds. A cirque, a single bite taken out of the ridge at about four thousand meters, where the main massif curls around and closes most of the sky. From below you would never see it. From above you would think it was just shadow. It is smaller than maps suggest — you could walk its rim in twenty minutes if you could walk the rim, which you can't. Cathedral-scale, hamlet-scale. One room with walls of stone that did not agree to be walls.

The rock is young. That is the first thing you feel even in the dark. It has not been smoothed. It is a fresh break, basalt and splintered andesite still sharp where it was torn, not weathered to curves. Everything is edge and tooth and loose scree that hasn't found its angle of repose yet. The cliff faces are stepped and undercut, with hanging slabs that ring when the wind hits them. At the back wall a recent rockfall has exposed a face so clean it looks wet even when dry, a dark glassy scar about thirty meters high. Talus fans out from it in a fresh apron that no lichen has claimed yet. The ground underfoot is not soil so much as crushed rock held together by roots.

Because it should be alpine barren at this height and cold, but it isn't. It is lush to the point of choking.

The cool is constant, maybe 6 or 7 degrees Celsius, the kind of cool that lives on skin as damp rather than temperature. Your breath makes a short cloud and disappears. The damp is not wetness. There is no lake here, no river. Water exists as a condition, not a body. It condenses. The stone sweats. Clouds catch on the rim and get shredded, and for ten minutes it rains hard and vertical, and then it stops and the cloud is gone and the water is gone except where it was caught. Pockets of it sit in cupped rocks, in the axils of leaves, in shallow basins lined with black peat that never quite dry. Between rains, the air itself drips. Every surface beads. You hear it before you see it — ticking off leaf tips onto stone.

And the vegetation is impossible and dense and completely indifferent to your expectations of altitude. No trees, not here, nothing woody could hold, but everything else has come in thick and layered until there is no bare ground. Cushion mosses three hands deep, saturated and springy, bright in a way you can see even in low light. Ferns, but stunted and leathery alpine ferns packed so tight their fronds overlap like shingles. Lichen, crust and leaf and hair, draping the rocks. Saxifrage and alpine azalea and dense low mats of things with waxy leaves that store water, all pressing against each other, elbowing for space. It is not a meadow. It is a single, continuous upholstery of plant life, knee to chest high in places, so dense you cannot put a boot down without crushing something. It grows fast here. Too fast. Shoots push up pale and then harden overnight. A frond unfurls while you watch. The peat builds while you listen. It is turbulent, hyperactive growth in the cold, as if the short season has made it frantic.

There is no animal in it. That takes a moment to notice because your brain keeps expecting sound from a living thing and not getting it. No insects ticking in the moss. No birds cutting overhead. No marmot whistle. No worms even, turned up in the peat — nothing moving but plants and rock and water and machines. The absence makes the green feel heavier, as if the plants are growing without being eaten, without check, filling every niche an animal would have taken. Pollen hangs in the damp air and never gets taken. Spores drift and settle. Leaves die and mat down and become new ground without ever being chewed.

It is dark. Even at noon it would be dark, but now — whatever now is under a lid of cloud that has sealed the bowl — it is true darkness, not night but the darkness of a deep room with one small skylight. The rim lets in a bruised, diffuse light that has no source. You can see your hands, you can see the near fronds, but beyond ten meters everything is mass without color. The rock walls are darker than the sky. The few high snowfields above the rim give off a faint albedo that helps nothing. You see by contrast, not illumination. The green looks almost black until you are close.

Nothing is built here. That is a rule of this place. No house, no wall, no foundation, no cairn that lasts more than a day before the moss takes it or the scree slides it away. No one lives here because nothing can stay still enough to be lived in.

And yet it is industrial.

The industry does not build. It moves. It never becomes architecture.

Strung across the bowl on cables that sing under tension are the machines. Two diesel generators on skids, half-sunk in the moss, coughing heat and stinking of hot oil in the cold clean air — they shudder and hunt and never quite run smooth at this altitude. From them, thick black cables run up the rock to a switchbox that spits and hums. A tracked drill rig sits canted on the talus apron, its mast folded down while its engine idles loud, ticking over to keep warm, its bit still dripping rock flour and water. A cableway winch thumps on the eastern rim, spooling and unspooling steel line with a brake that screeches. Pumps, low and rectangular, hammer in the peat pockets where water collects, sucking and spitting through corrugated hose. None of it is anchored to concrete. Everything is on pallets, on skids, on temporary mats that have already been half-swallowed by moss. Everything is provisional. This is not a mine with buildings. It is a mine that is passing through.

The sound is loud. Not deafening, but enveloping and inescapable in a space this small and this walled. The generators roar and the sound bounces off the young rock and comes back flat and hard. The wind over the rim is a separate roar, a higher, cleaner white noise that cuts out every few seconds and is replaced by a gust that flaps every fern frond at once with a sound like canvas. Water ticks everywhere. The pumps clank. The cableway creaks as its carriage shudders along the line hauling a bucket of crushed rock that drips. Stone pings and settles in the scree. And underneath it all, the vegetative crackle of growth itself — fronds pushing, peat sighing as it takes water, moss swelling. All of it is amplified because there is no animal sound to soften it, no birdsong to give it scale. It is just rock and plant and engine.

The dynamic is the point. This place does not hold still.

While you stand there the cloud drops in again and it rains for ninety seconds, hard enough to make the generators sputter, then stops. A section of moss on the south slope, overwatered, slumps three feet downhill in a slow green avalanche and exposes wet black earth that immediately begins to steam in the generator heat. The drill rig's operator — you haven't seen a person, maybe the machines are running themselves on timers between crews — throttles up and the bit screams against fresh basalt, and the vibration brings down a patter of chips from the new scar. A fern, released by the slump, springs upright. The pumps change pitch as their pocket empties, suck air, rattle, then find water again. The whole bowl is negotiating with itself moment to moment: growth against slide, condensation against drainage, machine against altitude, cold against diesel heat. Nothing resolves.

You don't stay long. The bowl is too small to stay in, too dense to move through without damage, too loud and too changeable to rest. The rules are simple here and enforced by physics: come damp, stay cool, keep your light low, don't expect anything you make to remain, and don't expect anything to wait for you. The plants will close your footprints in a day. The rock will take back the cut the drill made in a season. The damp will take the heat off your skin before you have felt warm.
