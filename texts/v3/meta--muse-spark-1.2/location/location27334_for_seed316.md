This place has no horizon in the way a horizon should work.

At first it looks flat. Not flat like a plain, flat like a table that has been left out for so long the world has forgotten it was ever anything else. A low country the size of a continent, or a continent the size of a low country - there is no high ground left to judge scale from. This is what is left after everything that could be worn down has been worn down.

It is old the way bedrock is old. Not ruined, not broken. Settled. Resolved. The elevation has been negotiated away over an epoch so long that mountains are a rumor in the stone. What remains is a peneplain scoured to a gentle, almost mathematical undulation: broad, shallow pans kilometers across but only a few meters deep, their rims so soft you only know you've crossed one when the water around your ankles changes temperature. Between them, the land swells into whale-backs and shield-backs of stone, smooth as worn teeth, polished by water and wind and time until they shine dully even in dim light.

And it is packed.

There is no empty space here. Not a hand's breadth. The ground is a tiling of those low stone domes set shoulder to shoulder, fitted together like scales, like cobblestones laid by something with infinite patience. Between every dome and every other dome is water. Not rivers, not lakes — there is nowhere for a river to go on land this level — but a continuous, ankle-to-knee-deep sheet, a braided skin of water that never quite decides if it is a swamp or a flood or a dew. It finds every low, it sheets over every flat, it beads and weeps directly out of the rock itself. The stone is porous at this age, sweating. You can watch a dry dome darken from the bottom up as water wells silently out of its pores. The air is saturated too, warm and close and condensing on everything, on your skin, on the stone, dripping back down without ever having rained.

The light never gets strong. A low, warm, cloud-locked dimness hangs over everything, the color of late afternoon after a storm that never fully clears. The clouds are not high. They are low and heavy and vast enough to have their own weather systems inside themselves - a continent of cloud over a continent of wet stone, rolling and bruising and glowing faintly from within, as if the heat of the stone below were lighting them. The sun is somewhere behind it all, a diffused smear, never a point. Shadows are soft or nonexistent. Distance eats itself; things a mile away look the same as things a hundred miles away, because the haze and the repetition make scale slip.

That haze is not empty. The air is thick with fine mist, with midges of water that never fall, with a warm mineral steam rising off every surface. You breathe water as much as air. Everything smells of wet rock and iron and warm mud, and of something faintly organic, like algae cooking.

### What grows, what lives, what remains

Vegetation is sparse, and that sparseness makes it more present, not less. Nothing can root deeply here - the stone is too close, the water too constant. So it clings in stubborn, isolated crowns. There will be nothing for half a kilometer of glistening stone and skin-water, and then a sudden, dense tuft of black-green reeds as tall as a person, their roots gripping a crack, their leaves rattling. Or a mat of thick, waxy succulents spread flat over a dome like a spill, holding water in its leaves. Or a single tree, stunted and wind-bent even though there is little wind, its bark pale and swollen with stored water, its branches draped in long, pale lichen that drips constantly. The green is dark, almost blue-black in the dim light, and desperately alive against the grey-pink stone.

Animals are rare to the point of feeling accidental. You can walk for hours hearing nothing alive but the reeds shivering. Then, far off across the sheet-water, a tall wader on legs like stilts will pick its way with impossible slowness, its body a grey that matches the stone, its head down, feeding on something in the ooze that you cannot see. Or a low, heavy amphibian thing will haul itself onto a dome, blink with filmed eyes, and slide back under without a ripple. The sky, when the cloud briefly thins, shows nothing — no flocks — until a single vast glider, pale and silent, circles on thermals that shouldn't exist over flat wet ground, and is gone. Life here is not abundant. It is durable, and it is spaced out, as if the land can only spare so much.

And there are marks. Not cities, not ruins in any human sense. Scattered things, half-swallowed by the ancient flat. A standing stone, alone, its faces smoothed until any carving is only a suggestion of geometry under your palm. A low ring of boulders that holds water more perfectly still than any natural pan, as if leveled. A causeway of fitted slabs that runs straight for a kilometer and then disappears under a sheet of water and does not reappear. No tools, no metal, no lights. Whatever made them left no tech, only placement. The stone itself is the artifact now, rearranged just enough to make you notice that someone once thought straight lines were important here, long before the water came back.

### The sound and the unrest

You expect a place this flat and old to be silent. It is not. It is deafening.

The water makes most of it — not crashing, because there is nowhere to crash, but a ceaseless, continent-wide murmur and hiss and gurgle as that endless skin of water moves. It moves constantly over the shallow lips between pans, whispering over stone edges in a million tiny waterfalls an inch high, braiding and re-braiding around the domes. The cloud drinks it up and drips it back down in a constant patter, a drizzle that is not rain but condensation falling from two meters up. The stone itself ticks and sighs as it warms and cools under the dim glow, expanding, exhaling water. And under that, deeper, a low thrumming, like blood in your ears, like the plain itself resonating.

Because nothing here stays still, even though it looks like it has been still forever.

This is a turbulent place. A hyperactive place, despite its age. The surface churns and remakes itself while you watch. Mud boils up in a pan and settles into a new island in an hour. Algal films bloom across a sheet of water, turn the water wine-red, and then tear apart in a sudden, glassy surge that comes from no wind you can feel. The water sheet shivers and reverses direction for no topographical reason. On a larger scale, the cloud above roils with hyper-fast weather — cells of darker and lighter vapor forming and collapsing in minutes, dropping curtains of warm drizzle on one square kilometer while the next one steams dry. You can stand and see three different rains happening at three different distances, none of them moving toward you the way weather should.

And the weirdness is the rule, not the exception.

Distance misbehaves. You walk toward a reed clump that looks a hundred meters away and it is still a hundred meters away twenty minutes later, then you blink and you are inside it. The standing stone you left behind is suddenly ahead of you again, or there are two of them. The water sometimes flows uphill over the gentle curve of a dome, clinging, defying weight, before dropping off the other side with a slap. Reflections don't match their sources; the cloud reflected in a pan will show a break that isn't in the sky. Sound arrives before its cause — you hear the splash long before the mud geyser erupts. Gravity feels negotiable at the edges; if you stand still long enough on a high dome, you feel a faint lightness, as if the world has forgotten to hold you tightly here at the end of its life.

No compass would work here, not because of magnetism but because north keeps moving.

It is a warm, wet, dim, ancient flat the size of a world, crowded beyond crowding with stone and water and steam, almost empty of green and of beasts, haunted by a few inexplicable placements, roaring with its own quiet noise, and constantly undoing and redoing itself under a sky that has become its own ground. It feels less like a landscape you are in and more like a surface you are on — the final, saturated skin of a planet that has eroded down to a membrane where rock sweats, air condenses, and reality has thinned enough to wrinkle.

If you stay long enough, your footprints fill with water that is slightly warmer than the water around it, and then smooth over, and then are gone.
