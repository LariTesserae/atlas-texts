This place is a basin.

Not a valley, not a crater — lower than it should be. A shallow, broad dish of land pressed just a few meters above sea level, so low you can feel the weight of the air. The air doesn't move here. It hangs. It is hot in a way that has nothing to do with sun.

You notice the heat first, before you see anything. A wet, close heat at around body temperature, the kind that makes your shirt stick to you in seconds. The light is wrong for heat like this. There is no hard sunlight. The sky is a low, uniform sheet of cloud the color of wet concrete, bright enough to see by but dim enough that the town has left its lights on all day. Has always left its lights on.

The town fills the basin, or the basin was made to hold the town. It's not large. You can stand at one edge and see the other — maybe three hundred meters across, a single hamlet-sized works, a single industrial organism. It feels like the inside of a room with a very high ceiling: the cloud deck is the roof, the slick black rim of rock around the edge is the walls. Cathedral scale. Everything that matters is in here.

The rock is young. That is the strangest thing at first until you get used to it. This is not weathered country. The basin's lip is fresh basalt, sharp and glassy where it broke, still stair-stepped in clean, right-angled terraces where the ground cracked and dropped. No soil has softened it. No river has rounded it. Water weeps straight out of the stone in cold, constant ribbons, cutting only hairline grooves before spilling down the black steps. You can see where the stone was still hot not long ago — the faint, oily rainbow on its surface, the way it hasn't yet decided to be ground.

And there is water everywhere, though there is no lake, no river. The water is in the air, on the surfaces, just underfoot. The ground inside the basin is flat, paved and re-paved and patched with concrete and iron plate and slag brick, but water finds every seam. It sweats up between the plates. It condenses on the underside of every pipe and drips, steadily, endlessly. Shallow runnels run alongside every walkway, warm as bathwater, carrying a thin film of oil. After rain? It is always after rain. The atmosphere condenses against the cooler basalt and against the metal of the town and falls again without ever really having been a storm.

Because the town is industrial, completely. There is nothing here that is not part of the work.

It is not a city of houses with factories among them. It is a factory that people happen to live inside. Three or four big structures dominate — a power house with its sawtooth roof, a processing mill, a waterworks, a long sorting shed — all built of brick gone dark with damp, and riveted iron, and poured concrete. Between them, smaller things: a row of workers' quarters with laundry strung on lines that never dry, a canteen, a substation that hums. Everything is organized, gridded, layered. Walkways run above pipes that run above culverts. Ladders and catwalks stitch the second story of one building to the roof of another. Gantry cranes on rusted rails span the central yard, though they rarely move now to anywhere new. The infrastructure is dense, almost knotted in places, and then — oddly — it stops, and there is a patch of empty, wet concrete the size of a yard, or an alley that opens onto nothing but the weeping basalt wall. The town is sparse for how built it is. Lots of space between presences. You can hear how much air there is between the buildings. It's not crowded. It's just complete.

It is loud. That is its constant.

Not chaotic loud, but steady, industrial loud that has become a kind of silence. The power house exhales through its vents with a low, deep roar. Pumps thud on a four-beat cycle you feel in your molars. Somewhere a press stamps — CLACK ... CLACK ... CLACK — with perfect regularity. Steam sighs out of pressure valves and makes the catwalks slippery. Water splashes and slaps in the runnels. And underneath it all is the electrical buzz of the substation, a 50-cycle whine that never varies. No one shouts over it. People just work inside it, their movements timed to it.

The light is dim and yellowed. The cloud-roof lets through a diffuse, sourceless glow that throws no shadows. The town adds its own: bare bulbs in cages over every doorway, amber sodium lamps over the yard, greenish fluorescents inside the sheds. All of it reflected and doubled in the wet surfaces, in puddles of warm water, in the sheen on the basalt. You can see clearly, but nothing is bright. Colors are muted except where rust bleeds orange or a safety rail has been repainted hazard yellow and already the paint is blistering from the damp heat.

There is almost no vegetation, and what there is knows better than to be ambitious. No trees. No fields. A few clumps of stiff saltgrass where dust has collected on a flat roof. Black-green algae slicking the runnels and the north side of every brick wall. A single mangrove-like shrub, stunted and windless, growing out of a crack in the basalt lip where fresh water seeps, its leaves leathery and beaded with condensation. The town scrubs the rest away, or the heat and the oil and the lack of soil does.

And yet it is full of animals.

You hear them first between the machine beats — the whine of mosquitoes in clouds that rise off the warm runnels, the chirr of crickets living in the expansion joints of the concrete. Dragonflies the size of your hand, amber-winged, hover over the culverts. Rats move openly along the pipes, sleek and unbothered. In the shallow, warm pools that collect in the lowest part of the yard, mosquito fish flicker by the thousands, and white egrets stand utterly still on their stilt legs right beside the stamping press, waiting. On the power house roof, pigeons nest by the heat vents. Geckos dot every wall. Swallows cut laps under the cloud-roof, feeding on the insects that breed in the endless damp. Life is abundant here precisely because the place is hot and wet and still — a marsh that happens to have been paved over and roofed with industry.

Nothing changes.

That is the rule of this place, and you feel it after ten minutes. The water level in the runnels never rises or falls. The pumps never pump faster or slower. The heat does not break at night because the cloud does not break and the lamps do not go out. The sharp edges of the basalt do not dull. The egret does not catch the fish. The fish do not die. The men and women in oil-stained coveralls walk the same route from quarters to mill to canteen and back at the same hour, not out of routine but because there is no other hour.

It is not dead. It is in equilibrium. Hot air holds as much water as it can hold and then lets it go onto the cool iron and the cool rock, which warms it and sends it back up again. Engines burn and make heat and the heat makes steam and the steam condenses. The same water circles. The same sound repeats. The same dim light persists. The town was built to use the constant seep and the constant heat, and now it keeps them constant.

If you stay long enough your breathing syncs to the pump. Your skin stays wet. Your ears stop trying to find a change in the roar. You understand this is not a landscape becoming something else. It emerged recently, sharply, all at once — a dropped basin, a factory set down inside it — and then it decided to stay exactly as it is: a small, wet, hot, loud, dim, living room of a town, hissing and dripping and buzzing to itself under its low sky, with plenty of space between its few, necessary things.
