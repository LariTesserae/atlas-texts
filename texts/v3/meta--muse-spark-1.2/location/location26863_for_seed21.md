It is high. That is the first thing the air tells you.

Not high and cold, the way high places are supposed to be. High and hot. You climb through thin, bright air for hours and expect it to cool, but the heat thickens as you rise, until the top is hotter than the base. A thermal inversion that has forgotten how to correct itself.

The place itself is small. You could walk its length before your water got warm in its bottle. A basin, a room, a grove made of stone - not a valley, not a range. A single feature with edges you can see all at once, like a cathedral whose walls are still being cut.

From the lip it looks simple: a shallow, sharp-edged bowl scooped out of dark rock maybe two hundred meters across. The stone is too new. That is the second wrong thing. At this elevation the rock should be rounded by wind and ice, softened. This rock hasn't learned to be old yet. It broke recently. The rim is a fracture, not a hill - a clean, unevian lip of basalt and glassy black scoria that still holds the memory of being molten. The faces are knife-sharp, unweathered, with conchoidal flaking where the heat left them. You can see where the ground heaved and cooled too fast, leaving stair-steps and blistered ridges, un-smoothed by any river. There has not been enough time for rivers. There has only been water.

And there is water everywhere, despite the heat.

It does not pool the way lowland water does. It hangs. The air at this height is saturated and hot and it cannot hold itself. It condenses on everything. The black rock weeps. Not from rain - from itself. At dawn the stone sweats, at noon it drips, at dusk it steams. Thin sheets of water slide down the sharp walls and bead on the undersides of overhangs and gather in the air as a low, warm fog that never quite becomes cloud and never quite clears. You hear it before you see it inside: a constant, sparse ticking. Drip. Drip. A long pause. Drip.

At the bottom of the bowl the water finally gives up and becomes ground. Not a lake. A skin. A marsh the size of a courtyard, ankle-deep, hot to the touch, perfectly clear over pale silt and then black again where the basalt shows through. It is so warm it breathes. Wisps of vapor rise off it in the normal, white daylight and vanish a few feet up. The water is near the surface everywhere - you can see it pushing up between rocks, welling in footprints, making the soil sigh when you step. The boundary between solid and liquid is undecided here.

Where there is soil - in the cracks, in the windblown pockets caught against the young stone - the vegetation is absurdly lush for how little of it there is.

It does not carpet. It islands. One dense clump of giant ferns will explode from a crevice, fronds as broad as doors, deep green and almost black, wet and shining, humming with moisture. Ten meters of bare, steaming rock. Then a grove of something like bamboo but thicker, with translucent stalks that show the water moving inside them. Then a mat of moss so thick and vivid it looks poured, harboring small white flowers that open and close with the condensing cycle. The sparsity makes the lushness stranger. Each plant has room to be itself completely, unpressed by neighbors, so they grow large and perfect and a little lonely.

It is quiet. Mostly.

The heat and the water should be loud with insects, with birds, but there are almost no animals. Or they do not stay. Once an hour you might see a single glider - a pale, manta-shaped thing that rides the hot vapor updrafts along the rim, never landing, never calling - cast a shadow over the marsh and gone. You hear the drip, the soft exhale of the hot pool, the faint crackle of the young basalt expanding in the sun. Your own breath is loud in the thin-hot air. When something alive does make a sound, a single low chirp from inside the fern island or a splash too deliberate to be a drip, it carries unnerving distance in the bowl.

The built things are not what you expect at first. You look for ruins and miss them, because they are not ruined.

There are three of them. Or maybe five, depending on how you count.

On the eastern wall, half-sunk into the sharp rock as if the rock grew around it rather than being cut for it, is a pylon. It is not metal. It is not stone. It is a lattice, black and open, maybe four meters tall, too thin for its height, tapering to a point that does not quite touch the air. It is cold. In this heat, it radiates cold without shadow, without mechanism, without sound. Water condenses on it faster than on the natural rock and runs down it in impossible sheets - uphill for an inch, sideways, then down - collecting around its base in a perfectly circular, perfectly still moat that never overflows and never evaporates despite steaming. The surface of that water is a mirror that doesn't reflect quite right; your reflection arrives a half-second late.

A hundred feet away, closer to the marsh, there is a low ring on the ground, knee-high, made of the same lattice but fallen - or grown that way - into a circle. The grass inside the ring grows differently. The fern fronds inside are doubled, or mirrored, their veining on the outside of the leaf. When the wind passes over the ring, the air inside ripples like water.

The third stands in the marsh itself, barely visible. At noon you think it's a heat shimmer. In the late light you see it's a field, vertical, thin as paper, holding a curtain of water in the air. A wall of rain that stays in place, suspended, each droplet holding position, not falling. You can walk around it and see it edge-on and it almost disappears. Put your hand near it and the hairs on your arm rise. It is active, whatever it is. Not abandoned. Maintaining itself. The engineering here does not explain itself. It is not hiding machinery; there is no machinery to hide. It holds the heat, holds the water, holds the bowl's weather in a slow, deliberate cycle. Perhaps it made the bowl.

The rules here are small and local and not quite negotiated with the world outside.

Water flows toward the center, but if you watch closely at the lattice moats it also flows slightly uphill before it decides to obey.

Heat does not dissipate the way it should at elevation. It gathers.

Sound does not echo the way it should off hard young stone. It is swallowed.

Time feels a little pooled as well. The sharpness of the rock says this place is weeks or years old. The silt in the marsh says centuries. The pylon says something else entirely, something that has not yet learned to erode.

If you stand in the middle of the marsh where the vapor rises warm around your calves and look up, the rim makes a perfect circle of bright, normal sky. The scale does what cathedrals do - it makes the small space feel vast, and the vast sky feel like a ceiling, close enough to touch. You can see every feature at once: the weeping wall, the cold pylon, the fern island, the curtain of held rain, the empty, steaming black between them. Nothing crowds. Everything breathes.

It is not abandoned. It is not inhabited. It is between. The systems - geological, hydrological, biological, and the other one - are all running at once, active but not hurried. Water condensing, rock still settling and ticking as it cools, plants unfurling visibly in the damp heat, the lattices quietly correcting the air.

Stay long enough and the warm fog will bead on your shoulders, your eyelashes, until you are weeping too, until you are part of the cycle for a little while.
