It is low country.

Not flat — low, in the way a palm held out holds water. A broad warm plain that breathes. The air sits heavy and still over it, sweet with rot and leaf-mould, and the horizon never gets far before it softens into heat-haze and green. This is young land. If you dig down a handspan you hit limestone that hasn't learned how to be stone yet — pale, almost soft, full of shells still sharp at the edges, pitted with little unopened pockets where the sea was only recently. There are no old valleys here, no river canyons cut deep. Where the ground breaks it breaks clean and new, as if something underneath inhaled and the surface gave way all at once.

You could walk across this forest for a day and not know it was there.

The canopy overhead is closed but not high. Thirty, forty feet up, the crowns tangle — kapok and fig and sapodilla, their leaves big as plates, their buttress roots running like walls through the understory. It is dim underneath even at noon. Light comes down in shafts that have been strained green and gold through layers of leaf, and then is gone. The temperature doesn't drop with the light. It stays warm, close, an even bath of warmth that makes sweat bead and stay there. Condensation collects on the underside of every leaf and drips at its own pace, not rain, not quite mist.

And then the ground goes missing.

A rim appears without warning under the roots — a lip of raw, just-broken limestone, still jagged, still dusted with the powder of its own collapse. The sinkhole is recent. You can tell because nothing has smoothed it. The blocks that fell inward lie where they fell, their broken faces bright white against the older gray, their edges still sharp enough to cut. No vines have had time to pull them apart. No water has had time to round them. It is an embryonic place, a room the earth has only just made for itself, perhaps forty meters across, perhaps sixty, not large. A hidden pocket. From the forest floor you see only a darkening and a breath of cooler air rising, damp as a mouth.

Inside, the rules change.

The walls are vertical for a little while and then bell out, so the floor of the sink is wider than its mouth and the sky becomes a ragged oval, far away, framed in leaves. The walls weep. Not a spring, not a stream — the stone is porous and damp. Water never flows here so much as it appears. In the morning the walls are beaded with condensation that runs together and falls in infrequent, fat drops. After a heavy afternoon rain, which comes only every few days and then all at once, shallow pools will stand between the fallen blocks for a day or two, dark and still, full of tadpoles and wriggling larvae, and then sink back into the stone and vanish. The people who live here remember where each pocket will be.

Because people do live here.

It is a town, but not as you expect a town to spread. It is intimate, pocket-scale — a whole settlement fitted into a single room. That is its strangeness and its sense. From the rim you might take it at first for a tangle of roots and thatch, but there is order if you let your eyes adjust to the dim.

Houses are built on the fallen blocks and between them, lifted just off the damp floor on short stilts of unshaped sapwood. Walls are wattle and pale mud plaster, roofs are thatch so thick they look like the forest floor lifted up, stitched with living vine that keeps growing after it is tied. No nail, no wire, no glass. Everything is cut, woven, weighted, grown-together. No tool here leaves a mark more complex than a clean cut. And yet there is infrastructure: a narrow causeway of flat limestone slabs — still bright, still unsmoothed by feet — laid to keep feet out of the mud between the pools; a shared cistern where the largest block was hollowed by hand to catch the wall-drip; a small plaza where the canopy opens just enough for a patch of sky to lay a column of light down onto packed earth. You can read the town: kitchen gardens tucked into the sun-patches, sleeping platforms under deeper shade, a low wall around the edge of the central pool to keep children from it. It is urban in the way a village that has become a little city is urban — dense with intention, layered, everyone within calling distance, but still small enough that a single voice can cross it.

And it is sparse. That is the other thing that takes a moment to understand. For all that it is a town, it is not crowded. There is much space left empty on purpose. Between any two houses is a gap where a fern bank or a wild papaya or a stand of helicon has been allowed to stay, even encouraged. The street is as much garden as street. Lushness here does not mean choked. The vegetation is luxuriant where it is — great fronds unfurling from the wall-cracks, figs sending down rope-roots that will in time knit the raw stone together, moss thick as a mattress on the north side of every block — but it is interrupted, scattered, held at arm's length from the dwellings. People leave the emptiness because the forest presses in and because the pools need room to appear and because the animals need corridors.

And the animals are everywhere, though you would not know it by sound.

At first the sink feels silent. The forest above is not loud — no cicada roar — but here the stone walls swallow what little there is. What you hear after a minute is very quiet: the tick of a condensation drop hitting a leaf, the soft rustle of a gecko shifting on thatch, the almost inaudible flick of a fish in a pool turning over. Then you start to see them. Small brown frogs plated to the damp walls, throats pulsing. Anoles flashing green across the causeway stones. Swifts that nest in the wall-pockets, dropping in and out of the oval of sky without a cry. Ants in a black river along a fallen trunk, carrying leaf-circles bigger than themselves. In the largest pool, blind white fish that fell in when the sink opened and have stayed. Under the houses, a sow and piglets. Fruit bats folded like dry leaves under the overhang. Nothing is rare. Everything is simply here, abundant and ordinary, moving through its own business at a pace that is patient and utterly active.

Because for all its quiet, the place never stops.

While you stand there the light shifts a hand's breadth as the sun moves behind the rim. A vine at the wall's edge, which was reaching, finds purchase and curls. In the plaza a woman sweeps the earth with a broom of twigs, and the dust she raises hangs in the light-column before settling. In a pool, a water beetle seizes a tadpole — quick, efficient, without splash — and just beyond it a fern releases a cloud of spores so fine you only see them because they catch the light. Decay and growth happen at the same rate. Thatch rots at the edges and is patched with fresh palm. A block that looked stable develops a hairline crack as water finds a new seam and the stone, still raw and unlearned, gives a little. The whole pocket is still coming into being. The town knows it lives in a place that has not finished forming, and so it builds lightly, as if it might be asked to move a house a few feet to the left when the floor settles.

The air in the sink is its own weather. Warm, dim, smelling of wet stone and smoke and ripe fruit. You breathe it and your skin stays damp. If you stay until evening, the oval of sky goes lilac and then quickly dark, and the bats unfold and go up like smoke, and from the walls the frogs begin their soft, almost silent trilling — not a chorus, individual notes spaced far apart, sparse as the houses. Someone lights a small cookfire; the smoke rises straight up until it hits the rim and spills.

From above, you would never know. The forest closes over the lip with hanging roots, the thatch matches the leaf litter, the stone looks like stone. An ordinary place, not weird, not enchanted, simply a new room that the low, warm, damp earth opened, and that vines and frogs and people, with nothing but hands and growing things, have made into a town that fits exactly inside it, leaving room for the forest to keep being forest around it.
