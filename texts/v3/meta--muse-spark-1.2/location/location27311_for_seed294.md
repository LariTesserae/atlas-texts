It is high ground, but not dry ground.

You are high and deep at the same time. Far below you, the true abyss drops away into black, but here the seabed has risen into a vast tableland — a drowned plateau, a continent-shoulder lifted kilometers above the floor and still a kilometer under the surface. The water above you is not empty space, it is weight. It is a cold, dim, olive-black glass pressing down with the mass of a sky made liquid.

Light comes from a long way off. The sun is a rumor. What reaches this height is filtered to nothing — a diffuse, sourceless grey-blue dusk that never brightens and never quite goes dark. There are no shadows because there is no single source to cast them. Everything is seen as if through smoked glass, edges softened by particulate and distance. Twenty meters away and the world dissolves into haze. A hundred meters away is myth.

And it is cold. Not the sharp cold of air and wind, but the vast, patient, soaking cold of deep water that has never known summer. It does not bite, it permeates. Stone holds it, water holds it, your bones if you had bones here would hold it. It slows everything and preserves everything.

The plateau itself should be bare rock, but there is no bare rock left.

This is the first contradiction that resolves itself when you look longer: there is no natural substrate. Not anymore. The mineral underneath — a pale, ancient basalt, almost white with age — only shows in the places where something has fallen or been torn away. And even that stone is not young. This is a world at the end of erosion. Wind never touched it, but water did, for epochs. For millions of years currents combed over it until every edge that could be softened was softened, every hill planed down to a long, low swell, every valley broadened into a shallow bowl. The landform is primordial, tired, settled into its final shape. Cliffs at the plateau's rim do drop away sheer for hundreds of meters, but even their faces are smooth as worn teeth, rounded, pitted with slow solution holes, draped in silt like dust on an old statue.

Upon that ancient, flattened, high table, everything that stands has been built.

Not built as people build. There are no tools here, no joints, no cut stone, no metal, no light, no machines. There was never a hand to make these things. And yet the plain is a city.

It is a city made by mouths and secretions, by accretion.

The fauna do it. They are everywhere and they are the only architects.

At first you think the plain is empty because it is sparse and quiet, but then your eyes adjust to the dim and you see the density is not in numbers but in work. Scattered across the silted swell — a hundred meters between one and the next, then fifty, then two hundred — rise the structures.

They are mineral. Without plants — without any green, any frond, any algae film even — the palette is entirely animal-mineral: bone-white, rust-pink, iron grey, sulfur yellow. Great low domes like inverted bowls, twenty meters across, perforated with perfect round vents. Spiraled towers that lean slightly with the prevailing current, built layer upon layer of paper-thin calcium whorls, taller than a house but hollow and brittle. Terraced mounds stepped like ziggurats, each terrace fringed with a palisade of upright tubes, each tube occupied. Long walls, not straight but meandering for half a kilometer, waist-high, porous as sponge, wavering across a shallow valley and then stopping for no reason.

All of it is built, and all of it is alive, or was. No rock is just rock. Under the silt, the ground itself is a conglomerate of old constructions fused together — crushed domes and collapsed towers compacted into a new false bedrock, which is then built upon again.

And it is turbulent. This is not a ruin. This is not still.

For how ancient and still and quiet the water is, the plain seethes.

The builders are abundant, but you rarely see a single one clearly. They are small, pale, cold-water things — blind shrimp-like artisans the length of your forearm, colonies of worm-things that operate as one, heavy slow urchin-bearers that carry plates on their backs. They move without hurry but without stopping, in a constant, chaotic churn that makes the stillness feel like an illusion.

A tower on the ridge is being unbuilt even as you watch. A cloud of the shrimp-things swarms its flank, dissolving its wall with secreted acid and carrying the softened grain away pellet by pellet, while on the opposite side another swarm adds new whorls, widening it. The tower shivers. Part of it sloughs. A section collapses in utter silence into a puff of white mud that hangs for minutes before settling. Twenty meters away, a low dome pulses — its vents all exhaling at once, jetting silt, then inhaling, then exhaling — and around its base a terrace is rising, a new lip of cemented sand being laid down grain by grain by thousands of worms working in a rippling wave.

There is no plan. No colony coordinates with the next mound over. Walls are started and abandoned. Domes overgrow each other. A whole boulevard of parallel ridges on the eastern swell appears to be the result of two different species building toward each other until they collided and then kept building over the wreck. It is an anthill the size of a town, but without a queen, without a central logic — pure, frantic, local making. Creation and destruction are the same activity. The city is never finished because finishing would be death.

Sound does not carry this frenzy. Water swallows it. The whole plateau is mostly quiet in a way that feels pressurized. If you listen — if you could listen as these animals do — you hear only the faintest clicks and scrapes transmitted through stone, the soft hydraulic sigh of a dome venting, the occasional muted *tick* as a plate falls, the endless faint sand-rain hiss of particles drifting down from above. The turbulence is visual, tactile. You feel it as a tremor in the water, a sudden cold jet when a vent opens, a cloudiness that blooms and clears.

There are rules here, though no one wrote them:

1. Nothing that is made stays made.
2. Nothing that lives builds for itself alone; every structure becomes substrate within a generation.
3. The water keeps everything cold enough and dark enough that nothing rots quickly and nothing grows fast. Even collapse is slow-motion.

You can walk — or drift — for a long time across this high, drowned plain and see no repetition. A field of empty tube-stumps like a harvested forest. A solitary arch, perfectly formed, with nothing under it, being slowly buried. A pit where a dome imploded and is now a crater being lined with pink shell. The scale is regional; you can see three or four large forms at once through the haze, and know there are hundreds more beyond visibility, scattered like thoughts across the ancient, flattened skull of the plateau.

And at the edge, if you go to the rim, the built world simply stops. The last wall runs right to the lip where the stone, finally unbuilt, curves over and falls. Beyond is just the drop — the cold, dim, particle-filled water column falling away into deeper dark, with a slow, permanent current shearing up the cliff face, carrying the fine dust of all that endless, quiet, frantic building out into the abyss where nothing will ever use it.

Below you, the deep waits. Above you, the weight of the ocean hangs like a low sky. In between, on this high, old, lightless table, the pale city builds and unbuilds itself forever.
