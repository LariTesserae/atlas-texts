It is high. That is the first thing your body knows before your eyes can make sense of it.

The air is too thin to be kind. It comes in cool and clean and insufficient, with a faint metallic taste like sucking on a stone. You breathe a little faster without deciding to. Normal sunlight — a white-yellow sun at a comfortable distance — falls on you without warmth to back it up. At this elevation the light is undiluted, it has not been filtered through enough atmosphere to soften. Shadows are hard-edged and saturated, blue-black underneath. The sky directly overhead is a deeper blue than it should be for daylight, a blue that wants to become violet at the zenith, and pale almost to white at the horizons, because the horizons are impossibly far away.

You are standing on what was once a mountain range.

Not mountains anymore. This is what mountains become when they have finished being mountains. A continent-sized tableland, crumpled and then worn down for so long that geology has given up. Elevation 2.7 out of 3 and erosion 3 out of 3 — an ancient高原 that never came down. The world here lifted, and then stayed lifted while wind and time sanded it to its final form.

The terrain looks flat until you walk it. Then you understand it is not flat at all, but so vastly, gently undulating that your mind corrects it to flatness. Broad swells kilometers across, like the frozen swell of a stone ocean. Between them, shallow pans and labyrinthine yardangs, wind-carved ribs of rock that run in parallel for hundreds of kilometers, all facing the same way, like contour lines drawn by a dull blade. Everything is rounded. There are no sharp peaks left, no cliffs that haven't collapsed into slopes of scree that have themselves been polished to pebbles, then to grit. The rock is exposed everywhere — banded gneiss and basalt gone pink and dove-grey from eons of sun, salt-cemented conglomerate, fields of desert varnish so dark they look wet but are dry as bone.

There is no water here, not in any way that counts. At noon the ground is flaked with salt where brine tried to exist a billion years ago and failed. In the deepest, most shadowed wind-grooves, you might find a lace of frost that formed before dawn and will be gone by mid-morning, never melting, just sublimating straight back into the thin air. The chemistry of this place is dry mineral, not aqueous. The air has a faint, alkaline smell when a gust stirs the dust. If you lick your lips they crack.

And yet. Things grow.

Sparse, stubborn, low. The vegetation doesn't form meadows or even patches; it exists as single presences with vast emptiness between them, which is the logic of everything here. Black lichen spreads in coins on the north sides of stones, so dark it looks like scorch marks. Glass-grass — you learn to call it that because you have no other word — grows as a single transparent filament at a time, a hollow silica needle a meter tall that sways in wind you can barely feel, catching the sun and prisming it. Sometimes there are three or four in a square kilometer. They root in nothing visible; they seem to drink directly from the rock's static. No trees. No shrubs. Nothing that tries to be tall, because tall was worn away long ago.

The scale defeats your sense of distance. Light travels so far through the thin, dustless air that a ridge you think is a half-hour walk is three days away. The curvature of the planet is visible, not dramatic, just a faint bow to the world in every direction. Weather happens at the scale of countries. You can see a storm system being born two hundred kilometers off as a smudge that becomes a spiral by evening. The groundPlan is too big for proper weather to cross it quickly, so the plateau makes its own.

Because despite its age — because *in spite of* its settled, primordial stillness — this place is turbulent.

The ground is ancient, near-final. The air is hyperactive. The silence and the turbulence are the same phenomenon.

For a long time you think it is wind, but it isn't. The air is mostly quiet. That is the most unsettling quality. Sound: 0.1/3. Your boots crunch on salt gravel and the sound is too loud and then immediately eaten by the thinness. You can hear your own pulse. A glass-grass filament humming when it vibrates makes you turn your head, grateful for a tone. Otherwise, near-silence. A profound, high-altitude quiet that makes you aware of the pressure in your ears.

Motion, instead, happens silently. Overhead, the middle sky churns.

They are the built things. Scattered — you might walk for days and see only one — and far apart enough that the empty density of the place remains intact. They are not ruins, not really, though they look ruined because everything ancient does. They are the machines that were left running.

From a distance you mistake one for a natural spire, until you see it is too perfect. A needle of matte material, two or three hundred meters tall but only a few meters wide, standing alone on a swell with no foundation or road leading to it. Some lean. Some have fallen, and where they have fallen they have not shattered; they have telescoped into the dust like pushed nails. They do not shine. They absorb light a little. At their tips, the air misbehaves.

These are things beyond modern engineering, and they are still working, mindlessly, turbulently. They do not need to be understood to be seen. Each tower — Loom, Anvil, you will call them whatever you need — pulls dust and thin air and field into itself and exhales something else. One moment the sky above a Loom is clear. The next, a silent, boiling caul of interference forms around it, a lens of heatless distortion the size of a town that flickers through shapes faster than your eye can track: lattices, vortices, brief, impossible geometries that look like architecture trying to assemble itself and then rejecting itself. Dust devils spin up from nothing, sprint in straight lines for kilometers, vanish. A sheet of salt a hundred meters across will rise vertically from the ground like a curtain, hover, shatter into powder. A cloud, perfectly rectangular, will condense for twenty seconds at waist height and rain light before evaporating.

It never repeats. It's not weather, it's fabrication weather. A storm-state that creates and destroys without pause, without noise, without purpose you can discern. The plateau itself is stable — the rock has been settled for geological eternities — but the space a kilometer above it is an anthill, a bazaar, a Solaris of mute creation. The towers are not maintaining the land. They are just doing what they were made to do, which may have been to make reality more precise, or more interesting. Now they do it for an empty continent, for no audience, and they do it constantly. The only pattern is tempo: it gets more violent near dusk, as if the change in light angles gives them more to work with.

You learn to not stand close. Not because it is dangerous in an obvious way, but because to be near one during an event makes your compass spin and your teeth ache and the glass-grass near you sings too loud. The rules of the world are thin here. Gravity is a little lighter than it should be for rock this dense, or maybe you are just breathing shallow. If you throw a stone it stays aloft a fraction longer. Sunlight through a distortion field splits into two shadows for a second.

Animals are as rare as buildings. Fauna 0.9/3 means if you are lucky you see evidence. A track in salt dust, three-toed, pressed yesterday. A shape gliding so high it could be mistaken for ash, riding the turbulent columns — a kite-thing, translucent as the grass, with a wingspan wider than you are tall, that never lands but drinks static from the Loom fields. It will circle a tower for hours without ever flapping. When it calls, it is a pressure change rather than a sound, a faint pop in your sinuses. Once, in the lee of a yardang, you find a living thing that is not plant or animal: a slow, colonial crust that spreads millimeters per year, that retracts when your shadow falls on it, as if photosensitive. It is not afraid of you. It just notices.

At night the cool comes in earnest. The temperature was never warm — cool enough that you keep your jacket zipped even in direct sun — but after sunset it drops to a still, brittle cold. The sun, normal and honest, sets without color because there is so little dust to catch it; it just thins and is gone, leaving that deep, star-clotted violet. The towers do not stop. In darkness their work becomes visible as pale auroras that crawl between them, silent lightning that branches without thunder, brief fountains of cold fire that write themselves on the sky and erase.

And the plateau remains. Ancient, vast, empty, holding it all.

You can walk for a week in any direction and see no change except the arrangement of ribs and salt pans, the occasional lichen coin, the occasional needle on the horizon that makes the sky boil. The erosional forms are so old they have become a kind of equilibrium, a final form that will not change in your lifetime or a hundred thousand. And above them, the turbulence never rests — a frantic, creative, silent storm of post-physical engineering, churning in place like thought over a body in deep sleep.

It is a desert that finished becoming a desert a long time ago, a mountain that survived its own wearing-down, a laboratory left on with no experimenter, too high and too dry and too old to be for you, and still, for as long as you are there, entirely, utterly present around you.
