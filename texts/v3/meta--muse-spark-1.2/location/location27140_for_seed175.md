It is high up, but not in the way that high usually means cold.

You climb a long time to get here — not a peak, but a shoulder. The world below falls away into heat-haze and you expect the air to thin and turn to knife-edge, but it doesn't. It stays warm. It holds. The stone itself holds heat like a fired pot left in the sun.

The place is not large. You could walk its rim in an afternoon if you wanted to, but you won't want to walk quickly. It is a single place, with edges — a shallow bowl cut into the top of the mesa, as if a great thumb had pressed down while the rock was still soft, and then the rock remembered that shape for a very long time.

That is the first thing you notice about the rock: how old it looks without looking broken. The erosion here is not dramatic. Nothing is collapsing. The cliffs that ring the bowl are not sheer fresh scars; they are rounded, lipped, weathered into themselves. Water has been finding the same paths for so long that the paths have become anatomy. Vertical channels, smooth as throats, run from the rim to the floor. Ledges have softened into stairs that no one built. Hollows under the rim where the wind has worried at a softer layer for a thousand thousand wet seasons, until they became long, low galleries, cool and stained dark with mineral bloom. Everything is in equilibrium. The water knows where to go, the stone knows where to let it.

And there is so much water for a place so high.

It doesn't come as a river. It comes as presence. The air itself is close to condensing — warm, saturated, heavy in the late afternoon. You breathe it and taste stone and green. Clouds snag on the mesa's edge and do not so much rain as exhale. They settle into the bowl and become mist, and then the mist becomes beads on every surface. The rock weeps.

You can hear it if you stand still long enough, which is easy, because there is nothing else to hear. On the cliffs, the seeps find their old grooves and run. Not in torrents, but in slow, clear sheets that catch the normal, even light and carry it downward. Where the galleries undercut the rim, water gathers at the lip and thickens before it falls — and sometimes it doesn't fall at all. This is the strangeness you only see after your eyes adjust.

In the stillest part of the bowl, where the air barely moves, the drips hang. A line of water beads along the underside of a sandstone ledge, each droplet swollen, elongated, trembling but not dropping. They catch light and hold it. They can stay there for minutes, for an hour. The humidity is so perfect, the air so warm and motionless, that evaporation and condensation have come into a balance so precise that water can simply wait. When a drop finally does let go, it falls straight and slow through warm air and strikes the moss below without a splash — a soft *tok*, absorbed instantly.

Below the cliffs, the floor of the bowl is not flat. It slumps gently toward the center, and the ground is not ground as you expect. It is a mat, a sponge. Peat and sandstone grit and living green in a layer thick enough to give under your foot and then spring back. Water is right under the surface everywhere, sometimes at the surface. You step and water rises around the edge of your sole without ever making a puddle that flows. In places it wells up clear between mosses and sits there, a shallow lens that reflects the sky and then, slowly, sinks back down.

Vegetation here is lush but not crowded. That is the other balance. Everything grows with great vigor — ferns with fronds as wide as your forearm, bottle-brush mosses that rise in deep, cushioned hummocks, reeds with stems flecked red, low trees with smooth, pale bark that peel in papery sheets — but they do not choke each other. They are spaced as if by agreement. A clump of fern, then ten feet of open, gleaming wet rock with only a film of algae, then a single standing tree, its roots spread wide and shallow over the sponge, holding a circle of shade. Then again, empty space. The lushness is intense where it is, and absent where it isn't, and the emptiness in between feels deliberate, swept clean. Light comes down even and full, white-gold through the thin high mist, and in the open spaces you can see the veins of the stone, the dark waterlines, the tiny terraces built by centuries of mineral deposit, each no taller than a fingernail.

There are no animals.

It takes a while to understand what that means. At first you keep listening for the insect stitching that should be here in this warmth, this wet — the drone of flies, the snap of a lizard. There is nothing. No birds pass over the bowl. No water-boatmen dimple the lenses. The ferns are un-nibbled, perfect. When a frond dies it just darkens and softens and is taken back by the mat, without interruption. The silence that results is not empty. It is a full, warm silence. The only sounds are the ones the place makes itself: the faint tick of a hanging drop finally falling, the almost inaudible sigh of water moving through moss, the slow exhale of cloud against stone. Your own breath sounds loud in it. Your own footstep, when you lift your boot and the sponge releases it, makes a soft sucking kiss that seems intrusive.

And yet someone has been here.

Not lived here. No houses, no walls, no fire rings that last. Nothing built. But at the edge of one of the open stone sheets, where a seep has left a smooth apron of silt, there are flakes.

Three, four stones. Not natural. One is a core of dark chert, palm-sized, with the curve of percussive removal still visible — the bulb where it was struck. Beside it, two sharp-edged flakes, left where they fell. And a third, a flake that was picked up and used, its edge brightly chipped from scraping something soft, perhaps a reed stem. They are not fresh. The edges have a faint milkiness where water has moved over them for season after season. The silt has half-swallowed them. Lichen, that strange glassy lichen that grows here in flat, translucent discs the color of river ice, has started to grow over one corner.

No one stays. Or if they do, they do not stay long enough to leave more than this: the ability to make an edge, to cut, to carry away. A hand's work, primitive, manual, and then gone. The place does not keep the mark any more than it keeps a footprint in the sponge. An hour after you leave your print, water will have risen and erased it.

If you sit at the center where the bowl is deepest, where the water lenses are clearest, you can see the weirdness double. The sky is reflected, but so is the underside of the ledges. The hanging drops reflect the moss. Light moves slowly here, diffused by mist, so there are no hard shadows. It feels as if the bowl is not entirely open to the sky but sealed — a room with no roof, a grove with no trees at its edge. Time moves slowly, visibly. You can watch a fern unroll over the course of an afternoon if you are patient. You can watch a mineral terrace grow a faint new line at its rim, white calcite precipitating out of the seep as it breathes. Nothing hurries. Water cycles from rock to air to rock. Green grows, darkens, dissolves. Stone softens. The flakes sit.

You will warm in the sun, and damp will condense on your skin, and the silence will keep holding you, and you will understand that this place has been like this for a very long time before any hand struck any stone, and will be like this for a very long time after, not because it is unchanging but because its changes are so slow and so settled that they look, to anything hurried, like stillness itself.
