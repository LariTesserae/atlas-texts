You don't find it on a map. You find it because the ground gives way.

Out on the low plain — that flat, black scrubland that stretches hot and empty for miles — the basalt suddenly drops. Not a valley, not a canyon. A pocket. A room cut straight down into the earth, as if someone took a cookie cutter to the young rock and lifted a plug out.

The edges are wrong for how old this country should look. This is young ground. The basalt hasn't learned to be smooth yet. The lip of the hollow is sharp enough to cut your hand, a fresh fracture of columnar stone that still holds the glossy black of its cooling. No river made this. It was blasted, or collapsed, or quarried and then abandoned — a perfect rectangular sink, maybe thirty meters long and half that wide, sunk three meters down into the lowland. From the plain you could walk within ten feet of it and miss it entirely. The heat-haze hides it.

And it is hot.

The air over the plain is already thick, midday white sun hammering straight down with normal, unfiltered light that makes everything stark and shadowless. But down in the pocket, the heat doubles. It collects. It doesn't move. The sun hits the dark rock and the shallow water and comes back up as steam.

Because the pocket is wet. Improbably, unreasonably wet for a low hot flat like this.

Water is everywhere, but not as a lake. It doesn't pool so much as it *perspires*. It weeps straight out of the sharp walls. The basalt is porous in bands, and you can see the dark tide-lines where the water bleeds out of the stone itself, countless cold beads forming on hot rock, swelling, and running down in slow, braided threads. It drips from the undersides of every ledge. It condenses on any surface cool enough to hold it — which, down here, is nothing but the metal. The air is so saturated that your skin never dries. Your breath comes back at you. Every inhalation tastes like hot iron and wet stone.

The floor of the pocket is not ground anymore. It's a thin, uneven pan of concrete, poured years ago and already cracked by heat and heave, but still holding. On top of the concrete is a skin of water — an inch, two inches deep — warm as a bath, covering everything. And on top of the water, a crust. Where the water has been evaporating and re-condensing for years, it has left its minerals behind: a pale, puffy travertine, mustard-white and rust-pink, building in lipped terraces and tiny, sharp-rimmed pools. The formations are young, fragile, razor-edged. They haven't had time to be worn round. If you step, they crunch like thin ice. The whole place is in the act of becoming a hot spring, but it hasn't decided to be one yet. It's still construction, still scaffolding.

That’s where the built things are.

There isn't much. The density is wrong for a factory — too empty. Scattered, the way things look when a single crew left in a hurry and never came back to clean up. One pumphouse at the far end, cinderblock, flat-roofed, its door gone. Inside it, a diesel engine and an electric pump, still running. It has been running for a long time. A fat, insulated pipe comes up out of the floor inside the house, turns at a right angle on rusted hangers, runs the length of the pocket, and then goes back down into the water at the other end. It sweats, it shivers, it ticks with heat expansion. Next to it, a smaller, newer pipe coughs a constant jet of scalding vapor into the air.

There are catwalks — just two, half-collapsed, made of open steel grating that is now slick with lime. A single utility pole, leaning, its wires long since cut, but its transformer still humming so loudly you feel it in your molars. A concrete spillway, stained black with algae. No fences. No signs. Just these few industrial artifacts, too spaced out, too loud for how small they are.

Because it is loud here.

You expect a hidden place to be quiet. This one isn't. The pumphouse engine thunders in the enclosed stone room, the sound trapped and amplified by the hard, close walls. The water hisses where it hits hot metal. The vapor jet screams — a constant, high-pressure shriek that never varies. The overflow drain at the low corner gurgles and slaps. Even the dripping has a volume to it, a thousand separate taps and plinks off stone and steel and shallow water. You have to raise your voice to hear yourself think, and after a minute you stop trying. The sound makes the pocket feel smaller than it is, intimate and pressurized, like being inside a kettle.

There is almost no life to soften it.

No birds come down into the steam. No insects skim the water. It is too hot, too loud, too mineral. The absence is complete — not even a fly. The only green is sparse and stubborn and strange. A few clumps of wiry, salt-tolerant grass pushed up through cracks in the concrete, bleached at the tips. A smear of dark olive algae on the north wall where the weeping is heaviest. Two ferns, no taller than your hand, growing directly out of the lime crust near the vapor jet, their fronds curled tight against the heat, furred with mineral dust. They shouldn't be there, and they barely are. The rest is bare: wet stone, wet concrete, wet steel, and the white mineral lace.

Nothing moves fast. This is a slow place, despite the roar. The water seems to boil, but it isn't boiling — it's just that hot. The travertine grows by millimeters a year, a new rim added each season. The rust advances across the catwalk in patient flakes. The basalt weeps at the same rate it did last summer. Even the engine, for all its noise, is doing the same work it did yesterday: lifting the same hot water out of the ground, running it through the same loop, spilling it back onto the same floor to evaporate again. A closed circle. The heat, the wet, the loudness — they hold the pocket in stasis. It isn't going anywhere. It is just being itself, intently, in secret.

If you sit on the sharp lip with your legs dangling into the steam, the plain at your back flat and shimmering and empty for miles, you feel the rules of this little room assert themselves:

One: water comes out of the rock forever. Two: the sun will always be straight overhead here, because the walls are too low to make real shade. Three: nothing that lives and moves will stay.

You get up and your footprints fill instantly behind you. The water smooths. The engine keeps hammering. The rocks keep weeping.
