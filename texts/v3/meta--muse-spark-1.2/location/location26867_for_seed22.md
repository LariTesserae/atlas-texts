It is low country, but not flat.

A fresh basin, a place that hasn't learned how to be old yet. The land sits low and wide under a dim sky, maybe a thousand feet above sea level if there is a sea — warm air pooled and held there like breath in cupped hands. The geology is young, sharp, insulted. Some recent violence — a rift opening, a flood basalt that cooled too fast, an uplift that hasn't had time to soften — has left the earth in sheets and blades. Black and iron-red stone stands in unweathered edges, stair-stepped mesas with crisp corners, cracks still clean as broken pottery, fissures thirty feet deep where the ground simply pulled apart and decided to stay that way. There has been no time for rivers to carve it smooth, no centuries of rain to round it. Instead water finds the cracks and sits there.

Because it is damp, not wet. This is not a swamp or a coastline. The basin should be dry by its size, but it isn't. The air itself is the water. Warm, about late-summer warm, the kind of warmth that makes things grow without ever making you sweat. Moisture beads on everything. At dawn — though dawn is hard to tell — condensation runs down the sharp basalt faces in dark threads, gathers in the stone basins, drips. In the deeper fissures there are pockets, clean and still: a pool not larger than a room, black as glass, holding a perfect reflection of the stone above it for days until it evaporates. Then a slow afternoon rain will come, not a storm, just a passing weight that lays itself down for twenty minutes and vanishes, leaving everything steaming and beaded again. You hear it hissing on the warm rock. There are no rivers here, only this habit of wetness that comes and goes.

And yet it is lush. Unreasonably lush for a place with so little standing water.

The vegetation has solved the problem of emptiness by becoming enormous and far apart. This is not a jungle's press of green. This is a cathedral forest scaled for a valley the size of a small country. From any one of those sharp ridges you can see for miles across the basin, and that is the scale of it — regional, open, multi-featured. You see three, four kinds of country at once. The stone terraces, the vine-draped towers, the meadow-like flats where something like grass grows chest high.

Each plant commands vast space. Trees — if you call them trees — rise on trunks as thick as houses, but there may be half a mile between one and the next. Their canopies are wide, flat, dark green-black, spreading a hundred yards across, dimming the already dim light even further. Between them, the air is empty. That's the rule of this place: an overwhelming amount of nothing per volume. The basin is hollow, vaulted. You can stand under one of those canopy disks and look up and see only struts of light where condensation catches, and otherwise just warm, empty air that goes up and up. The forest does not crowd. It punctuates.

In that emptiness, the vines do the work. They have learned to use emptiness as structure. Enormous lianas, as thick as your thigh, leave one tree and throw themselves across the void to the next, sagging in hundred-foot catenaries, sprouting curtains of heart-shaped leaves and air roots that never touch the ground. They hang there, damp, dripping. Below, the ground is covered in a dense, springy mat — mosses and ferns and broad-leafed creepers that thrive on condensation drip. Everything glistens. Everything smells of warm stone and crushed green and distant rain.

It should be silent, this emptiness. It is deafening.

First, because it is full of animals.

There is almost no place without movement, despite how empty the volume feels. The air between the great trees is not empty at all if you look. It is thick with fauna. Clouds of things — pale moths the size of your hand, beetles with mirrored backs, ribbon-tailed birds that seem to be made mostly of tail — move in constant, churning strata. They rise off the fern mat at dusk in such numbers they darken the dim light a fraction more. They roost in the vine curtains and set them shaking. Higher up, larger things glide. Something like a manta, but translucent and silent except for the displacement of air, circles slowly between the canopies, filtering the swarms. On the stone terraces, lizards with skin the color of cooling lava skitter and stop, skitter and stop. In the fissure pools, amphibians chorus. The predators are not rare either — you will see the quick, brutal interruption, a snap, a swirl of wings, and then the cloud re-forms, undiminished. It is an active place, always mid-cycle. Eating, being eaten, molting, blooming. The forest floor trembles with insects turning leaf litter into soil that hasn't existed long enough to be deep.

And the sound is not just them. That would be loud enough — the high, cicada-like sawing that never stops, layered ten deep, the croaks, the whirs, the sudden clattering ascent of a thousand wings at once. But there is a second source, larger and constant.

The city.

Because there is a city here, though from a distance you might mistake it for more vegetation. It is urban in scale and intent — not a ruin, not a few towers, but a settlement that was meant to hold a population in the millions, organized, infrastructural, layered. But it is almost entirely empty. That is the other kind of emptiness: the hollow city.

It does not sit on the ground. Much of it hangs.

The builders — who are not here, or who are here but not visible — did not understand weight the way we do. Their technology is beyond weather or concrete or steel. The structures are a kind of grown lattice, a matte black and pearl-gray material that looks like stone until you see it flex slightly with the warm wind. They are anchored into the sharp young rock at only a few points, and from those points they extrude, branch, cantilever impossibly. Platforms the size of city blocks float a few feet above the fern mat without columns. Towers lean at angles that should fall, held by fields you can feel as a faint pressure in your molars. Streets braid into the air and become bridges that become scaffolds that become open frameworks where vines now grow straight through them. There are levels and underlevels, transit tubes that are empty and clean and humming, doorways that open to a twenty-foot drop into empty air because the thing that used to dock there is gone.

It is all intact. It is all hollow. You can walk for a long time through a boulevard wide enough for a parade, flanked by buildings that could house thousands, and hear only your own feet, and the vines dripping, and the everywhere-sound.

The sound of the city itself is the loudest thing. The technology that holds it up is not silent. Each floating platform, each suspended street, emits a low, resonant tone — not a machine noise, but a held chord, as if the air inside the field is being stretched. Together across the basin, dozens of these structures create a beating, harmonic roar. In some places it is a perfect fifth that makes the water in the fissure pools ripple. Where two fields overlap the interference creates a physical vibration in your chest. It never stops. It is warm-weather infrastructure, idling forever, waiting for a density of people who never arrived, or who left. The animals use it. Some of the birds have learned to pitch their calls to the gaps in the chord. Some insects cluster on the undersides of the floating streets for the warmth the field gives off.

The light never gets bright. The sky above the basin is a uniform, low, pearl-gray — not quite overcast, not quite a dome. Maybe the canopy disks are part of it; maybe the city's fields diffuse the sun. It is dim enough that colors lose their edge, but warm enough that the dimness feels like evening in a greenhouse. Shadows are soft. The far ridges of the basin fade into haze, not darkness. At night it does not get much darker — the lattice itself gives off a faint, sourceless glow along its edges, like the afterimage of a light, and some of the fauna carry their own bioluminescence. The vine curtains glitter.

And there are the strange things, the places where the rules slip.

Gravity is not reliable near the older lattice structures. Water drips upward for a few inches before remembering to fall. A seed pod will hang motionless in midair for a full minute before drifting sideways. Time feels slightly wrong under the largest floating platform — sounds arrive a little late, your own breath seems to echo before you exhale. The vines that have grown through the lattice sometimes grow *inside* the material, their leaves appearing on the other side of a solid beam without a hole. And at the center of the basin, where the young rock is sharpest and freshest — a recent upthrust, still warm to the touch — there is a place where the city's lattice attempted to bridge a fissure and simply... stopped. The structure ends in midair in a clean, melted-looking terminus, and beyond it the air shimmers like heat haze, though the air is not hot enough for that. If you throw a stone through it, you hear it land twice.

Nobody maintains it. Nobody needs to. The stone stays sharp, the condensation keeps beading, the loud chord holds the streets aloft, the birds keep filtering the air between the vast, spaced-out trees, the pools fill and empty and fill again. It is a young, warm, dim, wet, loud, living, empty city in a forest that learned to be a city and a forest that learned to be a sky. You can stand on a floating avenue, with nothing solid for a hundred yards in any direction except ferns and air and stone and vine, and feel the whole valley breathing around you — plant, animal, field, stone — all in motion, all waiting.
