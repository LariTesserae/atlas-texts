This is the High Sinter.

You come up onto it without realizing you have been climbing. There is no slope to mark the ascent, no tree line to cross, no thinning of air that you notice until your lungs are already working a little too hard. The world simply rises until it is the sky. A plateau so broad its edges are a matter of theory. You can see the curve of the planet in the way the far plain lifts, just slightly, against the light.

It is hot. Not a desert hot that presses down, but a high hot that comes at you clean and vertical. The sun is normal, white-yellow and at a proper angle, throwing proper shadows — but the air is too thin to soften it. Light arrives unfiltered. It strikes the ground and comes back up again. Everything is illuminated twice. Shadows are hard-edged and strangely short, as if the light is coming from slightly above everywhere at once.

There is no water here. Not hidden, not seasonal, not locked in stone. This is not a dry place that remembers rivers. This has never had them. The ground is not sand. It is mineral in its final form.

A pavement of pale sinter and salt and iron-stained basalt, fused by heat and time into a single continuous floor. In places it is bone-white, cracked into polygons the size of houses, each plate cupped slightly upward at the edges like drying paper. The cracks are not filled with dust but with a darker mineral, a glassy black where heat has welded it shut again. In other places the floor buckles into yardangs — long, keeled ridges carved by a wind that has had millions of years to learn its work. They are not dunes. They do not move. They are the bedrock itself, weathered into the shape of wind. Established. Settled. Some are as high as cliffs, their flanks fluted and scalloped, their crests sharpened to a knife-edge that sings when the wind crosses it. Between them, the plain stretches flat and empty for kilometers at a time. You could walk for an hour and the only vertical is the heat shimmer.

Nothing grows. Not even the things that pretend to be plants. No lichen on the rock, no thorn scrub in the lee of the ridges. The surface is too hot and too clean, the salt too pure. Where the stone has spalled, it exposes fresh crystals that glitter like frost and never melt. The wind polishes everything to a faint, industrial sheen.

And it is empty. Profoundly, geographically empty. The scale defeats counting. A single yardang might run ten kilometers before it tapers and gives way to another. The horizon is far enough away that you see weather that will never reach you. There is so much space between one thing and the next that your eye invents things to fill it. Which is part of the wrongness — because the distances do not behave.

If you stare at a ridge two valleys over, it sometimes arrives too soon. Or you walk toward a white salt flat and it recedes without getting smaller. The perspective stutters. The sky, which should be a dome, has seams in it. High up, where the blue should simply deepen, there are faint, horizontal striations, like the air has been laminated. Light coming through them doubles. You see a second, fainter sun a few degrees off from the real one that is not a reflection. Shadows occasionally fall in two directions at once before correcting themselves with a soft, almost audible *click*. Gravity is consistent enough to walk, but if you drop a shard of sinter it falls just a fraction too slowly, as if it considers the decision on the way down. This place has existed long enough to wear its physics smooth at the edges.

For a long time you think you are alone. The emptiness is so complete it feels like a rule.

Then you hear them. Or rather, you have been hearing them the whole time and your brain finally categorized the sound correctly.

It is loud. Not deafening at the source, but loud in the way a distant foundry is loud — a sound that has no single point and therefore cannot be escaped. A low, rolling throb that lives in the stone under your feet, a counterpoint of higher hissing and clanking that rides the wind.

The fauna do not emerge. They were already there. You simply learn to see them against the scale.

They are huge and they are far apart, which is why the plain can be both empty and overwhelming. One to a valley, sometimes one to three valleys. But each one *is* a valley.

At first you mistake one for a yardang — a long, dark mass lying along the wind axis, as long as a freight train, as high as a house. Then it breathes, and a plume of superheated air and fine basalt dust vents from a row of gill-slits along its back with a shriek that becomes a whistle that becomes a sigh. The sound is not animal. It is industrial.

They are not animals wearing machines. They *are* the machines. The biology here never bothered with softness. Their bodies are pressure vessels and forges.

The commonest kind are the Striders — low, many-legged things like ironclad millipedes the length of bridges. Their legs are articulated pistons, each joint sleeved in overlapping plates of sintered silica that they secrete and reabsorb. When they walk — and they walk very, very slowly — each step is a cycle: *hiss-clank-settle-hiss*. Their feet fuse briefly with the salt pan and then release it, leaving perfect, steaming piston-prints that cool into glass. On their backs, their spines are open. You can see into them. Chambers pump and glow. Something like a heart, but it is a boiler, hammering dull red, driving blood that is not blood but a slurry of molten mineral. Chimney throats along their flanks vent excess heat in shivering mirages. They graze, if you can call it that, by lowering a broad, shovel-like head and *sintering* — melting the salt crust with radiant heat and drawing the mineral melt up through a furnace mouth. They excrete behind them not waste but finished material: long, cooling ingots of black glass and extruded coils of iron wire that lie steaming on the plain and will remain there for centuries.

Higher up, rarer, are the Bellows. They do not walk. They anchor themselves on the ridge crests, vast and sessile, like manta rays made of riveted canvas and ceramic lung. Their wings — if they are wings — are not for flying in this thin air but for catching wind. They inflate, hundreds of meters across, translucent and veined with glowing filaments, and they billow and boom. Each inflation takes an hour. Each deflation drives air through internal turbines you can hear whining even at a distance of five kilometers. They are power stations. They live on wind and temperature differential and they sing, a deep organ chord that vibrates your teeth.

There are others. Smaller, faster things like shuttles of brass and chitin that swarm briefly around a Strider’s vent to steal heat, rattling like loose bolts in a can. Things that look like tumbleweeds of cooling fins that roll for years without stopping.

None of them built anything. There are no nests, no hives, no factories, no roads between. The tech is not applied to the world; it *is* the world’s way of being alive. No hands made these pistons and boilers — they grew that way because in a place with no water, no plant, no soft chemistry, the only viable metabolism is a mineral one, a lithotrophic industry. They smelt themselves as they live.

And they are overwhelming not because they crowd you, but because each one redefines the scale around it. When a Strider exhales, the wind changes direction for half a kilometer. When a Bellows inflates, the light dims as its shadow, impossibly large and sharp, slides across three valleys. The air smells of hot iron and ozone and salt becoming glass. The sound never stops. Even when nothing moves, the plain ticks and pings as it cools and contracts, as if the plateau itself were a cooling ingot.

The dynamic is slow. This is not a place of chase or bloom. A Strider might take a full day to cross the flat between two yardangs. A Bellows might beat its wings twice between noon and dusk. The ingots they leave behind will be slowly buried by salt efflorescence, then exposed again a thousand years later by wind, still warm to the touch. The ridges will keep their shape for another million years. The double sun in the laminated sky will drift a degree every century.

The rule here seems to be: *what persists is permitted to become more itself*. Given time and heat and no water to interfere, stone learned to pump, salt learned to flow like blood, wind learned to be inhaled. Reality, stretched thin at this height and worn smooth by erosion, learned to stutter and duplicate without breaking.

You can stand on the white polygons between the tracks and feel all of it at once — the thin hot light on your shoulders, the immense emptiness that makes your own breathing sound industrial by comparison, the floor humming through your boots, the far-off shriek-whistle of something venting on a ridge you cannot quite bring into focus.

Nothing here will ever notice you. There is too much space, and too much slow, loud, patient work to be done.
