It is not a place you find by walking toward it. You find it by dropping out of the world.

Out on the mineral plain — a low, flat white that goes to every horizon without a hill or a rise to break it — there is a flaw. A seam. The salt crust, which everywhere else is a hard, blistered pavement, dimples here. A shallow saucer, no more than a meter deep, twenty across, as if something very heavy had once sat there and then been lifted away. The wind comes across the pan cool and thin and absolutely dry, and it does not eddy in this saucer, it falls in.

At the center of the saucer there is a mouth. Not a hole that was eroded. A hole that was made. Perfectly round, shoulder-width, its lip bound over with a dense, turned-over braid.

You have to get on your knees to see down into it. You have to put your hands on the braid.

Everything from here down is made.

Not built in the way that implies hands and tools and cutting. There are no tool marks, no right angles, no nails or mortar or glass or wire. No metal at all, no plastic, no worked stone. It is built the way a nest is built, or a cocoon, or a fruit. A slow secretion and weaving. The material is a fiber that is not quite wood and not quite reed — pale, grey-tan, as thick as a finger, as fine as a hair in places, all of it cured to a dry, cool hardness. It has been packed.

That is the first law of this pocket: there is no empty space that has not been occupied on purpose. The density is absolute. The walls of the chamber below are not walls the way a room has walls — they are a thatch so tight, a weave so close, that you cannot slip a fingernail between the strands. Warp and weft and then another layer over it, and another, and another, cross-hatched, coiled, bundled, stitched through with finer filaments that cinch everything drum-tight. Even the floor is not ground. Even the floor is woven. You are not standing on earth. The salt pan is gone. The natural substrate ends at the lip of the mouth. Everything under that is construction, continuous, without seam between floor and wall and ceiling. A single closed vessel, sunk like a seed into the low place of the plain.

It is small. It is a hidden scale. You think of landscapes as things you look out across; this is a landscape you look *inside*. Four long strides across if you could stretch out, but you can't, quite — the ceiling bows low. The tallest point is just high enough to sit upright in the center. To be inside is to be held. The edges curve in and meet you. It is an intimate room. An organ.

And it is bright.

The braid is not opaque. The fibers, for all their packing, have cured to a kind of translucency, like horn or old paper, and the light of the white pan above comes through everywhere at once. No shadows, no single sunbeam. A diffuse, high, cool white light that seems to originate from the weave itself. Every strand glows at its edge. The whole interior holds light the way a lantern holds light — bright enough that you squint at first, bright enough to see the dust.

Except there is no dust moving. This is the second law: nothing changes here.

The air is cool — not cold, but the steady, cellar-cool of deep shade on a dry day. You expect a bright place like this to be hot. It isn't. The light has no heat in it. The air is utterly dry, a mineral dryness that catches at the back of your throat and makes your lips feel immediately chapped. If you breathe in, you taste salt and old cellulose and nothing else. No damp, no rot, no water. What little water ever was here is gone, bound up as a faint white efflorescence in the deepest crevices of the weave, a tracer of brine that dried years or centuries ago and never came back. The fibers themselves have a faint salty crust where they cross. They are seasoned. They are in equilibrium.

You can see how old and settled it is. This is not new thatch. The outer layers where the light is strongest have been polished by time and wind and light itself to a smooth, almost waxy sheen. The edges of the weave are rounded, softened. No fraying, no sharp ends. The whole chamber has the slightly sagging, self-consistent shape of something that has stood for so long it has stopped deciding what shape to be. Erosion here did not wear it away — it perfected it. It brought it to balance and left it there.

There is vegetation, but only as a guest in the construction. It did not make this place, it lives in it sparely, the way lichen lives on a fence. In the dimmer interstices near the floor, where the braid tightens into knots, there are flat rosettes of grey-green lichen, dry and crisp, no bigger than a coin. A few wiry air-plants like rust-colored wire have anchored themselves to the wall, their roots not roots at all but holdfasts gripping the weave, their leaves curled tight against desiccation. They are not growing. They are not dying. Like everything else here, they are paused. They have been sparse and patient for a very long time.

There is no animal life. No insects stitching the air, no beetles in the fibers, no bones, no droppings, no hum of anything living. If something made this — and something did, with an instinct older than hands — it is gone, and nothing has replaced it.

And it is loud.

That is what stays with you. From outside, the wind over the pan is a thin, cool hiss. Down inside the pocket, that same wind is caught and amplified by the perfect, hard, woven dome. It does not gust. It does not vary. Because the day does not vary — no clouds, no thermal shift, no weather — the air moves at one constant pressure over the mouth and the chamber resonates. It is a single, dense chord, low and high at once: the deep thrumming of air across the bottle-mouth above you, and the higher, papery buzz of a thousand taut fibers vibrating sympathetically, like a huge, dry harp strung too tight. It is loud enough you have to raise your voice to hear yourself, loud enough you feel it in your sternum and teeth, and it never changes. It has no attack, no decay. You could close your eyes and you would not be able to tell if you had been here for a minute or an hour. The sound is time-invariant. It was here when you arrived and it will be here exactly the same after you leave.

If you sit in the center and put your palm flat to the woven floor, you feel both the coolness and the faint tremor of that sound coming up through the packed fibers. The light is everywhere, the weave is everywhere, the cool dry air is everywhere. There is no corner that was forgotten, no patch of raw earth left untouched. It is a total environment, made and then finished, sealed in its own stillness.

On the way out you will see that the mouth does not close. It does not need to. Nothing enters that would disturb it. Nothing leaves.
