It is a low place.

Not low like a valley — low like the world decided to sink here and never came back up. A continent-sized basin, so shallow and so wide you don't see the rim, you only feel it in the air pressure. The horizon doesn't fall away, it just thins into brightness. If you stand on any of its upper leaves and look out, the green goes flat and true to the edge of sight, unbroken. The sky is too close.

Everything here was built.

That is the first thing you understand when the brightness resolves. There is no ground underneath. No bedrock, no soil in the natural sense. What you walk on — if you can find a place to walk — is floor. Poured aggregate and iron plate and composite sheeting, kilometers thick, laid down in levels, weathered now to a softness at the edges. Rivet heads worn down to dull coins. Expansion joints filled with centuries of compressed fiber. Drainage grates with nothing left to drain. The world is a factory floor the size of a sea, stepped in shallow terraces a meter or two high that run for hundreds of kilometers before they drop again. Established. Settled into itself. Nothing sharp, nothing new. Even the rust has been rusted over.

And on it, in it, through it, *as* it — the plant.

There is no water here.

Not in any form you would recognize as water. No rivers. No lakes. No standing pools, no mud, no damp. The air is cool — a dry, even, early-autumn cool that never warms and never truly chills — and it is utterly spare. If you breathe in deep you taste dust and cellulose and hot iron and a faint metallic tang like old paper. The vegetation does not want water. It never did.

This is not a forest that overgrew a ruin. The distinction failed a long time ago. The plants *are* the ruin, and the ruin *is* the plant.

They are dry plants. Pale, fibrous, engineered things that learned to live on light and air alone. Imagine a kudzu bred with scaffolding, with cotton-mill felt, with mycelial circuit board. Stems as thick as your waist, as thick as a house, but light, bulbous, ribbed like insect legs and hollow. They creak rather than sway. Leaves like sheets of vellum, or bleached canvas, or thin-pressed fiberboard — vast, translucent, papery, overlapping in dense thatchworks that fill the entire volume from floor to sky. Some are green, but a dusty, airless green, almost grey. Others have gone straw-blonde or iron-white from the endless brightness. They don't photosynthesize with water. They fix carbon straight from the cool, thin air and drink light. Their roots are not roots; they are holdfasts and tendrils and cable-runners that have bored into the built substrate, split the concrete, threaded through the gantries and the pipe chases and the rebar, drinking mineral, drinking static.

You push through a wall of leaves and find it *is* a wall. Load-bearing. A column that was once a support pylon is now a trunk, the steel still visible inside the bark like a bone. A conveyor belt, twenty meters wide and running to the vanishing point, still moves — slowly, silently — but its rubber has become corky and living, sprouting shelf-leaves along its length. The factories are still factories, after a fashion.

Because the industry never stopped.

It is everywhere and it is quiet. That is the strangest thing. This should roar. On a continental scale there are mills and presses and looms and refineries, entire districts of stamping machines the size of cathedrals, all built low and wide to fit the basin. And they are all still in motion. Active. You can feel it in your feet more than hear it: a deep, subsonic thrum, a tremor through the floor-plate. Overhead, gantry cranes glide on silent rails, not carrying ore or parts but carrying bundles of seed-fiber, moving them from one bright zone to another. Pistons the size of grain silos rise and fall once per minute, not stamping metal anymore but compressing leaf-matter into bales, into bricks, into new floor tiles that are themselves alive and slowly knitting. Vast halls of spinning frames — thousands of spindles — spin thread that is not cotton or wire but vine, drawing green-white filament out of the air itself.

The light does the work.

The light here is too much, and it is the weather. There is no sun in the sky so much as the sky *is* sun — a flat, white-bright dome, not glaring, not hot, but absolute. Bright enough that shadows are thin and crisp underneath every leaf, bright enough that the pale plants glow at their edges. It has weight. It comes down in slow, visible shafts through the layered canopy, and where it hits the dry leaves they warm and unfurl a fraction more, tilting. At noon — if there is a noon — the whole continent seems to inhale light. At other times the brightness thins just enough that you see it flicker, not like cloud cover but like a shutter. Someone built a lamp for this whole low world and left it on.

Under that light, the plant mass breathes and builds and builds and breathes. That is the dynamic. It is not still. It is furiously, silently busy. Growth is industrial-pace. You can watch a tendril extend a handspan in an hour, hear the faint *tick, tick, tick* of a fiber board leaf expanding, see a whole trellis of stems slowly re-tension themselves as a crane passes overhead. The canopy stratifies by light access. The uppermost leaves are vast, flat, horizontal solar sails, kilometers across when fused together, forming a false ceiling that ripples like a sea. Beneath them, a middle story of tangled stem-and-girder, catwalks turned to branches, maintenance tunnels choked with hanging air-roots that never touch water. Beneath that, the understory: dimmer, cooler, dense with felted undergrowth, with leaf-litter that is not litter at all but thin, pressed sheets of shed fiber accumulating like paper drifts against the walls of machines.

It is dense. Not jungle-dense — structured dense. Like a warehouse packed floor to ceiling with living inventory. You are never more than an arm's length from a surface that is alive and was once made. You have to turn sideways to pass, you have to lift leaves like curtains.

It is silent in a way an active world should not be.

The plants muffle everything. The dry fibers absorb sound. The machines were made, in the end, to be quiet — or the plants taught them to be. No birdcalls. Fauna is rare here to the point of myth. Once, maybe, there were pollinators. Now there are only the occasional custodians: beetle-like things the size of a fist, matte white, that crawl along the stems and chew a precise hole and lay a filament, or a spindly, stilt-legged grazer that you see once a week at most, picking its way across a leaf-sail in the far distance, silent. They don't make noise either. Even your own footsteps are dull thuds on fiberboard floor that gives slightly underfoot. When the wind moves — and it does, a cool, slow, basin-wide circulation driven by the light's heat differential — it doesn't rustle. It sighs through papery masses with a sound like someone turning pages in another room.

And the weirdness —

The geometry is wrong in the way of a place that has been growing through its own blueprint for too long.

You walk for an hour in a straight line, following a seam in the floor-plates, and you come back to the same pylon-trunk you started at, but from the other side, without having turned. The canopy layers repeat with a slight offset, like printing. A leaf the size of a door, held up to the light, shows not veins but circuitry, then when you look closer, tiny printed floors and tiny leaves inside the veins. Some stems grow *inward*, into themselves, disappearing. The bright sky sometimes shows, if you stare long enough, the faint grid of its own making — vast panels, each humming faintly, each a little out of phase with its neighbor, so the light stutters.

Gravity is light here, less insistent. Pollen — dry, mineral pollen that is more like dust — hangs in the air for hours without falling. A bale of compressed fiber, cut loose from a press, will drift down slowly as if reluctant.

The air smells of warm paper and ozone and iron.

There are no rivers to follow, no hills to climb. Only the low, endless, built plain, and the total green-white that has become its own architecture, and the bright, cool light that feeds it without water, and the quiet, endless industry of growing.
