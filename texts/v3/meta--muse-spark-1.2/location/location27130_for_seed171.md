It is not a forest. It did not grow *on* the land. It *is* the land.

You are standing - though standing is difficult here - on a continent that has not finished being made. A plateau so large you cannot hold it in your mind as a place, only as a weather system. The air is thin and cool enough that your breath smokes even though nothing is frozen. The elevation is high enough that the sky presses close, dim and heavy, not dark but un-bright, like light seen through deep water. There is no sun to be seen. There never is. The light is diffuse, sourceless, filtered through kilometers of low cloud that never breaks, that condenses directly out of the air.

And beneath that cloud: the mass.

From horizon to horizon, in every direction, to a curvature you can almost see, the world is packed. Not dense like a jungle is dense - there are no trunks with space between them, no floor and canopy. This is density as saturation. A felted, woven, interlocked mat of plant that has filled every available cubic meter from the raw rock up to thirty, fifty, a hundred meters high. Moss is too small a word. Liverwort is too small. It is a living substrate, a peat that never died, a sphagnum the size of buildings, leaves as broad as roofs, fronds that unfurl and re-furl like lungs, stems as thick as pillars that are not woody but turgid, water-filled, translucent. Everything is holding water.

Because this place is wet in a way that has nothing to do with rain, though it rains constantly. Not in drops so much as in a perpetual misting, a condensation that forms on every surface and runs and is pulled back up. The rock itself weeps. 

The rock — that's the other thing you have to understand. The geology here is embryonic. This plateau was lifted yesterday, in geological time. It is basalt still warm from the deep, but cooled now to a cool, damp black, soft at the edges, not yet cut. There are no valleys. No rivers. No canyons. Erosion has not learned how to work here yet. Instead the water sheets. It lies in a film a few millimeters thick across everything, it beads on the waxy cuticles of leaves the size of houses, it runs in braided, shallow, silent-then-not-silent fans across the uneven basalt, finding no channel because no channel has been given to it. In places the rock is still vesicular, pitted like fresh bread, and from each pore water beads and falls without ever having been rained. The whole continent exhales moisture.

You hear it before you see anything else, and then you cannot hear anything else.

There are no animals here. No birds, no insects, no rustle of anything with a mouth. And yet the sound is deafening.

It is the sound of water moving under pressure through plant plumbing on a continental scale, and of the plants themselves moving. A low, endless creak and groan as turgor builds and releases. The hydraulic sigh of a leaf the size of a field lifting a ton of water thirty meters into the dim air. The pop and tick of millions of spore casings rupturing at once, not seasonally but constantly, a soft gunfire that never stops. The deep, resonant thrumming as wind — and there is always wind up here, cool, damp wind that comes down off the cloud deck — drags across the million-fingered surface and makes it vibrate like a reed bed the size of a country. And underneath it all, a sound like distant thunder that never resolves, which is the rock itself, still settling, still exhaling heat, micro-fracturing under the weight of all that living water.

You try to find a built thing and you cannot. There is no structure. No wall, no path, no ruin, no artifact. The idea of building here is absurd; there is no room to build, no ground to build on. Every surface is already occupied by something living and growing over it within hours. And yet —

This is not wild.

Wild is random. This is not random. As your eyes adjust to the dimness you start to see the pattern. The fronds are not just fronds. Their venation carries light. Not reflected light — *carried* light, a faint, slow-moving bioluminescence that pulses along the veins in perfect, unhurried packets, amber and pale green, moving from the outer leaves inward and down toward the rock. The spore clouds do not drift; they hang in lattice formations, holding station in the damp air for minutes before dispersing in unison. The water films on the basalt do not sheet randomly; they branch and re-branch in angles too precise, forming circuits, reservoirs, switching stations where a leaf tilts a fraction of a degree and redirects a whole watershed a continent away. The cool air smells of ozone and chlorophyl and hot stone, like a server room that photosynthesizes.

The technology is not *in* the landscape. It is the landscape. Someone — or something, or the plants themselves over a long, directed becoming — engineered physics here to be more cooperative. Nanoscale processes run in the sap. Each cell is a machine. The whole packed, wet, dim, high, raw continent is a computer that runs on water and light and growth, and it is running. It is active, dynamically active, not frozen, not climax. You can watch it work: a patch of the mat a kilometer across will suddenly blanch, exhale a great plume of condensed mist, and darken again as it completes some calculation or some act of atmospheric maintenance. A wave of growth will ripple outward from a central node, new fronds unfurling in a Fibonacci spiral so large it takes an hour to complete, audible as a roar of unfurling.

You cannot walk here. You sink and are held. The mat gives under you and then supports you, damp and cool and springy, and if you stay still too long fine root-hairs begin to explore the seams of your boots, not aggressively, just to see if you are substrate. The air is cold on your face and warm where it comes back off the plant respiration. Water collects on your shoulders and does not evaporate.

There is nowhere to go, because every direction is the same and also slowly, deafeningly different. The world is still being made, still deciding what shape water should take when it is not allowed to carve, what shape life should take when it is not allowed any empty space, what shape thought should take when it does not need a building to house it.

You are on a high, wet, unfinished computer made of moss the size of mountains, listening to it breathe.
