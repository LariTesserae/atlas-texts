It is not a valley. It is not a mountain. It is a room inside a mountain that has not finished being a mountain yet.

You get to it near the top where the air should be too thin and too cold to breathe, but it isn't cold. From the outside, from the clouds, you wouldn't see it at all. The peak is still under construction. Raw, toothy scaffolding of black basalt and undressed limestone rises out of the cloud sea like the bones of a cathedral that forgot it was supposed to be a geology. Everything is cut. Everything is placed. There is no natural ground here. Even the ground is floor.

You squeeze through the wind-gap — a doorway left between two leaning megaliths that haven't settled against each other yet, still sharp from the chisel, still weeping stone-milk — and you are inside.

The scale betrays you. From outside you thought the summit would be vast and empty. Inside it is pocket-small, hidden, intimate as a lung. A single chamber, maybe twenty paces across, no more. A cistern-court. A hanging pocket garden built into the very crown of the world.

And it is packed.

There is no spare surface.

Every wall, every ledge, every inch of floor and ceiling is made and then made again. Terraces cut into terraces cut into terraces, three, four, five tiers deep, all of them shallow stone troughs and fitted trays mortared by hand. Bamboo scaffolding still lashed with wet cord holds up the half-finished ceiling. Wooden lattices, hand-split and dark with damp, crisscross the air to give the vines something to hold. Clay jars, basalt basins, woven baskets lined with pitch, all of them full. You cannot put your foot down without stepping on the edge of something that was built to hold something else. Even the air is occupied — vines cross it, roots hang in it, steam hangs in it.

Nothing here is weathered. That is the strangest thing at first, before the stranger things start. This place has an erosion of zero. The stone has no softness. The chisel marks are still bright and angry on every block, each strike a white scar. Corners are knife-sharp, not yet rounded by a single rain. The mortar is still curing, still exhaling heat. The world here is embryonic, raw-geology, just-coming-into-being. It hasn't learned how to be eroded yet. It is still deciding what shape water should give it, and water is already here, impatient.

Because it is wet. Unreasonably wet for this height.

Heat gathers in this pocket and doesn't leave. The stone itself gives off heat — the freshly placed rock, the lime curing, the low dim sun trapped and multiplied. It is hot the way a mouth is hot, a greenhouse hot, a fever hot. Sweat beads on you instantly and doesn't evaporate.

And because it is hot and enclosed, the water has nowhere to go. It hangs. It returns.

It does not rain here so much as condense. The atmosphere begets itself. Steam rises off the troughs and hits the low, unfinished ceiling of stone and bamboo and becomes cloud right there, three feet above your head. It thickens, greys, drips. The ceiling weeps. Every horizontal surface weeps. The vertical walls weep. The chisel scars run with water as if the rock itself is sweating out the sea it remembers. You hear it before you see it clearly in the dim —

It is dim. For being at the top of the world, there is almost no light. The sun is a hot white coin somewhere beyond the cloud sea, but only slits of it get in, cut by the scaffolding and the half-roof. Light comes in bars and blades, solid with motes and steam, and then it... pools. It doesn't behave. On the wet stone, light puddles like spilled oil, collecting in the basins instead of on them. You wade through ankle-deep light in one corner and think your eyes are wrong until you see a vine drinking it.

And it is loud.

Not with animals. There is almost no fauna here — too high, too new, too made. Once in a while a single, huge, pale moth, the size of your hand, blunders through the steam, or a blind white beetle clicks over a mortar seam, but they are rare intruders. The loudness is all abiotic and vegetative, and it is constant.

Water ticking, dripping, plinking, pouring, hissing where it hits hot stone and flashes back to steam. Vines creaking as they grow — audibly — tightening on lattices, splitting clay pots with a soft *pop*. Bamboo groaning and settling under the weight of cloud. The wind outside trying to get in through the seams and whistling, and the wind inside trying to get out. Somewhere a basin overflows and it sounds like a throat swallowing. The whole pocket breathes and sweats and shifts. It is an active place, impatient, multiple cycles running at once and out of sync: water cycling every minute from trough to air to ceiling to trough again, plants visibly unfurling, stone still expanding with heat. Nothing rests.

The vegetation is absurd. Lush to the point of insolence, given that all of it is planted and tended by hand with primitive tools. No machines, no pumps, nothing you couldn't make with fire and stone and fiber.

You see the tool marks everywhere: the adze on the lattice, the thumbprint in the mortar, the woven vine ties, the stone hammers left sitting in puddles. Someone — many someones — carried every basket of soil up here on their backs. And what soil! Black, composted, carried up in jars, now overflowing. From it comes everything, jammed together without room for air: broad taro-like leaves the size of shields, slick and hot, dripping; curtains of aerial roots that never touch ground but drink the steam; mosses thick as carpet that have already taken the fresh stone and made it green and furred; gourds and tubers swelling so fast you can hear their skins stretch; ferns that uncoil as you watch, tight fists opening finger by finger.

It is all too much for the containers. Vines burst their baskets and root in the mortar. Leaves press against leaves until they sweat on each other. There is no fallow, no gap. Packed, saturated, fully occupied. If a seed fell here, it would have to fight for a millimeter of dark.

Then the weirdness comes on, because this high, hot, wet, dim, packed, hand-made pocket hasn't yet agreed to the rules.

Gravity here is a suggestion that the water is ignoring. In the central cistern — a great, shallow, black basalt bowl that takes up half the floor — water climbs the sides. It beads up and runs *upward* along the chisel grooves, toward the rim, before falling again as rain that falls sideways. A tangle of roots hangs unsupported in the middle of the air, not hanging *from* anything, just holding its shape like embroidery on invisible cloth. And it grows in two directions at once, tips reaching both up and down.

Light, as you noticed, has weight. The bars of sun that get through the roof don't fade; they collect in low places. If you cup your hands in a basin, you can scoop light and it runs between your fingers like warm water, pooling briefly before evaporating.

Time is damp too. In the farthest corner, where a scaffolding post is still oozing sap, you see a vine bud, open, flower, fruit, and drop all while a drop of water takes to fall from the ceiling to the floor. Blink and the fruit is rot and the rot is soil again. In the opposite corner, a puddle holds perfectly still, not rippling even when a drop hits it, as if the moment of impact hasn't decided to happen yet.

Sound also pools. A drip from five minutes ago is still echoing faintly over your left shoulder, as if the air in that corner is thicker and keeps sounds the way amber keeps insects.

The rules of this place, insofar as they have been written by hand in stone and vine, are:

1.  **Everything is made, but everything is alive.** No surface was found; every surface was placed. And every placed surface is already being taken apart by what grows on it.
2.  **Water goes where it wants, which is everywhere at once.** Up, sideways, into air, into stone, into light. Do not expect a watershed. There is only the cycle of the room.
3.  **The mountain is still soft.** Strike it and it rings like wet clay, not rock. It is still learning to be hard. Your handprint will stay in a block if you press long enough, until the moss covers it.

If you stand still long enough the steam erases you to your shoulders and the chamber seems larger than it is — then smaller again. The pale moth lands on your wet forearm and you feel its feet. Somewhere a clay pot cracks open from the inside with a sharp report like a knuckle popping, and a new shoot, bright green and steaming, forces its way out into the already crowded air, looking for a hold that isn't there but will be made for it.

Outside, far below the cloud, the world is vast and weathered and old. Inside, at the extreme tip of it all, is this one hidden, hot, dripping room that hasn't yet learned to be old, that was built by hands to hold a jungle in the sky, and that is now improvising physics to have enough space for everything it contains.
