You come up onto it without ever having climbed.

That's the first strange thing. You have been walking uphill for a long time, but there was never a slope. The ground rose in such shallow, such patient steps that your calves never noticed. One moment you were in the foothills and the next the air thinned and cooled just a little and you realized you were high. Very high.

This is a table that was once a mountain range.

An old place. An oldest place. The kind of high country that has forgotten it was ever high. There are no peaks here. No ridges, no talus, no sharpness of any kind. Everything that could be broken has been broken. Everything that could be carried away by wind and water has been carried away, until what is left is what could not be carried. A peneplain perched two miles in the sky.

The stone is warm under your hand. Not sun-warm, though the sun is out — a normal, high, yellow-white sun that makes short shadows at noon — but earth-warm, like skin. The bedrock is exposed everywhere in great, bald domes the size of houses and then the size of towns, smoothed until they shine dully. They are not grey or brown exactly. They are the color of old bone, of weathered sandstone, of lichen-bleached skull, banded with slow rusts and desert varnish. Between the domes, the ground is not ground but residue: a fine, deep powder of that same rock ground to flour over a hundred million years, packed hard as adobe. You can see where water has tried to cut into it and failed. There are shallow, broad swales where a river ought to be, a kilometer wide and only a meter deep, meandering with no urgency, their banks so gently rounded you wouldn't know you were standing in a valley until you looked back and saw you were.

It is warm here, consistently warm, though the air is thin and dry. At this height you expect cold. Instead the wind that moves over the plain is like breath from an oven left open. It doesn't gust. It sighs one way for hours. 70, 80 degrees and utterly even. You sweat, but the sweat evaporates immediately.

Vegetation is an afterthought. It has conceded.

There are no forests, no grasslands. What grows here grows because it has learned to be almost not a plant. Low things, far apart. A black, leathery lichen that crusts the stone domes in plates the size of your palm. Cushions of silver-green moss that live only in the crack-shade on the north side of the domes. Occasional solitary stalks, perhaps thirty per acre, not more: a woody, twisted stem as thick as your thumb, two feet high, with two or three waxy leaves held vertically to spill the sun, and a deep taproot you know must go down ten times the height of the plant to find anything. They do not flower. Or they flower once a decade.

It looks empty at first. Until you crouch.

The damp gives it away. This is not a wet country, but it is not a dry one either. Water exists here as a rumor, as a memory that passes through. In the mornings, before the sun is high, the thin air can't hold its little moisture and it condenses. Every stone dome sweats. The flour-fine dust between them darkens a shade. In the low swales, where the ancient riverbeds are, the condensation collects and threads — not a stream, not even a creek — a braided seep, a lace of dark wet sand and shallow pans that are damp to the touch but will be gone by noon. Under the overhang where one dome leans against another — and there are many such places, because the stone has sagged and settled into itself like warm wax — the seep lingers. There you find pockets the size of a basin or a bathtub where water truly sits, brown and still, capped with dust. A few hours later it will be mud, then dry. Then it will be back again tomorrow without ever having rained. When rain does come, which is occasional and brief, it doesn't erode. The old earth just drinks it and waits.

And that is where everything lives.

Because at first you thought density meant crowding, meant jungle. Here density means something else. It means the plain is covered, absolutely covered, with presences that are almost touching, layer over layer, but they are not plants.

They are animals. They are everywhere.

You have to be still for a minute to see them, because they are quiet and they are slow and they are the exact color of the stone and the dust. Then the ground moves.

The entire surface between the domes, the entire swale floor, the underside of every stone, is a living pavement. Not insects exactly, though you think of insects first. Things with low, domed carapaces the size of a thumbnail to the size of a dinner plate, matte, pitted, sand-colored, edged with fine cilia. They tessellate. They fit together with almost no gap, each one snug against the next like cobblestones, but each one alive, each one breathing slowly. Some are flat and wide. Some are humped high. Where one dome meets another, they pile two, three deep, clinging to vertical rock in overlapping shingles. When you look closer you see that their shells are not shells, but something grown from the dust itself — agglutinated grains cemented with secretion — and that they open underneath, just barely, with a pale, wet fringe.

There are millions of them. Billions. Across the open scale of this place, which is not a field but a country, not a valley but a series of valleys that repeat until haze takes them, every acre you can see is paved with them. You walk among them and they do not scatter. You step carefully in the narrow seams where their edges don't quite meet. If you stand still too long, the smaller ones will begin to edge onto the leather of your boots.

There is no sound. That is the overwhelming part.

A biomass this huge should roar, hum, click. This one doesn't. The pavement of creatures makes almost no noise. If you put your ear close to them — and you do — you hear a faint, collective sigh, the wet fringe mouths opening and closing once every twenty, thirty seconds, drawing damp from the air and from the stone. A soft *ah* like a lung. When the seep water is present in the morning, they move. Slowly. So slowly you can only tell they have moved because the pattern of the seams has changed. They inch toward moisture, piling a little deeper where the sand is darkest, filtering the water through those fringes, leaving behind a faint tracery of clean sand.

Above them, other fauna moves, but with the same patience. Pale things like long-legged crabs, as big as dogs, pick their way across the pavement with absurd, stilted delicacy, never crushing the ones below, probing with needle mouths into the seams. Low, slug-like gliders the length of your arm leave a gloss on the stone that evaporates by midday. In the air — warm, thin air — there are things that float rather than fly: translucent bladders, ribbed like jellyfish, drifting two or three meters off the ground, trailing threads that brush the pavement and reel up with a dusting of smaller, mite-like passengers. None of them call. The stilt-walkers don't click. The bladders don't buzz. Even the wind is quiet here, because there is nothing sharp for it to whistle against.

The weirdness is not that they are alien. It's that they behave like geology.

They are the erosion now. Or they are what erosion became when the world stopped eroding. The stone no longer wears by water and wind alone; it wears by being slowly, endlessly mouthed. The domes are smooth not just from weather but from a hundred million years of those pale fringes lapping at them. The flour-fine dust is not just rock flour — it is frass, it is shell-cement, it is the powdered exhalation of this living crust. The ancient, settled landscape has come to an equilibrium where rock and animal are not different categories.

And scattered through it all are the built things, though built is the wrong word because nothing here builds with tools. There are structures, but they are made by the pavement itself, or by whatever came before the pavement learned to do it.

Here and there, on the highest, driest backs of the stone domes where even the seep never reaches, are low mounds, maybe a meter across, made of the same agglutinated dust but more deliberately. They are spirals. Perfect, low, flattened spirals like a snail shell laid on its side and stepped on, with a single opening at the center just big enough to put your fist in. No creature enters or leaves them while you watch. They are cool inside, and damp, even at noon. When you breathe near the opening, a faint puff of cooler, moss-scented air sighs back out. Some of the spirals are broken, caved in, their walls returned to dust. Some are fresh, their edges still sharp. Counting them, you realize there are dozens visible from any one dome top, spread across the open country like villages.

You understand after a while that the empty ones are not abandoned. They are waiting. The pavement flows around them, never over them.

There is a rule here, though no one wrote it: Stay low. Stay slow. Stay damp. Conserve. Do not make noise, because noise is wasted water. Do not rise far above the stone, because then the wind will take you. The sky is huge and normal and indifferent, and the light falls straight down and without mercy, and the only way to live high and warm and dry is to become a second skin on the old, old rock, and wait for the dew.

If you sit with your back to one of those warm domes in the afternoon, when the seep has gone and the pavement has settled again into perfect stillness, you can feel the faint vibration through the stone itself — all those millions of slow breaths rising and falling together, once every half minute, across the whole high plain. The hill you are sitting on breathes. The valley breathes. The country breathes, quiet and dense and patient, and it has been breathing like this since the mountains forgot they were mountains.
