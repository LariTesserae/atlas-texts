You are high up, but you would not know it from the shape of the land.

This is not a mountain range. This is what is left after mountains have lived and died. A continent lifted long ago and then left alone to be softened for eras without end — a peneplain at the roof of the world. The elevation is in the air itself, in the way your lungs have to work a little harder, in the way the clouds do not sit above you but move *through* you, at chest-height and knee-height, catching on the ground. The horizon never declares itself. It simply fades into green haze, and the curvature feels wrong, as if the world here has swollen outward.

It is warm. Not hot in a sharp, desert way, but a deep, bath-warm that never leaves. The air is saturated and heavy and dim, as if the light has to swim to reach you. There is no hard sun. There is a luminous overcast that never quite breaks — a sky that is not sky but a low, pearlescent ceiling of condensation a few hundred feet above the canopy. The light filters down in a perpetual late afternoon, green and amber, attenuated by mile after mile of living filter. Everything glows faintly, without casting a clear shadow.

And it is wet. Not with lakes or rivers in any way you expect them, but with water that has nowhere to be and everywhere to go. The rock beneath — ancient basalt and sandstone worn to the smoothness of river stones the size of counties — weeps. Water beads on every surface, constantly condensing out of the warm air and rolling down. The ground is not ground. The ground is a sponge.

Vegetation is not *on* the landscape. It *is* the landscape.

You walk not on soil but on plant. A mattress of moss and liverwort and cushion-plants feet deep, then yards deep, that yields under your weight and slowly rebounds. Below that, a peat black and dense as history, made of ten thousand generations of itself. Above that, the structure: not trees as you know them, but architectures that have solved the problem of being a mountain after the mountains eroded away.

Trunks as wide as houses rise and then soften, their bark not bark but a shag of epiphytes growing on epiphytes — ferns growing on mosses growing on lichens growing on the tree — until the tree is a vertical bog, a cliff of green. Their crowns do not form a canopy so much as they braid together into a continuous, porous continent. Leaves the size of doors overlap like shingles, each one cupped to collect the endless condensation and then tip it in slow, deliberate pours to the leaf below. In places the intergrowth is so complete you can walk for an hour on a platform of interlaced branches and aerial roots twenty stories above the true floor, never seeing sky, never seeing soil.

It is ancient in a way that feels biological. This place has finished becoming. Erosion has done all it can do; the hills are so broad and so low you only realize you are climbing one when your ears pop. Valleys are not cut, but sagged — vast, shallow basins a day's walk across where water gathers not as a lake but as a bright, ankle-deep sheet reflecting the dim sky through a thatch of reeds and floating ferns. The rivers do not run. They *seep*, braiding into a thousand slow threads through the root-mat, silent, tea-colored, warm to the touch.

Nothing here is fast. Growth is measured in the thickening of bark. A flower may take seven years to open, and stay open for a month. A fallen giant does not crash — there is no space to fall — it leans slowly into its neighbors over decades and is digested where it stands, becoming a mossy ridge you can walk along for a mile before you realize it was once a single being. The air itself seems to damp any sudden movement. Sound is swallowed.

It is mostly quiet, not because it is empty but because it is dense and soft. The peat absorbs footfalls. The leaves muffle wind. What you hear is close and small and layered: the constant ticking and dripping of condensation falling from leaf to leaf to leaf, the sigh of water moving through root-stuff, a low frogsong that seems to come from inside the wood itself, the occasional soft *whump* as a water-heavy bromeliad discharges its load. When a bird calls — and there are birds, though you rarely see them as separate from the leaves — the sound is startlingly clear and then gone, as if the forest decided to allow it briefly.

And there is abundant fauna, but it has learned the rule of this place: do not stand out. Everything is camouflaged, layered, half-plant. Moths the size of your hand with bark-patterned wings that drink fog. Glossy black amphibians that live their whole lives in the water cups of single leaves. Beetles like polished jade moving through the peat tunnels. Things that are not quite arthropod and not quite vine, that hang motionless for hours and then extend a tendril with exquisite slowness. In the great sheet-marshes, heron-like waders on stilt legs the color of young reed move so slowly you think they are dead stalks until they spear. Under the root-mat, something large moves occasionally — you feel it as a pressure wave in the spongy floor, a long undulation passing beneath you — and never see it.

The weirdness is not spectacle. It is that the rules have gently changed.

Gravity feels less insistent here because the plant holds you. Water does not obey watersheds; it moves upward as readily as down, drawn by capillary action through living tissue for miles, so that a hilltop can be wetter than the hollow below. Some of the largest leaves are translucent and faintly luminous, not with bioluminescence but with what looks like trapped light, as if they stored the dim day and release it at dusk in a pale exhalation. Pollen hangs in the still air in dense, slow-moving clouds that behave like weather systems, drifting for weeks without dispersing. And the plants remember. You can see where something warm-blooded brushed past years ago — the moss there grows in a slightly different direction, a wake frozen in green.

There were people here, or something like people. Not many, not recently. That is the other quiet.

You find their marks scattered and half-digested. Not cities. Not even villages. A single low wall of fitted stone, now so sunk in moss it looks like a natural outcrop until you see the corner. A stair cut into the ancient basalt that now carries a small seep-spring like a handrail of water. A circle of standing stones on a broad, almost imperceptible rise where the wind is a little stronger, each stone bearded with lichen, leaning inward toward each other like conspirators. No metal. No glass. No ruin of anything that needed fire to make. Only stone set by hand, patiently, and then abandoned to the slow green. Whatever they built, they built it to be taken back.

If you stay still long enough, the place corrects around you. Condensation gathers on your shoulders. A vine-feeler, pale and blind, extends an inch toward the heat of your body, tests the air, and then, finding you neither soil nor rain, withdraws. The light does not change. The cloud-ceiling sags lower, brushes the uppermost leaves, and lifts again. A long way off — so far away that you feel it in your chest before you hear it — a section of the great mat, saturated beyond holding, slumps a foot lower with a sigh that takes a full minute to finish.

You understand then that this is not a forest on a plateau.

The plateau *is* a forest that learned, over unfathomable time, how to be a plateau — how to hold water in its body against the thin high air, how to keep itself warm and dim under its own weather, how to make its own soil out of its own dead while never exposing rock to sky. It is vast enough to have its own climate and slow enough to outlast yours. It does not need you to see it. It is simply, and totally, there.
