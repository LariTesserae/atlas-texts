It is not on any map because it is not large enough to be on a map.

You find it by going low.

The land around it is a flat pan of weathered salt and basalt, scoured to a polish by a wind that has had a long time to learn its job. Established, settled, balanced — the erosion here isn't catastrophic, it's patient. The flat is scored with vent-lines and pressure ridges, the color of old bone and rust, and it holds heat even after the sun has gone. But you don't stay on the flat. At a place where the crust buckles, there is a mouth, no wider than a doorway, a dark seam where two plates did not quite meet. You have to crouch to enter.

Inside, the temperature spikes.

Not the bright, solar heat of the pan outside, but a trapped heat, a kiln-heat, a low and stored heat that has been gathering for centuries. The air inside is absolutely, completely dry. Not arid the way a desert is arid — there is no buried water, no deep aquifer, no memory of rain. This is an anhydrous pocket. The rock exhales no damp. The salt floor does not sweat. Your mouth dries in seconds. Your nostrils seal with the smell of hot dust and hot stone and hot iron, metallic on the tongue. Nothing here uses water. Nothing can.

And yet it is lush.

That is the first contradiction that forces you to stop and recalibrate your eyes, because the dimness makes you think at first that you are looking at shadow.

The pocket is roughly oval, maybe thirty meters long, fifteen across, the size of a large house or a small plaza — a hidden scale, a single room let into the earth like a bubble in cooling lava, or a blister in the salt. The floor is low, sunken ten or twelve meters below the pan, so that standing on its floor you are standing in the very bottom of the world, with the weight of the plain above you. The walls curve inward and then over, not quite a dome, not quite a cave, made of a rough, vesicular basalt that has been worked.

Because this is a town.

Not a ruin field. A town, in miniature, in full urban density, but emptied. On the eastern curve, where the wall is less steep, the inhabitants — with nothing but hand tools — cut and stacked. Primitive technology: piled basalt block on basalt block without mortar, the stone dressed with stone hammers until the faces kissed. Timber that was never timber — petrified drift of the salt pan, perhaps — cut into lintels and ladders. Woven hangings now stiff as bark. Terraces rise in three uneven tiers, narrow streets between them that were never wide enough for carts, only for shoulders. Balconies, ovens, cisterns that never held water, but held grain or salt or ash. Doorways without doors. Shutters carved with simple geometries, sunbursts and spirals chiseled by patient hands. Everything weathered, rounded at the corners, the soot of old fires still blackening the backs of the niches. Established. Long-existed. No collapse, no catastrophe — just the slow smoothing of things that have been left to be.

And nowhere, anyone. The density is wrong for a town. A town should be crowded, should be close. Here each presence is marooned in vast space. Between one terrace house and the next there is too much air. Between the built wall and the far wall there is an emptiness that feels deliberate, hollowed. It is not ruined, it is vacated, the way a pot is empty. You could shout and the sound would not come back crowded.

There are no animals. Not even the suggestion of them. No nests in the eaves, no guano, no insect husks, no burrows, no bone. No lichen that hides mites. No hum. The absence has a texture; it makes the lushness stranger.

Because the western half of the pocket, where no one built, is a thicket.

In the dimness it looks at first black, then, as your eyes adjust, a deep, dusty viridian, a felted green-gray that drinks the low light. It is not green the way watered leaves are green. This is a dry green. The plants — if you can call them plants — do not need water and have never needed it. They are not succulents; they have no water to store. They are a different chemistry altogether, a foliage of salt and silica and waxy fiber. 

They grow as dense, overlapping fans and fronds and puffed columns, a foot to eight feet tall, crowding each other for the dim light and the hot air. The leaves, if they are leaves, are not leaves: they are flat, branching crystals of a matte, fibrous mineral, furry to the touch like ash felt, edged with fine hairs that rattle. The trunks are not wood but tightly wound bundles of desiccated filament, like rope left in a kiln. From a distance, lush — impossibly lush for a place with no water — a jungle in miniature compressed into a single wall. Up close, you see it is utterly dry. When you brush it, it does not bruise, it sheds a fine, scentless powder that hangs in the hot air for a long time before settling.

This is its own rule: life here runs on heat and light and airborne minerals, not water. The fronds open not toward rain but toward the thermal gradient. The dim light — which comes from nowhere obvious, a diffuse, amber-grey glow that seems to emanate from the salt itself, from the heat itself, not from any sun-hole — is enough. The plants photosynthesize what little there is, or thermosynthesize, converting the endless dry heat into growth. They metabolize the salt dust.

And it is active. Violently, restlessly active, in a way that makes the lack of fauna even more uncanny.

You expect a place like this, dim and sealed and empty, to be still. It is not.

The thicket moves. Without wind, fronds unfurl with audible pops. Seed-pods — dry, geometric capsules the size of fists — swell and split along seams with sharp cracks that echo like handclaps off the stone terraces. Plumes of that fine powder discharge into the air. Filaments creep, a millimeter at a time but constantly, curling around basalt. The sound is loud for such a small space. Too loud. The dome focuses it. Every crack, every rustle of those stiff, glassy leaves hissing against each other, every tick of the cooling and heating stone, every sigh of hot air shifting as the pocket breathes through its narrow mouth — it stacks and amplifies until the little town thrums. You can hear the plants growing. You can hear the houses expanding in the heat, the woven fibers creaking where they still hang.

At intervals, a larger event: a whole column of the felted fronds will shudder and collapse under its own dry weight, falling into the thicket with a soft, thunderous *whoomph*, throwing up a cloud of mineral dust that hangs and glitters in the dim amber for minutes. Within an hour, the tendrils around it are already reaching over the fallen column, covering it.

No animal tends it. No animal eats it. No animal pollinates it. The plants split, dust, re-root, and die entirely on their own, in their own hot, dry, mineral cycle. The town beside it does nothing to interfere, and the thicket does nothing to overgrow the terraces — there is an old balance here, a line as neat as a property boundary where the stone was swept, perhaps, or salted, or simply where the chemistry changes. The vegetation stops a foot short of the lowest terrace wall, even though spores must fall there daily.

The whole pocket has that slightly unusual quality — not alien, not fantastic, just *off*, the way a place built for people but now tended only by dry crystal ferns is off. The light has no source. The heat has no source. The lushness has no water. The town has no people. Processes have continued without their original actors, and have settled into a new, stable strangeness.

You sit on the edge of a terrace, in the hollow, empty air, sweating instantly in the dry heat, listening to the loud, patient, vegetable city breathe and crackle and grow in the dimness, and you understand that you are a temporary volume inside an intimate, self-sufficient world — a hidden urban pocket the size of a room, that has been here long before you, and will be here long after, hot and dry and improbably green and completely, completely quiet of any footstep but your own.
