On the high table the air is too bright to be so cold.

You come up to it slowly, because there is no other way to come up to a place like this — the switchback that brought you here is cut into pale, rain-dark stone for two hours before it finally levels, and then the ground just stops rising and opens. The elevation hits you first as a thinness. Breath comes short. Sound carries too far and too sharply. The light has weight at this height; it presses on the skin of your face and hands even while the wind chills them.

This is not a peak. It is a shelf, a broad, uneven step in the world about a kilometer across and several kilometers long, hanging thousands of meters above the lowlands you can no longer properly see. The lowlands are there — a smudged, too-blue distance far below the eastern scarp — but if you stare at them they seem to slide out of focus, as if the plateau is higher than the atmosphere should allow. The sky is close and painful. No clouds hide the sun, yet the sun is not quite one sun; its light is too white, too present, spilling over every edge without softening, making every wet surface glare and every shadow a hard, ink-cut line.

The plateau itself is dense.

Not dense with life. There has never been life here, or not life that you would recognize, and the rock remembers that. Every available surface is occupied, but by mineral form. The ground is not ground so much as a crowded settlement of stone. Shallow basins and low pillars, chimneys and tables and fins of basalt and chert and a pale, pitted limestone that sweats continuously — all of it weathered into a settled, old shape. Nothing here is sharp with new breaking. Everything has been here a long time, rounded by wind and water until the forms fit together like worn teeth. You have to pick your way. The pillars stand shoulder to shoulder in places, half a meter to three meters high, sometimes so close you turn sideways. Between them the rock is never dry.

Water is everywhere and nowhere that water should be.

There are no rivers, no lakes. The wetness is low and close and constant. At this elevation and temperature — cool enough that the sweat on your neck never quite warms — the air itself is condensing. You can watch it happen. The bright, cold wind moves over the dark rock, and on the leeward side of each pillar a film of water beads and thickens and runs. The stone weeps. Every surface glistens. In the basins, water stands a finger-deep, perfectly clear, perfectly still until the wind touches it, and then it shivers and overflows in a sheet no thicker than glass down into the next basin. The sound of it is everywhere: a low, ceaseless trickling and dripping and sighing, the rock exhaling water, water finding the next lower place, amplified by the chimneys and fins that act like throats.

Where the water stands longest, the rock has grown a skin. Not plant, not lichen, not fungus — a mineral bloom, thin as paper and brittle when dry, in bands of rust-orange and bone-white and a sulfurous yellow, precipitated salt and iron and silica. It cracks in polygons and curls at the edges. Under the water it softens to a translucent jelly that quivers when you step near, but never breaks. It is the only thing that could be mistaken for growth, and it is not alive.

And the sound — loud — is not the water alone. The elevation makes a flute of the whole plateau. Wind comes off the western rim without interruption, having had nothing to stop it for a hundred kilometers, and hits the field of pillars and is cut into hundreds of separate tones. Some pillars are hollow, weathered through at the base, and the wind moves through them with a hollow moaning that rises and falls. Some fins vibrate. The basins hum when the wind skims their surfaces. It is not a quiet place, despite having nothing in it that can call or sing. It is loud the way a factory is loud, or a shore in storm: an active, working loudness. You have to raise your voice to be heard by someone beside you, and even then your words sound flattened by the air.

Scattered through the dense stone crowd are the marks of hands.

They are not villages. No one lives here; nothing could live here for long. They are occasional, faint, and deeply weathered themselves, which makes them feel older than they should be for how simple they are. A low wall of dry-stacked flat stones, only three courses high, enclosing a space no bigger than a sleeping body, open to the sky and half-filled with clear water now. A cairn beside a narrow passage between pillars, stones placed with such care that you know they did not fall there, capped with a chipped disc of black obsidian that still catches the bright light and throws it back. In one larger hollow where the wind is less, a ring of stones where fires were, the ash long since washed into the mineral skin, but the surrounding rocks still darkened. Scatters of debitage — flakes of chert where someone sat and struck a tool, the sharp edges now dulled by centuries of rain.

Whoever built them built low, as if unwilling to add height to a place already so high, and built to be near the water without trying to control it. The structures do not dam or divert; they simply exist among the weeping basins, letting the water run through and around them. Some are so overgrown with precipitated crust they are becoming pillars themselves.

The weirdness is not in those things. It is in the rules that hold the plateau together and do not quite hold.

At first you think it is the brightness tricking your eyes. Then you notice that distance is not stable. A pillar fifty meters away will seem to lean very slightly toward you, and when you walk toward it, you arrive sooner than you should, as if the ground between flexed. If you stand at the eastern scarp and look down, the lowlands are sometimes below and sometimes, briefly, *above* you in a second, fainter horizon that hangs in the sky like a reflection without a surface to reflect in. Water runs downhill, always — except in two or three places among the densest pillars where a thin sheet can be seen to hesitate, thicken, and crawl a hand's breadth uphill before gravity remembers it. Light pools. On an overcast moment — though there are no clouds, the light itself occasionally dims as if a veil passed — you can see brightness collect in the basins like a heavier liquid under the water, not illuminating so much as occupying.

The air has a faint doubling quality, too, as does the sound. If you clap, the echo comes back a fraction too late from a direction where there is no wall to echo from, and it comes back doubled, slightly out of phase with itself.

No animal watches you here. No insect moves. The mineral skins do not grow toward the sun. Everything that is active is inorganic and, impossibly, continuous — the water condensing as fast as it evaporates, the wind sawing without cease, the light pouring down with a pressure you can feel on your shoulders. The plateau is established, not raw. It is not forming. It is maintaining, in a steady state that should not be steady, weathered into a balance that includes its own small impossibilities as if they had eroded into place along with the stone.

If you stay until the light starts to slant, the glare finally softens and the whole dense field glows. Each basin of water holds a hard white sky. Each wet pillar throws a shadow three times longer than it should be, and the shadows intersect and do not quite darken where they cross. The wind cools further. The weeping grows louder in the evening thin air, a chorus of drips and whispers from a thousand stone mouths, and the scattered walls and cairns settle deeper into the rock as if listening.

You understand, without being told, why the builders kept their work low and small. In a place where even light and gravity are crowded and loud, you do not build up. You make a small mark, and let the plateau carry it.
