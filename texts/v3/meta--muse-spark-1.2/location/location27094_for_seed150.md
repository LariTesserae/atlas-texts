It is a bowl.

Not a valley — a bowl cut high into the mountain where the wind has given up. You have to climb to get here, up through thinning air and naked scree, and then the rim appears, a sharp black lip of basalt still unweathered, still angry from when it was born. This is young rock. It hasn't learned to be soft. The cliffs that ring the place are vertical and fresh-broken, stepped with frost-shattered ledges, their faces weeping even in the cold. Not rivers — seeps. The stone sweats.

Inside, the bowl holds its own weather.

The light never gets strong. At this height the sun should be brutal, but the cloud sits down inside the rim like a lid. A low, cold, luminous dimness — not night, not day, but the long blue hour after a storm that never fully left. The air is thin and clean and so cold your breath stays visible for a long time, hanging in front of you before it slowly becomes part of the cloud. Everything condenses. Every leaf, every rock edge, every hair is beaded. It is not raining. It is wetter than rain; the atmosphere itself precipitates onto you.

And there is nothing inside the bowl that is not alive.

At first from the rim you think you are looking at a town. That's the trick of scale — the whole place is no larger than a hamlet, a single room made by the mountain — and it has the unmistakable shape of a settlement. Roofs and walls and avenues and courtyards and a kind of vertical layering that reads as urban even before you understand what you're seeing. Then you get closer and understand: there is no lumber. There is no stonework. There is no building.

There is only plant.

The floor of the bowl should be ground. It isn't. It is a mat, a deep, saturated pack of living matter a meter or more thick, so dense you can no longer tell where one organism ends and another begins. Sphagnum and peat moss so swollen with cold water it quakes under your weight, interwoven with liverworts black-green and glossy, with cushion plants grown into domes as hard as wood, with dwarf willow and birch that never got the memo to become trees. Here at treeline, in this cold, they have given up on height and chosen density instead. They grow sideways, and down, and into each other.

The krummholz has become architecture.

What looked like houses from above are hollowed Willows — not cut, *grown* hollow. Old stems as thick as a man's torso have bowed under snow and wind until they lay down, then rooted where they touched, then arched up again, making low, vaulted tunnels and chambers. The interior walls are living wood, smooth where countless seasons of growth have polished them, lined with a fur of pale lichen that gives off the faintest light, a phosphor dimness in the greater dimness. Between these arches, the moss has been walked — or rather, compressed by growth and meltwater — into streets. Not paved. Braided. Roots the thickness of your wrist run exposed along the ground like pipes and curbs, carrying water and sap, warm to the touch despite the air. You follow a root-street and it forks, and forks again, all at that tight, efficient, city-like density where there is no wasted space.

Everything is saturated. No bare rock shows inside the rim except where the weeping cliffs drip. No soil shows. Every surface is occupied two or three times over. Shelf fungi stack like balconies on the north sides. Vines that should not survive this cold — tough, rubbery, dark red — lash the willow-houses together, making a second story, then a third, a canopy so interwoven that the cloud-drip never falls directly; it patters leaf to leaf to leaf before it reaches you. From below it looks like a thatch, from above a continuous roof. In the places where the living wall parts, you see the layers: moss on bark on moss on fungal skin on bark again, a hundred years of growth pressed into a vertical sediment.

It is a city with no tools. No axe marks, no nails, no cut ends. The rule here is simple and absolute: if you want a room, you coax it. You wait. The willows here grow fast for the cold — that's the weirdness, the slight wrongness you can't place at first — they move. Not like animals, but you can hear them if you stay quiet long enough. In the mostly-quiet, that is the sound. Not silence — pressure.

It is so quiet the cold itself has a tone, a high thin ringing in the ears from the altitude. Underneath it: drip. Constant, arrhythmic dripping from a thousand leaf-tips onto a thousand lower leaves. The soft sigh of the cloud moving against the rim. And then, if you hold your breath, the creak. The slow, fibrous creak of wood adding a ring, of a root thickening around a stone, of a cushion plant inflating a millimeter as it drinks the seep. The whole hamlet breathes once per day. At noon — though noon is only a slightly less dim grey — the mass exhales a little warmth, and the condensate lifts for an hour as mist inside the mist.

Water is everywhere but never deep. It is near-surface water, perched water. You step and your boot sinks two inches and cold, clear water wells up around the moss, then drains away through invisible channels under the peat. The cliffs weep into shallow gutters that are not gutters but the axils of giant, cupped leaves, each holding a palmful of sky-water that reflects the grey. Little ice plates form on them at night, then shatter at the first drip. Along the eastern wall a spring has found a fault and comes out warm — not hot, just a degree above freezing, enough to keep a curtain of liverwort emerald all winter, enough that a thin veil of steam rises and instantly becomes rime on every twig above it.

Nothing lives here that is not plant, almost. Rare is the word. You might wait a whole dim day and see a single white moth, torpid, clinging to lichen, or hear the faint tunneling of a vole under the peat that never shows its face. Once, a ptarmigan will drop into the bowl, stare at the impossible green abundance in this cold high place, and leave. The ecology does not need fauna. It is a pure-plant metabolism, a closed loop of moss feeding willow feeding fungus feeding moss, so packed and self-sufficient it has become its own climate.

If you stay through the afternoon the cloud will lower until the rim disappears and you are inside a room with no ceiling. The light goes even dimmer, blue-grey, and the lichen on the house-walls brightens just enough to see by. The willow branches overhead, heavy with condensed water, bow lower. Ice needles begin to form on every point. The young rock ticks as it contracts.

And you understand the scale then. You can walk from one wall to the other in three hundred paces. You can know every street-root by evening. It is small enough to hold in your mind entire — a single, saturated, cold, breathing room built without hands, high up where weather is made — and packed enough that you will never find a corner that hasn't already been lived in by something green.
