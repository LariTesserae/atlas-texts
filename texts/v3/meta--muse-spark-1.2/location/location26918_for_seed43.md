You go down.

Not deep, not into the earth where it gets cold and crushing. Just below. Just under the skin of the surface, where the light still remembers how to find you.

A stair cut from brick, not stone - every brick laid by hand, each one slightly different in its firing, some blistered dark, some pale as bone. The stairwell is narrow and turns on itself twice, the walls so close your shoulders brush both sides. The plaster there has been touched so many times it has gone glossy, burnished by palms and backs and baskets over generations. It is warm to the touch.

At the bottom, it opens.

### The Basin

It is not a cave. There was never a cave here. This is a made place, entirely made, floor to ceiling to every corner. An old reservoir, a cistern, a holding vault - something built to hold water and then kept for holding people instead. You can tell by the vaulting. A low, broad barrel-arch of brick, maybe forty strides across and three times as long, the far end lost in a haze of air. Not big on the map of the world, hamlet-sized, a single great room sunk into the ground - but inside the scale becomes complicated, because nothing is empty.

The floor is not ground. It is brick, herringboned, patched and re-patched with flat stones and fired tiles where the bricks wore through. The walls are brick, the pillars that hold the vault are brick, the ceiling is brick. There is no exposed earth anywhere, no bedrock. Even where the old structure has failed, the repair is more brick, more lime plaster, more human work. It's been here so long the edges have softened. The corners are round. The bricks at the base have eroded into shallow bowls where feet have stepped for a hundred years. The mortar has receded, leaving each brick in relief, like ribs. This is not a ruin. It is settled, weathered into equilibrium. It has found the shape it wants to keep.

And it is full.

Every vertical surface is occupied. That's the first impression after the warmth hits you - a dense, layered habitation that makes the small space feel even smaller, fuller, closer. On the pillars, wooden platforms have been lashed with rope and rawhide, one above the other, two, three stories up a single column, each platform holding a dwelling of woven reed walls and cured hides, each with its own small fire pit blackening the bricks above it. Between pillars, hammocks and rope bridges sag. Along the walls, shallow niches originally meant for maintenance tools now hold families, shrines, kitchens, workshops. Clay jars are stacked to the ceiling. Dried gourds hang in nets. Ladders - all wood, all hand-adzed, pole ladders with rungs tied on with cord - lean at every angle. No metal, or almost none. A few hammered copper nails, green with age, a few knives of chipped stone and bone. Everything is hand-work, cord and wood and fired clay.

There is light, unexpectedly, normally. Not gloom. The vault is pierced. Four or five great oculi were left open to the sky when it was built, and now they throw solid columns of daylight down into the warm air. Dust, smoke, and tiny insects turn inside the light. Where the light lands, the brick glows amber. Away from the light, the vault holds a soft, even gloom, enough to see by, lit further by small oil lamps that never go out. The light moves through the day, crawling across the floor, and the whole hamlet moves with it.

It is warm here, always. The earth keeps its day-heat and the bodies and fires keep it warmer. A damp warmth, not stifling, but close. You feel it on your skin immediately. The air is never dry.

Because water is here, but not as a lake or a flood. The basin does not hold water anymore - it holds the *memory* of water. Seepage finds its way down through the oculi when it rains above, running in dark stains down the interior of the vault, gathering in runnels cut into the floor bricks that lead to a central cistern-well, a perfect circle of black water, perhaps six feet across, fenced with low brick. The well never dries, never overflows. On dry days the moisture comes differently, as condensation. The warm air meets the slightly cooler brick and beads there, constantly. The walls sweat. Droplets gather in the mortar lines and fall - *tik, tik, tik* - onto leaves, onto jar lids, onto sleeping faces. Children hold out cupped hands to catch it. Along the base of the pillars, where the light doesn't quite reach, that seep has made its own small geography: a dark green-black felt of moss, a few brave ferns unfurling from cracks, a vine with waxy leaves that has been trained by patient hands to run along the lower arches, its tendrils finding every crack. Vegetation is sparse - there isn't soil for more - but what is here is tended, precious, deliberate. A pot of chives on a ledge. A mat of lichen someone harvests for dye.

And everything is moving. This is not a quiet, preserved place.

The fauna makes sure of it. For such a small, enclosed space, it is shockingly abundant. Pigeons and swifts rocket through the vaulted space, nesting in the high brickwork where hands can't reach, their droppings white-streaking the upper arches. Geckoes freeze flat on the warm pillars, throats puffing. From the well come the croaks of small amber frogs, and dragonflies with glass wings skitter over the surface. Cockroaches and glossy black beetles work the mortar lines by the thousands, and where there are beetles, there are women with sticks gathering them into bowls, because they are food. Swallows have built mud cups on the lip of each oculus. Bats come at dusk, folding into the high shadows. Life layers on life, overlapping.

And humans are only part of the density. You cannot stand without something brushing you - a hanging cloth, a low beam, a sleeping dog curled on warm brick, a line of laundry, a basket of onions. The sound is the proof of that saturation. The vault is loud. It catches every noise and holds it. The clack of a hand-loom from a platform above. A baby crying and the echo multiplying it. Water dripping with a sharp, ceramic ping. The cooing of pigeons amplified until it sounds like voices. Someone scraping a pot, someone coughing, frogs, wings, feet on ladders, rope creaking, the low, constant murmur of many people talking at once in a space that was made to carry sound. It is not chaotic, exactly - it is the loudness of a market, of a harbor, of a household where everyone is awake.

There is a slight wrongness to it that you notice after a minute, a low weirdness that tells you your ears are playing tricks. Because the vault is so perfectly reverberant and so damp, sounds arrive a little late, a little doubled. A drip you see fall from the ceiling seems to make its sound half a breath after it lands. A voice from the far platforms seems to come from just behind your shoulder. The warm, wet brick seems to exhale when the light shifts. The place breathes.

If you stand at the edge of the well and look around, you understand the rules here without anyone telling you.

Nothing is wasted. No space is empty. No brick is not touched.
Water is waited for, never forced.
Light is followed. When the sun moves, work moves.
What grows is shared. What flies in is allowed to stay, because it eats what would otherwise overrun you.

Someone above you shakes out a mat and dust falls like snow through a sunbeam. A frog plops into the well. A ladder creaks as a boy climbs it with a bundle of kindling on his back, bare feet finding the worn notches. The walls go on sweating, brick by brick, drop by drop, in the warm, loud, crowded, patient dark just below the world.
