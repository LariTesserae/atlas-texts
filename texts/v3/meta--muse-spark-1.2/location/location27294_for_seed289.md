It is not a cave you find.

It is a cave you are let into, once, by accident, when the weight of the ground above forgets you for a second.

You slip below surface — not deep, just under. A shoulder's width under the skin of the world. The hill above you is still there, you can feel its cold through the ceiling, the roots of the thin grass hanging down like cut threads. But you are below it now, in the pocket.

At first you think it is small. Then you realize it is *hidden* small. The kind of small that contains too much. A single room, perhaps twenty paces across if you could walk it, but you cannot walk it because there is no room to walk.

The geology here is embryonic. Unfinished.

The walls are raw and newly made, though nothing is being made anymore. This is how rock looks in the first hour after it decides to be rock — sharp, unweathered, unapologetic. Basalt and birth-quartz, not eroded but *exposed*, as if the earth cracked open its knuckle and never bled or healed. The angles are too clean. The edges have not learned to be dull. Every face is a fresh fracture, glittering with a fine dust of unfallen stone that hangs in the air without falling. There are no riverbeds, no canyons, no wear. This place was not carved. It was pried apart and then left like that, forever.

And it is bright.

Unreasonably bright for a place with no sky. The light does not come from a source. It lives in the surfaces. The quartz teeth that pack the room — floor to ceiling, wall to wall — hold light inside them and give it back, amplified. They are not transparent, they are translucent, like ice with a sun caught in it. Every crystal leans into the next, crowded, saturated. There is no space between presences here. You cannot see the actual stone of the walls because the crystals have taken every inch, growing over each other in dense, overlapping books and spears, knee-high and head-high and interlocked like fingers laced too tight. The light bounces and bounces until it has no shadows left. It is a white, cool light, the color of noon through thin cloud, and it makes the damp air shine.

Because it is damp. Not wet. Damp is the precise word. There is not enough water here to be a pool or a stream. Just intermittent moisture, a memory of rain that never quite arrives. Condensation beads on every crystal point, fat and perfect, never dripping. The air is cool — cool enough to make your breath visible for a heartbeat before the stillness swallows it — and every cold surface sweats. If you touch a crystal, your fingertip comes away wet, but the droplet reforms behind you instantly. Pockets of water hide in the low places: a shallow scallop in the stone holding a palm's depth of absolutely clear water, a seam where water has gathered like quicksilver and refuses to evaporate or overflow. Everything glistens, but nothing flows.

Where there is light and damp, there is life, but it has arranged itself strangely.

Vegetation is sparse, almost shy. No trees, no brush. Only what can live on almost nothing, in almost no soil, with almost no space. A crust of lichen — pale mint, ash grey, rust orange — paints itself directly onto the light-filled quartz, not needing sun. In the few cracks where dust has collected, tight cushions of moss grow, star-shaped and hard to the touch, like they have mineralized. A single fern, impossibly, has unfolded itself in a corner, its fronds glassy and stunted, no taller than your hand, perfectly still. It does not grow. It does not wither. It simply is, like the rock.

Fauna, though, is abundant. Obscenely abundant for such a small room. Because the room is packed, so are they.

They are small and pale and loud. Blind crickets the size of your thumb cover the crystal field, their legs the color of wax, their antennae constantly testing air that never moves. White isopods crowd under every ledge, pill-bugs with translucent shells through which you can see their bright guts. On the water-pockets, salamanders — pink-white, with external gills like frilled lace — lie motionless with their eyes filmed over, dozens of them, packed flank to flank in water that barely covers their backs. Moths that have never seen night cling to the ceiling, their wings the exact texture of quartz dust, breathing without moving. Nothing has room to flee. If you shift your weight, you will touch three living things.

And they are loud. The sound has nowhere to go. The crickets never stop stridulating, but not in chorus — in a single, unbroken, high, metallic whine that reflects off every crystal face until it seems to come from inside your teeth. The salamanders click. The water, though it never drips, makes the *sound* of dripping, a constant bright *plip* that never lands. Even the light seems to hum. It is not deafening, it is *saturated*, like the air itself is ringing at a frequency the stone likes. You have to breathe shallowly to hear yourself think.

There are marks that you were not the first to find this.

Scattered, faint. Not a settlement, not ruins. Just the suggestion that someone once thought this pocket was important. A low cairn of stacked quartz shards near the center, built with care despite the fact that there was no need for a marker in a room you can see all of at once. A shallow niche cut into the raw wall with a flat stone for sitting, worn smooth on its edge by one person's hands. A circle of small, dark river stones brought from somewhere else — impossibly out of place here — arranged around one of the water scallops, as if to keep it clean. No tools. No metal, no wire, no plastic. Nothing that hums or ticks. If these were people, they came with only their hands and left nothing that could break.

That is the strangest thing: nothing here can break, or change, or end.

This is a place in equilibrium that has forgotten how to become something else. The condensation never falls. The crickets never tire or go silent or eat each other despite being packed jaw to jaw. The fern does not photosynthesize; it just holds its shape. The dust motes in the bright air are frozen in place, each one lit like a star, not drifting. You can feel it in your own lungs — the air you inhale is cool and damp and you exhale it, but the room does not get warmer or wetter. It accepts your presence and remains exactly itself. Time has pooled here the way water has, in a shallow scallop, and stopped.

If you stay long enough, you notice the weirdness is not in any one thing but in the rules themselves. Light here has a little weight; it presses gently on exposed skin. Gravity is a suggestion; the salamanders rest lightly on water that should not hold them. Sound arrives before it is made. When you lift your hand, the crickets anticipate the shadow and quiet for half a second — before your hand has moved.

It is not hostile. It is not welcoming. It is a packed, bright, cool, damp room tucked just under the world, raw and new and already complete, humming loudly to itself in a space too small for its own abundance, waiting for nothing, keeping itself perfectly, endlessly.
