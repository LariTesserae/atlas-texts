This is a low country that wasn't here long ago.

You can tell by the edges of everything. The land is flat and wide and warm, a shallow basin kilometers across held between two distant, dark shoulders of higher ground that you can only see as a deeper dimness on the horizon. The ground itself hasn't had time to soften. It's all sharp. Fresh basalt where the basin cracked open — or was opened — still angular, still glassy in places, in terraces and steps and clean vertical cuts where water has only just begun to learn to cut. The fissures are geometric. Some run for a mile in a straight line before they jog at a perfect angle, as if the rock remembered being poured. Between the terraces the soil is thin and black and very new, still gritting with unweathered mineral. It holds heat.

There isn't much standing water. Not here. Not a sea, not a lake. This place is damp the way a breath is damp.

In the heat, moisture never quite leaves the air. It hangs low, a warm haze that beads on everything without ever quite raining. In the early hours — though hour is the wrong word where the light never fully changes — condensation gathers in silver on every surface and then runs. You see it in long vertical threads strung between structures that drink the air. Vast, almost invisible nets of filament, kilometers of them, strung between towers like harp strings, shivering slightly, collecting dew and funneling it down in slow, bright beads to cisterns at their feet. Sometimes a wind moves through and the whole basin chimes faintly, inaudibly, as the droplets fall. The only other water is what the city keeps for itself: canal cuts that are more like deliberate cracks in the fresh rock, straight and narrow and not very deep, carrying water that is strangely clear and never seems to evaporate. They fork and fork again, a sparse vascular system for a city that is mostly empty space.

Because the vegetation came fast. Or was invited.

It is everywhere the city is not, and often where the city is. This low basin should be scrub, but it is lush to the point of being insistent. Not jungle-lush, not choked. Sparse and lush together — that shouldn't work but it does here. There is so much space between things that each plant has room to become enormous, and it does. Broadleafed things with waxy, heat-dark leaves that sweat at night. Clumps of pale, fleshy reeds that grow five meters tall along the canal cuts. Vines as thick as your torso that climb nothing, that simply arch from one terrace to the next like bridges, putting down aerial roots that never reach ground. On the vertical faces of the fresh basalt, mats of black-green moss and something like lichen that glows faintly when you aren't looking directly at it have taken hold in a season. The trees — if they are trees — are solitary. One every hundred meters, or more. Wide canopies, shallow roots that spider visibly across the rock, bark that peels in papery, warm sheets. Under each one is a cool circle of shadow where smaller things grow furiously. And then, between them, open warm air and stone.

The city respects that spacing. It doesn't sprawl. It punctuates.

From where you stand on one of the higher basalt steps, you can see across the whole basin and count the structures. There are maybe thirty, maybe forty in view, and you know there are hundreds more beyond the haze, spread across the valley like stones left with intention. They are too far apart to be a city in any old sense, but there is no doubt they are one city. They are too coordinated not to be.

None of them touch the ground the way a building should. Each is anchored at a single point — a narrow stem not much wider than one of those big trees — where it meets the rock, and then it widens as it rises. Some are low and disc-shaped, hovering just meters above the basalt on a cushion of bent air you can see as heat-shimmer even when the air is still. Some are taller, lattice towers of dark material that seems to drink the dim light and give nothing back, except at their seams where a thin amber light runs like a filament inside glass. Some are nothing more than a hollow frame, a rectangular absence in the air, and yet you can see the distortion where rain that never falls would fall through it, the world on the other side slightly misaligned.

The material is wrong in a quiet way. Surfaces that should be solid look like they are made of fine, settled dust that decided to hold shape. If you get close you see they are not solid at all — they are dense swarms. Nanoscale grains holding formation, constantly trading places, repairing, breathing. A wall that has been gouged by a falling branch fills itself in while you watch, without hurry. A walkway extends itself a few centimeters because someone thought about crossing. The city is not built. It is held. The physics holding it is an old argument now, something about mass lending and vacuum scaffolding, and the details no longer matter to the people who live here, if those shapes moving slowly between towers are people anymore.

The light is dim. Not dark, dim — a perpetual late afternoon that has forgotten evening. The sky is not a sky but a low, warm overcast that never breaks, not clouds but a luminous membrane, high and pearlescent and depthless. It gives off light rather than letting it through, a sourceless amber-grey that makes no shadows or too many soft ones at once. You can look directly at it without hurting your eyes. Because of it, colors are muted except for greens, which are impossibly saturated, and the amber seam-light of the structures, which pulses very slowly, out of sync from tower to tower, like breathing. At a distance the towers look darker than the sky behind them, cutouts. Up close, the swarm-surfaces reflect the sky incorrectly, a fraction of a second delayed.

It is mostly quiet. For a place so active, it should be noisy.

But the processes here don't make much sound. The vegetation grows audibly if you put your ear close — a faint creak and sigh as leaves unfurl, as roots prise at fresh rock with patient, hydraulic strength. The filament nets tick as condensate slides down them. The city hums at the very edge of hearing, not a mechanical hum but a pressure hum, the sound of air being politely told to behave differently around those hovering discs. Sometimes a whole tower will shift a few meters laterally, silently, rings of dust rising around its stem where the rock readjusts, and settle again. There are no engines. There are no voices carried on the wind, though you occasionally see a figure on a high balcony, very still, looking out.

Fauna is rare, and shy of the quiet. You could spend a day and see nothing move except plants and buildings. Then, in the corner of your eye, a white glider — not a bird, too many joints in the wing — will detach from the underside of a floating disc where it had been mistaken for shadow, and sail for a long time without flapping between the solitary trees, landing on a vine-bridge and folding itself into it. Things live in the canals, pale and eyeless, that surface only to sip air and vanish. At night — if the dimming can be called night, when the sky membrane lowers its output by a few percent and the seam-lights become more prominent — you hear a single call, high and glassy, repeated at long intervals, moving slowly around the perimeter of the basin. Something large, alone, making its circuit.

The rules here are not written, but you learn them quickly by being in the place:

One: Nothing is dense. Not the city, not the forest, not the air. Everything is given room to be large and alone, and everything prefers it that way. Crowding is a kind of failure.

Two: Things grow together. The vine will grow over the swarm-wall and the wall will accommodate it, forming a hollow for it without breaking. The condensation net will collect water for the reeds and the reeds will shade the cistern. The city does not clear land. It threads.

Three: The ground is young and it remembers being made. Do not trust a sharp edge to stay sharp, or a flat place to stay flat. The basin is still settling into the shape it was told to have. Sometimes at noon — the brightest dim — you can feel a low tremor pass underfoot and see, far off, a new terrace shiver into existence along a perfectly straight fault, exposing rock that steams slightly in the damp warmth.

Four: Light is rationed, sound is rationed, water is rationed, but not ungenerously. There is enough of each if you are patient. If you stand still long enough in the warm, dim, quiet, the city will extend a small lip of itself toward you to catch the dew for you, and a broad leaf nearby will tilt to shade you a little more.

You stand on the fresh basalt and look out across the open, scattered, impossible valley-city, where the warm air beads on your skin and the distant towers breathe amber, and somewhere between two solitary trees a white wing silently opens and closes again.
