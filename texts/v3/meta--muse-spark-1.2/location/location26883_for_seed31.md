It is high up. That is the first thing you understand, before you see it.

The air is clear and thin enough that light lands without diffusion, but there is plenty of water in it - you can feel it on your skin as a warm, faint pressure. The plateau runs away in every direction until it cuts itself off in air. Below the edge there is nothing but a sea of cloud held far down in the valleys, white and level like a second ground. Above, the sky is a normal blue, warm, high-sun blue with soft-built cumulus that never quite gathers into storm. The sun is ordinary and strong.

This is not an old mountain. It is young in the way a broken plate is young - the edges are still sharp. The whole tableland looks like it was lifted recently, a single block of dark basalt and paler limestone thrust up fresh from below. The cliffs at the rim are vertical and stepped, unweathered, still showing the clean, almost chiselled lines where the rock sheared. There are no softened slopes, no scree aprons worn smooth. Where the plateau has cracked it has cracked cleanly, leaving slot canyons with walls like cut walls, and sinkholes that drop straight down to water. The stone near the surface is pocked and porous; water finds it easily.

And water is everywhere. Not as lakes or rivers, exactly. There is no central river. Instead the plateau weeps.

At this elevation you would expect aridity, cold, wind-scoured rock, but it is warm here - equationally warm, a steady late-summer warmth that holds day and night with very little change. The warmth lifts water constantly from the cloud sea below and from the stone itself. It condenses on every cool surface at night and by mid-morning drips back. The rock weeps. The cliffs weep in dark vertical streaks. The flat ground holds water just under a thin skin of soil, so that anywhere you press a foot you can see it darken and well up. Springs break without warning from basalt seams, cold and perfectly clear, and run a few dozen meters before disappearing again into the porous ground.

The town is built around this fact.

It is not dense. From any high point you can see the whole settlement spread out across perhaps two valleys' worth of plateau, and still see more empty plateau beyond it. That is the organization of it: there is a lot of space left intentionally empty. Houses are low, one or two stories, built of the same stone they stand on - the dark basalt cut by hand into blocks, the limestone dressed paler for corners and lintels. Roofs are thatch or split stone slab. Between them are wide, unpaved intervals of open rock, of shallow marsh, of garden. No building crowds another.

What connects them is water-work.

Because the technology here is hand-work - hammerstone, copper chisel, wooden wedge, fire-cracked rock, cord and lever - everything is built by moving the material that is already at hand, without mortar more complex than lime, without metal beyond simple tools. But the people have had time to be precise. The whole town is threaded with canals.

Not grand aqueducts. Shallow, stone-lined runnels, a forearm wide, a knee deep, cut with patient, repeating strokes into the plateau itself. They follow contours with a craftsman's exactness, carrying the weeping springs from their emergence points along the high ridges, around houses, through garden terraces, into marsh basins, and finally to the cisterns cut as square mouths into the rock. Sluice gates are simple slabs of stone that can be lifted or set. You can hear, if you stand still, the faint tick and trickle of water moving everywhere at once, a low engineered murmur.

Those garden terraces and marsh basins are where the green lives.

Vegetation is not a blanket. The density of life is sparse - there are long stretches of bare, damp black rock with only lichen powdering it - but where it does take hold it is lush to the point of excess. In every place water lingers, it erupts. The canals are bordered by dense lines of broad-leafed taro and reed, their leaves waxy and dripping. The shallow marshes between neighborhoods are thick mats of green, sphagnum and fern and high, soft grasses that hide standing water up to the ankle. On the windward edges where condensation collects, moss grows inches thick on the stone walls themselves, a saturated, emerald felt that drips continuously. Palm-like cycads stand in clusters near the cisterns, and fruiting vines have been trained over every house wall. It is all very green, very wet, very alive - but it is patchy, like islands of jungle in a sea of stone and water and air, with clear empty space between each island.

And there are no animals.

That is the quiet.

At first you think you just haven't noticed them. Then you realize you have been listening for a long time and you have heard no insect hum, no bird call, no rustle in the reeds that is not water or wind. There are no midges over the marshes. No birds cross the open sky above the plateau. The cisterns hold no fish. The thatch holds no nesting. The green grows without being eaten, so it grows thick and untended at its edges, falling over itself and rotting back into peat where it stands. The dead leaves fall straight down and soften in water without being carried off.

Because of that, the town is profoundly quiet. The sound of the place is entirely mineral and vegetable and meteorological. Wind moving unbroken across the open table, across a scale big enough that you can see weather coming for an hour before it arrives. Water ticking in stone channels. The occasional soft knock of a wooden sluice being moved by a person. The drip from moss to pool. Footsteps on wet stone. Human voices carry a long way and then stop, because there is nothing else to answer them.

Nothing much changes here. That is the other rule of the place.

This is a system in equilibrium. The springs emit the same volume every day. The condensation in the morning equals the evaporation in the afternoon. The canals hold their level. The marshes neither flood nor dry; the water stays at the same line on the same stones year after year, marked by the same dark stain and the same green edge. The clouds below stay at the same altitude. The stone, being young, has not yet begun to crumble or soften; the sharp edges stay sharp. Houses are repaired stone for stone when needed, but the plan of the town does not expand. People farm the same terraces, open and close the same sluices.

You can stand on the rim where the plateau cuts off, with warm air rising from the cloud sea below and cool water seeping around your feet, and look back across the whole open extent - the scattered roofs, the silver threads of canals, the sudden lush basins of green between expanses of wet, empty rock, all held high and level under a normal sun - and feel the stability of it as a physical thing. The plateau will be weeping tomorrow in exactly the same places, in exactly the same way.
