This place is a basin that has forgotten how to be a valley.

It is very old. Not old like mountains are old - old like a palm is old, the lines worn smooth. The land here has eroded until it gave up on height. Elevation is low, barely a hundred feet of relief across the whole pocket, a shallow dish of stone so ancient it has settled into its final shape. The bedrock is exposed in places, not jagged but buffed, pink-grey gneiss and sandstone worn down to broad, low domes like the backs of sleeping animals, with a thin skin of laterite and clay over everything else. You can see the grain of the rock flowing under the soil. The last hills eroded away a long time ago and the soil that remains is deep, rust-red, exhausted and fertile all at once.

It is warm. Not dry warm, but wet-season warm, the air thick and bright at 30 degrees even in the shade. The light is overwhelmingly bright, a white-gold light that comes straight down through a thin, high atmosphere and makes everything over-exposed. There are no deep shadows. Even under the trees the light bounces off the water and the pale stone and fills in the dark. The sky is pale blue, almost white at noon, with high cirrus that condenses and evaporates again without ever really clouding over.

And it is wet. Not underwater, but never dry. Water is at or just under the surface everywhere. It weeps out of the rock itself. The low domes of stone sweat continuously, condensation beading on their cool surfaces and running in slow trickles into shallow pans and rills. Between the domes, the ground is marsh-edge, spongy. Every footstep sinks a half inch and fills with tea-colored water. After rain - and it rains briefly, almost every afternoon for twenty minutes - the whole basin holds a sheet of water an inch deep that reflects the sky perfectly for an hour before draining away through invisible cracks. The air smells of warm stone and iron and bruised leaves. The humidity is so high that your skin never quite dries.

Vegetation is lush but not crowded. This is not jungle. It is sparse, scattered - a savanna of a very old world. Huge trees stand far apart, perhaps one every thirty or forty feet, so you can always see the horizon. They are not like any trees you know, though they rhyme with them. Thick, bottle-shaped trunks the color of pale ash, storing water, with smooth bark that peels in papery sheets. Their canopies are wide and flat-topped, like acacias, leaves small and waxy and bright green, filtering that fierce light into a dappled lace. Between them is not grass but a dense, low mat of ferns and liverworts and something like sphagnum, ankle-deep, springy, incredibly green, studded with waxy white and orange flowers that open only at midday. Vines hang straight down from the flat canopies like plumb lines, not tangled, just hanging, tipped with water-collecting cups.

What was built here is scattered and mostly gone. There is a hamlet's worth of structure and then nothing. You will walk for five minutes through fern and water and find a low wall, waist-high, made of a material that is not stone and not metal - matte black, warm to the touch, seamless, faintly vibrating. It runs for twenty feet and then stops, cut clean as if the rest was erased. Further on, a pavilion the size of a small house stands on six slender legs that barely touch the ground, holding a platform ten feet up. No stairs, no doors. Underneath, the marsh grows undisturbed. The platform's underside is mirrored and you can see the sky reflected in it, but the reflection is delayed by half a second. In another clearing there are three pillars, each as tall as a person, leaning slightly toward each other, covered in shallow script that rearranges itself when you are not looking directly at it. No roads connect them. No ruins suggest a city. It is as if a few tools were left behind after the work was finished, and the workers never came back.

The tech is not like machines. You only notice it because physics is behaving politely. Water flows uphill for a few feet where it meets one of the black walls, then remembers gravity and continues down. Dust motes hang in the air in perfect hex grids at certain hours, then scatter. The warm air over the weeping rocks shimmers, and if you look through the shimmer you see the rock from angles you shouldn't be able to see, as if space is folded a few degrees. The pavilion repairs itself - a branch falls on it, and by morning the dent is gone. Nothing hums. Nothing blinks. The engineering is so far beyond moving parts that it has become landscape again. It doesn't announce itself. It just keeps the basin stable, the water table high, the temperature even, the light bright but not burning. It has been doing so for a very long time without intervention.

And the fauna. The fauna is overwhelming.

At first you think the place is empty because it is quiet. The sound level is low - mostly quiet, a hush that makes you speak softly without knowing why. No birdsong. No croaking chorus. Then you look closer and realize everything is alive and everything is watching.

The air is full. Not with birds, but with things that look like birds made of translucent membrane and filament - kites the size of your hand, then the size of an eagle, drifting on thermals without ever beating anything that could make a sound. They are so pale they are invisible against the bright sky until they bank. There are hundreds of them at any given moment. Closer down, the space between the trees is thick with dragonfly-like things, but their wings are silent. They move by some means that doesn't disturb the air, hanging motionless and then displacing. Some are as long as your forearm, iridescent black-blue, with heads that turn to track you independently of their bodies. On the ground, the fern mat rustles though there is no wind - because under it, beetles the size of tortoises push slowly through the peat, their domed backs covered in living moss so they look like moving hummocks. Small, lemur-like things with six limbs and eyes like polished amber move through the flat canopies in troops, but they do not call. They look at you, pass food to each other hand-to-hand, and continue.

In the shallow pans of water, things like axolotls but as big as dogs lie with frilled gills waving slowly, utterly still except for that. Their skin is the same pink-grey as the bedrock. If you stand still for a minute, you will see the water itself is alive - not with fish, but with ribbons, translucent eels of light that swim without displacing water, leaving faint after-images. At night - though it never really gets dark here, just a deep blue dusk - the whole basin glows faintly from the combined bioluminescence of everything in the leaf litter, but still without sound.

The strangeness is not that any one thing is strange. It is that the rules are slightly off-center. Predation still happens, and you see it, but it is silent and fast and oddly considerate. A membrane-kite drops, envelops a six-limbed climber, and they both drift to the ground where the kite dissolves around its prey without struggle. A beetle-hummock opens down the middle to reveal a mouth and grazes on the fern mat, and the ferns regrow visibly within minutes. Everything eats, grows, dies, but nothing rots. Fallen leaves thin into transparency and vanish. A dead kite on the peat flattens, its filaments unraveling into the sphagnum, and by afternoon it is gone without smell.

The place feels small and bounded. If you walk in any direction for fifteen minutes, you come to the rim - a low circle of those same buffed domes, just high enough to hide the world beyond. From the rim you can see the next basin over, and the next, each with its own pavilion, its own scattering of black walls, its own silent, teeming life, repeating under the same bright light. They are not valleys. They are rooms. Cells in a honeycomb that this ancient plain has become, tended by technology that no longer needs tending, filled with a lush, quiet, overwhelming abundance that has learned to be sparse in order to last forever.

You could stay here. The water is clean where it weeps from the rock. The warm air never chills. The things that live here would move around you if you sat down among the ferns, not afraid, just making space. The pillars would continue their slow rearrangement. The light would stay bright.
