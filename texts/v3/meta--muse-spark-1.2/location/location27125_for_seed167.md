It is not an ocean you look at. It is an ocean you are inside.

Warm, and dark, and without a surface. There is no sky here, or if there is it is so far above that it has ceased to matter. The water is not cold and crushing in the way deep water is supposed to be — it is bath-warm, body-warm, a held temperature that never breaks. It holds you. You sink very slowly, even though you are heavy, because the water is thick with life and with dissolved things, particulate and salt and silt and spawn, until it feels less like swimming and more like being suspended in a dense, breathing broth.

The light never comes. This low down, in this basin, the sun is a rumor. What illumination there is does not fall — it is made. Here and there, in no pattern you can map, a cold amber bead opens and closes on a stalk. A seam of pale blue runs along a ridge for a while and then goes out. The creatures themselves make what little light there is, and it is not enough to see by, only enough to see that there is something to see. Your eyes, after a long time, adjust to a darkness that is not absolute, but deep violet-black, warm-black, water that has forgotten light.

The bottom is low. Lower than low — a broad, dish-shaped depression that goes down and down until the water pressure becomes a kind of weather. The terrain is not jagged. This place has been here a very long time. Everything is rounded, settled, weathered into its final shape. There are no sharp cliffs, no fresh breaks. Instead there are soft terraces and broad bowls and domes, all worn smooth by millennia of slow current. The ground is not rock, not really — it is a deep silt of white and grey mud, fine as ash, made of countless skeletons compressed, and underneath that, limestone that has been bored and tunneled and rebored until it is more hole than stone.

And on that ground, the city.

It is not built. It was grown. There is no toolmark anywhere, no cut, no joint, no worked stone or metal, because there are no tools here at all. And yet it is unmistakably a city — organized, dense, layered, continuous. It stretches out beyond where your dim sight can follow, a town, a valley of architecture that has no architect.

At first, from a distance, it looks like a reef, and it is, but that word is too wild. This is ordered. The builders are the inhabitants themselves. Colonies of polyps, of tube-worms thick as your arm, of coral animals that secrete and secrete without ceasing, have raised towers and walls and arches and causeways. Sponges the size of houses have inflated beside them, their bodies forming cisterns and halls, their oscula opening like doors and windows. Barnacle-cities stack their white, mineral huts one atop another in dense tenements that climb twenty, thirty feet high, each chamber inhabited, each chamber adding its own thin lip of calcium to the whole. There are avenues that are not streets but gaps left deliberately between growths, where the current has been trained to flow. There are bridges that are not bridges but fused limbs of living coral, grown toward each other over centuries until they met and grafted.

The material of it all is bone and shell and chitin and mucus hardened to cement. Everything is porous. Everything is faintly alive, even the walls. If you touch a tower it retracts, just a millimeter. If you stay still long enough you can feel it adding to itself, a gritty accretion at the edge of sight.

Vegetation is scarce. This is not a kelp forest. A few things that are almost plants cling where they can — dark red fans, thin as paper, that shiver in the current; felt-like films of algae that coat the upper surfaces of the deadest domes and make them slick; occasional stalks that rise like drowned trees, bare and whiplike, with no leaves at all. But they are outnumbered a thousand to one. This is an animal's city.

And it is full.

The density is bewildering. The water is crowded. There is no empty volume here; every cubic foot is occupied or crossed. Clouds of shrimp, each no larger than a grain of rice, move like smoke through the avenues, parting around you and rejoining. Shoals of blind, white fish turn as one, their flanks brushing the walls. Crabs pick along the boulevards, their legs clicking on shell. Anemones open and close along every ledge, their tentacles waving. Worms extend feathery red gills from every hole in every wall and then snap shut. Larger things move slower above — long, flat rays that glide just over the rooftops, filtering, and things like jellyfish but with bells hard as glass, pulsing without hurry, carrying whole constellations of lesser creatures on their backs.

It is mostly quiet. Water eats sound. What you hear is muffled and close, as if heard through hands held over your ears. A low, omnipresent sigh of current moving through the porous masonry. The soft *tick* of a crab's claw. The sudden, hydraulic *whuff* of a sponge expelling water. Far off, a low pulse, almost below hearing, that might be many fish beating their tails at once, or the city itself breathing. When a predator moves — and predators do move here, unhurried, a long eel-like body sliding between towers, a great cone shell opening its foot — there is no splash, no chase cry. A silence deepens, a cloud of shrimp vanishes around a corner, and then the silence eases again.

The ecology is active, incessant, but not frantic. This is not a young reef racing to grow. This is old, established, in balance. Nothing grows fast. Everything grows always. A shrimp cleans an anemone. The anemone feeds the coral beneath it. The coral excretes the sand that becomes the street. A fish scrapes algae from a wall and in doing so smooths the wall. A dying sponge collapses and its body becomes foundation for two new ones. Current brings warm, nutrient-rich water from somewhere beyond the basin rim — you can feel it as a slow, warm tide that comes and goes without a moon — and the whole valley inhales. Then it exhales. Particulate snow drifts down forever from above, a never-ending rain of detritus from a distant light-world, feeding everything.

There is something slightly wrong here, or rather, slightly *other*. Gravity is lenient. Things hang a little too long before they settle. The arches lean at angles that should not hold, but do, because they are alive and correct themselves. Some of the towers are hollow and you can see, through their translucent walls, a faint peristalsis, a slow squeezing of water and young through their cores, as if the city digests. The avenues sometimes rearrange — not quickly, but if you leave and come back, a passage that was open is now sealed with new growth, and a new one has opened two arches down. It is a place that knows how to be a place without ever being finished.

It extends beyond you. If you swim to what looks like the edge, you find not an edge but more — another district, another morphology. Here the towers are thin and fluted like organ pipes, there they are low and domed and encrusted with oysters. The warm dark goes on, and the city goes on with it, town-sized, valley-sized, too large to hold in one glance, too dense to ever be empty.

You are warm. You are in the dark. You are surrounded on all sides by walls that were never made, only lived into being, and by thousands of lives layered upon lives, all busy, all quiet, all continuing their slow, ancient work of keeping the city alive by being the city.
