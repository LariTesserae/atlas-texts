It is a room that forgot it was a room.

Not a forest, not a building — a built grove. A single, bounded place about the size of a hamlet's commons, or a cathedral if a cathedral had been laid on its side and left to grow. You can see its edges from anywhere inside it, but you can never quite hold them in your head at the same time.

### The Terrain

There is no ground here. Not really.

The floor is made. Plank over plank over plank, fitted tongue-and-groove from some pale, resinous wood that never grew in this place, laid down over a subfloor of fired clay tiles, which are laid over a subfloor of woven reed mats, which are laid over more wood. It's low — a shallow basin, maybe two meters below the outer sill — so the whole grove sits in a gentle, deliberate hollow, like a bowl made to hold something. It holds warm air instead.

Nothing is eroded. Everything is young and sharp. The cut-ends of the planks still show saw teeth. The clay tiles still have thumbprints in their glaze where they were pressed. The joinery is all primitive and visible: wooden pegs swollen tight, lashings of oiled cord, mortise and tenon, adobe fillets where wall meets floor. No nails. No welds. Hand tools only. You can see the chisel marks on every beam, the unevenness of a drawknife's pass. The corners are crisp enough to catch cloth. This place was made recently, or it remakes itself to seem recent. Dust hasn't had time to round anything.

And there is nowhere that is not made. Even where you expect earth there is more making: the "soil" is a deep, soft mat of shredded fiber, coir and straw and felted wool and hair, dyed dark brown and laid down in dense, damp seeming layers. It is not soil. Nothing decays into it. It was combed and carded and spread.

There is no water. Not a trickle, not a pool, not a spring. The air is warm — a steady, bathhouse-warm, late-summer-afternoon warm that lives in the wood and the fiber and makes the resin sweat a faint, sweet smell. But it is a dry warmth. If you press your palm to the fiber-mat you expect cool moisture and find only a dry, almost waxy give, like pressing a palm to a thick rug left in the sun. The environment is sealed. Above, the roof is a vault of translucent paper — countless sheets of oiled parchment stretched over a lattice of bent saplings — diffusing normal, even daylight with no visible sun. Light falls straight down, shadowless at noon, ambering a little toward evening without a sunset. The humidity is near zero. Your lips dry. The plants should be dead.

### The Vegetation

They are not dead. They are lush.

It is a jungle of made-things that behave like plants. From every made surface — from seams between floorboards, from the walls which are themselves just vertical looms of wattle and daub, from the underside of the vault — growth erupts, dense and layered and near-touching.

But look closer and the lushness is impossible.

The leaves are not leaves. They are cut and hammered from thin bark, from beaten tin and copper so thin you can see light through it, from folded paper and starched linen and lacquered palm sheaths, all in greens so saturated they look wet. Veins are stitched with thread. Edges are fringed with frayed fiber to mimic serration. Some are broad as dinner plates, overlapping like shingles. Some are needle-fine and hang in curtains from the vault, brushing your head and shoulders as you pass. Vines are braided rope, unwinding themselves slowly along the beams, putting out new tendrils that are simply more rope, fraying open into leaf-shapes at the tip. Flowers are knots.

And they are alive. Not metaphorically. They swell. They track the diffused light. They open and close on a slow cycle — a full bloom takes three or four days, you can watch a petal of hammered copper relax by a millimeter an hour if you sit still long enough. They do not drink water. They drink the warm air and the light. Their undersides are coated in a fine, dry wax that smells faintly of beeswax and hot metal; if you brush them, your fingers come away with a dusting of pollen that is not pollen but fine mineral powder, iron-red and ochre, that they exude and reabsorb. There are no roots in any earth. The "roots" are just more braiding, stitching themselves into the fiber-mat, into the cracks between tiles, anchoring with wooden toggles. Lift one and the floor beneath is still perfect, unbroken — the plant was never penetrating, only pretending to, and the pretense was enough.

It's dense. Claustrophobically, beautifully dense. The grove is not spacious. You move sideways through it. Leaves press against your arms, your back. The air between growths is narrow, branching, rejoining. You cannot see more than five or six meters in any direction because there is always another curtain of foliage, another trellis of gourd-vines made from gourds that were never grown but carved from wood and hung. Everything is within arm's reach of something else.

### The Fauna

And it is full of animals. Abundantly, impossibly full for a place with no water to drink.

They are small and mostly quiet — that is the rule here: 0.8/3 quiet, so that the lushness seems silent.

Beetles the size of your thumb, their carapaces made from polished lacquered wood, clicking very softly as they traverse leaf to leaf, unfolding wings that are clearly paper and yet lift them. Moths with wingspans as wide as your hand, cut from felt, dusted with that same mineral pollen, hanging utterly still until you breathe near them and then relocating without a sound. Lizards of woven leather and tiny, hammered scales that run up vertical wattle without regard for gravity, pausing to bask in the warm light, throats pulsing slowly. Nests — dozens — tucked into every fork of the rope-vines, made from the same fiber as the floor but inhabited by finches whose feathers are individually stitched, who open beaks lined with undyed cotton and make no song.

They don't drink either. They rasp at the wax on the leaves, they chew the mineral pollen, they lick condensation that never condenses. When one dies, it does not rot. It dries further, stiffens, and is quietly dismantled by smaller things — mites like animated grains of sawdust — and its materials are rewoven into a new leaf, a new beetle. Nothing is lost. Water was never part of the cycle.

If you sit very still for a long time, you hear them. Not a chorus. Isolated, patient sounds: the tick of a beetle's foot on tin-leaf, the soft creak of a vine-braid tightening as it grows a centimeter lower, the faint rustle of felt wings. The dominant sound is the building itself settling — wood sighing in the warmth.

### The Rules, The Wrongness

This is where the weirdness lives. 2.9/3 is not "strange plants." It is wrong geometry, a quiet breakdown of what a place is allowed to be.

First: Scale and distance do not hold. The grove is small. You can see the far wattle wall from where you stand. But walk toward it through the dense growth — pushing aside copper leaves — and it takes longer than it should. Ten steps become twenty, the wall seems to recede a little, not out of malice but out of inattention, as if space is loosely stitched here and the stitches are a little slack. Turn around and the place you came from is suddenly closer than it could be. The room is the size of a room, but the path through it is the size of a forest.

Second: The built and the grown are not separate categories. A beam will, over the course of a slow afternoon, put out a bud at a joint. A leaf will harden at its stem into true wood, indistinguishable from the trellis it hangs from. You cannot tell where carpentry ends and botany begins because the distinction was never enforced. Things become each other when you aren't looking directly.

Third: Gravity is polite but inconsistent. Dust motes hang a little too long before falling. A fallen felt-moth on the fiber-mat may be on the ceiling tomorrow, without having flown. Vines hang down, but a few hang sideways, at a respectful 90 degrees, and no one corrects them.

Fourth: There is no outside. The oiled-paper vault lets in light but shows no sky. The walls, if you part the wattle and look between the daub, show only more wattle — another layer, and another. The place is total. Everything is made. There is no natural substrate beneath the making, no bedrock, no outside weather. The warm, dry air is the same air that was sealed in when the last tile was laid, endlessly recirculated through leaf and lung and fiber. It has never been new.

And it is slow. So slow. Growth is by fractions of millimeters per hour. A beetle crosses a leaf over the course of a morning. The light in the vault dims so gradually you only notice you can see your hands less well and then realize an hour has passed. Change happens on the scale of days, not moments. This patient dynamic makes the wrongness harder to notice — you have to be quiet, warm, and attentive to see the wall breathe outward by an inch while you watch a flower made of knotted cord open.

If you stay long enough — and the warm, dense, quiet grove invites staying — you start to feel the intended logic: this is a garden made by hands that did not have water to spare, so they made a garden that does not need it. A primitive technology of absolute craft, where every leaf was cut and every creature stitched, and then — because the making was so thorough, so loving, so dense — the made things learned to be lush on their own, on light and warm air and attention alone. A sealed world that decided to be alive anyway, without ever solving the problem of water, by simply refusing to require it.

You can lie down on the dry, soft fiber-mat. Leaves of tin and linen brush your face. A leather lizard watches you from a beam that was a branch a week ago. The light is even and normal and kind. Somewhere above you, the paper vault glows. Somewhere beyond the walls, there is nothing — or more walls — and it doesn't matter, because here is everything, small and total and warm and utterly, quietly, alive in a way that should not work.
