It is noon and it has always been noon.

You are standing low. Not low on a hill, low the way a bowl is low — a broad, shallow basin that holds heat. The air does not move. It hangs. It is bright the way water is bright, too much light to hold in one place, spilling over everything. The sky is white, not blue, a white that has burned through its own color. There is no shadow directly under anything, as if the light is coming from everywhere at once, from the sky and from the wet brick and from the leaves themselves.

The ground is not ground.

That is the first thing you have to understand. From the horizon to the horizon, edge to edge of the valley, there is no soil, no bedrock, no natural shore. Everything underfoot is made. A floor the size of a country, laid down by hands.

It is not a city the way you know a city. It is a weave. A platform. Low terraces of sun-baked mud brick still holding the sharp thumb-prints of the makers, and between them, mats and lattices of split river-cane and palm fiber, lashed with vine cord, overlapping in sheets so vast you could walk for a day and never step off them. The cane is still green-gold, fresh-cut, the ends splinter-white and sharp. The brick has not yet rounded. This whole place is young. It has not had time to be worn down. Every corner is a right angle. Every channel is cut clean. You can see where the adze slipped, where a hand smoothed the mortar. It smells of fresh clay and cut sap.

And yet — no one could have built this much, this fast, with these tools. Wooden mallets and chipped stone blades lie abandoned on the walkways. Simple looms. Stacks of unbaked brick sweating in the heat. But the work extends beyond reason, beyond any workforce. The terraces tile outward in repeating, perfect geometry until perspective flattens them into a haze. If you count the irrigation squares on your left, and then count them again coming back, the number is different.

Water is everywhere without being deep anywhere.

It doesn't flow in rivers. It seeps. It weeps out of the brick itself, darkening the clay to rust-red and then to black. It beads on the underside of every woven platform and falls, endlessly, in slow, fat drops that never make a splash. It collects in the shallow, hand-shaped basins — a foot deep, no more — that checker the entire basin. Each basin is a paddy, a cistern, a garden. The water in them is warm as blood, still as glass, and skin-thick with green. Condensation runs down every vertical surface even though nothing is cool. The air itself seems to perspire. You breathe and the air is sweet and metallic on your tongue.

From this made-water, everything grows.

Not politely. Not in rows, though the hands that built this place clearly tried for rows. The vegetation is lush to the point of disobedience. Hothouse ferns as big as doors punch through the cane lattice from below, their fiddleheads uncurling and then — stopping. Elephant ears with leaves broad enough to lie down on. Mangroves that were planted as stakes for scaffolding have taken root through the floor and exploded into trees, their prop roots bursting the brickwork. Their leaves are waxy, dusted white with salt and light. Fruits hang heavy and ripe — gourds, figs, a kind of pale melon — swollen, split open, fragrant, wasped, but none of it rots. Wires of morning glory have stitched the whole weave together in a net of white and violet trumpets, all open at once.

It should be a jungle. It should be choking. But it is sparse.

That is the wrongness. The abundance is spread thin across too much space. A colossal banyan stands alone in its perfect square of water, its canopy a complete world, and then a hundred empty squares of empty water around it. A thicket of cane and flowering reed will crowd one terrace so densely you cannot see the weave beneath it, and then the next terrace over is bare brick, sun-bleached, holding nothing but a single bowl of water and its perfect reflection. The built world is total, but the living world has not filled it. Or it has chosen not to. There is so much room between presences that each plant, each tree, each half-collapsed hut of thatch and bent pole feels like an island.

The huts are everywhere and nowhere. Primitive, empty. Four posts, a roof of woven palm that is still green. Inside, a hearth of laid stones, cold. A clay jar. A sleeping mat. No people. Or, no people right now. The tools are left mid-task as if everyone just stood up and walked away between one heartbeat and the next.

Because the animals remain. Abundantly.

You notice them only when you stop trying to look. The water basins are alive to their edges — small scaled fish the color of light, thousands of them, hanging motionless in the warm water, facing the same direction, not swimming. Amphibians with translucent throats that do not inflate. Clouds of dragonflies with wings like mica, hovering in place, their wings blurred but their bodies absolutely still in the air, as if pinned there. On the walkways, herons stand on one leg, eyes half-closed. Long green snakes are looped over the cane rails, not moving. In the great leaves above, you hear the bulk of something — monkeys, or fruit bats, or birds with heavy chests — shifting, rustling, but when you look there is only the leaf, trembling slightly.

None of them make sound. That is the profound quiet.

It isn't silence. Silence is an absence. This is a held breath. The water drips — *tok* — from weave to basin. *Tok.* Seven seconds later. *Tok.* A fig splits somewhere with a soft pop and does not fall. A dragonfly wing ticks. Otherwise the hot, bright, wet air absorbs everything. Your own footsteps on the cane are muffled, as if the weave is listening and not answering back. The heat should be full of insects, of chorus. Instead the valley holds its equilibrium like a bowl of water held perfectly level.

And here is the breakdown, the place where your mind snags:

Nothing is changing.

The heat is extreme, the light is merciless, the water is shallow, open to the sky — it should evaporate in an hour. It doesn't. The level in each basin never drops, though the brick weeps endlessly. The fruit is ripe to bursting but never falls, never spoils. The ferns are frozen mid-unfurl. The sun does not move. When you stared at the white sky you thought it was cloud, but it isn't. It is brightness itself, a depthless, sourceless glare. There are no shadows to tell time. If you pour water from one basin to another, for a moment it flows uphill to get there, and then settles, perfectly still again.

If you try to break a piece of fresh cane, it bends and then remembers its shape. If you press your hand into the warm mud brick, the print stays sharp for a long time and then, very slowly, smooths itself out, as if the brick exhales.

Time is not passing here. Not frozen like ice. Held like a breath. The whole valley — this impossible, hand-made, hand-woven lowland the size of a province, built by simple tools in a time that never happened, overgrown and empty and teeming — is in perfect stasis. It is wet and hot and bright and alive and utterly, quietly, never-ending.

You walk a little further, along a straight causeway of lashed cane that goes to the hazy center and does not get any closer. The water weeps. The leaves hold their green light. Somewhere a heron blinks, very, very slowly.
