It is an ocean that should not be where it is.

Not at the bottom of the world, in a basin, but at the top — a sea perched on a height so vast it has its own weather, its own horizon curve. A continent of stone lifted into the cool air, kilometers above where any ocean has any right to be, and drowned anyway.

The plateau comes first, even before the water. You feel it in the pressure in your ears, in the way the light is clean and unfiltered. The air above the water is thin and cold, 8 or 10 degrees, the kind of cool that doesn't bite but seeps. Normal daylight — a white-yellow sun at a normal angle — but it has further to fall here, through thin atmosphere and then through water. By the time it reaches the stone, it is scattered and moving.

The stone itself is new.

This is not old earth. There are no soft hills, no river valleys worn smooth over eons, no beaches. The basalt is raw, embryonic. It cooled not long ago on a geological clock — glass-black in places, still holding a faint warmth deep in its mass if you put your hand to it, though the water above has already chilled its skin. It is blocky and stepped, great hexagonal columns and sudden cliffs and flatiron plains where the lava froze mid-flow, like a poured floor that never had time to be lived on. Edges are sharp enough to cut. There is no sand. Sand has not been invented here yet. When the water moves — and it never stops moving — it drags over these raw edges and makes not a hiss but a grinding, low keen, as if the planet is still filing itself down for the first time.

And the water is everything.

It is not a sea *on* the plateau. It *is* the plateau's sky, its atmosphere. From the thin-air surface down to the stone, thirty, fifty, a hundred meters deep depending on where the underlying topography steps, it is continuous, all-liquid. To stand on the highest column tops is to stand neck-deep. To step off is to be submerged. There is no shoreline in any familiar sense; the ocean simply ends where the plateau ends, a sheer wall of water that meets the sky. At the continent's rim, it doesn't spill. It holds. It hangs there, a vertical cliff of ocean, thrashing but not falling, the surface tension of something that is not surface tension at all.

Inside, the water is not still. It cannot be still. This place is turbulent in the way a storm is turbulent, but without wind as you know it — the medium itself is in storm. Currents tear across the plain in slow, continent-wide gyres, then collide and shear. Upwellings punch up from the cold stone and burst toward that thin interface with the air, where they flatten and spread like smoke hitting a ceiling. In other places the water piles and folds, forming standing waves twenty meters high that do not travel, that just stand there roaring, glassy on one side and white and shredded on the other, for hours before collapsing all at once with a sound like a mountain being dropped.

It is loud. You hear it not as waves lapping but as a constant, low, planetary roar, compounded of a thousand kinds of water-noise at once: the deep organ-note of pressure moving over stone, the crackle of cavitation as currents rip around basalt columns, the ceaseless rain-like hammering on the upper surface where the suspended ocean exchanges with the atmosphere and the air tries to take water back.

Because the top is not a surface either. From below it looks like a sky made of hammered light, rippling and breathing. From above — if you could hover in that thin, cold air and look down — it would look like the earth had grown a second, shallow sky of liquid, perfectly flat until a gyre hits it and it domes upward. And it rains, but inverted: the atmosphere here is saturated, heavy with the ocean's exhalation, so there is a permanent, dense mist, a wall of rain that never quite falls, hanging in the meters just above the water, where droplets form and are pulled back down before they can become weather. Light coming through this layer breaks into moving columns, searchlights sweeping the drowned basalt below, normal daylight made strange by its medium.

That is the trick of the place. The light *is* normal. The sun is not dim or red or flickering. It is a familiar, daytime sun. But familiar light through unfamiliar miles of moving water does unfamiliar things. It throws caustic nets that race across the black stone at speed, lattices of brightness that shrink and swell as the surface lenses above reshape themselves. At noon the whole submerged plain glitters, not with reflection but with refraction, as if the water itself were emitting.

Nothing lives in it. Or rather, almost nothing.

In any other ocean at this temperature, with this light, you would expect fish, plankton blooms, the dart of bodies. Here, there is a sterility that is audible in the absence of clicks, whistles, splashes. No gulls above the hanging edge, no insects. The water is too new, or too clean, or too held in place by whatever holds it. If you open your eyes under it, the water is startlingly clear — you can see for a kilometer through cold, empty volume — and empty is the word. The scale defeats comprehension. The plain goes on beyond haze, beyond curvature; the columns repeat until they are a texture. Between presences there is only water and stone and light.

Presences, sparse: vegetation, but not as forest, not even as meadow. On the highest stones, where light is strongest and the turbulence briefly slackens, there are colonies. They are not green in the way you expect green. They are dark olive, rust-red, black-green mats of something between algae and lichen and bacterial film, clinging flat to the basalt as if painted on. They do not sway; there is no stem to sway. They are low, crustose, leathery sheets that creep millimeter by millimeter across the newborn rock, holding on against currents that would strip anything taller. In the lee of a column, where an eddy makes a pocket of relative calm, one mat might have grown to the size of a table, its edges frayed and lifting slightly in the flow, trailing filaments that tear off and vanish into the gyre. Kilometers away, another. That is all. The world is not barren, but it is profoundly empty. You could drift for an hour through cold, bright water and see nothing but stone and light and that endless roar.

And you would feel, without seeing, the engineering.

There are no ruins. No towers, no pipes, no roads, no artifacts. The stone is untouched and the water is untouched in the human sense. But the water should have fallen off the plateau a million years ago, cut canyons down its flanks, and become a proper sea at the bottom of the world. It hasn't. At the rim, where the ocean becomes a wall, you can swim to the edge and put out your hand. The water pushes against something that is not there. If you watch fine silt caught in the current near the brink, you see it track along an invisible curve, as if following the lines of a magnetic field made solid. Sometimes, when a large standing wave collapses, the spray doesn't fall; it hangs for a fraction of a second too long, droplets suspended in air before remembering gravity.

The tech, if that is the word, is not *in* the landscape. It *is* the landscape's rules. The ocean is being held, or perhaps being *made* — lifted, desalinated, poured, kept cold and turbulent on purpose while the world underneath is still cooling and forming. The whole continent is a petri dish, a reservoir, a womb. The water itself may be the machine: not water as you know it but a lattice of structured water, engineered at a scale below your eye can see, that has decided to behave collectively like an ocean held aloft. When light hits it at certain angles, you catch a faint, oily iridescence that water should not have, a pixelation of rainbows shifting a little too crisply.

The rule here seems to be: *erode, but not yet.* Let the turbulence do its work, let the raw geology feel its first water, but don't let it spill. Hold it until the stone is ready. Hold it until life — other than those patient mats — is invited.

If you stay long enough, treading water above a black, sharp plain under a sun that shines through an ocean-sky, you start to hear the place not as an ocean at all but as a construction site at planetary scale. The roar resolves into work: the grinding of stone being shaped for the first time, the surge of liquid being cycled and recirculated by an invisible, planet-sized pump, the hammering of that low mist that is not weather but exhaust.

And then a gyre shifts, a standing wave collapses, the light-nets race, and a whole valley of basalt you hadn't seen before is briefly illuminated, raw and empty and waiting, and the water rushes to fill it, loud enough that you feel it in your chest before you hear it at all.
