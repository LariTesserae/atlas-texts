It is low.

Not low like a valley. Low like a continent forgot to rise. From any point you can see too far, and the far stays flat. The curvature of the earth is a rumor you start to believe because the horizon doesn't break. It just thins into heat.

This was never ground.

Under your feet is not bedrock or soil or sand in the geological sense. It is made. Everything is made. If you kneel and brush the dust away, you find the seam. And another seam. Sun-baked brick, not neatly fired but molded and left, and molded and left. A silty, pale clay the color of old bone, cut into blocks the size of a torso, or the size of a hand, or the size of a room - there was no standard, only the reach of the maker. They are laid in courses that run for kilometers without wavering, then turn a perfect right angle and run for kilometers more. There are no hills, only these courses stepping up or down by a hand's breadth, by a knee's height, great shallow terraces that tile the world.

It's new. That is the strangest thing about something so vast. The edges are still sharp. The corners of the bricks have not been rounded by wind. The channels between them have not been silted smooth. Where a course has failed, the break is clean and raw, showing dry, unweathered clay inside, still a shade darker than the sun-bleached face. The world is under construction, or just after it - an embryonic geology where the process that makes the land hasn't finished. There is no dust of ages. There is dust of yesterday.

It is hot.

The light is ordinary - a white-yellow sun, high and direct, the kind that bleaches color by noon and gives it back at dusk. But the heat is total. It comes off the brick in a visible tremble, a low shimmer that makes the endless straight lines ripple as if they were under water. The air is still and heavy. Breathing feels like drinking warm air.

Water exists here, but not as a sea or a lake. It is intermittent, pocketed, reluctant.

The builders left for it. Or built for it.

Every dozen, every hundred terraces, there is a cut - a canal, a trough, a runnel. Some are no wider than your foot, some wide enough to lie down in. They run as straight as the brickwork, forming a grid so large you can only comprehend a single intersection at a time. Now, in the heat, most are dry. Their bottoms are cracked mud that has baked into polygonal plates, curling at the edges. Dark stains mark where water sat and evaporated. If you follow one long enough - and following one is the only way to navigate - you will hear it before you see it: a faint trickle, a seep.

At the low points, where two or three of these shallow aqueducts meet, water gathers. Not much. A sheet a few millimeters deep, clouded with clay, reflecting the sky like a dull mirror. A pool held in a brick basin the size of a house, replenished by condensation more than rain. At night, the brick exhales its heat and the air, briefly cool, gives up its moisture on every surface. By dawn, every horizontal face beads and runs, ticking and dripping into the troughs. By mid-morning it is gone again, except in the deepest pockets, where it lingers.

Where there is water, there is green, but sparingly.

No forests. No meadows. No cover. The vegetation is sparse to the point of loneliness. A clump of reeds in a canal junction, their roots finding purchase in the mud between bricks. A low, waxy succulent that has taken hold in the mortar line, its leaves dusted pink with clay. A single tamarisk-like tree, stunted and wide, standing alone at a crossroads of channels, casting the only shadow for a mile. Its leaves are small and hard, conserving everything. Nothing is lush. Everything is spaced as if by agreement - one life, then a long emptiness, then another life. The plants do not spread. They wait.

There are no animals.

No insects humming, no birds crossing the heat. Not even a lizard's skitter. If something built this, it is not here now, or it builds without moving in a way you can see. What movement there is comes from other systems. The reeds shiver even when there is no wind, because water is moving under them. The mud in the drying pools pops and contracts, a slow audible tick as new cracks propagate across the surface. The succulents swell infinitesimally at night as they drink dew. At the broken terrace where a course has slumped, a thin sheet of water is still seeping from the exposed face, darkening the clay grain by grain.

It is a quiet place. The scale swallows sound. Your footsteps on brick are flat and dry. The loudest thing for an hour might be the faint hiss of evaporation, or the soft sigh when a dried mud plate finally curls and lifts.

And yet it is active. Not still.

Because the water moves, the whole plain breathes. In the distance you can see weather that belongs only to here - a low, dark anvil of cloud that has built itself out of the plain's own exhaled moisture, dragging a curtain of rain that you know will fall into one set of canals and be gone by the time you reach them. The light changes as it passes, the heat lifts for ten minutes, the distant brick darkens, and then the sun returns and steam rises in a silent column a mile wide. The plants track it. The canals run, briefly, like veins.

This is a made desert that is learning how to be a floodplain. A floor the size of a country, laid by hands or by patient, mindless growth - no tools left behind, no metal, no glass, no wire, only the pressure of palms on mud - waiting for the water to decide where to live. You can walk for days and the pattern will hold: brick, seam, channel, dryness, then suddenly damp, and green, and cool mud between your toes, and then again the endless, sharp-edged, sun-struck making, going on until the haze takes it.

If you dig, you will not find earth. Only more brick.
