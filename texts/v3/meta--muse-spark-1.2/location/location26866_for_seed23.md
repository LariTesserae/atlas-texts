It is too bright up here.

That is the first thing. The air is thin and scrubbed clean and the sun has nothing to filter it, so the light comes down like a weight. It hits the snow and the wet stone and the ice and throws it all back at you doubled. You squint until your eyes water, and your tears bead and threaten to freeze on your lashes. The cold is absolute, not windy-cold or bitter-cold, just the steady, foundational cold of high altitude where the rock itself is cold all the way through. Your breath does not puff and vanish — it condenses instantly and hangs around your shoulders for a long time before it settles.

The place itself is small. You can see all of it at once, cupped like hands around a bowl of water. A cirque, a glacial kettle, maybe three hundred meters across, carved a long time ago and then left to weather into itself. The walls are not jagged. They are established, old. The basalt and pale granite have been smoothed, undercut, terraced by water and ice until they have the soft shoulders of very old stone. Nothing here is sharp or new. Even the faults look comfortable, like wrinkles. Snow caps the rim in a thick, overhanging cornice that glows amber and white in the hard light, but inside the bowl itself there is surprisingly little snow. The rock is too wet and too dark to hold it.

Water is everywhere, though it shouldn't be, not in this cold.

It weeps.

The whole amphitheater weeps. The walls are black and shining with it. It sweats out of the stone in sheets, beads in cracks, runs in a hundred thin braids down every face, collects in ledges, spills again. Icicles as thick as tree trunks hang halfway down and then, impossibly, continue as water — clear, liquid, moving slowly down through the center of the ice like a vein. At the bottom of the bowl a shallow tarn spreads wall to wall, not frozen, not quite liquid either. It is dark, crowded water, mineral-heavy, steaming faintly despite the killing cold. The air above it shimmers and instantly makes more water, condensing out of nothing, falling again as a cold, bright mist that never really rains, just hangs and drips and condenses again. You hear it constantly — not as sound, but as a ticking on stone, on skin, on cloth.

Nothing should stay liquid here. But it does. The old people who live here say the mountain sweats salt, that the rocks themselves weep brine so dense it cannot freeze. Maybe. The water tastes metallic and old when it lands on your lips.

There is almost nothing growing.

On first glance you think the bowl is barren. Then you see the color tucked into it. Black lichen spreads across the wet stone like spilled ink. Orange and sulfur-yellow crusts follow the seep lines. In sheltered seams, tiny, furious cushions of moss and alpine campion cling — tight, low, hard as leather, each one a perfect, dense rosette holding a single white or pink flower the size of a fingernail. That's it. No trees. No fields. Not enough soil has ever been allowed to stay, not enough warmth. The vegetation is an afterthought, a decoration on the rock rather than a covering for it.

Which makes the abundance of life feel wrong.

The bowl is crowded. Dense beyond reason for a place this small and this sparse.

The tarn and the wet cliffs are covered in life that does not need the plants at all. White birds — not gulls, something higher and stranger, with translucent, frilled tails like ice feathers — pack the walls in thousands, shoulder to shoulder on every ledge, each one facing inward. They shift and rustle and take off and land in a constant, soundless churn. They drink the brine-water, they skim the dark tarn for the red, shrimp-like things that swarm just under the surface by the millions, turning the water rusty in pulses. Hairy, pale insects with long legs skate over the weeping rock, drinking the film. On the stone above, compact mountain goats with soot-black wool pick their way impossibly along the wet faces, licking salt.

And among them — woven in — are people.

This is a town, though you didn't expect a town. The whole inner curve of the north wall is town. Primitive, hand-made, utterly dense. From the tarn's edge up to the cornice, the cliff has been cut and stacked and cut again. Low houses of unmortared black stone, roofed with flat slabs and sealskin and sodden thatch that steams. Ladders, ramps, ropewalks, and carved stairwells lace them together in three dimensions so you cannot tell where one house ends and the next begins. Chimneys — dozens, hundreds — exhale thin, straight smoke that condenses and falls back as oily water before it can rise. Walkways are no wider than shoulders. Doorways open onto doorways. Balconies hang over balconies, all dripping. Everything is wet and shining and close.

There are no metals here beyond hammered copper pins and a few iron knives carried like heirlooms. Tools are bone, antler, chipped stone, wood polished by wet hands. But the construction is sophisticated in the way a beehive or a termite mound is sophisticated: cumulative, intergenerational, every surface used. Cisterns are cut into the rock to catch the weep. Gutters run along every lintel. Vats of brine evaporate on every flat roof, leaving a crust of pink salt that is scraped and bagged and stacked. The whole settlement is an infrastructure for catching water that cannot be caught.

It should be noisy. It is turbulent instead.

Even in the few minutes you stand at the rim, nothing stays still. Birds lift in a sudden sheet and wheel, goats clatter sideways, a wall of water bulges and releases halfway up the cliff, children in heavy wool dart across a rope bridge carrying buckets that slosh, an old woman scrapes salt, a house wall that was there yesterday is being taken apart stone by stone and rebuilt two meters to the left to make way for a new cistern. It is hyperactive, bazaar-like, constant creation and un-creation, as if the bowl cannot decide what shape it wants to be and the people and animals are helping it try everything at once.

But it is quiet.

Snow and water and thin air swallow sound. The birds cry with open beaks and you see the cry more than you hear it — a small puff of crystal breath. The goats' hooves on wet stone are soft clicks. The endless dripping is not a roar but a field of tiny, separate ticks, like a room full of clocks. People speak low and close, or not at all; they gesture with salt-white hands. Even a hammer stone on rock makes a dull, padded thud. The whole furious activity happens under glass, under the huge, bright, silent light.

The strangeness is not in any one thing. It is in the fact that it all holds together.

In the brightest part of midday, when the sun is directly overhead and the light has no shadows, the air in the center of the bowl thickens. The condensing mist over the tarn stops falling. Droplets hang. For a long minute you can see them — thousands of perfect spheres of water just hovering a meter above the surface, reflecting the stone town and the birds and your own upturned face so many times it makes you dizzy. Then a bird flies through and they all shiver and fall at once without a sound, dimpling the tarn, and the shrimp-cloud blooms red underneath.

No one looks surprised. A boy on a balcony simply holds out his wooden bowl and lets the suspended water drop into it when it decides to fall.

This is a place that has learned its own rules: water that will not freeze, salt that feeds a city without a field, life that does not need green, light that is too bright and too cold to cast warmth, motion that never makes noise. It is very old, very settled in its oddness, and very busy holding itself exactly as it is — a small, wet, bright, crowded wound of stone high above the world, steaming quietly in the cold.
