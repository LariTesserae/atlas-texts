It is high up and it is drowned.

That is the first thing that doesn't make sense until you have been here a while. You expect high places to be dry, to be thin-aired and scoured and cold. This one is warm and thick and submerged, not under a sea that came up to meet it, but under a sea that lives up here, that belongs up here.

Think of a continent lifted. A plateau the size of a country, or larger than a country - large enough that it has its own weather tucked inside itself - lifted three or four kilometers into the sky in some former age of the world and then forgotten there. And in all that time, wind and warm rain and chemistry ground it down. This is ancient ground. Not jagged. Not dramatic. Everything that was once sharp has been patiented away. The mountains are still here but they have become long, broad swells, like the backs of sleeping animals under a blanket. Valleys are shallow and U-shaped and a thousand feet deep but so wide you can mistake them for flatness until you notice the slow, continent-scale tilt. The rock is old exposed basalt and sandstone, black and rust-red, rounded to a polish, pitted with potholes the size of houses where rivers sat for a million years and drilled perfect circles and then left. Nothing is new here. Even the mud feels old, deep, settled into its final shape.

And over all of it, warm water.

Not a flood that will recede. Not a lake. An ocean that is also the air. You are submerged without a surface anywhere. The light comes from very far above, filtered down through depth after depth of water that never quite becomes air. It's dim, even at midday — not night, but a perpetual late afternoon under cloud and under water at once. A green-amber dusk. You can see maybe thirty feet clearly, then everything dissolves into haze, not murk but distance made of water. Sunlight comes down in slow, visible shafts that drift like kelp stems, and where they hit the old stone they make it glow.

The water is warm. Body-warm, bath-warm. It does not chill and it does not press. You can breathe it. That is the first reality-break the place asks you to accept, and after that the others come easier. You breathe in and what fills your lungs is wet and oxygenated and silent. It has weight but not crushing weight. Things are buoyant in a way that has forgotten which direction is up. Silt hangs motionless for hours. A leaf will fall sideways. 

And it is lush.

Because the light is dim the plants do not reach for the sun the way they do elsewhere. They reach for each other. The vegetation here is not trees as you know them but something between kelp and fern and coral, rooted in the ancient stone, growing through the water itself. From every rounded ridge and softened cliff, forests hang. They do not stand, they *suspend*. Great curtains of fronded columns, thirty, fifty feet tall, their trunks as thick as houses and spongy, their leaves black-green and translucent at the edges where the dim light catches them. Between them, a second layer — vine-thick braids that knit pillar to pillar — and between those, a third layer of feather-mosses and liverworts that grow on the water itself, forming floating meadows. And between those, a fourth layer: particulate gardens, snowfalls of pollen and spore and seed-husk that never settle, just drift and germinate in mid-water, sprouting tiny, perfect radishes of root that dangle and die and sprout again.

It is dense. Per cubic foot there is more living matter than empty water. You have to push through it, not swim so much as wade through a vertical jungle where every layer is occupied. Light filters and is caught and re-emitted by leaf after leaf until there is no shadow left, only a soft, even glow.

It should be loud with life. It is not. The fauna is almost absent. That is the strange loneliness of this place. For all this frantic growth, almost nothing moves under its own will. Perhaps once there were fish or cetaceans or insects, but now only rarely will you see something pass — a long, pale glider the size of a bus, so slow you think it's part of the forest until you see its rhythmic pulse, its single eye closed against the silt. Or a shoal of transparent bells that contract once an hour and drift on. The forests have no birds. The mud has no footprints. The dynamic here is not animal. It is vegetal and mineral and meteorological.

Because for all its quiet, it is *active*. This is not a dead, preserved drowned world. It is in constant slow motion. You can feel the currents that move through the high valleys — warm rivers inside the ocean, distinct as wind, carrying temperatures a few degrees different, carrying clouds of spores from one forest to another. You can watch a whole hillside of the spongy trunks lean collectively over the course of an afternoon as a current shifts. You can watch the ancient stone itself erode further, grain by grain, the basalt dissolving into the warm water like sugar, the iron bleeding out in long rust plumes that hang for days before being drunk by the moss. Weather happens *inside* the water: at scale, differences in heat and salinity create storms that never break the surface because there is no surface. A storm here is a slow, vast vortex a mile wide, where the water goes suddenly clear and cold and all the floating meadows tilt and spin. You see its approach hours away as a change in the tint of the amber.

The rules are not quite ours.

Distance is unreliable. You set out toward a swell that looks a half hour away and you pass through three forests and a cold current and when you look back the place you started is still right behind you, or is suddenly much further. Gravity is polite here, optional. Water sometimes clings in vertical walls that hold their shape for days — a sheer plane of water meeting water, with leaves growing sideways out of it. Sometimes rain falls *upward*, droplets of denser water rising through thinner water, beading on the underside of floating moss. Sounds do not propagate correctly. A stone knocked against stone makes a soft *thunk* that you feel in your chest more than hear, and then for minutes afterward you hear it again, fainter, from a different direction, as if the landscape is remembering it.

Here and there, almost too sparse to call ruins, there are the built things. They confirm that someone was here before the water became the air, or while it was becoming. On the crest of one of those broad, whale-back ridges you will find a line of cut stone, too regular to be natural — a low wall, knee-high, running straight for a hundred yards and then stopping at nothing. Or a stair cut into the ancient basalt, each step dished deep by water and time, leading down into a hillside that is no longer a hillside but a sponge-forest. Or a single small structure, roofless, its doorway now a home for fronds. No cities. No metal. No tools left. Whoever built them built lightly and then was either drowned gently or learned to breathe the warm water and then left anyway.

If you stay still long enough, which is easy because the silence encourages it, you can feel the deep time of the place settling on you. The water is so quiet. There is no lapping, no crashing. The only sounds are the small ones the active world makes: the creak of a forest leaning, the soft pop of a seed-pod opening and releasing a bead of oil that rises, the sigh of a current through fronds, your own breath moving in and out, in and out, turning water to water.

Looking out, through the dense, warm, dim suspension, across the endless, flattened, ancient highlands under their impossible ocean, you understand that this is not a place waiting to be drained or restored. This is its final form. It has eroded until it can erode no more, until high and low hardly matter, until land and sea have negotiated a new compromise where both are true at once. It will stay like this, vast and warm and lush and nearly empty, making its own slow weather in its own slow light, for a very long time after you have learned how to breathe here.
