You are below.

Not deep in the way of pressure and heat, but deep in the way of being enclosed. The world above is a memory of weight. Here, the sky has been replaced.

At first you think you have come into a mine, or a vault. The scale corrects you.

The cavern is regional. A valley that never saw weather.

Its ceiling is flat and far above, 300, maybe 400 meters of unbroken basalt and salt-crete, not arched but *cut* — a recent cut. The geology here is embryonic. This place has not been eroded, it has been revealed. The walls are not smoothed by wind or water because there has never been water to smooth them. They are raw and stepped, the marks of thermal lances and chemical saws still sharp on the stone, a staircase of black glass and pink halite that has not yet learned to crumble. Piles of spoil lie where they fell, angular, unsorted, dust on dust. There are no riverbeds, no alluvial fans, no polish. The ground is not ground at all but a floor laid down deliberately over the rough: plates of pressed mineral fiber and iron, thrumming faintly with the cold that comes up from deeper rock. The air is cool, a consistent 8 or 9 degrees — cool enough that your breath does not fog but your hands want to stay in pockets. Cool enough that everything keeps.

And it is dry. Profoundly dry.

Not desert-dry, where you can still find a seep if you dig. Here the aridity is complete. The rock exhales a faint salt-bitter tang and nothing else. No puddles, no condensation, no damp in the corners. Even the dust is hydrophobic and lifts off your boots in clouds that hang for a long time before settling. Chemistry here does not use water. It can't. What water there was in this deep basin was baked out or locked long ago in the halite. What remains is sealed.

Which makes the forest impossible, until you understand it.

Vegetation is not *in* this place. It *is* this place.

The cavern is a forest, but not a forest that grew in soil. It is a single living architecture that has taken the void as its substrate. From floor to ceiling, from wall to wall for kilometers, stand the Columns.

Each one is a plant, if that word still applies. They are wide as apartment buildings, 40 meters across at the base, rising without branching straight up to kiss the basalt ceiling and spread there into a flat, interlocking canopy. Their skin is not bark but a dry, fibrous case, layers of dead cork the color of paper and bone, peeling in vast, soundless sheets. Inside that case is living tissue that is not wet — it is waxy, resinous, saturated with salts and ammonia-oils that never evaporate. You can smell it when a sheet cracks: sharp, medicinal, like camphor and iodine.

They are spaced far apart. This is not a jungle. The urban plan demands emptiness. Between any two Columns there might be a half-kilometer of open floor, empty air, a boulevard of lit dust. The density is sparse, disciplined. But because each Column is so huge and their canopy tops join together miles overhead into a single continuous ceiling-leaf, the *mass* of plant is total. You are always under plant, beside plant, looking through plant. The walls of the cavern that are not saw-cut rock are living root-mat, a dense felt of white aerial roots as thick as cables, drinking nothing but the faint humidity of the air and the mineral exhalation of the stone itself. You can put your hand on a wall and feel it faintly warm, faintly giving.

The city is not built *around* the Columns. It is built *from* them and *in* them.

This was not a settlement that cleared land. It was grown and machined into place at once. The lower trunks of the Columns have been hollowed, windowed, terraced. Balconies are cut directly into the living cork and edged with iron railings. Factories are annexes that cling like shelf fungi to the sides of the trunks, their corrugated tin and basalt-brick walls stitched into the plant with rivets as long as your arm. Between the Columns run streets on the mineral floor, straight, industrial, lined with gaslights and conduit, connecting one trunk-city to the next. From a distance at the center of the open floor, you can see five or six of them lit like pillars in a cathedral the size of a county — each one a vertical town, each one humming.

And it is industrial. It has to be. To keep a living system of this size alive without water requires work.

You hear it before you see it: the pulse of the atmospheric processors, great low engines set in the spoil-piles that pull the cavern's air through salt filters and push it back out cool and mineral-dry. The fabrication mills at the base of Column Three, where sheets of cork are harvested as they slough — not cut, harvested — and pressed into building board, insulation, paper, fuel. The cableways that creak overhead, carrying vats of amber plant-oil from trunk to refinery, not water, not sap but a slow, clear hydrocarbon that the Columns sweat and store in blistered reservoirs under their skin. The people here tap it the way other worlds tap maple. They burn it in low, clean lamps. They run looms with it. The whole economy is lipid, not hydraulic.

Light is normal here, which is the strangest thing. You expected gloom or the green of bioluminescence. Instead the industrial light is daylight-balanced and even. The canopy is not opaque — it is translucent at its thinnest joins, and great shafts have been bored straight up through kilometers of rock to the surface, capped with Fresnel mirrors that throw true sun down in hard white columns. Where the sun-shafts don't reach, the city makes its own sun: arc-lamps on towers, and the Columns themselves, whose youngest leaves far overhead are edged with a pale, steady photophore light, not bright enough to read by but bright enough to make the air glow after the shifts end. You can see color correctly here. The rust red of iron, the bone white of cork, the dull olive of the root-felt. It looks like day, held permanently at late morning.

It is mostly quiet. For an industrial town the size of a valley, the quiet is uncanny. Sound is swallowed by the dry air and the acreage of cork. The mills are insulated, the engines throttled to a purr, the cableways rubber-dampened. What you hear is not noise but activity at a distance: a sheet of bark the size of a billboard detaching and gliding down to land with a soft *whumph* half a mile away. The sigh of a pressure valve. Footsteps echoing. The wind from the processors moving dust in a low hush across the boulevard.

But the place is not still. It is active, insistently alive.

The Columns grow. You can see it if you watch a root-tip for ten minutes — a white questing tendril extending a millimeter, tasting the dry air, secreting acid that etches the basalt to anchor itself. Higher up, new balconies are being extruded, not built: the Column, coaxed by scaffolds and hormone sprays from the technicians, bulges out a new lip of cork that will harden into a floor by next month. The fauna moves through this.

There is so much animal life, and none of it drinks.

They are abundant but scattered, like everything else here. Pale, dry things that have co-evolved with the dry forest. Great moth-like grazers with wingspans as wide as doors, whose larvae bore through dead cork and whose adults drift on the processor winds from Column to Column, dusting everything with fine, pepper-smelling scales. Armored isopods the size of dogs that scrape the root-felt with rasping mouths, leaving pale trails. Thin, stilt-legged browsers that pick at the oil blisters, their bodies translucent with stored hydrocarbon, their joints clicking. In the high canopy, things sing — not birds, but something with a tympanum that makes a single, clear, glassy note every thirty seconds, answered a kilometer away by another. Between the Columns, feral maintenance drones — semi-biological, semi-mechanical, grown as much as assembled — pick their way on many-jointed legs, pruning, patching.

The rule of this world is simple and strange: everything that lives here has learned to be parched. Membranes are waxed shut. Waste is powdered, not excreted. The plants pull their hydrogen not from water but from the trace brines in the salt and the air, and they build their bodies out of what they can keep. Death does not rot; it desiccates, it cures. A dead moth on the boulevard will lie there for months, intact, slowly bleaching, until an isopod finds it and mills it to dust.

You stand in the middle of the boulevard between Column Two and Column Three. To your left, the cut wall of the world is still raw, still being born, still showing the chisel marks of its making. To your right, a trunk-town rises 300 meters, its windows lit warm against the cool air, smoke from its oil refinery rising straight up in the windless, weighty air until it hits the canopy and spreads along it like a low cloud. Far ahead, the boulevard narrows to a point and then opens again — more Columns, more towns, more open, dusty, plant-held space, repeating until light and dust hide the end. You can feel the scale not as crowding but as a kind of cultivated emptiness — a city that has decided to leave most of itself empty so the living pillars can breathe.

The air tastes of salt and paper. The light is steady. Somewhere above, a sheet of cork peels with a sigh that is almost inaudible.
