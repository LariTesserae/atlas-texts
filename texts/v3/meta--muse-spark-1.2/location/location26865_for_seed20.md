It is not an ocean that you go down into. It is an ocean you climb up to.

The high country here never stopped lifting. The plateau came up young and fast, still sharp-jawed and unfinished, a slab of dark basalt and raw limestone thrust up a thousand meters too high while the world was flooded. The water didn't drain off it. It stayed.

You come over the last ridge — lungs working a little harder in the warm, wet air — and the ground simply ends in water. Not a lake. A sea, but a sea the size of a room.

The whole place is perhaps as large as a cathedral nave, or a village square, hemmed on all sides by that fresh, uneroded stone. The walls are too new to be smooth. They are stair-stepped, flaked, broken into cubist teeth and ledges where the rock cooled and cracked and was never worn down. Quartz still glitters in the seams where it cooled. Nothing is rounded. Every edge could cut your palm. And pouring over every edge, from no visible source, is water.

This is what **submerged, at elevation** means here: The basin at the top of the mountain is full to the brim and then more than full. The water does not have a surface. It is the air. The atmosphere here has thickened and condensed until it is liquid, warm as blood, dim and green-black and holding light the way deep sea holds light — which is to say, hardly at all.

You have to wade into sky.

At the rim, warm rain becomes wall. Not falling rain, a standing wall of it, a vertical ocean held in place by something that is not quite gravity. It hangs. It shivers. From a few meters away it looks like glass, or like the side of an aquarium the size of the world. Up close you see it is churning violently, full of currents that fold into themselves. And it is loud beyond any sound you have a name for.

### The Sound

You hear it before you see the basin, a low pressure in your teeth. Then it resolves into roaring.

Not the crash of surf. The continuous, deafening, white roar of a waterfall that never lands. The wall of water around the plateau pours and pours over the sharp lip of the mountain and then — instead of falling — it curls. It rises. It recirculates. Tons of warm water peeling up and over and down again in a thunder that occupies your entire skull, that vibrates your ribs, that makes it impossible to hear your own breathing or your own thoughts. You feel it in your sternum. If you open your mouth to shout, the sound is taken and shredded instantly. The world here has only one voice and it never stops.

Because of the noise, the turbulence looks silent at first, until you realize the chaos *is* the noise made visible.

### The Turbulence

Nothing here is stable. This is a young place, still being made and unmade several times a minute.

The water inside the basin is not still. It boils without heat. Columns of clear warm water suddenly darken and braid themselves into vortices that drill down to the stone floor and suck grit up into spirals, then collapse just as quickly. Sheets of water peel off the main mass and hang in mid-air for three or four heartbeats, perfectly flat and vertical, like liquid windows, before shattering into a thousand warm droplets that do not fall — they drift sideways and rejoin the mass.

The light is dim — the sun is somewhere above the water-sky, diffused to a sourceless amber dusk — but it strobes. Because the water itself is moving so fast, shafts of that dim light are caught, bent, thrown. One moment the whole basin is deep twilight, ink-blue and bottle-green, the next a vortex lenses the light and the basalt floor flashes gold-white, every sharp chip and crack thrown into brutal relief.

And the rules unstick.

Gravity here is negotiable. Water runs uphill along an invisible grade, pools on the underside of an overhang, drips *up*. You see a bubble the size of your head drift downward and pop against the stone floor like it fell. You see a loose shard of basalt, dislodged by the current, hang suspended for a long minute before deciding to sink sideways. If you stay still too long, your own sense of up tilts. The basin is too small to have a horizon; the water walls curve inward slightly, and if you stare at them you get the distinct, nauseating feeling that the whole flooded saucer is the inside of a throat that could close.

This is the weirdness, not as decoration but as climate. Reality hasn't broken here all at once; it has thinned, like ice over a warm spring, and water is finding the cracks.

### The Floor, The Sparse Things That Live

When the vortexes clear for a second you can see the bottom.

It is bare, young stone. No soil has had time to form. The water has not yet learned to smooth it. There are shallow pans of warm silt and crushed shell — impossibly, shells here, at this height — and in that silt, sparse life holds on with stubbornness rather than abundance.

Vegetation is an afterthought. A few clumps of black-green algae slick the leeward ledges, stringy and whipping in the current like hair. One, maybe two, stunted trees — not really trees — have taken root where the stone cracked. They are pale, leafless, mangrove-like, with knee roots that grip the rock and thin, translucent branches that do not so much grow as they are extruded, reaching into the water column and filtering it. They bear no leaves, only small, waxy bladders that inflate and pop. Between them, vast expanses of nothing: clean, empty, warm stone, swept bare every few minutes by a surge.

Fauna is rarer still. You would think a sea would be full, but this sea is too small, too violent, too new. You might see nothing for an hour but the churn. Then — a flash. A single, long, ribbon-fish, blind and white as milk, as long as your arm, is thrown vertical by a current and hangs there, staring with vestigial eyes, before it snaps sideways and vanishes into the wall. Or small, shrimp-like things, virtually transparent, that cling to the underside of the suspended water-sheets until the sheets burst. They make no sound you could ever hear over the roar. Their presence only makes the emptiness feel larger.

The water is warm. Not hot-spring warm, but body warm, amniotic. When you wade in to your chest — and you will, because the edge is not clear and the water invites — it does not shock. It envelops. It is the temperature of being inside something living. The dim light filters through it and makes your hands look green and far away. You can hold your breath and dip your head under the wall and the roar dulls for an instant to a deeper, even more immense thrum, the sound of the whole mountain humming through liquid sky.

### The Built Things

There were people here before the water stayed, or after it came. It's hard to tell because what they left is so scattered and so modest against the violence.

No city. No machines — there is no tech here at all, not even rust. Only stone set weakly against stone.

On the eastern lip, where the basalt makes a natural shelf a little out of the worst churn, four low walls enclose nothing. A single room without a roof, its corners still sharp because erosion has not yet softened them. Inside, a flat altar stone, or a table, pitted by warm water. On it, someone left a bowl, also stone, now filled permanently with water that vibrates with the sound. 

A little further down, half-submerged and half-exposed as the water level flexes a meter up and down every minute, a stair cut directly into the living rock — seven steps — leading from nowhere into the deepest part of the basin and stopping. The lowest two steps are already smoothed slightly, but the top ones still have chisel marks.

That is all. No road to it, no quarry, no debris field. Just those faint, stubborn marks, scattered like afterthoughts, already being undone by water that lifts a pebble, rolls it, sets it down elsewhere. The place feels hamlet-sized because it *was* — a small, high hermitage or watch post that has become an aquarium for the sky.

If you stand on the stair and let the warm, deafening water surge around your knees and look up, you see the paradox whole: a tiny, sharp-edged mountain bowl, only a few dozen strides across, holding a piece of deep ocean that should by all rights be kilometers below you. The dim light comes from everywhere and nowhere. The roar makes your bones ring. A loose sheet of water detaches from the wall above you, hangs perfectly still for a breath, reflecting your doubled, wavering shape back at you — and then collapses, turbulent, warm, and screaming loud, rejoining the sea that never stops moving.

It is too small to live in and too loud to think in. It does not want you. It is too young to have learned to be kind. But for as long as you stand there, with that warm water at your throat and the whole sky drowned just above your head, you are inside a place that has forgotten how to be a mountain or an ocean and is trying, violently and beautifully, to be both at once.
