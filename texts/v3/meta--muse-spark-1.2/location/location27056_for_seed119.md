It is not a valley. It is a room that decided to be a valley.

You find it low, lower than the land around it should allow — a shallow dish sunk into the plain so that you have to step *down* into it, even though the plain itself is already low and flat and near sea-level. From the outside it looks like nothing: a low lip of stone in the grass, like the coping of a well. From the inside, it is a world.

Everything is made.

There is no ground here, no soil, no exposed rock that was not placed by a hand. The floor is flagstone, massive and irregular, basalt and limestone fitted with the patient tightness of people who had only stone hammers and wooden wedges and time. You can still see the pock-marks of their work, each slab dimpled by hand-pecking, the edges not sawn but nibbled. The walls are the same — dry-stone, no mortar, but so well laid and so long settled that they have become a kind of geology. The enclosure is roughly oval, perhaps forty strides across at its longest, and no taller than three men. A hamlet could have lived on its floor. A cathedral could have fit inside its air. You can see the whole of it from any one place, and that is the scale of it: comprehensible, bounded, a single feature with a rim. Beyond the rim, the world continues, but inside, the rules are its own.

It is old in the way a riverbed is old. Not ruined — established. Weathered into equilibrium. The stones have softened. Corners that were once sharp are now rounded as river cobbles. The steps cut into the south wall — broad, shallow, for carrying water jars on the head — are worn down in the center to a smooth trough by ten thousand bare feet. Lichen has had time to learn the stones, and then been scrubbed off by water, and learned them again in a paler color. Fine white calcite beards hang from every joint where water has wept through for centuries and left its mineral behind. Cracks have been repaired with smaller stones, then those repairs have been weathered in turn. Nothing is collapsing. Nothing is new. It is in balance with itself.

It is cool here. Always. The stones hold a lowland cool that has nothing to do with shade, though it is dim. Light comes from above and it is not much — the sky is a narrow, high opening. The builders left no roof, but the air itself has made one. At about two head-heights up, mist thickens and hangs, not quite fog, not quite cloud, a low ceiling of condensed breath that diffuses the daylight to a perpetual late-afternoon grey. The sun when it is out is a bright coin seen through frosted glass, never direct. Colors are stone-colors, water-colors.

And it is wet.

Not flooded. Wet the way a lung is wet. Water is on every surface and in the air before it can fall. It seeps. The walls weep constantly, not in streams but in sheets and beads that gather on the undersides of overhangs and run sideways along the tilted flags before dropping. The floor never dries. A thin film lives on the stone, ankle-deep in the lower channels, no deeper than your sole in most places, but always moving, always whispering. There are channels cut into the floor — hand-chiseled runnels no wider than your hand — that braid and re-braid, carrying the weep-water to a central sump. The sump is not a drain. It is a shallow, perfectly round basin, mortared, where water rests and darkens before it sighs back into the stone or into the air. After rain, it is a mirror a foot deep. Now, after no rain, it is still wet, because the stones themselves exhale. You can hear the condensing atmosphere tick against the rock, beads forming where there were none.

Nothing grows here the way a marsh grows. Vegetation is sparse, almost reluctant. There is no soil for it to root in, only the thin grit that has collected in joints. So it takes what it can: a felt of dark green and black moss in the mortar lines, beards of lichen on the north-facing wall, three clumps of reeds in the sump where silt has settled enough to hold them, and a single, stunted fig that has found a cracked corner of the eastern wall. Its roots are not in earth but in the wall itself, prying between stones, white and flat as fingers. Its few leaves are dusty and small and very bright. Most of the world is stone and water and nothing else.

Which makes the animals more startling.

The place feels empty at first. Hollow. Almost nothing per volume — you could lie on the flags and feel the vastness of the air above you pressing down, a near-void despite its smallness. The distance between any two things is huge. Then you notice the movement, and you realize emptiness was an illusion of scale.

It is abundant. Loud and active and strange.

The air is full of midges and pale moths that live and die in the mist-ceiling, and therefore the air is full of swifts and swallows that have learned to live inside this room instead of the sky. They have nested in the chisel-holes in the walls, hundreds of them, mud and grass tucked into the tool-marks. They cut the dim air in silence, but their wings make a dry rustle like paper.

The water is full. In the runnels and the sump live things that should not be able to live in such shallow water: flat slate-colored newts as long as your hand, transparent shrimp, black tadpoles in such numbers that the dark water seems to boil, and a kind of loach that walks on its fins from puddle to puddle. They are in constant motion, feeding, fleeing. On the stones, colonies of snails with mineral-white shells graze the moss. And the sound that dominates everything is the frogs.

There are dozens — maybe hundreds — of them, small ochre and green frogs with throats that inflate like amber balloons. They sit on each stone, each in its own vast territory of wet flag, and they call. In the stone enclosure the call is deafening, amplified and thrown back and forth until it has no source. It is not a chorus. It is a clamor, overlapping, echoing, loud enough that you feel it in your sternum. Beneath it, always, is the other loudness: water dripping. Not a plink, but a complex percussion — fat drops from the mist-ceiling falling eight feet to stone, thin trickles joining runnels, the sump overflowing in a clear, continuous spilling. The frogs call through it and the birds cut through both. At times a heron — too large for this small place, absurdly large — stands motionless in the sump on legs like stilts, and then strikes, and the splash is like a dropped pot.

And the weirdness is quiet, until you see it.

Water here does not behave quite as it should. On the cool stone, it clings too long. Drops run uphill along the slight camber of the flags before they remember to run down. In the sump, if you watch, you will see a bead of water detach from the surface and drift *up* a hand's breadth before popping in the air, contributing to the low cloud. The mist-ceiling never rains inside, even when it is heavy; it just hangs, breathing. The frogs know it. They sit with their eyes half-closed as water beads on their backs and then slides off sideways.

It is a made place that has become a natural place without ever ceasing to be made. Its nature is not wilderness but maintenance — water maintaining stone, stone maintaining water, animals maintaining the noise, and the dim, cool air maintaining the whole cycle. It invites you to sit on a worn step and just listen to how active it is, how much happens in such a small, empty room where every surface was once struck by a primitive hammer and has been struck ever since by water.

If you stay until the light thins further, the swifts disappear into their holes and the frogs get louder, and the condensing air begins to glow faintly with the foxfire of the moss, and you understand the rule of this world: nothing enters, nothing leaves, except water, which never really leaves at all.
