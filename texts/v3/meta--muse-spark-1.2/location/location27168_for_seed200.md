This place has not finished being made.

It is low. Not a valley with walls, but a low place in the absolute sense - a country-scale basin that is barely above sea level, so low the air has weight. The atmosphere sits on you. It is hot in the old, original way: a wet heat that does not come from the sun directly but from the earth and the air itself, a heat that was here before the light arrived and will be here after. The air is thick enough to drink, saturated, condensing on itself without needing rain.

There has not been erosion yet. That is the strangest thing about it. In most places the land tells you what water has done to it - the cut of rivers, the gullies, the sorted gravel. Here there is no cut. The geology is raw and recent, embryonic. The ground is not soil as you know it. It is a warm, dark, unconsolidated emergence - a flat of basalt still soft at the edges, of siltstone that hasn't decided to be stone, of ash and marl and lime pressed together while still damp, like a plain of clay left out after the kiln was lit. It is level for miles, for a region, pocked and blistered and hummocked rather than carved. Water has not learned to flow in lines yet. Instead it exists as a skin.

Water is everywhere but nowhere channeled. It does not run, it seeps and weeps and stands. The surface of the land sweats. Every rock, and the rocks are low and spongy and still warm to the touch, weeps a constant slow bead from its pores. The ground sighs water from between its grains when you press it. It has just rained, or it is about to rain, or it is raining so finely you cannot call it rain - a condensation that forms directly on your skin, on the undersides of leaves, and gathers and falls in fat, warm drops. Shallow pans and sheets lie across the plain, ankle-deep, knee-deep, mirroring nothing because the light is too dim to make a mirror. There are no rivers. There are no banks. There is just wet ground becoming wetter ground becoming standing ground-water under a mat of life. The water is warm, tea-colored, alive.

The light is dim despite the heat. The sun, wherever it is, does not reach here cleanly. It is filtered twice: once by a high, permanent haze of exhaled vapor that never lifts, a low cloud that is not a cloud but just the air itself holding water, and again by what grows. The vegetation is lush to the point of impossibility, and dense in a way that makes density a physical law. This is not forest as in trunks and canopy and understory as separate things. It is a single, layered, near-touching mass that has colonized every inch of the raw plain the moment it cooled enough to hold.

At ground level, a felt of mosses and liverworts and prostrate ferns as thick as a mattress, saturated, springy, hissing with water when stepped on. Out of that, a tangle of reeds and fleshy stalks, broad-leafed herbs with leaves the size of tables, their surfaces waxy and cupped to hold water. Above that, a second story of quick, soft-wooded trees - not trees really but giant herbs, hollow, fast-growing, their bark still green and photosynthesizing, their crowns interlocking so completely that they form a roof. And on and through all of it, vines, epiphytes, stranglers, pitchers, sheets of climbers that make it impossible to tell where one plant ends and another begins. The green is dark, almost black in the dim, with sudden, violent gloss where water catches what light there is. Everything is touching everything else. There is no bare ground. Even the standing water is stitched through with floating leaves, with rafts of vegetation, with roots hanging down like curtains into the warm broth. The air smells like crushed leaf and hot mud and chlorophyll and iron.

Nothing here was built. No stone stacked, no wood cut, no path worn. And yet it does not feel empty, because it is absolutely full of animals.

The fauna is overwhelming. Not abundant. Overwhelming. The biomass of small bodies exceeds the biomass of leaves. If you stand still, the landscape resolves into motion. The moss shivers and is revealed to be insects. The water glints and is revealed to be the backs of amphibians, thousands per pool, their throats pulsing in unison. The tree trunks are beaded with snails the size of fists, with crabs that climb. The air, thick as it is, is particulate with life - dragonflies as long as your forearm with wings like mica, beetles that move with a heavy, audible clatter from leaf to leaf, small warm-blooded things - not birds, not bats, something in between - that launch in clouds when a branch sags, then settle again.

Everything sings at once. The sound is loud, not in spikes but as a continuous pressure. It is a wet, hot, layered drone: the low thrum of countless amphibians inflating their throats, a bass note you feel in your molars; the mid-range saw of insects stridulating without pause, without variation; the high, glassy chirp of the flying things; the constant plop and drip of condensation falling from leaf to leaf to water, a percussion that never stops. You have to raise your voice to think.

And the weirdness is quiet. For all its riot, this place is wrong in a way that takes a moment to notice.

There are no seasons here and no real succession. The dynamic is near zero. This lushness is not growing *toward* something, not colonizing, not decaying and renewing. It is in equilibrium, frozen while alive. A leaf falls and another grows in the exact same spot at the exact same rate. The water level does not rise or fall - it weeps at the same rate it evaporates. The calls of the animals do not crest at dawn or dusk because there is no dawn or dusk, only this same dim, hot, wet light. The dragonflies do not migrate; they orbit. The amphibians do not go quiet. If you watched for a year, you would see no change. If you watched for a hundred, the plain would still be embryonic, still flat, still not yet cut by rivers, as if time has been instructed to hold its breath.

The rules of this world are simple and absolute:

1. Nothing is allowed to be far from anything else. Closeness is mandatory.
2. Water will not gather to a point. It must be spread thin.
3. Light will not be direct. It must be used after it has been broken and shared many times.
4. Nothing dies more than it is replaced, instantly, exactly.

You can walk for hours in any direction - and the scale allows it, this is not a patch but a country, a basin you could spend a lifetime crossing - and the character will not change. Another warm pan of water under black-green leaves. Another weeping rock draped in moss that is really a colony of beetles. Another curtain of roots drinking the air. The plain seems to extend past the horizon not because it is large but because it is the same, and sameness has no edge.

It is hot and it is dim and it is wet and it is loud and it is absolutely, teemingly alive, and yet it feels untouched, not in the sense of pristine but in the sense of pre-touch, pre-hand, pre-tool, pre-history. Like a world that exhaled once and then decided to keep holding that breath forever.
