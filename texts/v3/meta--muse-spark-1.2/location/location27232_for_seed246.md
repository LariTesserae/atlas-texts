It is a bowl.

Not a valley in the way valleys are cut, but a bowl left behind after everything else has been taken away. High up — high enough that the air thins and sharpens — the plateau should have been flat and featureless, scoured by wind. Instead there is this one shallow depression, almost perfectly round, maybe three hundred meters across, rim to rim. You could walk its circumference before lunch. You could stand on its lip and see the whole of it at once, like looking into a cupped hand.

This is old country. Not old like ruins-old. Old like the planet got tired of making new shapes here and decided to stop.

The stone of the plateau is not stone anymore, not really. It's a pale, wind-polished gneiss that has been exposed so long it has lost all edges. The rim of the bowl is smooth as a river cobble, the slope down into it is a long, gentle curve, no cliffs, no talus, no drama left. Everything that could erode has eroded. Everything sharp has been sanded down to a kindness. Even the built things inside it have been sanded.

Because there is a town in the bowl. There was a town.

You notice it second, after the shape of the land, because it doesn't look built. It looks grown, then worn. Low, terraced rings follow the inside of the bowl down toward the center, like contour lines made solid. The walls are the same gneiss as the plateau, but set and fitted — dry-stacked without mortar, each stone shaped once by hands and then reshaped for a thousand winters by wind and water. The roofs are gone. The doorways are still there, but they are not rectangular anymore; frost-heave and thaw and lichen have pulled them into soft ovals, like eyes half-closed. It is urban in its logic — there are streets, narrow radial alleys that drain toward the middle, there are party walls shared between what were clearly houses, there are cisterns and stepped plazas — but there is no straight line left in it. It has settled into its final form. The town has become geology.

There are no tools here. No metal, no glass, no wire. If there ever were, they rusted and blew away long before the stone lost its corners. What remains is only what can survive without maintenance: stone, and the things that grow on stone.

And things do grow. Abundantly, improbably.

In the cold — and it is cold, a dry, high, breath-steaming cold where spit freezes before it lands — the bowl traps a different climate. The wind that screams across the plateau shears over the rim and leaves the air inside almost still. Moisture condenses on the cold stone and doesn't leave. It beads. It drips. It never quite rains, it just sweats.

Every wall weeps. In the mornings the whole town glistens with condensation that has collected overnight, a skin of water on every surface that catches the flat white light and makes the stone look dark and alive. By midday it gathers and runs. Not in a river — there is no river here, not enough water for that — but in thin, braided trickles that follow the radial streets down to the center. There, where the bowl is deepest, there is a pocket of water that is not a pond and not a marsh but something in between: a black, peaty tarn maybe twenty meters across, rimmed with spongy moss and sedge so thick you would think it was summer somewhere else. The water is tea-dark and cold enough to ache.

From that center, life radiates outward.

The vegetation should not be this lush for this altitude, for this cold. But the bowl is a greenhouse without a roof. The moss is the main citizen. It covers everything — not in a thin dusting but in deep, hummocked cushions, ankle-deep, knee-deep in the sheltered alleys. Emerald and olive and rust-red, it has swallowed the lower courses of walls, softened steps into ramps, made mattresses out of abandoned rooms. Out of it poke stunted, wind-trained growth: dwarf willow with leaves the size of coins, huddled together in copses inside what were once houses; lichen that hangs from lintels in pale beards; and a kind of hardy, fleshy saxifrage that grows in such dense rosettes it looks poured.

It is all very still and very busy at once. Nothing is static. You can hear it, and then you can see it if you stand quietly: the drip, drip, drip of a hundred walls draining; the sigh as a moss cushion releases stored water and swells; the crackle of ice forming in a shadowed alley at noon and melting again an hour later. The plants are not passive. They are creeping visibly, curling over stone, splitting it a grain at a time with root and frost. The tarn in the center breathes — its level rises and falls a finger's breadth over the day as condensation gathers and seeps away.

And it is loud. That is the strangest thing about how empty it is.

There is almost nothing here. The density of life is incredibly low if you count bodies. One willow copse per house. One bird, perhaps. You can stand in the center and see no movement at all for long minutes, and feel the hollow cathedral scale of the place — the bowl acting as an amphitheater for absence. The streets are empty. The rooms are empty, filled only with moss.

But the bowl is a drum. Every sound it has, it amplifies.

The wind over the rim, even though it doesn't enter, pulls a low, constant tone across the opening, like blowing over a bottle. Inside, the trickle of water is not a trickle but a chorus — dozens of small channels joining, echoing off curved stone, clicking and gurgling. The stone itself makes noise: at intervals, somewhere in the terraces, a slab that has been wedged by ice for centuries will shift a millimeter and pop with a report like a gunshot, the sound rolling around the bowl for seconds. And the ice — the clear, black ice that forms each night in the alleys and on the tarn — sings as it expands, a high, glassy chime that sustains even after the sun hits it.

It is a loud, active, empty town where no one lives.

Fauna is rare to the point of rumor. You might see one thing a day. A white hare that lives under the moss and leaves perfect print lines across a dusting of frost before vanishing. A raven that rides the rim updraft and never lands, calling once, the call circling the bowl three times before fading. In the tarn, something small and dark — flatworms, or cold-water beetles — moves under the peat, seen only as a ripple against the reflection.

The strangeness is not that any of this is impossible. It is that it all holds together wrong. This should be barren alpine rock. This should be a ruin buried in snow. Instead it is a damp, lush, overgrown town-small garden held in the palm of an ancient, wind-smoothed mountain, kept cold and wet and weirdly alive by its own shape. The walls perspire. The moss glows. The water moves uphill as frost before it moves down as drip. At noon, when the light is flat and white and absolutely normal — no auroras, no strange colors, just clean high-altitude daylight that makes everything sharp — you can see your breath and see the steam rising off the moss at the same time, and for a moment you are not sure which way heat is supposed to flow here.

The rule of the place seems to be: what is made will be unmade slowly, but what grows will use the unmaking exactly as fast as it happens. Stone to sand to soil to moss to stone again. The town is not decaying. It is being translated, very, very slowly, from architecture back into landscape, without ever ceasing to be both.

If you stay until evening, the condensation freezes again. Every leaf, every stone edge, gets outlined in white. The bowl glitters. The wind picks up over the rim and the bottle-tone deepens, and you understand why whoever built here chose this one shallow shelter in a sea of exposed plateau: not to be hidden, but to be able to hear themselves think. And now there is no one left to hear it, but the bowl still listens to itself, dripping and popping and chiming in the cold, lush and empty and patient.
