This place is not on the mountain. It *is* the mountain, or what is left of it.

At an elevation where there should be nothing but wind-scoured rock and snow that never melts, the peak has been cut off. Leveled. Capped. Someone, a long time ago, poured a floor where the summit should have been and then built walls around the sky to keep it.

It is small. You understand its size all at once, which is strange for a place so high. You can stand at the western parapet and see the entire thing in a single turn of the head — a single, bounded room the size of a hamlet, a cathedral nave, a dry dock for airships that never came. A shallow, imperfect oval of concrete and steel, perhaps three hundred meters across. The wall that rings it is not high, maybe fifteen meters, but it is absolute. Beyond it, the world drops away in a sheer, constructed cliff-face — poured, corrugated, riveted, weather-stained — falling thousands of meters into cloud ocean. There is no natural ground left here to stand on. The floor under your feet is all made-things: iron plate gone black-green with age, poured aggregate patched and re-patched, vast grates over dark chasms. Even the scree has been replaced by ballast gravel, graded and sorted by machine.

And it is empty.

That is the first thing you feel after the altitude hits you — the thin, bright ache behind the eyes, the heart working too hard. The emptiness is not absence. It is scale. In this entire bowl of made-land, there are perhaps four things.

One is the Mill in the center.

It is not a building so much as an engine that forgot to be housed. A low, long assembly of boilers and turbines and condensers and pistons the size of rooms, all coupled together without a skin, as if its walls were stripped off and sold. It was built to do something industrial — to pump, to compress, to generate, to refine air itself — but whatever it was making, it still makes, slowly. It turns. It breathes. The flywheels, twenty feet in diameter and rusted to a deep, velvety maroon, rotate at a pace you could watch for an hour and barely see move, yet the sound they produce is total.

That is the second thing you notice: the sound is deafening.

Not sharp, not percussive. A low, omnipresent, bone-conducted roar. The Mill's slow exhalation, the vast ventilation fans set into the outer wall that have never stopped pulling, the wind itself being torn and re-torn as it hits the lip of this artificial summit. It is the sound of a factory the size of a town heard from inside its own chest. You feel it in your molars, in the soles of your boots on the iron floor. You have to lean close to another person to be heard, and even then your words come out thin and flattened. And yet, nothing moves quickly. The sound is constant because the process is so slow it has become a kind of climate. Pressure gauges with cracked glass faces tick one notch per day. Conveyor chains creep a few inches an hour, carrying nothing.

The heat is the reason anything lives up here at all.

At this height the air should be lethal-cold, but the Mill bleeds waste-heat. Warm is the right word — not hot, not tropical, but a stubborn, humid, bathhouse warmth that pools inside the walled oval and refuses to leave. It keeps the bowl five or ten degrees above the world. Moisture behaves strangely here because there is no real water. No lake, no stream, no snowmelt. On paper it is dry. In reality, it is damp. Always.

Water exists as condensation. The cold sky and the warm machines conspire to make it out of nothing. At night, fog pours over the parapet like a slow tide and beads on every surface. By morning, everything drips. You hear it underneath the roar if you listen — a constant, patient ticking and trickling. Dew the size of grapes hangs from every rivet. Rust-pits become temporary pools. Along the undersides of pipes, along the shaded side of the wall, clear water gathers and runs in invented, temporary rivers that only exist for a few hours before they evaporate again. The floor never quite dries. Your boots leave damp prints on iron.

Because of that damp and that warmth, and because the Mill has been here long enough to weather into a kind of geology, it is overgrown. Ferociously, impossibly overgrown — but not in the way a jungle is overgrown.

Vegetation here is lush but lonely. This is not a mat of green. It is a collection of exiles. Since there is no soil, the plants have made soil from the place itself. A species of black-green fern grows nowhere but from the seams between concrete slabs, its roots splitting the aggregate with patient, hydraulic force. Vines as thick as your wrist have learned to use copper pipe as trellis and insulation as mulch, hanging in heavy, waxy curtains from the gantry arms of the Mill where they sway in the fan-wind. Moss, brilliant and almost luminescent, has colonized the northern quadrant of the floor entirely, forming a single continuous carpet that is thick, springy, and warm to the touch, like a living rug over the iron grates. Bromeliads big as basins sit in the bowls of turbine housings, collecting condensation in their cups. Everything is healthy, glossy, over-watered, over-fed on heat and iron and rust dust. But between these islands of riotous growth are vast, empty stretches of bare, damp floor where nothing grows, where the distance between two ferns might be fifty meters of echoing emptiness.

That emptiness is the rule.

The fauna have learned it. They are abundant — you see movement everywhere if you are still — but never crowded. Never flocking. A loud, solitary crow-type bird with soot-black, oil-sheened feathers rides the updrafts over the bowl, crying once, then gliding for minutes in silence before it lands on the far parapet. Fat, slow bumblebees, adapted to the thin air, drone individually from vine-flower to vine-flower with a sound that almost cuts through the Mill's roar. Lizards the color of rust and concrete bask on the warm pipes, utterly still. Insects with long, translucent wings hatch from the temporary pools, live for a day, and die on the moss. And there are the other things — the strange ones. Things that have mistaken the built world for natural law.

A kind of pale, blind moth that navigates purely by vibration, its wings patterned exactly like flaked rust. A colony of swifts that have built their nests not of mud but of steel shavings and dried moss, cemented with condensation, inside the housing of a dead piston where it is always warm. Something small and mammalian that you glimpse only as a flicker between grates, that has learned to drink only from the lip of condensation trays.

Nothing here knows that it is unnatural. The erosion has gone on so long that age has made it natural. The edges of all the concrete are rounded, lichen-cracked, softened. Rebar shows like exposed bone, but it too is furry with oxide. The iron plates have settled and warped over decades into gentle, hill-like undulations. The built world has weathered into a weathered state, a long-existed, in-balance state where rust and root hold equal claim and neither is winning quickly.

Light here is normal, which makes it stranger. At this height you expect a searing, ultraviolet glare. Instead the bowl holds a soft, high-altitude daylight, diffused by the perpetual thin fog that forms and burns off, forms and burns off. Shadows are crisp but not harsh. Colors are true. You can see the green of the fern and the red of the rust and the blue-white of the sky beyond the wall without any filter. The world looks like it should make sense, which is why it so clearly doesn't.

The rules of this place are simple and unspoken:

1. Nothing that grows here touches earth.
2. Water is never more than a visitor; it arrives as air and leaves as air.
3. Sound never stops, but movement never hurries. If you stay to watch, the Mill's great arm will eventually complete its stroke. It will take an afternoon.
4. You can walk from one end of this world to the other before your breath is gone, and when you reach the wall, there is nowhere else to go but down.

If you stand in the exact center, on the moss between two of the Mill's vast, slow-turning wheels, the whole hamlet-sized world rings around you. Warm vapor condenses on your shoulders. A single bee passes, impossibly loud for a moment. The floor vibrates through your feet. And in the vast, hollow intervals between vine and fern, between machine and wall, the emptiness holds — not as loneliness, but as a kind of cathedral hush underneath the deafening — waiting for the next bead of water to fall.
