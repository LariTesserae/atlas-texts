It is night, and has been night for a very long time.

Not the cold night of high places, but a warm night. The air at this altitude should be thin and biting and it is thin, but it is not cold. It holds heat like a held breath. If you stood here you would feel it first on your skin before you saw anything - a dry, high warmth, the kind that comes off sun-baked stone hours after the sun is gone. Except there is no sun here. Not now. Maybe not ever directly.

This is a high country. Not a peak, but a lifted world.

A plateau so vast it has weather that belongs only to itself, a continent raised whole into the dark. The rock is old basalt and red sandstone and iron, laid down in flat, endless beds and then left to weather for epochs until the weathering itself became a kind of equilibrium. Nothing is jagged here. Everything is settled-form. The wind and the rare rain have carved this place not into sharpness but into patience - broad mesas with shoulders rounded smooth, canyons that are not gashes but long, slow folds, like cloth draped over a table. Arroyos with floors of polished gravel, buttes that stand like the worn molars of some old animal. All of it at an elevation where the horizon does not end so much as it falls away with the curve of the world. You can see weather moving a day away from you, a bruise-colored veil crawling across the dark.

There are no structures. No walls, no roads, no ruins, no foundations. No cut stone upon cut stone. No straight line that was made by a hand. The ground is unbroken except by itself. This is important to understand, because at first you will think you hear an engine.

You don't see light, at first. The sky is overcast with high, warm cloud that traps the heat in - a low, close darkness, not starry, not moonlit. A darkness that has depth and temperature. Your eyes adjust and find that there is light, but it is not from above. It is sparse and low and biological and geological.

Vegetation is scattered and spare - not a forest, not a grassland, but a dryland spacing. Here a clump of black, waxy succulents holding themselves low to the stone, there a stand of wiry, pale scrub with leaves like needles that bead with condensation. Further off, a lone, flat-topped thorn tree, its bark fissured and iron-dark, its roots finding a crack and disappearing. Between them is a great deal of nothing: warm stone, wind-scoured dust, pavements of cracked earth that tick as they cool. The plants are far apart by design. Each one commands its own circle of empty ground.

Water is not absent, but it is not generous. It is a dampness, an intermittence. The air itself carries it. As the warm plateau air cools by a few degrees in the deep night, it sweats. Condensation beads on every edge, on the thorns, on the basalt. It runs in slow, fat drops down rock faces and collects in shallow tinajas - natural basins polished into the stone over ten thousand years of this same nightly dew. Some hold a few inches of dark water for a week before the warm wind takes it again. Some are deep, shaded cisterns in the canyon folds where a spring line seeps, black and cold, and a little oasis gathers - a denser thicket of rushes, a slick of algae, the smell of mud. And sometimes it does rain. Not often, but when it does it comes all at once, a warm, heavy sheet that hammers the mesa top for an hour and then vanishes into the cracks, turning the arroyos into brief, silent rivers that run with red foam and are gone by morning. The country remembers water without being shaped by its abundance.

And there is life. A startling amount of it, for how empty it looks.

It is sparse in distribution but abundant in total, because the scale is so huge. You have to be still to see it. First, the ground seems to move - not with wind, but with insects. Low, broad beetles with backs like hammered copper, moving in lines between the succulents. Things that click and rustle. In the basins, amphibians with translucent skin cluster at the water's edge, their throats pulsing. On the stone flats, herds of something like gazelle but heavier, with ash-gray hides and wide, splayed feet, move far away in the dark, maybe thirty strong, taking hours to cross a single mesa face. Above, things fly on silent wings - leathery, large, drifting on the warm updrafts off the cliffs, not flapping so much as soaring.

They are quiet. Almost all of them are quiet. The predators do not cry out. The prey do not alarm. The whole ecology moves with a muffled, active industry. You see a kill only by the sudden absence of movement.

And then you hear it again - the engine.

It is not a machine that was built. There is nothing built here. But the place itself is industrial.

Down in the canyon folds, where the iron strata is exposed, the rock sweats a different kind of sweat. Veins of magnetite and living ore run through the sandstone like nerves, and where they are exposed to the damp air they hum. In the deep cisterns, colonies of eusocial things have made their nests not of wax but of slag.

Look closer at one of those copper-backed beetles: its abdomen is a furnace. It chews the iron-rich dust and the sparse, hard leaves and inside its gut, bacteria smelt it. It excretes a pellet of refined, still-warm iron and carries it, with a thousand others, to a mound that is not a hill but a foundry. The mound breathes. It is full of chambers and chimneys and bellows made of chitin and mud, and it glows from within - a dull, cherry-red glow. The air above it shivers. At night, from a distance, whole fields of these mounds pulse like factories on a night shift, dimly lit, exhaling warm, metallic steam in coordinated sighs. Pistons made of muscle and carapace pump. Assembly lines of smaller, blind workers carry half-formed larvae sheathed in iron casings along grooves worn smooth as conveyor belts.

Elsewhere, on the basalt flats, the ground itself does it without the insects. Geothermal heat bleeds up through cracks. Natural vents exhale on a perfect four-stroke rhythm - *hiss, chug, hiss, chug* - as pressure builds and releases in subterranean chambers, hot water hammering columns of stone up and down like cylinders. The stone around them has been polished by this endless motion into perfect bores. Water flashes to steam, condenses, flashes again. Sparks of natural voltaics crawl between the iron ridges during the warm wind, St. Elmo's fire running along the mesa edge for miles, a low, crackling electrification with no wire, no grid, only stone and air and damp.

It is all untouched and all mechanized. Scaled production without a producer. Electrified without a plug. An industry that evolved rather than was invented, and that has been running long enough to be part of the erosion, part of the balance. The slag mounds weather at the same rate as the buttes. The piston-vents are as old as the canyons.

The weirdness is not dramatic. It is a slight wrongness in how the dark behaves. Sounds carry too far and then stop abruptly, as if the warm air has pockets where it absorbs noise. The bioluminescence of the furnace-mounds and the faint foxfire of fungi on the thorn trees makes shadows that do not quite line up with their sources. The warmth has no source you can point to - no sun, no lava, just the retained heat of a high continent that breathes like a sleeping animal.

Mostly, it is quiet. The wind over the great, flat stone makes a low, continuous tone, almost below hearing. The piston-vents chug. The beetle-foundries tick and sigh. A distant herd moves, and their feet on stone make a soft, intermittent patter that is swallowed by the vastness. The scale defeats the sound. For every noise there are ten square miles of silence to dilute it. You could walk for days between waterholes and hear nothing but your own blood and the faint, industrial hum of the earth working its shift in the dark.

And always, the country continues its own slow, active work: the succulents swelling with nightly dew, the foundries cooling and firing, the predators circling the herds, the canyons deepening by a grain of sand at a time, the warm dark holding everything together under its huge, close sky.
