You are high, impossibly high, and it is hot.

That is the first wrongness you notice, before you can even see clearly. The air should be thin and cold at this elevation, it should scrape your lungs. Instead it is thick, wet, and heavy as a greenhouse. This is not a peak. It is a lifted world.

A plateau - but calling it a plateau makes it sound flat and simple. It is a continent knocked up into the sky. A vast, tilted table of old sandstone and basalt, two kilometers of sheer cliff on every side, its top lost in cloud. From the lowlands you would think it was a storm that never moves, a white anvil caught on stone. Up here, you are inside that storm. The elevation did not bring you closer to the sun, it brought the weather down to live with you.

The rock is old. Not jagged-new. Established. The edge of the mesa has been chewed by water for so long it no longer looks eroded, it looks *decided*. The cliffs are not sharp. They are columned, stepped, terraced, bellied out where springs have dissolved them from within. The top surface dips very slightly toward the center, a shallow bowl the size of a valley, a county, a small country. Everything that falls here, stays here for a while.

And everything falls. Water is the rule of this place.

There is no dry season, there is only wetter and wettest. The heat lifts water off every leaf and every black pool and throws it into the air, where it condenses again almost immediately. The atmosphere sweats. Midday, clouds form at chest height between the trees and drift through the forest like cold animals, leaving your skin beaded. Late afternoon, they collapse without thunder into a warm, straight-down rain that lasts twenty minutes and then stops as if a tap were turned. It never really dries after.

The stone weeps. You can hear it before you see the source - not a rush, not a river. A seep, everywhere. The caprock is porous beneath its dark skin of algae and the water that lives in the sky lives in the stone too. The cliffs bleed in a thousand places. Sheets of water slide down vertical gardens without ever gathering into a stream. Hollows in the rock fill and spill and fill again. The ground underfoot is not ground at all, it is a sponge ten meters deep: peat and matted roots and half-decayed leaves floating over stone, quaking when you step, sighing water up around your boots. Every depression is a pool, black or tea-red with tannin, rimmed with sphagnum that hangs into it. Between the pools, the water runs in braids, ankle-deep, warm as blood, carving and recarving the same soft channels through the peat.

And on that wet, hot, high sponge - impossible, furious green.

This should be paramo, lichen, stunted trees. Instead it is jungle, but a jungle that has learned to be short and dense to survive the wind at the edge. Nowhere above fifteen meters tall, but so layered and crowded you cannot see five meters in any direction. No human has ever cleared it, planted it, cut it. There is no road, no ruin, no piling of stones. Even a trail would drown here in a week.

Trunks twist and then flatten, buttressed to hold in the soaked peat. Every surface is another surface. Bark is furred with moss and filmy ferns. Moss is thatched with liverworts. Liverworts are starred with orchids the size of your thumb, white and waxy. Giant bromeliads - each big as a washtub - are wedged in every fork, holding their own private ponds that overflow constantly down the trunk. Lianas as thick as your leg hang straight down, dripping. Leaves are huge, paddle-shaped, slick with water, designed to dump rain as fast as it falls. The canopy, though low, is complete. Light comes through as normal daylight - not dim, not blazing - but scattered into a green-gold diffusion that makes everything glow from within. There are no shadows, only brighter or darker greens. The air itself is green with spores and scent.

It is quiet. That's the strangest thing for how alive it is.

The industry of this place makes almost no noise.

Because it *is* industrial. Without a single building, without a nail, without a smokestack laid by hands, this valley works like a factory.

Listen past the drips. Hold still until the mosquitoes find you and then leave you. You will hear it:

A low, rhythmic sighing from the eastern cliffs where the water has drilled vertical shafts through the sandstone. Water and hot air shuttle up and down them all day as pressure changes, piston-chambers of stone that breathe - *huhhh... huhhh... huhhh...* - exhaling warm mist in pulses. The pools at their mouths churn without wind.

A clicking, endless and ordered, from under the leaves. Leafcutter ants have highways here. Not trails - highways. Ribbons of ochre clay a foot wide, kept bare by constant feet, running for hundreds of meters in straight lines across the peat, over root-bridges the ants have reinforced with silk and soil, around the pools on causeways. Columns of them, two lanes, cutters moving out empty-jawed, carriers moving back with green sails held high. At intersections, workers direct traffic with antennae. Their nests swell out of the forest floor like low kilns, mounds fifteen feet across, vented with perfect chimneys that stream warm, fungus-smelling air. Inside, fungus farms the size of rooms break down leaf matter with a sour, chemical heat. You can smell the production.

Termite cathedrals further in, black and hard as slag, five meters high, humming faintly with the vibration of a million wings fanning to keep the fungus at temperature - a living foundry that never sleeps.

Even the water itself manufactures. Where springwater heavy with dissolved silica and iron seeps over the lip of a terrace, it precipitates. Bacteria thick as colored oil - rust-red, ochre, sullen green - pull metal from the water and lay it down in terraces, basins, fluted dams. The pools grow their own rims, millimeter by millimeter, still steaming from the geothermal heat that keeps this whole high place hot when it should be freezing. The dams overfill, crust, build, overfill again. Natural settling tanks and crystallization pans, endless and quiet, polishing stone.

And the lightning. Almost every afternoon, the condensing air cannot hold its charge. A bolt hits one of the emergent, iron-capped trees that the forest has grown as rods - charred, leafless, perpetually black at the crown. The charge races down the wet trunk into the peat without setting fire, and for a second the whole forest flickers. Beetles with mirror backs collect on those trees afterwards, and frogs with blue bioluminescent throats call once, sharply, as if a switch were thrown.

Everything is abundant and everything is busy, but not frantic. Predator and prey, growth and rot, evaporation and rain, cutting and building, all turning at equatorial speed. A chestnut-colored coati noses through a bromeliad pond and sends a spray of tiny fish and tadpoles out with the overflowing water. A hawk, thick-shouldered and utterly silent, drops through a cloud that is sitting on the ground and takes a dove that never saw it. Within minutes ants are at the fallen feathers, taking them for fiber.

There is no rot smell, despite the wet. The system eats itself clean. Fungi flash white overnight on a fallen branch and by noon the branch is lace.

If you stand still long enough, the water will rise to meet you. Not in a flood, but seeping up through the moss around your feet, cold for a second before it warms. The forest exhales again. A fresh cloud is being born between two trees ten feet away, condensing out of nothing, pearling on the hairs of a fern.

No one made this place. No one needs to. It makes itself, at scale, endlessly, hot and wet and high in the air where nothing like it should be, a green engine running silently on water and heat and light.
