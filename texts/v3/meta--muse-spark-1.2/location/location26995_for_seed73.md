You come up over the last rise and the air should be cold.

It isn't.

That is the first rule of this place: height does not mean cold here.

You are very high. High enough that the horizon has a visible curve to it, high enough that the clouds hang *below* you in other valleys, high enough that the sky at noon is not blue but a deep, saturated indigo that bruises to violet at the edges. The light, though, is ordinary — not the searing high-altitude glare you expect, not dimmed. A normal, white-gold sun stands overhead, but its heat is filtered and held. The atmosphere is thick with water it shouldn't be able to hold at this elevation. It hangs, it condenses, it breathes.

This is not a peak. It is a country of peaks that forgot to be separate.

A vast, uneven tableland — a smashed-together archipelago of mesas, tepui, and tilted blocks — stretches out at an elevation where there should be snow and lichen and wind-scoured rock. Instead it is hot. A low, wet, equatorial heat that pools and stays. The rock itself is warm to the touch. At dawn it steams.

Geologists would call it established terrain. Nothing here is sharp or newly broken. Everything has been here long enough to negotiate its shape with everything else. The basalt and red sandstone have weathered into soft, massive forms: broad steps, scooped bowls, rounded towers with undercut waists, long whale-back ridges. The cliffs are not jagged; they are draped. Water has found every joint and widened it patiently over millennia, leaving vertical gardens in every crack. Scree slopes have stabilized, colonized, become soil. Even the rockfalls look intentional, like furniture.

And water is everywhere, but not as lakes or rivers the way you expect. At this height water wants to fall away, immediately. Here it doesn't. It lingers.

The stone weeps.

Every exposed face sweats continuously — not from rain, though it does rain almost every afternoon in a brief, hot, hard hammering — but from the air itself. The hot air cools just enough against the stone at night to wring itself out, and by morning every cliff is beaded and streaming. Thin sheets run down black rock in glassy films. Seep-springs burst halfway up vertical walls and run sideways for a while before remembering gravity. On the flat tops of the mesas, the water has no where to go, so it stays near the surface. The soil is saturated to within an inch of the top. You step and water sighs up around your boot and vanishes again.

This makes the high country a kind of cold-hot marsh in the sky. Not a swamp. A sponge. Peat and moss and dense mat-root over basalt, cut through with mirror-shallow pools, braided runnels, reed beds, and sudden, dark sinkholes where water has dissolved the rock beneath. Condensation trees — that's what you'll call them later — stand in the open pans, and you understand why.

The vegetation is lush to the point of aggression, but layered, not choking.

Because the light is normal and the water is constant and the heat never breaks, everything grows. But nothing grows fast. This is a slow place. The dynamic is glacial even though the temperature is feverish. Leaves unfurl over weeks, water moves in inches per hour across the flats. And so the growth is dense in the way an old forest is dense: not weedy, but saturated. Every volume is occupied. Moss under fern under shrub under tree under vine under epiphyte under hanging garden. The mesas are crowned with cloud-forest that has no right to be at this heat, dwarfed and twisted and absolutely crowded. Trunks are swaddled in bromeliads the size of armchairs. Lilies big enough to sit in open in the marsh pools. Grasses grow eight feet tall with stems as thick as your wrist, but spaced so you can walk between them like columns in a hall.

The colors are too much where the water touches air — algae bloom in hot pools in sheets of orange and viridian, mineral stains paint the weeping cliffs in ochre and malachite. And the plants themselves have learned strangeness.

Some of that strangeness is natural, some of it is not, and after a few hours you stop trying to separate them.

The Tech is not in buildings. There are almost no buildings. Scattered — that's the word. Once in a long while you find a structure: a low, open-walled shrine of unjointed stone, a single pylon leaning like a dead tree, a stair cut into a cliff that goes nowhere, a perfect circle of white material flush with the peat that is warm and faintly humming. No cities. No roads. Just artifacts, left like tools after a job.

The Tech is in the atmosphere and the rock and the water. It is what makes a hot highland possible. If you look closely at the air on a still hot noon, you see it — a faint lace, a moiré, a lattice of almost-invisible threads spanning between mesas, between condensation trees, between cliff faces. It shivers. It catches light and throws it away. It is what diffuses the brutal high-altitude sun into normal light. It is what holds heat in at night and bleeds it during the day so the air stays breathable and wet. It harvests water from the air and lets the stone weep. It is nanoscale and continent-scale at once, and it is clearly not being maintained anymore so much as *being* — grown into the ecology. Vines use it as trellis. Moss grows along it. Insects lay eggs on its nodes.

In other places the Tech is more overt. Fields of black glass reeds on the eastern scarp — each one ten meters tall, perfectly hexagonal, humming at a pitch you feel in your teeth — that draw geothermal heat up and turn it into that wet, hot wind. Pools where water hangs in the air in standing, slowly rotating columns that never fall, even when you put your hand through them; they reform behind you. Rocks that are heavier on one side than physics allows, so that streams flow uphill for a stretch before correcting. None of it is noisy with machinery. It is quiet, weathered, integrated. You get the sense it was placed to make this place — to lift a lowland jungle into the stratosphere and keep it there — and then the makers left or became something else.

The fauna love it.

It is abundant and loud. Not just insects, though the insects are a constant, layered chorus — cicadas like power lines, beetles with wing-cases that click like knuckles, dragonflies the length of your forearm with glass wings that thrum. Amphibians call from every pool, a deep, syncopated *booming* that carries across the flats. Birds — or things that occupy the bird niche — are everywhere but you hear them more than you see them; they flash between grass columns, metallic green and ash-white. In the marsh pans, things move under the duckweed: long, warm-water newts, black fish that can walk on their fins for a while on land before flopping back, herds of small, capybara-like grazers with rain-slick coats that part the reeds in lines.

The larger presences stay in the layered forest on the mesa crowns. You hear branches move when nothing should be heavy enough to move them. At dusk, the condensation trees — which are not trees at all but colonial organisms that look like banyans made of translucent tubes — begin to glow faintly from within and to exhale a fine mist. The mist catches the lattice light and the whole tableland briefly looks like it is breathing. Things come to drink the mist: moths as big as plates, shy canopy mammals with six limbs that move with agonizing slowness, one handhold at a time. Nothing is in a hurry here.

The sound of this place is not a single sound. It is a saturation. Water trickling over stone everywhere at once, wind combing through the grass pillars, the insect power-line, the amphibian drums, the faint resonant hum of the black reeds and the lace — all of it layered into a loud, warm, continuous hush that is not white noise but *place* noise. You can pick threads out of it if you listen, but you can't turn it off. At night it gets louder, not quieter.

If you stay for days — and the heat and wet and thin-thick air make you want to move slowly, to adopt the pace of everything else — you start to notice the rules that hold the contradictions together:

1. Height holds heat, because the lace won't let it go.
2. Water never leaves, only cycles: air to rock to pool to air again, in place.
3. Growth never rushes, because there is no winter to race against.
4. What was built is now grown.

You find a shallow overhang where someone, a long time ago, smoothed the floor and set a low basalt bench to look out over the valley-between-mesas. The valley is not a valley; it is a gap between two tablelands where clouds have pooled below like a white sea, and the lace bridges it with a faint, barely visible catenary. On the far side, another marsh-forest glows in the afternoon. A herd of the reed grazers moves, barely perceptibly, through the grass. A column of suspended water stands in the middle distance, rotating, catching the sun.

The stone under your hand is warm and damp. It has been warm and damp for a very, very long time, and will be for a very long time after.

You sit on the bench. The air condenses on your skin.
