It is not a forest. It is not a city in a forest.

It is a city that *is* a forest, or a forest that decided to be a city, and then forgot which one it was supposed to be.

You find it by going down. Not far down, not into depth — the land here is low, the oldest kind of low. This whole country was once mountains and then it stopped being mountains. Time wore it down to a broad, shallow bowl, a palm of basalt and compressed dust leagues across, so flat and so old that the horizon doesn't look like an edge anymore, just a softening. Wind has had ten million years to sand every ridge into a slope, every slope into a sigh. Nothing is sharp. Even the stones are round as river eggs, though no river has run here in an age.

And in the middle of that bowl, where the ground sags just a little lower, there is a break. A pocket. A hidden room in the world.

From the surface you would walk right over it. A fissure no wider than a house, a lip of dark, dry bark overlapping stone. You have to kneel and then slip sideways, and then you are inside.

And inside is impossibly larger than outside. Not vast — intimate, contained. A single hollow, the size of a cathedral nave or a small town square, sealed over. The roof is not rock. The roof is living.

Everything here is plant, but not plant as you know it.

There is almost no water in this place. The air is cool — a constant, cellar-cool that makes your breath faintly visible — and utterly dry. Your lips crack within minutes. The ground underfoot is not soil but a felt of desiccated fibers, salt-pale, rust-pale, like compacted paper or bone-dry cork that gives slightly and then springs back. It smells of dust and wax and old woodsmoke, with no dampness at all. If you dig, you find no groundwater, no rot. These plants do not drink. They keep themselves with oils and resins, with a slow, waxy chemistry that beads on their surfaces like amber sweat. Cut one and it does not bleed sap; it weeps a clear, cool, non-evaporating glycerin that seals the wound within an hour.

And they are everywhere. The walls are walls only because the plants grew as walls. Huge, hollow trunks like the pillars of a basilica, but joined, fused, arching into one another. Ribs of black-brown timber as thick as houses, braided with vines as thick as your waist that have hardened into something more like rope-wood or tendon. From them hang curtains of leaf — but the leaves are not green. In perfect darkness, green is a waste. They are black-purple, grey-black, the color of dried blood and slate, broad as blankets, stiff, overlapping like roof tiles. They tile the ceiling in a seamless thatch a dozen layers deep, so not a photon from the surface world gets through. The darkness is absolute. 0. Not dim. Not dusk. The dark of being inside a seed, inside a closed hand.

You learn to see not with eyes but with hands and ears. Because this place is loud.

In a normal forest, sound is animal. Here there are no animals. No birds, no insects, no rats in the thatch, no echo of anything with a throat or wings. And yet the pocket is never quiet. The plants are the sound.

They grow so slowly you cannot see it — a millimeter a year, a creak a decade — but when they do move, the whole hollow speaks. The great trunks groan as their fibers tighten in the cool. The stiff leaves tick and pop as they expand and contract. Seed pods the size of barrels, dry as gourds, hang from the high arches and suddenly split along ancient seams with a report like a rifle shot, spilling not seeds but a cloud of papery, oil-filled sails that helicopter down for minutes, brushing the walls with a dry whisper. Somewhere, always, a vine under tension gives way with a deep *thunk* that vibrates through the soles of your feet. The city amplifies it.

Because it is a city.

Once you adjust to the dark, you realize the plant *is* the infrastructure, but it has been helped. Guided. The hollow trunks are hollow for a reason — they are houses. Doorways have been cut with adzes, the edges still showing the chipped scallops of stone tools. Inside, the pith has been scooped and smoothed, floors leveled with beaten fiber mats. Ladders of lashed limb-wood lean against pillars. Rope bridges of braided vine-fiber, frayed but still strong, connect balconies that were coaxed from living burls. On one wall, a whole terrace of shelves has been carved directly into the living wood, each niche holding clay pots — hand-formed, low-fired, primitive — now empty and bone-dry, for storing the resinous oil the plants exude.

No metal. No glass. No wire. Everything is hand-work, simple fabrication. Wood, stone, fiber, clay, wax. You can see where fire was used sparingly, char marks to hollow a larger room, smoke stains on a low ceiling where a hearth once sat. But there is no ash now. No one has kept a fire for a very long time.

The scale of it is what disorients. It is urban in organization — streets, alleys, a central avenue that runs the length of the hollow between two rows of trunk-houses, a plaza where the roots braid into a kind of amphitheater — but it is all contained in this one hidden room. You can walk from one end of the town to the other in five minutes, and in that walk pass fifty dwellings. It is a town that chose to fit inside a single fruit, a single lung. Beyond its walls there is no other town. Only the endless, flattened, ancient desert above.

And it is empty in a way that has nothing to do with abandonment. Even when it was inhabited, it must have felt empty, because the spaces between things are so great. The architecture is plant-hollow and air. The avenue is wide not because they needed width, but because the two trunks that form it grew wide apart and no one could move them. The plaza is vast and vacant, the ceiling lost in darkness above it. Density here is a trick: there is so much living mass — tons and tons of timber and leaf and root — but it is all pushed to the edges, to the structure itself, leaving the middle as a held breath, a hollow, a near-void. You feel exposed in the middle of so much life.

The strangeness is not that the plants grew into a city. It is that they seem to remember being one. New growth follows old carpentry. A fresh shoot will sprout along the straight line of a cut lintel, as if the plant has learned the right angle. A vine will thicken exactly where a rope bridge once needed a support, bridging a gap no vine needed to bridge for light or water. The living mass maintains the urban plan without inhabitants, slowly, patiently. A doorway cut a hundred years ago has not healed over; the plant has lignified its rim, framed it. A collapsed balcony has been cradled by new roots growing up underneath like hands.

Nothing hurries here. That is the rule. Water is scarce, light is absent, heat is low, and so everything budgets, everything waits. A pod takes twenty years to ripen and splits in a second. A root takes a century to cross an alley and then holds it for a millennium. Change happens, but across durations a human would call stillness. You could sit for a day in the cool dark and hear the town creak and settle and think nothing had changed — and then notice that a leaf above you has turned, just a fraction, toward your body heat.

You listen in the dark. The air is cool on your skin, utterly dry, scented with wax and dust. Above you the slow city breathes, cracks, sighs. Below you the ancient ground, worn smooth as skin, holds the hidden pocket like a seed that never needed to sprout.

There are no animals to hear it with you.
