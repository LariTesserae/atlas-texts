It is not a valley, exactly. It is not a plain. It is a shallow place - a low bowl pressed into the earth before the earth learned how to be earth.

The ground here hasn't finished being made.

You arrive by dropping - not descending, just suddenly being lower. The world outside might have mountains, might have weather, but here the elevation gives up. A wide, almost imperceptibly dished saucer of raw basalt maybe three hundred meters across, rimmed by a lip of dark stone no higher than your knee. You could step over the rim without noticing you had entered, if not for the change in air. This is an embryonic geology. No rivers have cut it. No wind has softened it. The stone was extruded and cooled last week, or last eon - time is not behaving here - and it still holds the shine of cooling, a black glass with bubbles frozen mid-rise, flow-ripples like thumbprints in cooling wax. It is unweathered, un-scarred, pre-erosion. There are no gullies, no scree, no soil to speak of. Just the raw, slightly warm-then-cold slab of planet, and on top of it, the city.

Because there is a city.

In a place this small - you could walk its diameter in ten minutes if there were a straight road, which there is not - there should not be a city. A hamlet, maybe. A research station. But this is urban density compressed into grove-scale, cathedral-scale, single-room-scale. It is a town folded until it touches itself.

It starts at the rim and does not stop until the center. No streets in the way you expect streets. The built fills the bowl to the brim and then a little higher, in layers. Dwellings, or what you assume are dwellings, stacked and braided and grown through each other. The material is not concrete, not steel, though it borrows their colors. Closer it looks poured, then grown. Matte black basalt at the foundation where it grips the raw geology, then rising into translucent amber, opalescent white, a kind of solidified air that holds weight without pillars. Some walls are thick as paper and opaque on one side, transparent on the other. Some corridors are not corridors at all but gaps left between structures that have leaned together for warmth. Fire escapes that were never needed fold into balconies that were never planned. Every surface is utilized. Every cubic meter contains three or four presences - a doorway behind a doorway behind a shutter that is also a solar collector that is also a memory lattice. It is not cramped, exactly. It is saturated. Like a circuit board viewed from very close.

And it is cold.

Not the theatrical cold of blizzards. A patient, high-latitude, interstitial cold. About minus six, all the time. The light is normal - a flat, overcast, mid-afternoon white that comes from everywhere and nowhere, shadowless, as if the sky were a great diffusion panel. The light does not warm. It illuminates without heat. Breath plumes and does not dissipate quickly; it hangs in short-lived clouds at mouth-height in the narrow alleys before settling as frost.

Water here is not abundant, but it is insistent. There is no river. There is damp. Condensation. The cold and the dense breathing of the city and its machines make every vertical surface weep. Amber walls are beaded. Black stone in the alleys is slick with a film that never quite becomes a flow. In the morning, which comes slowly and without a sunrise, everything is rimed with hoarfrost that melts by noontime into a slow drip. Drip, collect, pool. Small pockets of water appear in the embryonic seams of the basalt - perfectly round kettle holes, shallow as saucers, holding sky-light for days until the cold drinks them or they evaporate. Once in a while - not on a schedule - a fine, cold rain falls straight down for seven minutes, hissing on warm vents, and then stops.

Because of that damp and that cold, life is sparse and stubborn.

There is lichen, mostly. Not green - a pale, almost luminous grey-green and a deep rust red - clinging to the windward edges of the higher structures where the condensation runs thickest. In the lee of a heat exchanger, a patch of needle grass has established itself in a crack, no larger than your hand, its blades frost-stiff. On the lowest roofs, a kind of moss grows that is not moss, but close: dense, low, black-green, beading water like mercury. It is the only soft thing in the city.

Fauna is an event. You might spend days here and see nothing alive that was not built. Then a single crow, or something that remembers being a crow, will pass overhead, casting a too-slow shadow, and not land. In one of the standing pools, there are water-boatmen, three of them, skating in perfect geometric triangles. They have been there since you arrived. They have not eaten.

The built is not inert. This is the beyond-modern part, the part your eyes try to edit.

The city maintains itself. Walls heal when scraped. At night - though night here is only a slight dimming of that flat light - you can hear the faintest ticking as the amber material knits. Doorframes resize themselves by a centimeter when you are not looking. A staircase on the eastern incline has thirteen steps going up and nine steps going down. A building on the north side exists in two places at once; if you enter its lower door you exit from a balcony two blocks south, and the people who live there consider this unremarkable plumbing.

Gravity is local. In the center of the bowl, dust hangs in the air for minutes after being kicked. On the western edge, your coat pulls a little heavier. Angles are not reliable. If you stand at the exact center and turn slowly, you will count five corners on a four-cornered room. The sky above the bowl sometimes reflects the city instead of itself - you will look up and see the underside of an alley, inverted, with a figure looking down at you who is also you from three seconds ago.

No one is alarmed. Or rather, the few presences you do see - figures in insulated, seamless clothes that seem to be part of the walls - move as if this were all expected infrastructure. They walk through walls that become doors for them. They carry nothing. They exchange no words. Their faces are calm in the way of people who have delegated physics to maintenance systems they no longer fully understand.

The rules here, insofar as there are rules:

1.  The place is small but not limited. You can keep walking without reaching an edge, because the alleys fold. The city is hamlet-sized on the map and borderless on foot.

2.  Nothing erodes, nothing weathers, except by permission. The raw basalt should be scoured by ice and rain, but it isn't. The buildings should decay, but they don't. Age happens elsewhere.

3.  Sound is muffled by the density and the cold. Footsteps absorb. Even the ventilation - a deep, geothermal sigh that rises through the center every hour or so - is felt in the soles of your feet more than heard. Mostly it is quiet, a high, thin quiet where you hear the drip of condensation from a pipe thirty feet above you, and your own pulse.

4.  Change is slow. So slow you might think it is static. But if you mark a wall with a scratch, by evening the scratch will have migrated two inches to the left. The pools evaporate over days, refill over days. The lichen grows a millimeter a year. The city learns, very gradually, how to be more itself. It is patient. It has nowhere to be, being already everywhere it needs to be in this shallow, cold, damp, newborn bowl.

You can leave by stepping over the rim again. Most don't, right away. There is a comfort in the density, in the way the impossible infrastructure holds you close without touching, in the way the light stays even and the air stays cold and damp on your cheeks. It is a small urbanism at the beginning of the world, waiting for erosion that may never come.
