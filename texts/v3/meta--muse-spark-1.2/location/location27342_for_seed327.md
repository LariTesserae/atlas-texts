It is very high.

Not high like a hill you climb and catch your breath. High like you have left the rest of the world below an ocean of air. The trail up is a long, switched-back scar through scree and stunted pine and then - suddenly - it isn't. It levels. It opens.

You come over the lip expecting more mountain and instead you find a room.

That is the only word for it. A room with no roof. A shallow bowl, perhaps three hundred meters across at its widest, cut into the flank of the range like a thumbprint in cold clay. On three sides the mountain continues up in dark, weathered walls - not sheer, not new. These cliffs have had time to settle. They are basalt and old slate, softened at the shoulders and chins, bearded everywhere with lichen the color of rust and bone. They lean slightly inward, as if listening. On the fourth side, the bowl simply ends. The ground falls away in a clean, grass-soft lip and then there is nothing but distance, the white-blue gulf of valleys thousands of feet below, the peaks on the far side of the range floating like islands. The wind comes over that open edge constantly.

The floor of the bowl is not flat. It is stepped, terraced by time and water and patient hands.

Because it is wet here. Improbably wet for this height.

It should be thin, dry, sun-blasted. Instead the air is cool - cold enough that your hands stay tucked in your sleeves, warm enough that nothing freezes hard. A cool that lives in the stone. And the stone weeps.

Every vertical face sweats. Water beads along the stratified rock, gathers in seams, runs in dark varnish-stripes down to the shelves below. It doesn't rain so much as the air itself condenses. You can see it in the late morning, when the sun finally clears the eastern wall: little wandering veils of mist that form from nothing over the grass, thicken, touch a leaf, and vanish into a droplet. The atmosphere is always near its dew point. Your boots never quite dry. Even when the sky above the rim is a hard, normal blue, the bowl holds its own weather.

That water has made everything lush in a tight, alpine way.

It's not jungle lush. It's cushion lush. The terraces are thick with it - moss so deep it hollows under your step, star-moss and haircap and a black-green sphagnum that squelches. Tufts of hard fescue and alpine clover. Rosettes of saxifrage clinging to every rock with white, five-petaled flowers. In the wettest places, where the weeping walls feed small pools, cotton-grass shivers. Dwarf willow no higher than your knee but decades old, its branches twisted and prostrate, forming dark, springy mats. Everything is low and dense and intensely green, the green that comes from growing slow and not being grazed to death. When the wind parts the mist you see that the whole floor is patterned, like a quilt of different greens and ochres and the brown-black of wet peat.

The shape of the land is established. Nothing here is raw or collapsing. The water has had a very long time to cut its little runnels - six, seven of them, each no wider than a stride - that braid and re-braid across the shelves, falling in bright stair-steps from pool to pool before they join and slip over the open lip in a thin, wind-shredded veil. You can see where the water used to run higher, old dry channels now mossed over, perfectly smooth. The pools themselves have soft, peaty banks undercut just enough to make dark overhangs. Rounded boulders, dropped by an old glacier and then worried for millennia by weather, sit half-sunk in the turf like sleeping animals, their north sides furred with lichen.

And it is empty.

That is the first strange thing about its abundance. If you stand still you can see the entire bowl at once - from wall to wall, from the tarns to the void-edge - and there is almost nothing in it per volume. Huge intervals of air and moss and stone between any two presences. No forest to hide in, no thickets. The emptiness is architectural, hollow, as if the mountain scooped this place out and forgot to fill it. A single boulder holds a whole world of lichen. A single pool holds sky. You could throw a stone from one end and watch it a long time before it landed.

And yet it is not lonely, because it is loud and it is alive. Actively, insistently alive.

The sound hits you first after the wind. The wind itself is a constant low note over the rim, funneling into the bowl and circling - it doesn't blow through, it circles, slow and audible, around the curved walls. Over that, always, the water: trickling, dripping, spilling from shelf to shelf in a thousand small hard conversations. The weeping rocks tick and patter even when it isn't raining, as if the mountain is counting.

Then the fauna. They are everywhere but spaced, as if each has been allotted a great territory inside this small place.

Insects, for one - impossible numbers for this altitude. Clouds of midges and pale alpine bumblebees that work the saxifrage with a low, thrumming roar you feel in your chest. Dragonflies with sooty, glass wings that hunt over the pools, their wings clicking.

Pikas call to each other from rock to rock, sharp, piercing whistles that echo off the walls. You hear one, then a long empty pause, then an answer from impossibly far - but far here is only forty meters. Marmots whistle too, deeper. And above them all, the birds. This is a waystation for them, a green bowl of food and water in a sea of rock. Redstarts, accentors, snowfinches, a pair of choughs that ride the circling wind and never seem to land, crying *kee-ah, kee-ah* that reverberates. At dusk, when the insects rise in a column over the warmest pool, swallows cut through them so fast you hear the stitch of their wings.

You see them not as a flock but as single bright motions with vast empty air between. One bee, then a long silence of moss. One bird, then the whole sky empty. Abundance measured not by crowding but by persistence.

There are marks that people have been here. Not many. Not a village - nothing could winter here for long, the snow would seal the bowl like a lid. But scattered, faint, at the scale of hands.

On the driest terrace, near the largest pool, a low sheepfold of dry-stone, its walls only waist-high and partly tumbled, the stones already sinking into the turf and wearing their own moss caps. No roof ever. Beside it, a more careful thing: a cairn of flat slate stones, stacked not as a trail marker but as a small, open-sided shrine, about the height of your chest. Inside, protected from the circling wind, are offerings left and left again over years - a wooden spoon, blackened with use, a coil of wool yarn gone grey with rain, a carved marmot no bigger than a thumb, its ears worn smooth from handling. Further along the wall, where the rock weeps most heavily, someone has wedged alder branches to make a lean-to frame, lashed with sinew and root-cord, now half-collapsed under the weight of living moss that has grown over it.

Primitive work. Everything is what a person could carry up and make with a knife and hands. No metal nails, no sawn planks - just stone, wood, wool, bone. The places where fire has been are shallow scoops lined with soot-darkened stones. You can see the polish on the rocks at sitting-height, where generations of watchers have rested their backs while watching flocks that summer here for a few weeks and then are gone.

Nothing is new. Nothing is ruined, either. Just worn to the point where it belongs.

At noon the bowl is ordinary - bright, cool, normal high-altitude light, shadows short and hard. But if you stay until the sun lowers behind the western wall, something slightly unusual happens that you only notice peripherally.

The water droplets that hang in the air before they fall - the condensation that never quite becomes rain - they catch the light and hang a fraction too long. For a heartbeat they seem to drift upward with the circling wind before gravity remembers them. And the pools, which are peaty and dark, reflect the cliffs even when the wind is ruffling them, as if the surface has a second, calmer skin just beneath the ripples. It's not uncanny. It's just a little wrong, a little more still and a little more slow than it should be, like the place is breathing on a different rhythm than you are.

By evening the mists are back, forming from nothing between the tussocks. The trickling gets louder as the air cools. The pikas call less, the insects hum lower, and a pair of fox kits - russet against the green - come out to drink at the second pool, pausing with their reflections perfect beneath them, ears swiveling at the enormous, circling sound of wind and water and wings held inside stone walls.

It is a small place. You can hold its whole shape in your mind. And it is an active place, never still - water moving, air condensing, grass growing, wings beating, stones very, very slowly settling deeper into moss. An empty bowl, full to its brim with motion, perched so high the world below is weather.
