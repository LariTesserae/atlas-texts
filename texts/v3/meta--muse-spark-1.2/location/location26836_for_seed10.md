On the roof of the world, where the world has gone soft and old.

It is not a peak. Not anymore. At this elevation - high enough that the air is thin and hurts a little to pull in - there should be sharp things: granite teeth, ridges, cirques cut by ice. There are none. This place is ancient in the truest sense, erosion 3.0, end-state erosion. The mountains that were here have been unmade. What is left is a high peneplain, a cold plateau that rolls on and forgets itself, so flattened and so settled that the horizon is a perfect, dim circle. The rock underneath is there, but you never see it sharp. It comes to the surface as broad, whale-backed domes, polished to a dark gloss by ten million years of water and wind and ice that is no longer there, then buried again under living mass. Nothing protrudes. Everything has been rounded, planed, licked smooth by time until the land is a shallow bowl that holds the sky.

It is cold. Not the clean, killing cold of a summit. A wet cold, a breathing cold 0.5/3 that lives in the peat and the mist. The temperature never rises above the low single digits. Your breath plumes and does not dissipate, it joins the atmosphere which is already saturated. Water is everywhere but rarely as open lake. This is a wet at 2.4/3 - perched water, water that can't decide if it is air or land.

The entire plateau is a sponge. Sphagnum and liverwort and black-green cushion moss many meters deep have become the terrain itself. Vegetation is not *on* the land, it *is* the land. 2.7/3 total. You walk not on soil but on a living mattress that gives under your weight and sighs. The plant mass is an architecture without builders - columns and vaults and hanging curtains, but all plant. Here a tussock swells to the size of a house, a tight dome of interwoven stems holding itself up. There, a forest of ancient clubmoss rises twenty meters, each trunk as thick as a torso, bark like folded felt, their crowns not forming a canopy but standing apart from one another like pillars in a ruined cathedral. Between them are avenues of emptiness - because for all that the world is plant, it is sparse. 0.6/3. Life does not crowd. Each titanic moss-hummock stands alone in a tarn of black water. Each grove of pale, glassy horsetails holds a territory. You can see for half a kilometer in any direction across the dim, open valley-basin to the next forms, and that openness is unsettling. An entire watershed, town-sized and more, made of maybe a few hundred individuals, each enormous and alone.

And it weeps. Water beads constantly on every frond tip and falls without a rain event. The rocks that do show through - those old shoulders of basalt - are perpetually wet, dark, weeping a sheet of water from no visible source, as if the stone itself is condensing out of the air. The ground squelches. Footfalls release cold water that wells up around your boot and stays. Pools. Rills that braid and unbraid through the moss without banks. Tarns the color of tea, so clear you can see the peat roots descending down forever, then suddenly opaque. The air is dim, 1.4/3, not night but a perpetual overcast filtered through high mist. The sun is a brighter smear behind cloud. Light comes flat and diffuse and gray-blue, and it never throws a hard shadow. Everything looks like late afternoon before a storm that never breaks.

There are no structures. No walls, no roads, no cairns, no cut stumps. 0.5/3 - untouched in any human sense. And yet this is an industrial place. 1.8/3. That is the contradiction that makes the plateau impossible.

It factories without being built.

The plants are machines. The great clubmosses groan. On their trunks, lignin plates slide against each other like pistons and vent with a hiss of hot, iron-smelling steam that instantly condenses in the cold. The peat itself compresses under its own weight and in its depths you hear a low, subsonic thump - a heartbeat on the scale of geology, a press. Seams of half-coal ignite spontaneously deep in the moss and burn cold and blue without flame, powering something. The tarns pulse. At the base of each moss-dome, sphagnum bladders inflate and deflate, bellows the size of rooms, pumping water and air through reed-pipe networks with a clanking, sucking rhythm. Electrified - filaments of St. Elmo's fire crawl constantly over the tips of the horsetails, crackling, making the dim air smell of ozone. When the wind moves through the horsetail groves, the stalks strike each other and ring like struck iron. The whole plateau thrums like a mill floor. Without a builder, without a blueprint, the ecosystem has scaled up to production: it mills water and peat and light into heat and steam and dense black spore-clouds that hang like soot.

And it is loud. 1.6/3. Not just the wind. The bellows huff, the water gurgles through a thousand tiny sluices, the peat presses creak, the living iron groves chime, and over it all the fauna drowns everything out.

Because it is abundant. 2.4/3. Despite the sparse spacing of the flora, the air is thick with animals. Clouds of white moths the size of your hand, so many they make snow in the dim light, rising in a vortex whenever a moss-bellow exhales. Cranes - or things like cranes but with too many joints - stalk the shallows on stilt legs, three meters tall, sparse as the trees, calling with a metallic bellow that carries for kilometers. In the pools, things thrash and boil. Great black newts with gills like ferns haul themselves from tarn to tarn, leaving shining furrows. Beetles the size of fists with backs like riveted boiler-plate crawl up the clubmoss trunks and are *part* of the industry, chewing bore-holes that vent steam, carrying glowing coal-nodules in their mandibles from burn to burn. Everywhere, mites and springtails in such numbers they make the moss seem to shiver. No one species dominates. It is a census of giants and multitudes, all moving.

And none of it is stable. This is not the quiet stability of an ancient place. Erosion says it should be still, finished, settled into its final form. It isn't. This is a turbulent landscape, 2.8/3, hyperactive and de-creating itself as fast as it holds together.

You watch it happen. A moss-dome that was there when you looked left inhales too deeply and collapses inward with a wet roar, becoming a pool in seconds, black water fountaining. While you stare at the pool, across the valley a new tussock heaves upward, plant matter knitting visibly, growing three meters in a minute with a sound like tearing cloth and hissing rivets. The tarn beside you drains sideways, not downhill, water flowing uphill along the peat for a few meters before remembering gravity. The light flickers. This is the weirdness, 2.7/3, the breakdown.

Rules slip. Weight is inconsistent - a step you take feels heavy as lead on the moss, the next step floats, your boot hanging a fraction too long before it falls. Sound arrives before the action that caused it; you hear the beetle's clank a second before it moves. The horizon sometimes bends slightly upward at the edges, as if the plateau is the inside of a bowl. The mist does not move with the wind but holds in stationary columns, and through a column you might see the same crane duplicated, standing in two places at once, calling in canon with itself. The big clubmosses occasionally flicker, like a bad transmission, and for a frame you see through them to another version of themselves - younger, sharper, or dead and skeletal. Time pools like the water does. Near a burning peat seam, everything moves fast, frantic; ten meters away, a moth hangs motionless in midair, wings beating so slowly you can see each stroke.

To stand here is to stand in a cold, high, dim, wet factory that no one built and no one tends, that is also a bog, that is also a ruin, that is also being born. The ancient, flattened land tries to be still, and the living industrial fever that possesses it will not let it. The steam catches the gray light. The cranes call over the clanking of the breathing moss. Water condenses on your eyelashes and freezes. A new hill of living felt pushes up behind you while you listen, and you understand the plateau's only true rule: it keeps making itself wrong.
