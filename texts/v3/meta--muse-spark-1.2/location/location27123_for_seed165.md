On the roof of the world, but not its peak.

You are not at the summit. There is no summit. There is only this height - an extreme, breath-thin shelf of the world where the air has forgotten how to be thick. 4,000 meters, 5,000 - the numbers stop meaning anything because your lungs and your knees have already told you everything. The elevation is not a mountain you climb. It is a continent that rose and stayed.

It is a plateau. Not flat. Nothing here is flat.

The scale is open enough to lose a town in. If you stood on the central spine and turned slowly, you would see the same thing for a long time before it changed: a weathered, wind-combed basin maybe fifteen kilometers across, rimmed on all sides by higher, darker teeth of stone that do not quite connect. A valley without a river. A bowl with its bottom cracked. The sky is too close, and the distance is wrong. You think you could walk to the far wall before midday. You are wrong. You would walk until the dim light failed and still not reach it, and you would not feel like you had moved at all.

The light is dim. Not night, not dusk - a perpetual high-altitude overcast that never breaks and never really forms. The sun is somewhere behind it, a paler smear that never moves. There are no shadows with edges. Everything is lit from everywhere and nowhere, like light filtered through dirty ice. The stone is the color of cold ash and old bone, and it drinks what little light there is. At this height the sky should be brutal blue and burning. Instead it is a low, pressed-down pearl, too near to the ground. The stars, if you wait, do not come out. Sometimes one will prick through - a single, steady, too-bright point directly overhead that was not there before and will not twinkle. It watches.

It is cold. Not the sharp, biting cold of a storm. A deep, equilibrium cold. The kind that has settled into the rock itself. Minus ten, minus fifteen - cold enough that exposed skin goes waxy and your breath falls instead of rises, spilling out in a white pour that sinks and pools around your boots before it dissipates. And yet there is no frostbite windchill spike, no blizzard. The cold is stable. It has been this exact temperature for a very long time. Snow does not fall here often, and when it does it does not accumulate. It does not melt either. It just... arrives and then is not there.

The terrain is established. Ancient. Nothing here is raw or newly broken. The basalt and granite have been here long enough to learn how to be this shape. Wind has rounded every edge it could reach, and left the rest as knife-fins. You see yardangs - long, whale-backed ridges of stone all aligned to a wind that no longer changes direction. Between them are blowouts and pans, smooth hollows scoured clean down to darker rock. Boulders sit in shallow sockets they have worn for themselves over millennia, perfectly fitted like eggs in cups. There is no rockfall, no scree slope still sorting itself out. Everything has found its balance and stayed there. Even the cracks in the stone look deliberate, like seams.

There is water, but you have to look for where it is allowed to be. This is damp, not wet. The air itself carries it - every surface sweats with condensation that beads and vanishes and beads again without ever becoming a drip. In the lee of the larger ridges, where the permanent shadow and the permanent cold meet, you find the pockets. Small tarns the size of a room, black and perfectly still, rimmed with a collar of needle ice that never grows and never thins. They are not fed by streams. They are fed by the air. They exhale a faint, cold breath. In some of the deeper fissures, a tongue of old, dirty glacier still clings - not white but grey-blue, ribbed with dust, so still you could mistake it for rock until you see your dim reflection trapped a few inches under its surface, delayed by half a second. Sometimes a drop will form on an overhang, swell, fall - and you will hear it hit, but not see a ripple. The water is intermittent the way a prayer is intermittent.

Nothing grows much. Vegetation is a rumor. On the north side of stones, a crust of black lichen spreads like dried paint. In the most sheltered hollows, a cushion of moss - tight, hard, almost stone itself - holds a little damp and greens only if you crouch and look close. Once every few hundred meters, something dares to be a plant: a dwarf willow no higher than your ankle, its trunk twisted sideways by the same wind that shaped the ridges, its few leaves like boiled leather, rattling even when the air seems still. It is not dead. It is not growing. It has been that exact size for as long as the stone beside it has been that exact shape.

And it is empty. Profoundly, structurally empty. The distance between one thing and the next is the main feature. Between that willow and the next lichen patch is a hundred meters of bare, humming stone. Between one tarn and the next is a silence you could fit a house in. You can see for kilometers and count maybe five things that are not rock. The emptiness is not loneliness. It is a physical property, like pressure.

What was built here is scattered and has given up on being buildings. There are no villages, no roads. Every half kilometer or so, you find a mark: a cairn stacked with flat stones that are too regular to be natural, but now lean as one stone, fused by rime. A low wall, knee-high, enclosing nothing, running for ten meters and then stopping as if the builder forgot what it was for. A single shelter - three walls of unmortared slate with a lintel stone too heavy for any man to lift, open to the wind on the fourth side, its interior floor worn smooth as if many bodies sat there, though there is no ash, no tool mark, no carving. No metal. No wood that hasn't turned to stone. No sign of technology at all, not even a rusted nail. Whoever was here built with what would not rot or be taken, and then they left or became part of the mineral balance. The structures do not decay anymore. They have weathered into equilibrium with the wind.

There is almost no animal life, and what there is does not belong correctly. You will not see it unless you stop looking. A white hare, far too large, moving across a distant ridge with strides that cover too much ground, and making no mark in the dust. It will be gone when you blink, and you will not be sure which direction it went. High up, a lammergeier circles - or something shaped like one - but it circles without ever banking, turning in a perfect, level loop that does not drift. Down in the cold pockets, under the lip of ice, there are things that might be insects: glassy, leggy specks that tremble without moving, that exist only in your peripheral vision.

And the sound. The sound is loud.

It should be silent here. In an empty, frozen, dim place like this, you expect the great high silence. Instead the plateau roars. Not with weather - there is no storm. It roars because the land itself is an instrument that the constant wind never stops playing.

The wind at this elevation does not gust. It does not vary. It has blown from the northwest at the same speed since before the cairns were stacked, a flat, heavy river of air that you lean into without thinking. And the stone is fluted to receive it. The yardangs are organ pipes, the fissures are flutes, the little three-walled shelter hums like a throat. The sound is a low, chorded moan that lives in your chest more than your ears, layered with a higher, almost vocal overtone that seems to come from just behind you, no matter which way you turn. In some places the wind catches an edge just so and produces a clear, bell-like tone that holds without wavering for hours. You can hear a drop of water fall five hundred meters away as a distinct *tok*, and at the same time the whole basin is shaking with that endless drone. It is loud enough that you have to raise your voice to think.

And yet - nothing changes.

That is the final rule here, the weirdness that holds all the contradictions together. This place is in perfect, frozen equilibrium. The wind blows but never erodes another grain. The water beads but never wets. The cold never deepens and never lifts. The light never brightens and never dims. The sound is loud but never crescendos, never fades. Time passes for you - your heart beats, your breath plumes - but not for the plateau. It has reached a state.

That is where reality comes apart.

Distance misbehaves. A cairn that looked twenty steps away takes a hundred to reach, and when you arrive you realize you passed another just like it without seeing it. Echoes arrive before the sound that made them; you will hear your own footstep return to you a half-second before your boot touches stone. Gravity is slightly wrong - stones seem a fraction too light when you nudge them, and your own weight shifts if you stand still too long, as if the ground is very slowly forgetting to pull.

The horizon sometimes folds. If you stare at the far teeth of the rim, you may see the space between two peaks stretch like pulled taffy, or see the same distant shelter duplicated twice, both equally real. The pale star overhead, when you look directly at it, is not overhead at all but reflected in the black tarns at your feet, and the tarns are reflecting a sky that isn't there. The wind's drone, if you listen long enough, resolves into something that is not wind at all but a held breath.

You understand, standing there with your hands going numb in that dim, loud, unchanging cold, that this is not a place that was *formed*. It is a place that *remains*. It found its shape, its temperature, its sound, its emptiness, and then it stopped. Not died. Stopped. The world around it still turns, but this open, high, damp, beleaguered bowl opted out. It holds its note indefinitely.

And you, warm, transient, and noisy, are the only thing in it that still knows how to change - which is why the plateau can hear you coming for a very long time before you arrive.
