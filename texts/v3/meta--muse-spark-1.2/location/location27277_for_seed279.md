It is low country, but not flat.

At first from a distance you think it's a salt plain or a frozen lake, because the light doesn't catch right. A dim, even light under a low ceiling of cloud that never quite becomes fog and never quite lifts. No shadows. The sky is a wet pearl, bright enough to see by for miles but with no sun in it, and the land beneath it holds that same color.

Then you get closer and the plain resolves.

It is not flat at all. It is dense.

The whole valley — and it is a valley, though you wouldn't know it until you've walked an hour in any direction and felt the very gentle rise on all sides, a shallow bowl kilometers across — is filled with teeth.

They are mineral, not plant, not built. A forest of young rock that has not yet learned to be rounded. Thin blades and fluted spires and broken chimneys of dark glass and matte basalt, most of them between knee height and twice your height, standing so close together you have to turn sideways to pass between them. Not a field of boulders — a thicket. A city-scale thicket of stone. Some are needle-thin and translucent at the edges, volcanic glass still sharp enough to cut. Others are thicker, columnar, with vertical striations where they cooled too fast. All of them are fresh. The edges have not softened. Where one has fallen — and many have, tipped into its neighbors like dominoes that never finished falling — the break is clean and glittering, sugary black.

This is young country. Recently made. You can feel that it wasn't carved. It was extruded, or exhaled, or pushed up from below while still semi-liquid and then flash-cooled. There is no soil between the blades. Only more rock, a low, uneven pavement of the same material, jointed and cracked in polygons, still holding the memory of shrinking as it cooled.

And it weeps.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. There are no rivers here, no lakes. The air itself is wet, cool on the skin, cool in the lungs — a late-autumn cool that makes your hands ache if you keep them still. Everything condenses. The stone sweats. Each blade, each spire, each horizontal slab is filmed and beaded with water that never quite dries. It gathers at the tips and falls. It runs down the fluting in quick, bright threads. It collects in the joints of the pavement in sheets a few millimeters deep that reflect the dim sky perfectly, so you are walking on a broken mirror. When you step, water jumps from one shallow pan to the next with a soft slap.

There are places where the water does not behave. It clings as a skin over vertical faces for a long time before gravity takes it, thick as oil. It forms hanging beads at the undersides of overhangs that swell to the size of your fist and stay there, shivering but not falling. The sound of all that water is the loudest thing here — and it is loud.

You expected silence in a place with no plants and almost no animals. Instead the valley roars, quietly. Not a crash, a constant presence. Thousands of drips per second, out of sync, ticking and tapping on stone and shallow pools. Water trickling down a hundred thousand flutes. And the wind — even a weak wind — moving through the dense thicket becomes complicated. It is split and split again, whistling through narrow gaps, humming in the hollow chimneys, moaning low in the larger tubes. The rock itself seems to make sound when the temperature shifts, a faint tick and ping as the glass contracts in the cool. You have to raise your voice to be heard by someone two meters away. And yet nothing is moving fast. The drips fall. The water sheet creeps a centimeter a minute across the pavement. The shadows do not move. The whole process is patient to the point of stillness. You could sit for an hour and the only change would be that a bead that was on the verge of falling has finally fallen.

There is no vegetation. Not a lichen, not a moss, not a blade of grass hiding between stones. At first you keep looking for it, because wet and cool should mean green, but there is none. The only softness is a kind of dark film in places, a bacterial slick in the most sheltered pans, rust-red or dull olive, that could be mistaken for algae until you touch it and it doesn't smear like life, it flakes like iron. Maybe it is life, but not as you recognize it. The rock is otherwise bare, and that bareness makes the wetness stranger. A marsh with no reeds. A rainforest of glass.

Because the rock is so dense and the light so flat, distance becomes hard to judge. A spire thirty meters away looks the same as one three meters away. You lose scale. The repeating, near-touching forms create a maze with no walls. Every direction looks the same until you mark it.

Which others have done, sparsely.

The built things here are not technology. There is nothing to find that hums or blinks or needs power. What you find, every few hundred meters if you walk long enough, are small, stubborn human marks. A low wall, two courses high, made of the same blades snapped off and stacked, now also weeping, enclosing nothing. A cairn, carefully balanced, its stones already filmed over. A single-room shelter with no roof, its doorway aligned to nothing you can see. A line of stones set on end like teeth set in the pavement, running for twenty meters and then stopping. They are not ruins of a city. They are attempts, or offerings, or waymarkers left by people who also had to navigate the sameness and the sound. They are old but not eroded — the sharpness of the rock has preserved even their chisel marks. No footprints remain between them; water erases everything soft within hours.

Nothing lives here in any abundance. Rarely, you hear something that is not water or wind. A pale amphibian, almost translucent, the length of your hand, that lives in the pavement pans and darts when your shadow falls over it. Insect-like things with too many legs that pick their way up the damp flutes and freeze when you breathe on them. Far off, sometimes, a call that is not quite a bird — a single, flat note that echoes and multiplies in the stone thicket until you can't tell where it came from, and then silence again for a long time.

The rules of this place become clear only by staying:

Water does not gather, it disperses. It wants to be a film, not a flow.

Stone does not pile, it bristles. Space is vertical and narrow, not open and wide, even though the valley is kilometers across.

Light does not change. Morning and afternoon are the same dim pearl. Time is not told by shadows but by how cold your feet have become standing in the sheet-water.

Sound is not tethered to event. It is the air of the place itself, loud and slow, like being inside a vast, dripping instrument that is being played very, very gradually.

And you are only ever in the middle of it. No matter how far you walk, the dense, wet, sharp, humming thicket continues, low and wide and cool, extending past any ridge you thought would be its edge, showing you another identical bowl beyond.
