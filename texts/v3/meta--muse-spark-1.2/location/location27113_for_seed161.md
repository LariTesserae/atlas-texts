It is not a mountain anymore.

What elevation does at this extreme is stop being a height and become a place entire to itself. You climb for a long time to get here — or more accurately, you would have to, if there were still a way up. But there isn't, not really. The sides are a sheer cut of old stone going down further than your eyes can resolve in the haze, and the top is flat.

Not flat like a table. Flat like something that has been left alone to be weathered until it gave up on having features. This is ancient erosion, the final form. Three billion years of wind with no rain to help it, grinding everything down to a patience. The rock up here is not jagged. It is swollen and soft-looking, pillowed basalt and sandstone worn until the edges are round as knuckles. The soil — if you can call it soil — is powder. A deep, pinkish-grey mineral flour, meters thick, so fine it hangs in the air if you scuff it. It tastes of salt and iron. In places it has cemented into a crust you can walk on; in others it breaks under your weight and your foot sinks to the ankle with a sigh.

There is no water here. Not in any way you expect water to be. No spring, no stream, no pond, no seep. The air itself is dry enough that your lips crack within an hour. And yet it is warm — a steady, late-afternoon warmth that never quite becomes heat, never quite cools. The warmth is trapped. At this height the atmosphere should be thin and cold, but the basin of the plateau holds a lens of dusty air, and the dim light filters down through it and stays.

The light is dim. Not dark, not night, but a perpetual overcast without clouds. High above, a veil of fine aeolian dust hangs permanently over the summit, left over from the world below. The sun is a pale coin behind it, bright enough to cast a shadow, never bright enough to make you squint. Everything is seen as if through amber. Colors are muted except up close — the rust of the stone, the green that covers everything else.

Because for all its dryness, it is lush. That is the first contradiction you have to let go of.

Nothing here drinks water. Or nothing uses water the way lowland things do. The vegetation is dense to the point of being urban in its own way — a low, tangled jungle that has decided to live on air. The dominant forms are not trees but colonies: broad, leathery rosettes the size of dinner plates that grow flat against the rock and against walls and against each other, overlapping like shingles. Their leaves are thick, waxy, grey-green, and fringed with white salt hairs that pull moisture straight out of the night air. From them hang curtains of a hair-like black lichen that never touches the ground, swaying even when there is no wind, collecting dew that never falls. Vine-things with woody, corky stems as thick as your wrist run everywhere, rootless, anchoring themselves with clasping tendrils to stone and to timber and to each other. They flower rarely, and when they do it's a waxy, blood-red trumpet that opens for a single dim day and then withers to a salty pod.

You cannot see more than a few feet into it without pushing it aside. The green has taken the ground and the walls and the roofs. It does not grow *around* the town. It grows *through* it, and the town has learned to grow with it.

The town is small. You could walk from one cliff edge to the other in ten minutes, if there were a straight road, which there isn't. It is hamlet-sized, bounded absolutely. On all sides the ancient stone simply ends, cut off in a wind-rounded lip, and then there is nothing but air and haze and a fall that would take a stone a long time to finish. That boundary is the most important architecture here. Every building turns its back to it, or faces it with low walls of stacked, unmortared stone. No one lives at the very edge except the vines.

Inside that boundary, it is dense. Not crowded in a frantic way, but saturated — a medieval density of hand-built things layered over centuries. Houses of soft-cut plateau stone and sun-baked brick, of woven lichen-fiber and cork-wood frames, pressed shoulder to shoulder along lanes so narrow you have to turn sideways. Second stories overhang the first, propped with rough posts; roofs are flat and connected by plank bridges and by the living mat of rosettes that have been encouraged to grow there for insulation. Courtyards are not open spaces but rooms without ceilings, shared by three or four families, full of hanging drying racks, clay jars, salt-pans, wind-chimes made of shell-like mineral flakes. There is infrastructure, but it is all manual and legible: cisterns that never hold water but hold the night's catch of brine from dew-nets — great black webs of woven hair strung between rooftops that glitter with captured moisture at dawn and are wrung out by hand. Gutters carved directly into the rock to guide dust away from doorways. Communal ovens. Nothing is metal except a few precious copper tools, hammered thin and patched endlessly, and the iron nails that are treated like heirlooms.

Primitive does not mean crude. The stonework is exquisite because it has had a thousand years to be refined without ever needing to change. The walls are fitted without mortar so precisely you cannot slide a blade between them, because the stone is soft enough to rub smooth against itself. The textiles are dense and beautiful. Everything is made to last, to be repaired, to be grown over.

And it is loud.

You expect silence at a height like this, with slowness. But the slowness here is not quiet. The dynamic of this place is patient to the point of stillness — stone erodes by microns per century, the vines put out a single new leaf a month, a house might be rebuilt once every five generations — and inside that stillness, life is ferociously, constantly noisy.

Fauna is everywhere, abundant beyond the ability of the dryness to explain it, all small and close and layered into the green. The air is full of them. Clouds of tiny, pale moths that never land, that live their whole lives on the wind above the rosettes. Beetles the size of your thumb with polished, dusty-pink elytra that thrum as they blunder from flower to flower. Lizards, dozens of varieties, flat-bodied and salt-crusted, skittering over walls and under leaves. And the birds — not true birds, but something convergent: leathery, bat-like gliders with four wings that chirrup and click incessantly, nesting in the eaves and in the vine tangles, arguing, feeding, launching themselves into the dim air to snatch moths. At night — though night here is only a deeper dimming — the insects take over entirely. A low, resonant, almost electrical buzz rises from every surface, from the mat of rosettes itself, so loud you have to raise your voice to be heard across a courtyard. It is not a chaotic noise. It is layered and rhythmic, the sound of a million small bodies doing the same slow work at the same time.

The rules here are simple and absolute because the plateau permits nothing else:

1. Nothing leaves. The water you have is the water you catch. The food you have is what grows on rock and air. Waste is not waste; it is dried, powdered, and returned to the vines.
2. Do not cut the mat. You harvest leaves by taking the oldest outer ones, you prune vines only where they threaten a wall. A house that kills its roof garden will bake in the trapped warmth and die.
3. Stay back from the edge. Not because you will fall — everyone knows the wind there — but because the salt-hairs at the lip are the oldest growth, the seed-bank for the whole plateau, and they are not to be disturbed.

People move slowly here. Not from laziness, but because the air is thin and warm and every action has to be deliberate if it is not to kick up choking dust or tear a tendril. They speak loudly over the insect drone, and laugh loudly, and work with a slow, continuous industry — scraping salt, wringing nets, rubbing stone on stone.

If you stand on a rooftop at midday, when the dim light is at its strongest and the warmth makes the dust-lens shimmer, you can see the entire world at once: the dense, green-furred cluster of the town in its shallow bowl of ancient rock, the black webs trembling between roofs, the pale motes of gliders above, the absolute, soft, eroded flatness extending to a rounded rim on every side, and beyond that, nothing. No other high places. No below. Just haze.

It feels less like a place you have come to and more like a place that has been waiting, settled into its final shape, content to repeat its slow, loud, lush, dry life indefinitely without needing anything else.
