It is night here, but not the night of a sky. The night of a place that makes its own darkness.

The air is low and warm and it does not move so much as press. You are down in it, not high — a broad basin maybe a day's walk across, sunk between shoulders of new rock, low enough that the air has weight. It pools here. It holds heat from the day and from the ground itself, a damp, mammalian warmth that sits on your skin and in your lungs. Breathing is drinking. Every inhalation tastes of leaf-mold and warm stone and the faint iron of fresh earth.

This is young land. You can feel it under your boots, if you had boots. The valley floor is not soft. It hasn't had time to be soft. The last upheaval — flow or thrust or vomit, something quick and geological — left it ridged and blistered and sharp. Black rock still angular where it cooled, not yet rounded by any river, broken into plates and spires and knives that jut through the soil like bones that haven't settled. Between them the soil is thin, dark, gritty with cinders, but furiously fertile. Wherever a lip of basalt casts a shadow, wherever a fissure exhales cooler air, life has seized it. There has been no time for erosion to make valleys within the valley, so water hasn't learned its path yet. It wanders.

And there isn't much water, not in the way you expect a jungle to have it. No lake, no broad river. This place is damp the way a lung is damp. In the heat of the afternoon, water lifts off every leaf and every breathing thing and hangs in the air. At night it comes back. It beads on every surface, fat droplets gathering on the undersides of leaves as big as shields, on the teeth of the rocks, on your arms, on the chitinous backs of the things moving around you. It gathers until it falls. It patters irregularly, a second rain with no clouds. In the lowest creases between the rock plates there are pockets — a thread of a stream that appears for twenty feet and then vanishes under a mat of roots, a hollow of black glass holding a foot of brown water that will be gone by midday, a seep that weeps constantly down one cliff face and feeds nothing but moss. You are never far from water, you are never in it. Your feet are wet without ever stepping in a puddle.

Then the green. In this darkness it should not be here, not like this. But it is overwhelming. It is not the green of meadow or temperate forest, it is a stacked, layered, jealous green that has learned to eat darkness. The canopy — if you can call it a canopy when there is no sun to catch — is fifty feet up, a near-solid ceiling of huge, waxy leaves that fit together like shingles, black-green and dripping condensation. Below that, another layer: tree ferns with trunks like corded muscle, their fronds unfurling as wide as a person is tall. Below that, shrubs with leaves that are soft and almost translucent, pulsing faintly with their own light. Below that, fungi the size of barrels, and mosses that hang in beards three feet long, and vines as thick as your thigh that have not decided if they are vines or snakes. Everything touches everything else. There is no bare ground, there is only a vertical thatch of living matter. If you stand still for a minute, a vine will find your shoulder. The rock itself is furry with growth. The air between plants is not empty; it is filled with hanging gardens, with seed fluff, with curtains of rootlets reaching down for the next rain of condensation. It smells like crushed green pepper and rot and fresh sap all at once.

You can't see far for the darkness and the density, but you can feel how far it goes. This basin repeats itself ridge after ridge of new rock and new green, each hollow a variation — one more fern-choked, one holding a steaming seep, one with a stand of those pale light-bearing trees. It is a regional thing, a country of this. You could walk for days and still be in variations of this same warm, dark, dense womb. The scale is too big to hold in one glance and too choked to see across anyway. You navigate by sound, and the sound is impossible.

It is deafening. Not in the industrial sense. In the biological sense. At first you think it is one noise and then your ear breaks it apart and realizes it is thousands of noises refusing to be background. A ceaseless, sawing roar of insects — not chirps but a continuous metallic drilling that vibrates in your molars. Under it, above it, through it: the popping chorus of amphibians, throat sacs inflating like pale balloons in the gloom, each pop distinct and then lost in the mass; the rustle and scuttle and click of an uncountable number of legs on leaf litter; the sudden shriek of something being taken; the flutter that is almost a wind; the low, wet croak that seems to come from the stone itself. You have to raise your voice to hear yourself think, and still you are only shouting into the choir. The sound has a physical pressure. It pushes.

Because nothing here is sparse. The fauna is not a population, it is a medium. Lift any leaf and there are ten things under it, and ten things on those things. Beetles the size of your fist with luminous spots on their backs that crawl in slow processions up the trunks. Clouds of moths so thick they look like smoke until they settle and become bark again. Things like shrimp but living in the damp leaf-mat, flicking transparent. Small, hot-blooded gliders that throw themselves from fern to fern, chittering. And eyes — everywhere, at every level, reflecting the little light there is. This is why it is turbulent. It is not a stable jungle. It is a hyperactive market at midnight, an anthill kicked over and scaled up to a valley. Nothing stays still long enough to claim territory. A fungal tower fruits and is devoured within an hour, its spores carried off on a dozen glittering backs. A vine grows six inches overnight and is stripped to its fibers by morning. Paths appear as thousands of feet beat them down through the moss and vanish again when a different appetite pulls the traffic elsewhere. Generation times are short. The air is thick with nuptial flights, with deaths, with frantic building and unbuilding. If you watch one square yard for ten minutes you will see a succession: colonization, frenzy, exhaustion, replacement.

The light, what little there is, is not from above. The leaf-shingle canopy is too dense for moon or stars, and a low haze of warm vapor holds even what might filter through. The darkness is complete enough that your eyes have properly adjusted and still found almost nothing — until you stopped looking for sky-light. The forest makes its own. Veins in the understory leaves carry a slow, amber bioluminescence that brightens when brushed. The fungi pulse, dimly, green-white, on their own slow breath. The beetles carry lanterns. Strands of slime mold on the rocks trace cold blue maps that fade and re-draw themselves as the organisms flow. It is never bright. It is enough to see movement, to see the sheen of wetness, to see that the leaf in front of your face is not a leaf but a mantis perfectly mimicking one until it decides you are too close and unfolds, impossibly, into something far larger than the leaf could have been.

No one built here. There is no cut stump, no stacked stone, no straight line that nature did not straighten by accident. Yet there is craft, everywhere, but not yours. Watch a colony of soot-black ants — each as long as your thumb — cut perfect discs from the waxy leaves and carry them like shields, then chew them into a pulp and daub them into papery baffles that redirect the condensation drip into their nursery hollows in the rock. Watch a bird-like thing with four wings and hands at the bend of each wing snap twigs with intentional breaks, then wedge them to lever bark away and tongue out grubs. Watch the gliders strip thorns and use them as awls to pierce fruit. No metal. No fire seen. Only held, used, modified objects — wood, thorn, stone flake, leaf — carried for a purpose and then discarded. Tool-use is ambient, democratized; half the forest seems to have discovered that a sharp edge is better than a dull one. If intelligence is here, it is not concentrated. It is spread thin and busy, a hundred species all halfway to clever.

The rule of this place, if it has one, is this: everything happens at once and close together. There is no empty. There is no quiet. There is no far away. Warmth plus wetness plus newness plus dark has made a pocket where life did not spread out, it piled up, it learned to live on itself, on its own light and its own moisture and its own constant death. It is young enough that it still remembers being rock, old enough that it has already tried every shape for a mouth.

If you stay until your eyes water from the sound, the condensation will keep falling on you, drop by drop, and the living ceiling above will shift and sigh, and something small and incredibly busy will try to use the edge of your sleeve as a tool.
