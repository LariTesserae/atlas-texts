It is low.

Not low like a valley between higher places. Low like the world decided to be low and stay there — a continent-wide basin so shallow you would not know you were in it except that the horizon never arrives. There is no slope to orient by, no ridge to climb to see further. The curvature, if there is one, is hidden under what grows.

Because what grows is everything.

At first you think it is a forest. Then you realize the word forest is wrong because a forest has ground and trees on it. Here there is no ground apart from plants. The substrate *is* plant.

The bedrock is young. It came up not long ago, geologically speaking — black, glassy basalt still sharp where it shows, unsoftened, its cooling cracks still crisp as broken plates. It hasn't had time to be worn down. But you almost never see it, because the living mass found it immediately. A felt, a mat, a thatch of roots and rhizomes and runners has covered it whole. Not soil with plants in it — plants that have become soil. Layer on layer, dense and near-touching, living on its own dead, which does not rot so much as compress into more structure. You walk not on earth but on a springy, interwoven raft of roots as thick as your wrist, as fine as hair, braided and re-braided until it can hold your weight, until it holds itself up over hollows and fissures in the rock beneath. Underfoot it gives slightly and returns. Everywhere is like this. For thousands of kilometers in every direction.

It is warm. Not hot, not tropical-scorching, but the steady, held warmth of a greenhouse at night, a compost warmth, a body warmth. The air is saturated and still. Water is everywhere but rarely as open water. That would be too simple, too decided. Instead the air itself weeps.

Condensation beads on every leaf, every aerial root, every hanging filament, gathers and runs without ever quite falling. The surfaces are always filmed, always slick. In the low places between root-hummocks, water stands not as ponds but as black lenses, shallow, skin-thin, reflecting nothing because there is almost no light to reflect. It seeps up from below, too — the water table is right at the surface, pressing up through the root-mat. Step and water sighs up around your foot and then is taken back into the fibers. The rocks, where they do outcrop, weep continuously, dark with perpetual damp, without any source of rain you can see.

There is no sky light.

Whether it is night or a sun that never penetrated, or a canopy so complete no photon gets through, is impossible to tell. The darkness is not absolute — if it were you could not see — but it is profound, a deep dusk that never changes. Your eyes adjust until you can make out forms by a light that has no source. The plants make a faint light of their own, not bioluminescence as display, but as byproduct. Along the undersides of the great leaves — leaves as broad as roofs, as small as scales, layered a hundred deep — there are lines and nodes that glow a dull, tired amber, like filament wire under low voltage. Veins of light run through stems like heated elements. At intervals, deeper in the mass, something pulses: a slow, amber-white throb, on for seconds, off for seconds, with no hurry.

This is the industry.

There are no buildings. Nothing was built. No cut, no joint, no brick, no artifact. And yet the whole continent is industrial.

You understand it as you stand still and let your eyes and ears recalibrate to the quiet. The plants are not wild in the way wild means unorganized. They are organized the way a refinery is organized. Sap moves not with the lazy drift of botany but with pressure, with purpose, in thick-walled tubes as wide as pipes, ticking and sighing. You can hear it if you put your hand on a trunk that is not a trunk but a column, a living smokestack, a vertical pressure vessel. Inside it, fluid hammers and releases. Valves — leaf-valves, sphincters of bark — open and close with a soft, hydraulic sigh: *chuff.  ...  chuff.*  Nutrient slurry is refined, fractioned, shunted. Somewhere above, or below, vast leaves unfurl not to catch sun — there is no sun — but to condense, to distill, to cool. They drip constant distilled water into troughs formed by other leaves. Roots extrude not just to seek but to mine, exuding acid to etch the fresh basalt, drawing up minerals in a chemical leach.

It is a factory that grew itself and requires no workers, no foremen, no exterior power. Electrified, after a fashion — you can smell the ozone after a pulse, see the faint arc sometimes jump between two filament-tips that have grown too close, a spark that is immediately quenched by the damp. The hum is electrical too, a low 50-cycle thrum that lives in the roots and makes the hairs on your arm rise. It does everything factories do — intake, processing, assembly, power generation, waste recirculation — but it does it as growth. Pistons are made of wood that tightens and releases. Conveyor belts are vines that creep in a loop, carrying packets of resin. Boilers are bulbous growths that warm the air from within.

And it never changes.

That is the strangest part, stranger than the dark factory-forest. In a young place you expect violence, succession, colonization, collapse. Here the youth of the rock is held in absolute equilibrium by the totality of the plant. Nothing grows larger. Nothing dies. Or rather, growth and death happen at precisely the same rate in the same place, so that net change is zero. A leaf unfurls to exactly replace the one that curled, in the same spot, to the same size, every time. The water that condenses runs down the same groove, at the same speed, forever. The pressure in the pipe-trunks builds to the same poundage and releases with the same *chuff* at the same interval. You could time your breath to it. You could leave and come back in a hundred years and the bead of water on that filament would be in the same place, catching the same dim amber.

The air does not move. The great leaves, though they make their own weather — exhaling warm vapor that becomes the perpetual dew, inhaling it again — never stir in wind, because there is no wind. Weather here is not a front that passes. It is a standing condition, a room climate the size of a continent. Warm, wet, still, dark.

There are no animals. No insect whine, no bird call, no rustle of anything passing through. That absence is not emptiness; it is density of another kind. The space that would be filled with fauna is filled instead with more plant, more function. Pollination, if it happens, is done by air pressure, by the mechanical brush of one filament against another on its timed swing. Dispersal is unnecessary — everything is already everywhere.

Sound, when there is any, is very quiet and very close. The drip of condensation from leaf tip to leaf trough, three meters below, a metronome too slow to be rhythm. The soft pop of a seed-packet, engineered like a pressure capsule, opening without force. The faint creak of root-fibers tightening. Your own footsteps muted immediately by the mat, swallowed.

If you stay long enough, the weirdness stops feeling weird. The rules reveal themselves:

1.  No part is separate. You cannot pull a vine without pulling the continent.
2.  Nothing is wasted. The amber light, the water, the mineral etched from basalt, the heat — all of it cycles without loss.
3.  Nothing is made. Everything is grown into its use.

Far in the dark, the industrial breathing continues. The vast, low, wet, warm, plant-made world holds itself exactly as it is, not from stasis but from perfect, living industry that has no need to progress.
