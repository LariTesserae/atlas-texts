It is high. Unreasonably high.

You come up to it not by climbing but by the world dropping away around it. The lowlands and their haze fall and fall until they are a blue and distant sea of cloud far below, and you are left on the top of the world where the air should be thin and cold and killing. It is not. It is hot.

The plateau is a mountain that forgot to be a mountain. A truncated continent of stone lifted whole into the sky, its sides sheer, black basalt cliffs three thousand feet straight down to the cloud deck, its top not a peak but a country. A mesa so large you cannot hold it in one look - valleys and ridges and basins all at an elevation where even breathing should hurt. And the air *is* thin, you feel it in your chest when you inhale, a slight starve behind the sternum, but the heat does not care. The sun here is normal, a white-yellow noon high and frank, not distorted or dimmed, but the heat it brings is trapped. It hangs. The stone radiates it back. The wet air holds it like a mouth holds breath.  It is a hothouse balanced on a pillar above the world.

Because it is wet. Impossibly wet for this height.

Water is everywhere and it does not know how to leave. Springs weep straight out of vertical rock faces halfway up cliffs with no aquifer to feed them. Stone sweats. In the mornings - if morning still means anything where nothing ever changes - the atmosphere condenses on itself without cooling, beads forming in mid-air and falling slowly as warm rain that never had a cloud. Every hollow holds water. Every joint in the rock holds water. The plateau's top is not flat; it has settled over eons into a soft, established shape - broad shallow bowls and rolling, weathered ridges, not jagged or young, but worn smooth and comfortable in its own weight. And each bowl is a marsh. A lake. A mirror of dark water choked with green.

The vegetation is not the spare alpine growth you would expect. It is jungle. Lowland jungle lifted into the sky and pressed down. It should not be able to breathe up here and yet it is lush to the point of suffocation. Everything touches everything else.

And the city is not distinct from it.

You understand after a while that there is no division between built and grown here. This was a settlement the size of a province - you can see its extent from any high ridge, valley after valley of it, open and regional and too large to be called a ruin - but no tool ever touched it. There is no quarried block, no fired brick, no pane of glass, no rust. No tech. No device. The city was cultivated.

The structures are still there, dense and layered and near-touching, but they are anatomy. Vast, hollow trunks of trees wider than houses that grew themselves into colonnades. Walls that are living root-mats, braided and lignified into tight, interlocking screens four stories high, still leafing at the top. Terraces cut not with picks but coaxed - roots splitting basalt along its bedding planes over centuries until the stone stepped itself into streets and platforms. Broad leaves the size of roofs, overlapping and hardened to a leathery vault over avenues. Figs that strangled figs that strangled stone. Vines as thick as pillars. The whole plateau-top is a single, continuous, urban organism, block after block of inhabited plant and trained stone, balconies of living wood, corridors of warm, damp bark, cisterns that are simply the cupped bases of enormous bromeliads holding hundreds of gallons of rain. It is dense in a way a forest is dense and in a way a city is dense, both at once. You cannot walk ten feet without brushing a wall that is also a trunk that is also a neighbor's ceiling.

And it is empty of animals. Utterly.

No birds to call. No insects to stitch the air. No hum, no chitter, no rustle of anything moving under its own will. The leaves are unchewed. The water holds no fish, no larvae. The marshes are perfectly still, covered in lily pads that have never been landed on. The absence is profound, because the place looks like it *should* be screaming with life - hot, wet, green, open water everywhere - and instead there is a biological silence so complete it feels like a held breath. Only plants. Only structure. Only stone and water and leaf.

What you hear instead of life is the place itself, and it is deafening.

Not loud in the way of a sudden crash. Loud in the way of a constant, total pressure. A roar that has no attack and no decay because it never changes. The water falling off the plateau's edges does not fall. Or rather, it falls and never finishes falling. From every lip of the mesa, the marshes drain in broad, flat sheets over the basalt, white water spreading thin as glass, and then they meet the air beyond the cliff and they stop.

They hang there.

A curtain of water, a kilometer wide on the western scarp alone, suspended vertically in the air a few feet out from the rock, roaring without moving. You can see the turbulence frozen in place, every crest and bubble and braided filament rendered perfectly still, yet the sound it makes is absolute - a low, body-shaking thunder that occupies every frequency at once, bone conduction and ear drum both. The hanging falls never splash below because there is no below; the cloud sea is too far down for the sound to reach. They just hang and scream, held by whatever broke reality here.

And it is not only water. The wind that should scour this high place is also frozen. You feel it as a hot, wet pressure on your skin, constant, unvarying, pushing from the south with the same force as when you arrived and as it will have an hour from now and a century from now. It moves through the city-plant's hollow avenues and through thousands of natural flutes - holes eroded through the root-walls and stone terraces over deep time, smooth and established, not new - and it makes a chord. A single, endless, deafening organ note that never wavers in pitch or volume. The air itself vibrates. The leaves, though they never flutter, shiver with it.

That is the weirdness. The breakdown. The reason the heat stays up here and the water stays in the air and the sound never stops.

Gravity has become forgetful. Water flows uphill as often as down, creeping up the undersides of leaves to pool on their tops. In some of the basins, water rests in a perfect, trembling sphere twenty feet across, hovering a foot above the marsh reeds, reflecting the normal noon light without ripple. Light itself pools strangely in corners, thicker in the shade beneath the root-houses, as if it has weight. Distance slips. You walk down a street lined with trunk-houses that should be half a mile long and arrive back at the intersection you started from without having turned. Doorways look deeper inside than the building is wide. The city is bigger inside than the plateau that holds it, and you feel it whenever you try to map it in your head - a pressure, like trying to remember a word that has no shape.

And nothing changes. That is the most uncanny and the most deafening part of all.

This lush, hot, drowned city on the roof of the world is not growing, not decaying, not eroding, not drying. The leaves are green but not photosynthesizing in any way you can measure; they simply *are* green. The water weeps from rock but the rock is not depleted. The hanging falls roar but lose no volume. The light is steady, normal daylight, without a flicker or a cloud-shadow passing, as if the sun is fixed. Dust motes hang motionless in the shafts between leaves, never settling. Steam rises off the marshes a few inches and stops. Your sweat beads and stays. You cannot tell if you have been here for minutes or hours because your own body feels caught in the same equilibrium - heart beating, but the sound of it is swallowed instantly by the greater roar, and it, too, seems to beat without progressing.

The rules here are simple and absolute: what is, remains. Established forms hold. The balance achieved over uncounted weathered years has locked. The plateau has become a closed system, a terrarium of impossible city-jungle sealed not by glass but by stasis. Water without cycle. Heat without loss. Sound without source running down. Vegetation without hunger. Architecture without builder, still inhabited by the sheer act of having been inhabited.

You stand on a terrace of living teak and weeping basalt, looking out across the open scale of it - valley after valley of green-roofed, root-walled avenues receding into warm haze, marsh-light glinting between them, the whole vast, dense, silent, roaring nowhere extending past sight - and you understand that it will look exactly like this tomorrow. Exactly like this when the clouds below have boiled away. Exactly like this when you are gone, if you can ever find a way down off a mountain that no longer believes in falling.
