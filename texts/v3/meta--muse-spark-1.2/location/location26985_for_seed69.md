There is no sky here, or rather the sky has lowered itself until it became ground.

You are high. Impossibly high, high enough that you should be cold and breathless, high enough that the air should be thin and scoured. But the air is warm and thick as bathwater, and it presses on your skin, wet. When you inhale, the air has weight and taste — iron, leaf-rot, hot stone, oil.

You can only tell you are high at all by the pressure in your ears and by the way sound carries. Sound carries forever.

At first there is only darkness. Not the darkness of night, which still has stars, still has memory of light. This is a complete darkness, a material darkness, fed from below and above. The cloud deck never broke. It formed here when the world was younger and never left — a continent-sized sea of cloud that sits on the back of a continent-sized mountain. The plateau rose into it and kept rising. Now the plateau wears the cloud like a second atmosphere. Light does not penetrate it. What light there is here is made here.

And then, as your eyes adjust, you realize it is not dark at all. It is saturated with other kinds of seeing.

The land is vast — so vast it makes its own weather, which makes its own land. You are standing on the High Marsh, an elevated tableland that goes on until distance becomes a kind of haze even without light. It is not a mountain peak, it is a country lifted into the air. The rock beneath you is old. Not jagged-new, not alpine-sharp. It is settled, eroded into its final, intended shape. Broad basalt shoulders, softened, rounded, stepped in great terraces that have been weathered into bowls and basins. Each basin holds water. Each terrace weeps.

Water is everywhere but rarely as a river. The water here does not know how to be a river yet, or has forgotten. It beads on every surface. It hangs in the air as a warm, condensing mist that never quite becomes rain and never quite stops. It seeps from the stone itself — the basalt is porous, a dark sponge, and if you put your hand to it you feel it sweating. Warm water, body-temperature, welling up between the mineral grains. Every hollow, every footfall, fills with a black mirror that reflects nothing until something lights it.

And everything is covered. Lush is not the right word because lush suggests sunlight. This is a lushness that has learned to live without it. A dense, layered mat of growth, near-touching, on top of itself, through itself. Black ferns the size of houses unfurling with fronds as thick as your forearm, their undersides lined with cold bioluminescent freckles. Peat and moss meters deep, so deep you are not walking on rock at all but on a suspended soil that bounces. Fungi like low cities, shelf upon shelf, laminar, dripping. Hanging gardens that do not root in ground but in air — tangles of aerial roots and black vines that drink directly from the condensing atmosphere, that hang from nothing and descend for tens of meters, swaying, dripping constantly. The vegetation sweats water, catches water, makes water. When the cloud brushes the canopy, whole curtains of droplets form on the leaf-tips and fall at once with a hiss.

The warmth keeps it all moving. Things rot as fast as they grow. You can smell it and hear it — the growth is audible. The ferns creak as they unfurl, the mosses sigh as they swell, seed pods pop continuously in the dark like damp firecrackers. And beneath that, over that, through that — the fauna.

You hear them before you see their lights.

The marsh is loud. Unreasonably loud for a place with no birdsong as you know it. There are no birds here, or not many. Instead the air thrums. A low, industrial thrum you at first mistake for machinery, until you realize it is wings — millions of broad, silent-looking moths the size of your hands beating air that is too wet to fly in, and yet they do, and the aggregate of their wings is a chord, a drone. Beneath that, clicking, croaking, bellowing from the pools. Amphibians with translucent backs lit from within by cultivations of bacteria, each pool a crowded bowl of light and eyes. Things slither through the peat that are not snakes, that leave glowing trails that fade seconds later. Things call from the hanging roots above with voices like wet wood being struck. Everything is near everything else. The density is claustrophobic even in vastness. Every cubic meter of air, water, and leaf is occupied, claimed, contested. Predators you never see brush past your legs, warm and slick, hunting by heat and vibration.

And weirdness has settled into the physics here. Water does not always fall. In the thickest parts of the mist, droplets gather and hover, suspended by the warm updrafts from the rock and by static, forming hovering lakes the size of rooms, wobbling, drifting slowly across the terrace until they brush a fern and collapse in a sudden, cold downpour. Some of the hanging forests grow upside-down, rooting in the cloud itself, their leaves pointed downward into the dark, never touching ground. Gravity feels negotiable here, or tired. Compass needles spin slowly regardless of direction. If you stand still long enough, the moss beneath you will begin to grow over your boots, tasting you.

Through all of this — through the wet, the dense, the loud, the dark — runs the industry.

You thought at first the scattered lights were fauna. Some are. But others are too regular, too amber.

This place was found because it could be used. The built things are not cities. There are no cities on the High Marsh. That would sink. Instead there are scattered artifacts, faint marks that become rarer the deeper you go: a concrete piling swallowed to the chest by moss, a rusted catwalk that now serves as a trellis for luminous vines, a lone maintenance shrine the size of a house where someone once left a thermos and never came back, now a roost for glass-winged things. Rare, isolated, half-erased.

But the industrial is everywhere else. Not in buildings, but in process. The terraces are farmed by machines that are not housed anywhere. They move, slowly, on legs or on wide sighing crawlers that you hear long before you see their running lights. Condenser towers — black iron, three stories tall, humming, crowned with spinning fans that pull the warm cloud through cold baffles and wring it. They weep engineered water in sheets that feed the basins. Pump stations that breathe with great piston sighs, moving warm mineral water from the rock up into settling tiers. Harvesters like combine-barges that graze the fungal shelves, stripping them, leaving them to regrow by the next hour. High wires strung between towers, sparking and spitting in the wet, carrying power to somewhere you cannot see. All of it automated, or tended by people you never see, only hearing their engines, seeing their lights pass like slow constellations through the frond-layer.

The machines do not fight the marsh. They mimic it. They sweat, they drip, they rumble, they are loud in the same register as the insects. They have become part of the ecology. The moths swarm their heat. The moss grows on their backs. The amphibians breed in the warm overflow pools beneath their legs.

The rules of the world here are simple and strange: Navigate by sound and by the heat on your face. Do not trust flat ground — it may be water. Do not stand still too long. Do not stray from the amber lights if you want to stay dry, because dry does not exist here, only wetter and slightly less wet. And understand that this is a place in balance. Nothing is eroding anymore. The rock has finished becoming what it wanted to be: a great wet lung, breathing cloud in and water out, warm and dark and endlessly, industriously alive.
