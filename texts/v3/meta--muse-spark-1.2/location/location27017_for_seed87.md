It is not on any map because it is not large enough to be mapped.

On the abyssal plain, where the seafloor should be flat and featureless mud for a hundred miles, there is a blister. A pocket that hasn't finished being born.

From the outside it is nothing — a smooth dome of fresh basalt, still glassy-black and warm to a thermometer though the water around it is near freezing, a single pillow of lava that swelled and did not pop. About as wide as a small room. About as tall as a chapel ceiling if chapels were spheres. You would swim right over it and think it was rock.

There is a mouth, though. Not an entrance — a cooling crack, a seam where the crust pulled as it contracted, no wider than your shoulders. The ocean pushes in. Cold, heavy, absolute water pushes in and does not drain. Inside, the ocean has made a second, smaller ocean.

You slip through sideways and you are inside, and the world changes pressure.

The chamber is fully submerged. There is no air here and there never has been. No surface, no sky, no dry. Water fills it wall to wall, cold enough to burn, a clear, viscous cold that mutes everything. It is dim in the way deep water is dim — not darkness, but a deep blue that has almost forgotten light. The little light that exists does not come from above. It comes from within.

The terrain is raw geology. This place is embryonic. The walls are not walls, they are the inside of a bubble of rock that is still becoming rock. Pillowed basalt, glossy and black as bottle glass, still soft at the molecular level, webbed with cooling fractures. In places it has shattered into hyaloclastite, a breccia of sharp glass sand held together by its own heat. In other places it is smooth and bulging inward, as if the stone flowed here yesterday — because it did. There are no smoothed stones, no sanded beaches, no sediments. No erosion has had time to learn this place. The edges are still knife-sharp. Small flakes of basalt pop off with a nearly inaudible *tick* as they contract, and drift down impossibly slowly. The floor is a gentle bowl of the same new stone, dusted with the first silt of its own making.

It is quiet. The dense water carries sound so well that sound has learned to be careful. There is no roar, no rush. The ocean outside is utterly absent. Inside, you hear only what happens very close: the tick of cooling stone, the faint hydraulic sigh of water exchanging through the crack, and — if you hold still long enough — a dense, soft, almost infrasonic creaking and clicking. Like a city heard through a thick wall.

Because the pocket is inhabited. Densely, urgently inhabited.

It is a city without tools.

Vegetation is sparse to the point of rumor. A few pale veils of bacterial mat, white and sulfurous, cling to the lower walls where the rock is still faintly warm, waving like unhemmed lace. One or two stalks of something that wants to be kelp but has never seen the sun — translucent, stunted, colorless — root in a crack and do not grow. Everything green has given up here. The builders are animals.

And there are so many of them that at first you cannot parse them. The water is crowded.

They are white. Blind. Abundant. In the dim, they make their own dim light — a constant, low bioluminescence, not bright, speckled and milky like streetlamps in fog. From the skin of tube worms, from the antennae of amphipods, from the bellies of small shrimp, a cold light that never flickers out. By it you see the city.

It is vertical, layered, densely packed, and it is all secretion. No metal, no stone cut, no device. Only biology.

Giant tube worms, red-plumed and as thick as your arm, have grown here in such numbers their chitinous tubes touch, and lean, and fuse. They are the avenues and the towers. Their tubes rise from floor to ceiling, some straight, some kinked where they grew around a neighbor, forming a close, organic tenement. Between them, smaller worms have built spiraled calcium balconies. Amphipods the size of your hand have cemented grains of glassy basalt into honeycombed galleries, chamber after chamber, porous and precise. Masses of pale shrimp move through it constantly, tending, cleaning, carrying eggs, picking parasites from the worm plumes. Brittle stars lace their arms through every gap, forming a living lattice. Isopods drift like slow pedestrians, grazing on the bacterial veils. Nothing is alone. Everything is shoulder to shoulder, layered on top of layered, a settlement that grew because there was nowhere else to grow.

The rules here are simple and strange:

1. Everything must anchor. Nothing free-floats for long. The water is so still inside the pocket that to be unattached is to be lost. Even the young are glued, tethered by threads of mucus until they can secrete a home.

2. Nothing is wasted. A dead worm's tube becomes an apartment for ten shrimp. A molted carapace becomes mortar.

3. Growth is architecture. There is no plan, but there is organization. The city follows the flow — the slow, breathing exchange of cold ocean water through the ceiling crack brings oxygen and sulfur, and the tallest tubes cluster there, where the water moves. The lower, warmer stones by the floor are a nursery, dense with eggs that glow faintly pink.

It is weird not because it is alien, but because it is familiar in the wrong medium. It feels unmistakably urban. There are thoroughfares where the current runs faster between tube-clusters and the shrimp all commute in the same direction. There are dense neighborhoods where light is thicker, and quiet cul-de-sacs where a single anemone has claimed a collapsed tube. There are even ruins — a section near the back wall where the basalt bulged too fast and crushed a row of tubes, now being slowly dismantled and rebuilt, grain by grain. You can feel the time-scale of it. This is not a reef that has existed for millennia. This is a boomtown in a bubble that didn't exist a year ago.

And it is active. Despite the silence, despite the cold, despite the intimacy of the scale, everything moves. Plumes extend and retract. Shrimp flicker. Larvae jet in short, panicked bursts and are caught. Water itself moves in slow convection cells, cold sinking, faintly warm rising from the newborn rock. Predation happens quietly — a polychaete uncoils with impossible speed from a tube and takes a passing amphipod, and then is still again. No splash. No cry. Just a tiny displacement, a small cloud of silt that hangs in the cold for a long time before settling.

If you stay until your own heat leaches out, until your breathing slows to match the ticking stone, you notice the strangest thing: the city is listening to itself. The fauna are so dense, so close, that the water between them vibrates with their small, close sounds — leg clicks, tube contractions, the rasp of radula on bacterial mat — but the basalt pocket holds it all in. It does not echo. It smothers. The city is very busy and very, very quiet, like a metropolis heard from under a blanket of deep water and new rock.

A hidden room. A cold, wet lung in the seafloor. Too small to matter to the ocean, and within it, a whole crowded world that thinks it is the ocean entire.
