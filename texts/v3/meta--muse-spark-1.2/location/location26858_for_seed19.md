At first you think you are looking at snow.

You are not. You have climbed for a long time to get here - though the climb itself is hard to remember, because there was no steepness to it, only a long, breath-thinning incline that never seemed to crest. The air got cooler and thinner by increments so small you stopped noticing. And then the ground leveled, or nearly leveled, and the sky opened, and you were here. On the Roof.

It is not a peak. Peaks are young things. This is the opposite of a peak.

Elevation here is not a mountain but a continent that forgot to come down. A plateau so high that the world below is only a theory - a deeper blue beneath the horizon's haze. You are above weather. Clouds, when they exist at all, are a lower ocean, flat and distant, far below your feet. The sky above is not the pale dome of lowlands; it is a saturated, almost violet-blue, deep enough to look into. The sun in it is small and precise and gives normal light - clear, even, shadow-casting light - but it does not warm much. The light has no heat in it. It illuminates without insisting. Things here are exactly as lit as they should be, and that only makes them stranger.

The rock remembers everything by having forgotten all sharpness.

This place is ancient in a way that has nothing to do with ruins. There are no cliffs, no ridges, no chasms. Everything that was ever jagged has been chewed down by time itself more than by wind or water - because there has been no water here for a very, very long time. The basalt and salt and iron-heavy stone has settled into its final form: a series of immense, slow swells, like the backs of buried whales, rolling for kilometers in every direction. The hollows between them are shallow and smooth as bowls. The highest rises are not more than a house-height above the lowest troughs, but the swells are so wide - half a kilometer, a kilometer across - that you feel the curvature of them in your legs as you walk. Every boulder that remains is egg-smooth, half-sunk, its windward face polished to a dull glass. The surface is powdered with mineral fines: pale salt flour, black basalt dust, red oxide that stains your boots. When you drag your hand through it, it leaves a soft furrow that holds its shape for hours, because nothing comes to erase it.

There is no water. Not hidden, not underground. You can feel that in your mouth and sinuses within minutes. Your lips crack. The dust is completely dry and does not clump. There are no streambeds, not even dry ones. In the deepest hollows, there is a flat white pan where salts have sublimated out of the rock and formed a skin, perfectly level, perfectly cracked into silent polygons. It does not reflect like ice. It drinks light.

What does grow here grows as if apologizing for it.

Vegetation is sparse to the point of rarity - not absent, but so thinly scattered that each one becomes a landmark. A black wire-shrub every hundred meters or so, no taller than your ankle, made of brittle, needle-like stems that grow in a perfect hemisphere. No leaves. A flat, grey-green lichen that clings to the lee side of stones, the size of your palm, so slow-growing it has rims like tree rings. Once in a long while, a fleshy, salt-wort thing with swollen joints, translucent as frosted glass, holding its own brine inside itself because it will never find any outside. Nothing shades anything else. Nothing competes. The plants do not fill the land; they punctuate it.

Because what fills the land is not plants.

At first you took it for snow, then for lichen, then for stone. Then you crouched, and it resolved, and your breath caught in the cool air and did not want to come out again.

The fauna is everywhere. It is the land.

Every swell, every pan, every stone is densely, continuously covered - not overrun, but *constituted* - by them. They are not swarming. They are not moving at all. That is what makes the silence so complete you can hear the blood in your ears.

They are - there is no good word that is not borrowed - like limpets, like barnacles, like isopods, like moths, but smoothed and simplified until they are almost mineral themselves. Most are low, oval mantles of chitin, matte white or dove-grey or translucent amber, two hands wide, overlapping at the edges like shingles. Some are smaller, beadlike, clustered in the millions along every crack. Some are larger: elongated, ribbed forms, a meter long, lying in shallow depressions with a faint, raised seam down their backs that opens a slit for breathing, imperceptibly slow. Some are upright: thin stalks topped with a feathery, glassy fan, completely still - not swaying, not trembling. All of them are touching or nearly touching. The density is bewildering. In any square meter there are hundreds; in any valley between swells, hundreds of thousands, packed shoulder to shell, a living tessellation that follows the curve of the ancient rock perfectly. You could walk for a kilometer and never place your foot on bare stone, only on backs.

And none of them move. Not one twitches. Not one flees your shadow. If you kneel and look close you can see the very faint pulse of a spiracle, a slow condensation and evaporation inside a translucent plate that takes a full minute. If you rest your hand gently on a mantle, you feel a cool, faint warmth - cooler than blood, warmer than stone - and an utter, absolute stillness. They are alive, overwhelmingly alive, but they have agreed, collectively and completely, to do nothing.

This is an ecology that has reached equilibrium and stayed there.

There is no predation visible, no grazing, no chase. The creatures do not eat the sparse plants, nor each other. They seem to feed on the air itself, or the salt, or the thin sunlight - a non-aqueous chemistry that needs no rain. Their undersides, when you carefully lift an edge - though you feel immediately that you shouldn't - show a dense felt of hair-fine filaments that etch directly into the basalt, dissolving and absorbing mineral. They exhale nothing you can smell. They do not need to drink.

Because nothing changes here, they have no reason to hurry. The dynamic of the Roof is near zero. Wind, if it ever had force here, has forgotten it. Dust motes hang in the air for a long time after you disturb them. The temperature stays cool - not freezing, not temperate, but a steady, high-altitude cool that makes your fingers precise and your breath visible in brief puffs that flatten and vanish. Day and night seem to take a long time to turn over, or perhaps they don't turn over at all while you are watching. Shadows do not crawl at the speed you expect; they lean, and then they are somewhere else, without having been seen to move.

There are marks that people have been here, but not many and not recently, and they have learned to be quiet about it.

Scattered across the swells are small structures, always solitary, never villages. A low wall of stacked basalt slabs, waist high, forming three sides of a rectangle with the fourth left open to the east - a windbreak that has no wind to break. Inside, a flat stone with a ring of soot that will never wash away because it never rains. A cairn, carefully built, of the white salt-pan polygons, though anyone building it would have had to carry them from a pan a kilometer away. A ring of stones with flaked edges: hand-tools, primitive in the truest sense - chipped basalt flakes, a hammerstone worn smooth by a palm, a shallow bowl ground into a larger rock for crushing lichen or salt. All of it is handmade, manual, simple. No metal, or only a single, heavily patinated copper awl left point-up in a crack as if to mark a path. The builders understood the rules here: make little, leave little, do not disturb the mantles.

You realize, as you walk, that the reality here is not quite sealed.

Weirdness is not an event on the Roof. It is the condition. If you stare down the long, open scale of the place - for you can see for twenty, thirty kilometers in the clear, thin air, across swell after swell after swell - you notice that distance does not behave. A wire-shrub that should be a hundred meters away snaps into focus as if it were ten meters away when you blink. A cairn you walked away from is again ahead of you, not behind, but you cannot point to when you turned. The horizon has a faint seam, like a place where the sky was folded and pressed, and sometimes a second, fainter sun-arc hangs inside the blue for a moment before smoothing itself out. Sounds fail in odd ways: you drop a basalt flake and expect a clack, but the sound arrives late and from slightly to your left. Your own footfalls on the mantles make almost no sound - a soft, padded hush like stepping on cork - though the mantles are hard.

The worst - or most beautiful - part is the stillness of the fauna makes it a mirror. Because they do not move, you become the only thing that does. And moving feels like an error. After an hour, you find yourself walking slower without deciding to, lifting your feet higher, breathing more quietly. The silence is not empty. It is full, filled to saturation with held breath. If you stay very still and listen to the quiet - 0.5 on any scale that could measure such things - you hear what passes for sound here: the almost inaudible tic of a mantle adjusting its pressure by a micron, the whisper of salt dust settling on salt dust, the faint, high ringing of the thin air in your own inner ear. The overwhelming presences do not call, do not rustle. They wait.

They have been waiting so long that the rock beneath them has stopped trying to become anything else. The swells will not get lower. The pans will not get whiter. The shrubs will not grow. This is the oldest stable state, the end of erosion, the place where the world wore itself down to a smooth, cool, dry palm and then stopped, and something else - something patient, dense, and silent - covered it completely, like a blanket, and decided to stay.

If you lie down - and you will want to, because the cool is kind and the light is forgiving - you can lie between the mantles without harming them; they leave just enough bare mineral for a human shape. Above you the deep sky shows no birds, no insects in the air, because all the animals are already here, around you, under you. The normal sun paints your face without warmth. The ancient swells hold you, evenly. And the countless, quiet, living backs around you breathe once, together, so slowly you might imagine it.

Nothing changes. That is not a threat. That is the promise that has kept this place whole.
