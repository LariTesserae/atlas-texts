This valley is low.

Not low like a hole, low like a held breath. A broad, shallow bowl between two long shoulders of black rock, maybe thirty kilometers from one rim to the other, open enough that you can't take it in at once. You have to turn your head. The sky is too big for it and too close at the same time - a low, unbroken ceiling of cloud the color of wet slate that never quite lifts, never quite rains, just hangs and sweats.

It is dark here even at noon. Not night-dark, but the dark of deep winter at latitude sixty north when the sun doesn't clear the ridge. A flat, directionless gloom. Light comes diffused through kilometers of cloud, and from the snow that still holds on the high shoulders, and from a faint, sourceless amber that hangs in the air itself. Far above, you know there is an aurora turning, but down in the bowl you only see its reflection, a slow green smear on the underside of the cloud deck.

And it is cold. A damp, penetrating cold that lives in your lungs. Two, maybe three degrees below freezing, but the air is so wet it feels colder. Every surface is rimed or weeping. Your breath plumes and does not dissipate - it hangs, condenses, and falls again as frost on your shoulders.

This is young country. The ice only left it recently. You can tell by the way the rock has not yet learned to be soft. The valley floor is not smooth; it is a fresh scrape. Long, sharp-backed ridges of basalt and shattered gneiss run north-south like claw marks, still angular, still unpillowed by soil. Between them are scoops and kettles, raw basins gouged by the glacier, now full of water. No old rivers here, no deep meanders. Water hasn't had time to decide where it wants to go, so it goes everywhere.

It is wet the way a sponge is wet. The water table is at your boot soles. You step and the ground gives, not mud but a living mat - peat a meter thick, sphagnum and liverwort and star moss layered so densely you can feel the water move under it, a slow pulse underfoot. Every depression is a mirror-black pool, every rock face weeps. The basalt weeps. Condensation forms on every needle and drips, drips, drips without ever stopping. The air itself precipitates on your skin. In the hollows between the ridges, sedge marshes stand in inch-deep water that reflects the low sky perfectly, broken only where something has waded through. Even the air tastes like stone-water.

And yet it is impossibly lush. Cold and dark should mean barren, but this valley is choked with growth.

The ridges are combed with black spruce and stunted larch, not tall but incredibly dense, branches interlocking, trunks so close you cannot walk between them without turning sideways. They are bearded with old man's beard lichen that hangs in wet curtains, dripping. Under them, there is no forest floor, only more vegetation - dwarf willow and birch no higher than your knee but forming a continuous thicket, Labrador tea, crowberry, cotton-grass going to seed in white bursts. And over everything, the moss. Deep, saturated moss of a dozen species, electric green and rust red and near-black, swallowing boulders whole, muffling stone, holding water like a reservoir. In summer this place must be brief and frantic, but even now in the cold dark it is not dormant, just slowed. The green is still pushing.

Because it is warm underneath. Not much, but enough.

Scattered across the valley, at intervals that seem random until you see the pattern from higher up, are the structures. At first you mistake them for dead trees - thin, dark spires perhaps twenty meters tall, no wider than a person, without joints or seams. They stand alone or in pairs, half-sunk in the peat, leaning slightly as the frost heaves them. They are quiet in a way that makes the sound around them louder. Some are dark and inert, their surfaces matte and frosted over. Others are active. Those ones shimmer.

If you get close to an active one you see it isn't solid. Or it is, but its surface is moving. A field holds just off the skin of it, a faint heat-haze in the cold, and within that haze frost never forms. Snow melts in a perfect circle around its base, revealing black peat and a sudden, shocking flush of growth - tiny ferns and saxifrage flowering out of season in that one warm meter, as if the spire is bleeding spring. The air near it smells faintly of ozone and of turned earth. At night, or what passes for night, the active spires pulse with a very low amber light from within, like a coal breathing. The light doesn't illuminate so much as it stains the mist around it.

They are not mysterious. You can understand what they do if you watch long enough. They are heat pumps, or more than that - they are drawing on the temperature differential between the deep rock and the cold air, on the planet's own low-grade exhale. They take that difference and make it into something else. On the ridge crests where the wind scours, you see the other kind - wide, almost invisible webs strung between spires, filaments so fine you only see them when water beads on them. They harvest static from the cloud deck, from the condensing atmosphere itself. The whole valley is netted in infrastructure so fine and so distributed you could miss it if you weren't looking for the way the snow melts or the way the compass shivers. This is not a city. No one lives here. This is a farm for energy, or a nursery. It was built to keep this place from freezing solid, to keep the marsh from turning to permafrost, to accelerate something. The spires keep the ground at that knife-edge just above death.

That is why the valley is so loud, and so alive.

The sound hits you before the cold does. It is deafening, but not as a single noise. It's a saturation.

First, water: everywhere, the constant drip and trickle and gurgle of a landscape that cannot hold its water. Rivulets cutting fresh, sharp channels through peat, overfull pools spilling into each other with soft, flat slaps, the hiss of condensation beading and falling from ten million needles at once.

Then, wind: not a howl but a roar, low and continuous, as the cloud deck drags its belly over the ridge shoulders and is shredded on the spruce. The web-filaments between the spires sing in it, a high, glassy harmonic that cuts through the bass.

Then, the structures themselves: the active spires emit a deep, subsonic thrum you feel in your molars, a cycling on-off, on-off, as they dump heat. When several pulse out of phase the beat interferes and the whole valley seems to throb.

And over and through all of it, the fauna. This place should be silent in the cold dark, but it is crowded.

The marsh pools boil with life. Clouds of midges, improbable in the cold, hang in dense columns over every warm circle around a spire, and the birds that feed on them are everywhere - redpolls and snow buntings moving in flocks so large they sound like wind themselves, taking off in a single deafening clatter, settling again seconds later. On the peat mats, herds of caribou, smaller and shaggier than you expect, pick their way, their hooves sucking and popping in the wet moss, their breath steaming. Their coats are tangled with burrs and lichen. They do not startle easily; there are too many of them pressed together, moving shoulder to shoulder along the narrow dry ridges.

In the sedge, you hear rails and bitterns calling, amplified by the cold air. Geese - thousands - rest on the black pools, gabbling without pause, a wall of sound that reflects off the low cloud and comes back louder. Underfoot, lemmings rustle in runways through the moss, and the foxes that hunt them bark. Even the peat seems to move with beetles and spiders, too many legs per square meter to count, all working the warm, wet thatch.

Nothing here is strange. No six-legged deer, no glowing eyes. A spruce is a spruce. A caribou is a caribou. Even the spires, for all their impossible thinness and their silent heat, feel like engineering, not magic. If you cut one open - and someone has, one lies toppled and split on the south slope, its interior a clean, dry cavity full of folded black material like paper - you would find it obeys rules, just rules you haven't learned yet.

The valley is turbulent because nothing is at equilibrium. The young rock is still settling, slumping in small frost-quakes that send ripples across the pools. The water is still carving, finding its way, abandoning a channel overnight and cutting a new one by morning. The ice on the high shoulders calves with sharp cracks like gunfire, feeding new melt. The caribou churn the moss, the birds strip the midges, the midges return in a new column over a different spire. The spires themselves cycle, warm, cool, warm, and the little halos of summer around them bloom and die and bloom again. A wind gust tears the web-filaments and they re-tension themselves, humming a new note.

If you stand still long enough, the cold soaks through your boots from the living wet ground, and the sound becomes so complete it stops being sound and becomes pressure. The darkness does not lift. The green around you, impossibly vivid in the gloom, seems to drink what little light there is and hold it. Water beads on your eyelashes and runs. The valley does not care that you are there, but it is not hostile. It is just very, very busy - a cold, dark, dripping greenhouse the size of a town, running at full power in the middle of winter, humming and dripping and calling and growing all at once, dense and open and fresh-cut and ancient in its biology, trying with frantic, deafening energy to become whatever it is going to become next.
