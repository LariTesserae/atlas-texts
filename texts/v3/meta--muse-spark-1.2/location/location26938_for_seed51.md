It is a low country.

Not low like a beach or a plain, but low like a basin that has decided to stay low, that has settled into lowness over a very long time. The world here has had time to get comfortable with itself. The hills that ring it are not hills really, just the soft, tired shoulders of old ground - rounded, forested, worn down to their bones. Their slopes are not cut or gullied but smoothed, as if the land has been breathing in and out for so long it has eroded its own sharpness away. Nothing is new here. Even the stones are patient.

The basin is open and you can feel it. Not infinite, not an ocean - it's a scale you can hold in your head if you try: a valley the size of a small country, or a county, ringed but not closed. You can see three or four miles in any direction before mist and trees take the rest, and you know that if you walked for a day you would still be in the same kind of place, just a different fold of it. The sky is too big for the land, low and flat and dim.

The light never gets strong. There is no direct sun. The cloud deck is a thick, pearlescent felt that hangs a few hundred feet up, condensing as much as it covers. The light that filters through is a cool, late-afternoon gray even at noon - not dark, but dilute, like light that has been steeped in water. Colors don't pop; they deepen. Green becomes black-green. Water becomes silver-gray. Everything glows a little from within because the air itself is wet.

And the air is wet. Everything is wet.

Water is not a feature here, it is the medium. It is near the surface everywhere - you can press a palm to the moss and water will well up around your fingers without you digging. The ground is a sponge of peat and living mat, ten feet thick in places, floating. Between the hummocks are channels, not quite streams, not quite ditches, moving so slowly you can only tell by the way the duckweed drifts. Deeper in the middle of the basin they braid and deepen into true waterways, amber-black, cold, mirroring the dim sky perfectly until a ripple breaks them.

The rocks weep. Where the old, established shoulders of the basin push up through the peat, sandstone and shale seep constantly. Not springs - seeps. The stone sweats. Water beads on its undersides and falls without ever drying. In the cool air,  breath fogs, and the condensation never really leaves. It gathers on every leaf, every hair of moss, every hanging root, until the weight pulls it down in fat, cold drops that hit the litter below with a soft, endless patter. When wind moves through, the whole forest shakes off its water at once in a brief, cold rain that comes from below the clouds.

Vegetation is lush but not jungle-lush. It's a cool, northern lushness. Dense, layered, near-touching. There is no bare ground. From the canopy - low, stunted alder and black willow and a kind of broad-leafed tree that has no proper name, its bark pale and peeling like paper birch but its leaves as big as your hands, waxy and dripping - down through sub-canopy, through curtains of epiphyte and hanging moss that drape like wet beards, down to the shrub layer of Labrador-smelling scrub and berry canes, down to the ground which is not ground but a second canopy of sphagnum, liverwort, sundew, sedge, fern, all growing on top of each other, over each other, through each other. Saturation. If you pick up a handful of what looks like soil, it is roots and rhizomes and fungal thread, holding water like a lung holds air. The air between plants is also crowded - spores, seed down, midges in slow clouds, droplets suspended so long they seem to float.

There are no structures. Nothing built. No cut stumps, no stone walls, no trail, no ruin. No artifact at all to suggest that hands ever shaped this place. And yet -

And yet it is engineered. Intensely, impossibly engineered.

You notice it first in the sound. This place is loud. Not with animal noise. Water is loud: the constant drip and trickle and gurgle on every scale, from the ping of a single drop on a pitcher plant to the low, broad susurrus of the main channels moving through peat, and underneath that a deeper sound, a hum. A tone that is not wind or water. It comes from everywhere and nowhere. A low, resonant chord, like a transformer farm heard from miles away, or the thrum you feel in your teeth when you stand near a large, perfectly balanced machine. It modulates. When a cloud of spores releases, the hum shifts a quarter-tone higher for a few seconds. When you step onto a particularly deep mat of moss and your weight depresses it, the hum briefly warps around your feet.

If you watch the water long enough, you see it doesn't always obey. In one pool, the reflection shows a sky with two thin moons that aren't in the sky above. In another, a trickle runs uphill for a foot before remembering to fall. Droplets sometimes fall slow, as if the air is thicker for them. Leaves that should have fallen months ago hang suspended a hand's breadth above the peat, rotating slowly, never landing, never decaying. The geometry is settled but wrong; parallel trunks in the distance appear to converge and then diverge again if you walk. The horizon does a strange thing where it seems a little closer on your left than on your right, and if you turn quickly you can catch a flicker, like the world redrawing itself when you aren't looking directly at it.

The tech is not *in* the landscape. It *is* the landscape. The peat itself is computational. Each sphagnum tip is a switch. The water carries not just tannins but structured information - you can see it if you look at a sundew's bead at the right angle, where iridescence resolves into lattices of light that rearrange too fast and too precisely to be random. The rocks that weep are not rocks, or not only rocks; their sweat is coolant. The forest is a thinking machine that does not need to be built because it grew itself into this configuration, and the cool, dim, wet conditions are its operating temperature. It runs on the slow movement of water and the decay of leaves and the differential between cloud-top and peat-bottom. It has been running for a very long time, in balance, and it is not for you.

Its processes are active, always moving. Weather cycles through in hours, not days - mist condenses, falls, evaporates, condenses again. Clouds boil low and scrape the canopy, discharge their load, lift. The plants grow visibly if you are patient; you can watch a fern frond uncurl a millimeter while you stare, driven by the constant wet. Decay is just as fast; a fallen leaf is skeletonized by white fungal lace in a day, its matter pulled down into the peat-computer. And occasionally, rarely, an animal moves through.

Fauna is scarce here. This is a plant and water and physics place. Sometimes a large, crane-like bird with too many joints in its legs will pick its way through the shallows, utterly silent despite the loudness around it, and drink. Sometimes something the size of a deer but with a pelt that looks exactly like hanging moss will watch you from between trees and then not be there when you blink, without having moved. Insects are present but strangely muted - no swarms, just individual hunters. You get the sense that animals are not residents but diagnostic routines, briefly instantiated to check a parameter before being deleted again.

The rules of the world here are not quite ours, but they are consistent.

1.  Water seeks equilibrium, but equilibrium is not level.
2.  Light travels slightly slower through air that has passed through the canopy recently.
3.  Nothing can be truly still. Even stone sweats. Even the hum never holds one note for more than a few minutes.
4.  Looking directly at a thing stabilizes it. Things seen from the corner of the eye are more honest.

If you stay long enough, until your boots are soaked through and your shoulders are cold and damp, you begin to hear the loudness resolve into layers: water over water over hum over drip over your own blood in your ears, and over it all, very faint, a kind of clicking, as if uncountable tiny things are opening and closing.

It is not a place that was made. It is a place that made itself out of wet, cool light and old, settled ground, and then learned how to compute with what it had, and kept going until its computation became indistinguishable from weather, and its weather became indistinguishable from thought.
