It is low. Not low like a plain, low like a bowl.

The land here has settled into itself. A shallow basin not much larger than three or four city blocks would be elsewhere, rimmed on all sides by a weathered lip of concrete and sandstone that was cut and then left to soften. The cut is old. Its edges are no longer sharp. They are rounded, pitted, darkened, bearded with mineral stain where water has run for so long it has carved its own way regardless of the engineering. This place has not been made recently. It has arrived at its shape and decided to stay.

The bottom of the bowl never dries.

Water is everywhere but rarely as open water. It hangs in the air first - warm, dim, close - a permanent low cloud that never quite becomes rain and never quite clears, condensing on every surface. It beads on the underside of pipes, on brick, on leaves, and falls again in a slow, constant weeping. The ground is saturated to the surface. Under the iron walkways the soil is black and spongy, half mud half root-mat, and where the concrete has cracked, water wells up without a source, clear and warm. In the lowest quarter the settlement has become a marsh that it pretends is still a street: the pavement gives way to shallow, still water the color of tea, perhaps a foot deep, with the foundations of buildings rising out of it like pilings. Weeping rocks - no, weeping walls - all the retaining walls sweat. You can put your hand to brick that has been there for eighty years and feel it damp and alive under a skin of algae.

It is warm. Not hot enough to be uncomfortable, warm enough that the wet never feels cold. Steam and sweat are the same temperature. The air holds you.

And it is dim. The light has to come down through the cloud, down through the rim of the basin, down through the lattice overhead. Because the town built up, not out. This is a hamlet that grew vertically inside its own boundaries. Three, four, five stories of layered construction crowd the bowl from edge to edge - tenements and machine halls stitched together with catwalks, gantries, stairwells that go nowhere, corrugated roofs under brick facades under newer concrete pours. Nothing was ever demolished, only built over and around. From any point you can see the opposite wall of the basin, close enough to read the moss on it. The scale is intimate, enclosed, a room the size of a neighborhood. A cathedral of industry with no sky.

There is no distance between things. That is the rule here. Vegetation does not grow beside industry; it grows on it, through it, with it. And both are dense to the point of touching. Vine and pipe, fern and rebar, all braided. The buildings carry lush, almost aggressive green - not weeds but great shaggy mats of moss, sheets of liverwort, ferns that unfurl from mortar joints, narrow trees - alder and willow - rooted in the saturated seams between factory walls, their trunks slick and pale. Their leaves are large, waxy, dripping constantly. They thrive on the condensation and the waste heat. The green is so saturated the brick beneath it is rarely visible except where heat has recently scalded it bare. Even the machines have been colonized: the sides of boilers and dynamos and looms wear a felt of green, except where vibration shakes it off. It is a jungle scaled down to fit inside a factory block.

The factories are still running.

No one tends them, or if someone does, they are not seen. But the industrial heart is loud and slow. That is its character. Everything moves with a patient, almost geological deliberation, but with great force. A flywheel fifteen feet across turns once every twenty seconds, its axle groaning. Steam presses sigh out from release valves on a long exhale, then inhale again minutes later. Water wheels, half-submerged in the marsh-street, creak as they turn, lifting water only to pour it back. Conveyor chains clank overhead, carrying nothing, or carrying wet clumps of leaf matter from one sorting house to another for no reason that is visible. Transformers hum - a deep, chest-filling 60-cycle drone that you feel in your molars. Drips join into trickles, trickles into the hiss of pressure, the hum into a throb. It is very loud, but not chaotic. It is a loudness you can live inside, like living beside a waterfall. Your ear adjusts and finds the rhythm: drip... hiss... hum... groan... drip.

What makes it strange is not that nature has reclaimed industry, or that industry has consumed nature. It's that neither has won and neither is losing. They have reached an equilibrium that should be impossible. The rust does not advance. The moss does not overgrow the intake vents - it stops precisely at the lip, as if trimmed by heat. The water that should have flooded the generators years ago instead circulates around them, held in channels that the roots themselves have reinforced. The place looks balanced, long-existed, weathered into a state of mutual permission. A building that is half boiler-house and half swamp, and has been so long enough that you cannot tell where one was supposed to end.

And there are no animals.

That takes a moment to notice, and then you cannot un-notice it. Lush as it is - warm, wet, endlessly vegetated - there is no movement except the machines and the drip. No insects on the undersides of leaves. No birds cutting through the steam. No rats along the pipes, no gulls, no flies over the marsh. Even the water, though teeming with suspended green, holds no fish, no tadpoles. The leaves are perfect, uneaten. Spores fall and sprout without anything to carry them but water and air. The absence is not silent - the place is loud - but it is specific. The food chain has only two links: water and plant and machine, plant and machine breathing each other's exhaust. The air smells of ozone, wet brick, crushed leaf, and hot iron, but never of decay the way animal decay smells. When a leaf falls into the warm water it simply softens and becomes peat, slowly.

If you stand on the central gantry where you can see the whole bowl at once - the tiered roofs dripping, the green-lit steam, the slow turning wheels, the encircling wall with its dark weeping stains - you understand the rules all at once:

Water will always be present, arriving from above and below at once.

Light will always be filtered and low.

Things will grow until they touch something else, then grow around it.

Things built will not be torn down.

Things that turn will continue to turn, slowly, loudly, without hurry, for a long time yet.

And nothing with a mouth or an eye will come to interrupt it.
