It doesn't look like anything from above.

The desert is a flat, white pan of salt and baked silt that runs to every horizon, humming with heat. The light there is total — a high, cloudless, brassy bright that flattens everything and bleaches color out of the sky until it is almost white. You could walk across that pan for days and see nothing but the glitter of salt and the shiver of mirage, and you would walk right over it if you didn't know where to step.

The place is lower than the desert. Not much lower, but absolutely lower.

There is a seam in the pan, a closed lip of salt-rock, perhaps thirty meters long, no wider than a doorway. From the surface it looks like a dry crack, a place where the crust has buckled. You have to get on your belly to see that it doesn't end. It opens. The air that comes out of it is warm and smells like hot dust and green oil and old clay.

You lower yourself down.

And the world inverts.

You are inside a pocket. A hollow. Not a cave — it is open to the sky, but the sky is a thin, brilliant ribbon far above, a slot of white fire between two leaning walls of salt and sandstone. The hollow is maybe two hundred meters across at its widest and sixty meters deep, an oval bowl gouged or grown or collapsed into the crust. The sound changes the instant your feet leave the rim.

It is deafening.

Not loud like a machine. Loud like a living thing filling the air entirely. A dry, rustling, clicking, chiming roar that has no single source. At first you think it is insects — a trillion cicadas — but there are almost no animals here. It is the vegetation.

This is the contradiction that makes the place.

There is no water here. Not in any form you recognize. No seep, no spring, no pool, no damp line on the rock. The stone is utterly dry; it drinks any spill instantly. The air is so dry your lips crack within an hour, your sweat vanishes before it beads. If you spill a waterskin it doesn't puddle, it hisses on the salt and is gone, leaving a dark ghost that fades in minutes. The ground is salt-crystal and fired dust. You can taste the mineral on your teeth.

And yet it is lush.

Not lush like a jungle floor. Lush like a hanging garden that has forgotten gravity. The walls of the hollow are completely covered. From the rim to the bowl floor, every surface that isn't built has been taken by something green-gold and amber and dusty red, growing in curtains and beards and braids. It does not grow *in* the ground. It grows *on* it.

The dominant form looks like a vine but is not. It has no roots that go searching for water. Its holdfasts are flat, pale discs that cement directly to bare salt and sandstone, etching a faint acid shadow into the rock. From those discs hang ropes and sheets of waxy, translucent leaves, each leaf as thick as your thumb, as stiff as cured hide, overlapping like roof tiles. They are not green with chlorophyll the way earth-plants are; they are a deeper, glassier green, almost jade, veined with white. They don't transpire. They seal. When you snap one open — and the people who live here sometimes do — there is no juice, no sap. There is a dense, clear oil that smells sharply of turpentine and lime, that beads and does not evaporate. The plant makes its own waterless flesh out of light and air. It drinks sun. It drinks the hot wind. At night it tightens; by day it swells visibly, creaking.

Because the other sound, under the rustle, is growth. These things are active. You can watch a new runner extend a hand's breadth between morning and noon, throwing a pale thread across empty air until it finds rock and slaps down with an audible *tack*. The curtains shiver and shift against each other even when there is no breeze, rolling slow waves down the walls as they compete for that brutal light funneled from above. Where they brush, their stiff leaves clack and chime against one another — countless dry plates rattling — and that is the roar. A deafening dry forest of glass-leather leaves all trembling at once in the warm updraft. When the wind does come, funneled down through the slot, it plays the whole bowl like an instrument, and the sound becomes almost painful.

You notice the city then, because you had mistaken it for part of the plant.

It is urban. Unmistakably. This is not a ruin, not a scattering of huts. It is a town built all at once to fit the pocket, terraced in continuous rings down the inside of the bowl. Low, flat-roofed houses of salt-brick and sun-fired clay, plastered smooth and then weathered back to a soft, rounded profile. Everything has been touched and softened by long time — corners are round, steps are dished in the middle, lintels sag just slightly. Established. Settled. No sharp new cut. Wood is scarce and precious; what timber there is is dark, oiled, made from the cores of the hanging plants themselves, hard as bone. Ladders and walkways and rope bridges of braided fiber stitch the terraces together, crossing gaps where the green curtains have been trained to leave a lane. Kilns built into the wall still hold ash. Looms stand on rooftops under awnings of woven leaf. Pots, tools, shallow copper pans hammered thin and bright, all of simple make — hand-formed, hand-beaten, nothing that needed a foundry. Primitive, careful work.

And empty.

That is the second contradiction. It is a city scaled for hundreds, perhaps a thousand, but there is almost nothing inside it. The density is wrong. The streets — narrow, beautifully kept, swept clean of salt dust — are deserted. A plaza with a cistern that never held water sits vast and white and empty, its acoustic perfect for the chattering leaves above, so a single footstep echoes like a bell. For every house that shows life — a doorway curtained with hanging fibers, a mat rolled out, a smell of roasting waxy seeds — there are five that are open, bare, given over to the vines that have politely entered through window and roof and hung their curtains inside. No crowding. Vast spaces between presences. The city was built dense with structure, but not dense with bodies. It feels like a hive built too large for its bees, or a nest that expects a different kind of occupant.

The few people you do see move slowly in the heat, wrapped in pale, loose cloth that leaves only their eyes. They don't farm in any way you know. They garden the vertical. With long hooked poles of bone-wood, they prune the hanging sheets, harvest the thick leaves, slice them to render that lime-scented oil which they burn in shallow lamps and cook with and rub into their skin against the dryness. They collect the papery seed husks that the roar shakes loose — each husk rattles like a tiny drum — and weave them. They tend, more than they build.

They speak rarely here, because to be heard you must shout, and shouting dries the throat. They use hand signs across the plaza.

Fauna, when you see it, is rare and improbable. A flat, translucent glider the size of your hand that launches from a leaf curtain and rides the hot updraft in a slow circle, mouth open, drinking midges and dust. A salt lizard, the color of the brick, perfectly still on a wall until it blinks. Mostly, there is only the plant and the people and the stone.

The rules of the pocket are simple and absolute:

1. Light is food. The slot of sky is the only god. Everything leans toward it.
2. Nothing that holds water can survive exposed. You keep your body covered, your oil sealed, your mouth closed.
3. Sound is growth. The louder the bowl gets toward noon, the faster it grows, and the more dangerous it is to leave a house untended — a runner can stitch your door shut in a day.
4. The city will not expand. The pocket is the size it is. When a new terrace is needed, an old one is reclaimed by the green and cut back elsewhere. It is balanced, long-existed, patient.

If you stay until late afternoon, when the sun finally slips past the lip of salt above and the brilliant light goes from white to deep gold for perhaps twenty minutes, the whole bowl changes. The translucent leaves catch the side-light and glow, every wall becomes a lantern of amber and jade. The deafening rustle softens, just a little, as the plants relax. In that short, warm, luminous hush you can hear, for the first time, the faint crack and tick of the salt itself expanding and contracting, and the small, human sounds from the occupied houses — a mortar grinding, a child humming — incredibly clear and close in the vast, hollow, empty, lush, dry town hidden under the desert's skin.
