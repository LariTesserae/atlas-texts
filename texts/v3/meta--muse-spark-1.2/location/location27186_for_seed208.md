This place is low.

Not low like a valley cut between higher things. Low like the world has settled. Like water finding its own level after a very long time. The continent here has been worn until there is almost no contour left to wear — a peneplain, a shield, an ancient plain where elevation is a rumor. If you walk for an hour in any direction you might gain or lose three meters, and that counts as a hill. The horizon is a perfect, soft circle, far away in the dimness.

The rock is old. Not old in the way a mountain is old — craggy and storied — old the way a river stone is old. Everything has been rounded and re-rounded until edges don't exist. The basement shows through everywhere: gneiss and banded iron and milky chert folded in on itself billions of times, then planed flat by wind and water and time until it shines. There is no soil. Not really. There is grit, and sand so fine it feels like dust, and plates of stone that have exfoliated like skin. In the shallowest depressions, the stone is polished to a dark mirror. You can see the low sky in it, and your own blurred reflection when you pass.

It is wet. Not with rivers, not exactly. There is no channel deep enough to hold a river anymore; the gradients are too gentle. Instead water is everywhere at once. It sweats out of the rock itself. The stone weeps.

The air is cool — not biting, not winter. The kind of cool that lives in cellars and at the edge of spring, maybe 8 or 10 degrees, the cool that makes your hands ache if they stay wet too long. And they will stay wet. The atmosphere is saturated. Condensation never quite becomes rain, never quite stops. It beads on every surface, runs together, sheets. Water stands in films across the plain inches deep, in pans that stretch for a hundred meters and are only a finger deep, reflecting the dim sky and then disappearing into a throat of gravel. It rises underfoot when you step — the water table is right there, just under the stone, pressing up. You hear it before you see it, moving under plates, sighing through cracks. Weeping rocks line the faintest rises, their undersides always dripping though no spring feeds them. The stone exhales water.

Light is dim and diffuse. No direct sun has hit this place in a long time, or perhaps the sun here is just a paler star. The sky is a uniform pearl-grey, bright nowhere and dark nowhere — late dusk that never resolves into night. There are no shadows. The light comes from everywhere, bounced between low cloud and wet stone until it has no direction. Distances are hard to judge. Things far away look as close as things nearby because there is no contrast to give them scale.

Nothing green grows here. Not a blade, not a moss. This is a mineral world. If there was ever a time of plants, it is utterly gone — ground into that fine grit. What looks like color on the rocks is not vegetation but thin bacterial varnish, black and rust-red and sulfurous yellow, slick to the touch. No leaves to break the wind. No roots to hold anything. Just stone and water and the film that lives on them.

And yet it is full of animals.

At first, because the density of things is so sparse — so much empty space between any two presences — you think it is barren. You see one stone, then a hundred meters of shining pan, then another stone. But then you stop and look closer at the water film and the rock is moving.

The fauna does not need plants. It grazes stone. It filters water. It eats the bacterial slick and eats each other. Pale, translucent, amphibious things — not fish, not insects, not quite either. Some are flat as fingernails and skate on the surface tension of the pans, leaving wakes that catch the dim light. Some are long and segmented and live half-buried in the wet grit, with feathery gills that pulse. The most numerous are no bigger than your thumb, humped and eyeless, with a domed shell like a wet pebble and dozens of jointed legs underneath. They swarm. They cover the weeping rocks in tides, scraping the biofilm with rasping mouths, clicking shell against shell. When they move, the stone seems to crawl.

There are larger things too, sparsely distributed — that's what makes the density still feel sparse despite the abundance. Every few hundred meters a solitary hunter stands in the shallow water on stilt legs, as tall as a dog, as thin as folded paper, its body the same grey as the sky. It does not move until it stabs, impossibly fast, into the swarm. There are low, humped things like seals but with no eyes, that wallow in the deeper pans and grunt. Everything is wet, everything is pale or dark, camouflaged for a world without color.

You would think such a sparse, flat place would be silent. It is not. It is deafening.

The sound has no single source, which is why at first you can't locate it. It is the chorus. The small shelled grazers — millions of them across this open, valley-sized plain — each make a sound. A chirp, a click, a creak of shell. Alone it would be nothing. Together it is a roar that never stops, layered and pulsing, rising and falling like cicadas in late summer but magnified until the air vibrates. The rock itself amplifies it; the shallow water is a drum head. The weeping fissures hiss and gurgle underneath. The thin-legged hunters add a sudden, sharp clack. The wind, moving low and cool across a surface with nothing to stop it, drags across the water films and makes them hum.

You feel it in your chest before you hear it properly. You have to raise your voice to be heard, and then shout. If you stay too long your ears ring with it even after you plug them. At midday — though there is no midday, only a slight lessening of dimness — the chorus peaks until it is physical, a pressure. And then, without reason, it will dip for a minute, and in that dip you hear how vast and empty the plain really is, how far the sound was carrying.

People have been here, or something like people. Scattered, primitive — that is the word. The marks are small and infrequent, which makes them more striking against the emptiness. A cairn of balanced chert slabs, carefully stacked without mortar, with water beading and running down its sides. A low wall, ankle-high, two stones thick, running for twenty meters and then stopping for no reason — a fish weir, perhaps, though there are no fish. Shallow cups ground into the stone where water collects and biofilm is scraped and gathered. A shelter of sorts: three leaning slabs making a windbreak, with a fire-blackened circle inside where little could have burned except maybe dried colonies of the shelled grazers and the fatty bodies of the larger ones. Stone flakes where tools were knapped, left where they fell. Hand-tool work, simple-fabricated. No metal, or if there was it has rusted away to nothing. No roads. The structures are not built to last, but on this ancient surface they have lasted, because there is so little to knock them down. Lichen-like varnish is already growing over the oldest of them.

The rules of this place are simple and strange, unusual in the way that makes you recalibrate.

Water does not flow downhill here so much as it breathes. It rises and falls with a pulse that has more to do with air pressure than topography. The stone sweats on a cycle. The pans appear and connect and disconnect. An area that was dry — as dry as anything gets — an hour ago is now an inch deep and alive with skimming things, and an hour later it is empty again.

Sound is heavier than air. On cool, dim days like this — which is every day — the chorus can be felt as ripples on the water before it is heard.

Nothing rots in the way you expect. Without plants to become humus, the dead are quickly taken apart by crowds of grazers and filterers. A carcass of a stilt-hunter, if you find one, will be a clean white frame in a day, surrounded by a frenzied clicking circle that then disperses, leaving the stone polished cleaner than before.

And the plain is active, always in motion despite its flatness. Not dramatic — no storms, no quakes — but multiple processes at work at biological pace: the weeping, the condensing, the scraping, the swarming, the hunting, the stacking and abandoning of small walls. Predator and prey play out across water films thinner than paper. The bacterial colors shift slightly as they are grazed and regrow. Clouds lower, condense directly onto the stone, lift a fraction, lower again.

If you stand still for a long time and let the cool soak into your knees, let your eyes adjust to the lack of shadow, you start to see that the place is not empty at all. It is not a desert. It is a crowded, wet, noisy, cold, ancient lowland that has had all its high places removed, all its green removed, and has filled the vacancy with other things — with water that comes from inside the rock, with animals that eat rock, with a sound that replaces weather, and with small, scattered human attempts to make a shape in it that will hold water a little longer than the stone wants to.
