It is not on any map because it does not scale to maps.

High above the dry world, where the air thins to a cool, colorless knife, there is a mountain that is still being born. Not a range — a single tooth. A shield pushed up too fast through the crust, so young its stone hasn't learned how to be soft yet.

The summit is a blade of black glass. Basalt cooled too quick, basalt shattered as it froze. From a distance it looks like a needle, but as you get close you see the needle is fluted, folded, cracked open along one side where the gas that lifted it burst out at the end. A vent, a throat, a pocket. A wound not much bigger than a cathedral nave, or a gymnasium, or the interior of a grain silo — you could walk its length in four minutes if there was anywhere to walk.

The wind at that elevation should scour it clean. It doesn't. The lip of the vent is too sharp, too high, and it catches the wind and throws it over. Inside, the air is still. Cool — not cold enough to kill at noon, cold enough that you never stop feeling it on your teeth. 4 degrees, 6 degrees, sometimes 2 when the shadow moves. The atmosphere is thin and utterly dry. No snow accumulates here. Any ice that forms by night is gone by habit before midday, not melted — sublimated straight into vapor and taken. Your lips chap in minutes. Metal left out contracts with a soft *tick*.

The stone inside is everything. Black to violet basalt, vesicular where the gas bubbled, glass-smooth where it sheared. It is sharp in a way old mountains forget how to be. Every edge will cut. The floor of the pocket is not a floor but a choke of fallen shards and hexagonal columns that broke off the walls, still lying where they landed, at the exact angle they fell. Dust sits between them that has never been rain-washed — fine, pale basalt flour and salt, anhydrite, a white dry crust that squeaks under boots and tastes of batteries. There is no soil. There is no plant. Not a lichen, not a green film. The dry and the cold and the fresh stone have not yet permitted it.

And yet it is crowded. Unreasonably, impossibly crowded.

Because the pocket has been built in.

Not built *on* — built *in*, like a wasp nest in a keyhole. From wall to wall, from rubble to lip, a dense, vertical town of industry has been wedged into the void. It does not sprawl; it has nowhere to sprawl. It stacks. Scaffolding of black iron and reclaimed pipe, bolted directly into the glass, platforms of grated steel, corrugated sheet, basalt brick cut from the fall itself. Ladders, lifts, chutes, catwalks that overlap and cross and double back until the air inside the pocket is more structure than emptiness. It is urban in the way a termite mound is urban — every cubic meter accounted for, every surface a wall or a floor or a ceiling for something else. Smoke-blackened workshops the size of closets vent into alleys the width of shoulders. Small forges, assembly frames, wire-drawers, kilns that fire without water — everything scaled for hands and tight spaces. It is industrial not in the monumental sense but in the relentless sense: belt drives turning, small electric motors humming off a bank of wind turbines perched like insects on the outer lip where the gale never stops, cables humming with low voltage.

And the light is always dim. For an hour after dawn and an hour before dusk, the sun cuts straight across the mouth of the pocket and lays a hard bar of white across the upper scaffolding. The rest of the day it is shadow lit only by that reflected glow and by the work-light within — amber bulbs strung on lines, the cherry-orange of a forge, the blue-white spitting of a welder, the dull red of heating coils. Everything is seen as if through smoked glass. Thin air makes the dimness feel cleaner, more distant.

You would think such a place would roar. It doesn't. Sound dies quickly in the dry pocket, soaked up by stone and cloth and close walls. The machines are small and well-tended and run on rubber bushings. The wind roars *over* the pocket but not *in* it. What you hear instead, if you stand still on a catwalk, is a low, mostly-quiet layering: the click-tick of cooling metal, the soft whine of a motor three levels down, the whisper of feet on grating, a cough. And beneath and through it all — fauna.

This is the strangest thing. The place should be lifeless and it is teeming.

They are not the animals of green places. Nothing here drinks. Nothing grazes. The abundant life is dry-life, stone-life, air-life. Clouds of soot-moths the size of your thumb, their wings the exact color of basalt dust, that feed on skin flakes and oil smoke and the airborne flour of rock. In turn, the walls are stitched with their silk. Rust-rats — not rats at all — sleek, hairless things with translucent skin and oversized lungs for the altitude, that gnaw the iron oxide off the scaffolding and nest in warm motor casings. Pale cave swifts that never go down the mountain, that live their whole lives inside the pocket, flickering between struts, snapping moths. And the keepers — long, many-jointed arthropods like centipedes made of brushed aluminum, whether wild or semi-domesticated no one can tell, that patrol the stone and eat the salt crust and in turn are harvested for their chitin. Everything is layered, near-touching. You cannot put your hand on a railing without disturbing three living things.

Nothing is stable. That is the rule of a young place at extreme height made to hold a city.

The turbulent dynamic is quiet but constant. Because nothing has settled. The stone still pings and cracks as it relieves stress — a shard falls every hour from a wall and someone clears it or builds on it. The town rebuilds itself daily. A platform is erected in the morning, overloaded with parts by noon, collapsed and re-braced by evening. The moths swarm and disperse and swarm again in new patterns. The turbines stutter as the jet shifts. The forges flare and dim with the power. It is hyperactive the way a bazaar or an anthill is hyperactive — not explosive, but ceaseless small motions, a thousand negotiations of space in a volume too small to hold them.

If you stay long enough, you learn the rules of this pocket world:

1. Nothing liquid stays. Water, if you bring it, must be kept sealed or it will be gone.
2. No surface stays empty. If you leave a ledge clear, within a day it will be nested, bolted, or grown over.
3. Noise is waste. Loud things do not last here. The quiet persists.
4. The mountain is still growing, a millimeter a year, and the town grows with it, upwards and inwards.

From outside, you would never know it was there — a shadow in the tooth, a slight soot stain on the lip, a faint, thin column of heat-shimmer that vanishes in the dry wind. A hidden room at the top of the world, cool and sharp and dim, dense with quiet industry and the dry rustle of wings in close, cold stone.
