It is below.

Not deep in the mythic sense, not kilometers down where pressure turns stone to plastic. Just below. Enough that the sky becomes a memory of an opening.

From the surface you wouldn't find it unless you knew where to look — the scrub plain is flat and cracked and hot, pale grass shivering in heat that never quite breaks. Then the ground simply gives out. A mouth, maybe thirty meters across, with a lip that is still sharp. This place is young.

The rim hasn't learned to be soft yet. The collapse happened recently — in the time of stone, last week. Basalt columns snapped cleanly when the ceiling fell, and they still show the fresh, glassy black of their break, unweathered, edges you could cut your hand on. The walls drop vertically for about twelve meters, then step inward in raw, angular terraces where the rock sheared. No river smoothed this. No wind has had time to round it. Water has only just started its work, finding the joints and learning them.

The air gets heavier as you descend. The heat doesn't drop with the shade the way it should. It thickens.

At the bottom, the world is a single room. Not a cavern system, not a network — one chamber, roughly the size of a small church or a grove that has been roofed over. You can see the far wall from anywhere inside it without straining. The floor is not flat; it is the tumbled ceiling, great blocks of basalt the size of trucks and houses lying where they fell, still fitted to each other in a way that suggests they remember being whole. Between them, condensation has made its own geography.

This is not a wet place. There is no lake, no stream. The rock sweats.

The heat comes from below — a low, patient geothermal breath that never stops. It warms the stone from within, and the stone exhales it into the trapped air until the chamber sits at a constant, bathhouse-hot temperature. Somewhere above, outside, a rare rain falls and sinks through the porous plain and meets that heat and returns as vapor. It beads on the cooler undersides of the blocks, gathers into drops fat as grapes, and falls. *Plink.* A long minute later, another *plink* somewhere else. Between the falls, the air is saturated but still. Damp in the way a towel left in a closed room is damp. Everything you touch leaves a print that darkens and then slowly fades.

And because of that damp heat, because of that stillness, it is green.

Not the green that should grow in darkness.

On the fallen blocks, in the narrow canyons between them, on the vertical north wall where seepage stains the rock blackest — life has taken hold with an urgency that feels almost improper. Ferns, but not as you know them. Their fronds are vast, pale, translucent, the color of bone and lime. They unfurl from cracks with a waxy thickness, each leaf big as a tabletop, beaded constantly with condensation that runs down their central spines and drips off the tips. Mosses make deep, spongy mattresses over the basalt, so saturated you could wring water from a fistful. Vines as thick as your wrist crawl across the floor, not reaching for light — there is almost none — but spreading laterally, hugging the warm stone, rooting wherever a film of grit has collected. The green is lush, undeniably lush, but it is sparse. It does not carpet. It stations itself. A great fern here, a sudden absence for five meters of bare, sweating black rock, then a cluster of pale stalks leaning together, then nothing again. The chamber breathes around its plants; there is much more stone than leaf, much more shadow than green, and that emptiness makes each patch feel like an individual, like a tenant.

There are no animals to tend it. No insects hum. No birds call. No lizard rustles the fronds. Even the soil, if you dig into the thin black mat under the moss, has no worm, no beetle. The ferns uncurl in perfect silence, produce spores that fall straight down and are never eaten. It is unnatural quiet, a greenhouse that has forgotten pollinators. The leaves are un-nibbled, perfect, slightly monstrous in their perfection.

Light is the rarest thing here. The mouth overhead is a hard white coin at noon and a bruise at dusk, but its light never reaches the floor properly. It catches the upper ledges and dies on the way down. The true illumination — if you can call it that — is industrial and scattered and old.

When this sink was first opened, someone put machines in it.

On the largest of the fallen blocks sits a pump house, corrugated tin gone the color of dried blood with rust. Its windows are gone, but inside a single amber safety bulb still burns, powered by a cable that snakes up the wall to the surface, thrumming faintly with current. The bulb doesn't flicker; it has burned, perhaps, for years, and it paints everything within ten meters the color of weak tea. Further off, near the seep wall, a rusted cistern on legs, half-collapsed, still collects the wall-sweat and funnels it through a pipe that ticks with condensation. A catwalk, never finished, juts out from the east wall and ends in empty air, its grating loud underfoot only because everything else is so silent. A small diesel generator sits under a tarp, not running, but warm to the touch — residual heat, or the rock's heat conducted through its iron — and its fuel drum sweats as much as the stone does.

None of it seems to be working toward anything anymore. No one is here. The machines are on, or warm, or idling down so slowly you cannot tell if they are alive or just cooling. The cistern drips once every twenty seconds into a puddle that never overflows because it evaporates at the same rate it fills. The pump house fan turns, maybe one revolution per minute, stirring the hot, wet air without cooling it. Everything is in a state of patient, electrified pause. Industrial, yes — there are gauges and rivets and ceramic insulators furred with green — but industrial as a ruin is industrial, scaled for production and then abandoned to run itself.

The sound of the place is almost nothing. Your own breath is loud. The generator's low electrical hum, if you press your ear to its housing, is more felt than heard — a 60-cycle throb in your teeth. Otherwise, drip, tick, the almost inaudible sigh of a fern frond shifting a millimeter toward warmth. The rock itself, being young and sharp, makes no sound as it settles; it does its settling on a timescale you would need to watch for a decade to perceive — a flake of basalt loosening, a new hairline crack sighing open to admit one more drop per hour.

The rules here are simple and slightly wrong.

Light does not mean growth. Heat does. The plants lean not toward the shaft of sun nor toward the amber bulb, but toward the warmest stone, the places where the floor is hottest under your palm. They are pale because they don't bother with much chlorophyll; they feed as much on heat and mineral seep as on light, something between plant and fungus. Water does not flow; it cycles in place, rising as steam, falling as sweat, never leaving the room. Time does not rush. Rust does not flake so much as bloom, slowly, like another kind of moss, creeping over the catwalk one orange crystal at a time.

If you stay long enough, your skin prickles with the same condensation that beads on the leaves. Your shirt clings. You become another damp surface in the room, another place for water to collect and fall.

And when you climb back up the rebar ladder bolted to the sharp new wall, out of the hot dark green silence and into the dry, bright, empty plain, the coolness of the upper air feels, for a moment, like a different kind of darkness.
