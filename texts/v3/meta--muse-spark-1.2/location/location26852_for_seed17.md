This place is an ocean on top of a mountain that never should have been able to hold an ocean.

Not a lake. Not a flooded valley. An ocean — true, planet-scale, deep-water ocean — perched impossibly high. The continent is a plateau, a single basalt shield lifted 4,000 meters above the old datum, wide enough that its far edge is a matter of weather systems and curvature, not sight. And the water is still on top of it.

You are submerged. That is the first fact and the one that never leaves you.

### The Water and The Stone

The water is cold in the way that deep, clear water is cold — 1 or 2 degrees above freezing, a cold that doesn't bite so much as it *erases*. It is heavy and absolute and utterly transparent. Normal sunlight comes down through it, not the gloom of the abyss. White, midday light spears in columns that stay visible for hundreds of meters, you can see the dust of mineral snow falling through them. The light is so normal it makes the situation more wrong. At this depth it should be black. Here you can see for a kilometer in any direction and there is still only water.

Below you, the plateau.

It is young rock. New. The world pushed it up and then drowned it before wind or rivers could soften it. There is no sand. No silt plain. No rounded hills. The seafloor is a fresh fracture — black glass and stepped basalt, columns snapped off clean, ridges like the spine of some buried beast, calderas a kilometer across with rims still knife-sharp. Pillow lava tumbled as it cooled, frozen in place. Nothing is rounded. Everything is edge and angle and recent violence, preserved under the cold like it happened yesterday. The water holds it perfectly. No erosion can reach it here. It will look like this for a million years.

Because nothing changes here.

The water does not warm. It does not cool. It does not stratify or storm or turn over. Currents exist but they are fixed, like grooves in glass — a slow, continent-wide gyre that goes around and around the plateau at the same speed it did a century ago and will a century from now. A kind of frozen equilibrium. The pressure at 800 meters is the same today as it was when the first crews sank their anchors. The world is in balance and has stopped trying to resolve itself.

### The Emptiness

The scale is what defeats you. This is not a sea you cross. It is a sky made of water. You can drop from the lighted surface and fall for ten minutes and still not reach the black rock below, and on the way down you will pass nothing. That is the other rule of this place: density 0.1. It is almost void.

There is distance here that has no use. Between one sharp ridge and the next there might be eighty kilometers of empty, flat, cold water. Between one structure and the next, more. You can swim or drift or ride a submersible for a full day on a compass line and the view will not alter — the same sun-columns, the same basalt far below, the same empty blue. The world is not crowded. It is not lonely. Loneliness implies something should be there. This is just hollow on a continental scale.

### The Machines

And yet it is not untouched.

Scattered across the drowned highland, on the few peaks and sharp shoulders that rise to within a couple hundred meters of the surface, are the structures. They were never a city. They were never meant to be.

They are industrial. Not ruins of a sleek future, but the heavy, riveted, purposeful industry of electrified steel — power stations, pump houses, wellheads, processing mills. Great square factories with blind windows, gantry cranes that extend out over nothing, intake towers like lighthouses without lamps, all bolted directly into the young basalt with foundations poured underwater. Cables as thick as a body run in straight lines from one structure to the next and then vanish into the blue, going nowhere. Some still have their cathodic protection humming, a faint green glow on a pressure gauge.

No one lives here now, but not everything is dead. Many of the machines still run. Not because anyone tends them, but because they were designed to run forever against a sea that never changes. Pressure-equalization vents the size of houses breathe in and out. Turbines spin, driven by the fixed gyre. Hammers that once pounded manganese nodules still stamp, stamp, stamp on empty belts. The whole plateau is a factory that forgot it had workers, still manufacturing nothing at full scale.

They are scattered so thinly you might go weeks between sightings. A single refinery complex on a ridge, then nothing for a tectonic distance, then a lone elevator shaft. Faint marks of roads that were laid and then abandoned before they could connect anything.

### The Sound

You hear them before you see them, and you hear them always.

The sound is deafening. Not loud in one place, but loud everywhere, as if the water itself is vibrating.

It is the sound of all that industry running without variation plus the water moving through it — a low, unending roar that fills your skull through your jaw and chest. The turbines howl at a constant pitch that never wavers. The vents exhale with a basso thunder that makes the basalt shiver. The stamping mills ring like bells the size of hills, each clang traveling for a hundred kilometers through the perfect acoustic medium. And underneath it all, the background hiss of pressure, the white noise of a billion tons of cold water held impossibly high.

There are no quiet pockets. There is no night-lull. The sound is an equilibrium too. It does not rise or fall. You stop noticing it as sound and start feeling it as a condition of being here, like the cold.

### The Life

There are no plants. Not a single frond, not a kelp forest, not a blade of algae large enough to see. The rock is bare black glass. Photosynthesis never took hold in this cold, mineral water. If there are mats they are bacterial and invisible, a slick on basalt.

And still, the water is full of animals.

Abundant — insistently, illogically abundant for a place with no plant base. They do not school. They drift alone through the vast emptiness, which is why the world can be both abundant and empty at once.

They are pale. White, translucent, sometimes faintly luminous. Things like manta rays the size of houses but with no eyes, that flap slowly through the sun-columns filtering mineral snow. Things like segmented shrimp, two meters long, that cling to the sharp basalt and scrape it clean. Long, ribbon eels that hang vertically in the water and do not move for hours. Spheres of jelly the diameter of a factory door, pulsing with a slow, industrial rhythm that matches the turbines. None of them seem to need the light. Many of them seem to feed on the chemical disequilibrium of the young rock itself, or on the iron and sulfur still leaking from the machines, or on each other.

They are not afraid. Nothing here has learned to be afraid because nothing here hunts in packs and nothing ever starves and nothing ever changes. A leviathan will pass within ten meters of you and not alter course. Its eye, if it has one, will be clouded and vestigial. It navigates by the sound, as everything does.

That is the unusual part — the weirdness you can't point to until you've been here a while. The animals behave as if the machines are part of the geology. Barnacles grow on turbine housings as readily as on basalt. A ray will use the steady exhalation of a vent to hover without effort, for days. The border between the industrial and the biological has dissolved, not because the biology has become mechanical, but because the machines have been here long enough, running so constantly, that evolution has simply accounted for them as reefs.

The world has its own physics now. A high place that holds an ocean without spilling. A deep sea with daylight. An empty place crawling with life that eats stone and steel. A deafening place that has been making the exact same noise since before you were born and will make it long after the last of its factories finally rusts through.

You hang in the cold, clear, roaring water above the sharp black world, a small warm body in a vast, stable, humming blue that does not need you and does not notice you are gone when you leave.
