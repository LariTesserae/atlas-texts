It is a high place.

Not a mountaintop, not a peak you climb to and then descend from, but a height that goes on. A plateau so vast it has lost any courtesy of being called a plateau — a continent lifted. The air is thin and clear and very cold, the kind of cold that makes stone feel like metal and metal feel like ice, the kind where breath does not fog so much as fall, in small crystals that glitter before they hit the ground. The sky is a normal daylit sky, pale and huge, a high blue that deepens toward violet at noon because there is less air to soften it. The sun is not dim, not red, not strange — it is white and direct, and it throws hard shadows off every edge.

And there are edges everywhere. This is young country. The uplift is recent in geologic time. The rock has not had time to be softened. Fault scarps run for a hundred kilometers like fresh cuts, straight and sheer. Basalt columns stand in colonnades where the floodplain cracked as it cooled. Ridges are sharp as broken teeth, arêtes knife-thin, scree slopes still angular and unsorted at the foot of every wall because the frost has only just begun to shatter them. Valleys are deep, U-shaped, but their bottoms have not yet filled with soft alluvium; the rivers that made them are still small for the scale of the land they cut.

The water is not absent, but it is not generous. This is a dry cold. Most of the plateau is bare, wind-scoured stone and permafrost, but anywhere the stone folds — in lee hollows, under overhangs, at the foot of north-facing walls — there is damp. Meltwater seeps for three weeks in late summer and then stops. Ice condenses in shadowed culverts and drips all winter. The city, because it is a city, has learned to find every pocket and hold it. Cisterns cut into the rock. Channels carved by hand, a finger's width deep, running for miles along contour lines to bring a thread of glacier milk into town. Every roof is slightly pitched to catch condensation, rimed in the morning with frost that is scraped into jars. There are no lakes, no broad rivers; there is damp stone, black with lichen where the drip runs, and then twenty meters away, dust.

Where water runs, something grows, but not much. Vegetation is sparse to the point of being countable. Cushion moss in the drip lines, grey-green and tight to the rock. Lichen, orange and black, mapped across the stone like nations. In a sheltered fissure, a few hardy grasses shiver. On the windward terraces where melt is guided, there are plots of barley, low and brittle, and root vegetables under frames of stretched hide that act as cold frames. No trees. Sometimes a stunted, wind-flagged juniper, no higher than a man's knee, has taken a century to grow that tall and has been pruned by every passing hand for fuel. The green is an accent, not a cover.

And yet the place is crowded.

The city follows the eastern scarp for as far as the eye can see and further — a dense, layered, continuous settlement built along and into the sharp edge itself. It is not a modern city. There is no steel, no glass, no engine. Everything is hand-cut. Dry-stone walls of dark basalt and lighter sandstone, fitted without mortar and then chinked with clay against the wind. Terraces cut directly into the cliff face, so that from a distance the scarp looks like steps for giants. Houses press shoulder to shoulder, walls shared, roofs becoming the terrace for the house above. Alleyways are stairways. Ladders and rope bridges span fissures where the fresh fault has dropped a block. Infrastructure is everywhere but it is primitive infrastructure: stone aqueducts no wider than a forearm, granaries half-sunk into the rock where the cold does the preservation, windbreak walls that run for kilometers, cairns and chortens marking safe paths over ice.

It has been built slowly, and it still is. No cranes. A block is quarried with stone hammers and bronze-tipped picks, hauled on a sledge by yaks or by ten people with ropes, set by hand. A wall rises a course a week. A neighborhood takes a generation. The whole conurbation has taken many generations and is still growing at its edges, fresh stone bright against the soot-darkened old. The density is not just human density — though that is real, thousands of doors opening onto the same stepped street — it is saturation. For every flat surface there is a use: hides stretched to dry, grain spread to winnow in the thin wind, pots set to catch frost, dung patties drying for fuel, prayer flags snapping. People, animals, materials, all layered and near-touching.

The animals are everywhere, more than the sparse plants would seem to allow, but they live on the city as much as on the land. This is their ecology now. Ravens and choughs in constant, lazy circulation above the scarp, hundreds at a time, riding the updraft. Pikas in colonies under every stone pile, whistling. Dogs, semi-feral and numerous, sleeping in warm alleys in tangled piles. Flocks of small, hardy sheep and long-haired yaks moving through the streets at dusk, guided by children, their breath steaming, their bells the loudest sound for a long time. White hares. Snowfinches packed so tight on a windbreak wall that it seems to move. And high, very high, the hunters — a lammergeier turning slow circles, a snow leopard's tracks sometimes found on the outer terraces in the morning, gone by midday.

It is, despite all this life, a quiet place. There are no motors, no loudspeakers, no roar of traffic. Sound is muffled by snow and stone and distance. What you hear is wind over sharp edges, a thin, reedy tone. The creak of a rope bridge. The distant clink of a hammer on stone, carried a long way in the cold air. Yak bells. A door closing. At night, when the wind drops, the silence becomes so complete you can hear the ice inside the rock tick as it contracts. People speak quietly here; the air does not carry shouting well.

The rules of the world are ordinary, physical rules, working at an unusual scale. Heat rises slowly. Ice moves slowly. Seasons turn slowly. The glacier that feeds the great channel moves perhaps a meter a year. A blizzard can build for three days over the interior void of the plateau — the vastness makes its own weather, clouds forming in the middle of the land where no sea could have sent them — and then break against the scarp and bury the lower terraces for a week, and then it is still again. A house stands for two hundred years. A family tends the same barley plot for ten generations, moving stones a little more each year to keep the frost from heaving them. Nothing is fast. Change accumulates the way snow accumulates: a crystal at a time until the roof must be cleared.

At midday the light is hard and every stone casts a clean, dark line. At evening the cold deepens and every damp pocket freezes again into lace. Smoke from a thousand dung fires lies low and does not quite rise, a blue haze in the stepped streets. And above and behind and below, the young, sharp country goes on, vast beyond habitability, mostly empty, holding this long, dense, patiently built human seam along its edge like lichen holding to damp rock.
