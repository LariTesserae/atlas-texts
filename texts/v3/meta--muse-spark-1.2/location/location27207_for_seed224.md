It is not on the way to anywhere.

High enough that the air has thinned to a kind of clear oil, cold enough that breathing feels like inhaling powdered glass. Far below, the world falls away in tiers of dark stone and dirty cloud, but you are not looking out. You are looking in.

Because up here, near the summit ridge where the wind ought to be trying to take you off the mountain, there is a pocket.

You find it not because you see it but because the wind stops.

One step, and the thin, steady hiss that has been your companion for hours simply ceases, as if a door has closed behind you. The light changes. The sound drops out of the world.

The pocket is small. That is the first wrong thing about it. Mountains do not have rooms.

It is about the size of a chapel, or a cistern, or a single house interior if you took off the roof. You could walk its length in twenty paces. You could touch both walls if you stretched your arms, though you don't want to. The scale is intimate, contained, hidden — a bubble pressed into the peak like a thumbprint in cold clay. The ridge continues on either side, the great weathered bulk of the mountain going about its business, but here the mountain has folded inward and made a place that is not quite inside and not quite outside.

The floor is not snow.

At this cold, at this height, there should be snow. There should be rime, glacier, verglas. There is none. The water is gone from this place — not frozen, not hidden, just absent as a rule. The air is desiccated to a degree that feels deliberate. Your lips crack within minutes. Your breath does not fog; there is nothing to fog with. The ground is bare, dark mineral, a basalt so old it has settled into itself. Erosion here has not carved or gutted; it has softened. All the edges are rounded, wind-worn over a time that is not weather but patience. The stone is pitted and smooth at once, covered in a fine, dry dust of salt and feldspar that has never seen rain. In the dim, it looks almost like ash.

It is dim. The sky directly above the pocket is open, but the light does not fall correctly. The sun — or what should be sun — is a pale, diffused smear far above, not bright enough to cast a shadow, just enough to give the basalt a faint luster. More light comes from below, which is impossible. A faint, even, sourceless amber-grey that seems to be emitted by the stone itself, or by the air just above it. It is the light of late dusk held permanently. You can see your hands, the texture of the rock, but not far. The far wall of the pocket is visible and then, if you stare, slightly not.

Cold lives here as a structure. Not a wind chill, not a weather front. A dry, absolute cold that has saturated the rock. It radiates up through the soles of your boots. At 0.3 on any scale you care to name, it is the cold that preserves, not the cold that kills quickly. Things left here do not rot. They wait.

And things have been left.

Built things, but sparse. Not a settlement, not even a ruin. Scatters.

Near the center where the floor dips slightly, there is a low cairn — not piled by hands, or not human hands. Three slabs of a darker stone, not basalt, leaning against each other to make a hollow tripod a meter high. They are too precise, their faces too flat. The surfaces are matte and swallow light. Frost should have fractured them long ago, but they are unweathered while everything around them is worn smooth. That is how you know they are the new things.

A few meters away, half-sunk into the floor as if the basalt grew around it, a ring. It is the main presence. It does not sit on the ground so much as it holds itself a hand's breadth above it, silent. About as wide as your outstretched arms. Its thickness is nothing — no, not thin, but *unthick*, like an edge-on idea. It is dark until you look at it obliquely, and then it is full of dim, slow light, like oil moving under ice. It makes no sound. Even crouching next to it, you hear only the faint, high ringing in your own ears. The ring is the source of the quiet.

There are other marks. A line of glyphs or circuit traces etched into the floor, too shallow to cast shadow, running from the ring to the far wall and then stopping mid-stone as if the wall interrupted them. A single, waist-high pillar of what looks like packed salt or glass, eroded at the top into a bowl that holds nothing, not even dust. The emptiness between these objects is the true scale. There is so much space between presences. The pocket feels mostly empty on purpose.

Nothing lives here. No lichen on the stone, no moss in the cracks, no bird cutting overhead. On the ridge outside, there might be hardy alpine life, but in here the category of "biology" has been politely declined. It is too dry for plants, too cold and too thin for animals, and even the microbes have apparently decided not to bother. It is a mineral world, post- or pre-life. Only shape and cold and distance.

And the shapes are wrong.

That is the second wrong thing, the larger one. The weirdness is not decoration. It is the rule.

First, distance. The pocket is small, but if you walk from the ring to the far wall, it takes longer on the way back. Not much. A breath longer. A step more. If you place a stone by the cairn and look at it from the ring, it appears slightly larger than it should. If you look away and look back, it has shifted a finger's width to the left.

Second, geometry. The far wall is not curved, exactly, but parallel lines drawn on it would meet. The corner where floor meets wall is sometimes more than ninety degrees, even when you measure it by eye. Your sense of vertical slips. You feel that you are standing on the floor, but also that the floor is a wall and you are hanging from it by your boots.

Third, time. Nothing moves fast here. The ring's internal light moves like cold honey, a slow turning that might complete a rotation in an hour or a day; you cannot tell because you cannot watch that long without forgetting what you were watching for. The dust does not settle when you disturb it. It hangs, then decides, particle by particle, to fall up or down. A gust that enters the pocket — rare — does not dissipate; it circulates slowly, once, twice, three times around the interior, as if feeling the space, before it is allowed to leave. Processes here are patient to the point of burial.

The tech, if that is what it is, does not announce itself. There are no lights, no hum, no screens. It is tech in the sense that it is *intended*, that it is holding this pocket apart from the mountain by means you do not have physics for. The ring is not a machine with parts. It is a condition that is being maintained. An anchor. A suture. It feels less like it was built and more like it was persuaded to exist here, and continues to exist out of stubbornness. The stone around it has weathered for an age. The ring has not weathered at all. That is not durability; that is a disagreement about what time is.

When you stand in the center and listen — truly listen, in the profound silence that has a weight of 0.2 — you hear the only sound the place permits: not the wind outside, which cannot enter, but a faint, intermittent tick. Not mechanical. More like the contraction of stone in the cold, or the click of a cooling joint. Except it comes from the air above the ring, at irregular intervals of many seconds, soft as a knuckle popping. Each tick coincides with a barely perceptible flex in the light, as if the pocket inhales.

If you stay long enough, you realize the air is not still. Your exhalation, dry as it is, does not disperse. It thins into a faint, short-lived vapor that hangs in front of you and then shears sideways, drawn toward the ring as if the ring is drinking the tiny amount of moisture you brought with you. It does not want water here.

The rules of this landscape are simple and total:

1. Nothing wet may remain.
2. Nothing living may propagate.
3. Nothing may be far from anything else for long.
4. The mountain is old, but this room is older, even though it was made.

You leave by stepping back out through the place where the wind stops. The transition is a soft pressure change behind your ears. Behind you, the pocket is there and not there — visible as a slight concavity in the ridge if you know to look, invisible if you don't. From the outside, it looks like a shallow, wind-scoured hollow with a few dark stones. Nothing special. Easy to miss.

The cold comes back with the wind. The light becomes normal, harsh and thin at altitude. Sound returns, a quiet scouring hiss. But for a few minutes after, your paces still take that extra half-step, and the ticking stays with you, just behind your hearing, slow as geology, patient as the thing that is holding a small, empty, impossible room open on the top of the world.
