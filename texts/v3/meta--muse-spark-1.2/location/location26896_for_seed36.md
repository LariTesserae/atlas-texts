It is not a valley. It is a subtraction.

You come to it without warning because the land around it is flat and dry and ordinary — a pale, sun-baked plain of cracked lime and thin grass that goes to the horizon. And then the ground simply ends. A lip, sharp and clean as if it was cut yesterday, and beyond that, a drop.

The Sink is below surface, but not a cave. It is open to the sky. A vast, shallow bowl perhaps ten kilometers across, a kilometer deep, with walls that are still almost vertical. You can see the whole of it from any point on the rim, and at the same time you cannot see all of it at once because of what is inside.

The light is the first thing that is wrong. At the bottom, it should be dim, shadowed. It is not. The sun falls straight down into the bowl at noon and the pale stone of the walls reflects and throws it back and back until the bottom is brighter than the plain above. The light is white and total and hot, without diffusion. It has nowhere to hide. It flattens shadows into thin blue lines directly underneath everything and makes the air itself look white.

The heat comes with the light and stays. It is a wet heat, though there is no water to be seen. The Sink is a trap for hot air. It pools at the bottom and does not circulate. Breathing it is like breathing through a damp cloth that has been left in the sun. Your skin prickles instantly with sweat that does not evaporate. The stone of the rim burns to touch.

And yet the bowl is not dry. It is damp in a way that has nothing to do with rivers or lakes.

There is no standing water. No stream, no pond. The plain above is parched. But inside the Sink, every surface beads. In the morning, before the sun is directly overhead, the walls weep with condensation a foot thick, rivers of dew that form from the trapped humid air and run down the raw rock in slow, dark streaks, vanishing before they can pool. In the heat of the day the damp retreats into the green. Leaves exhale it. The underside of every frond drips once every few minutes, a single heavy drop that falls into the moss below and is instantly drunk again. The ground is not wet, but if you press your palm to it, your hand comes away cool and dark. It is a place of interstitial water — always present, never abundant, held in tissue and in air.

Because the vegetation is not on the land. It *is* the land.

From the rim it looks like the Sink has been filled to the brim with a single, dense, living mass. There is no soil visible. No rock on the floor. What fills the depression is plant, layer upon layer upon layer, so thick and so interwoven that it has become substrate. The canopy at the top is not canopy as you know it. It is a roof of broad, waxy, pale-green plates the size of doors, overlapping like shingles, angled to catch the brutal light. They do not rustle. Under them is a second layer of vine and trunk, but the trunks are not vertical — they grow sideways, horizontally, bracing against each other, forming arches and tunnels and chambers. Under that is a third layer, and a fourth, of ferns with trunks as thick as houses, of roots that hang in the air and never seek soil, of air-plants growing on air-plants. At the very bottom, where the rock should be, there is only a springy, deep mat of moss and liverwort meters thick, that holds your weight like a mattress.

It is dense to the point of architecture. You can walk *through* the green, not just under it. The plants have made hallways, vaulted naves, blind courtyards where their leaves have fused into walls. The open scale of the Sink — you can see the far wall a long way off — is contradicted by the intimacy of its interior, where at any moment you are touching vegetation on three sides. Light filters down through the plate-leaves in hard, bright shafts that make dust motes visible, except there is no dust.

And the strangest thing, after a few hours, is that you realize nothing is happening.

This is raw geology. The walls of the Sink are new. The rock is unweathered, razor-edged, without the softening of rain or wind. The edges of the rim are still sharp enough to cut. Fallen blocks at the foot of the cliffs lie where they fell, their fracture faces clean and bright, not yet dulled or lichen-spotted. It is a place that has just come into being, pre-erosion, as if the earth cracked open last week.

And yet it is finished. It is in equilibrium. Nothing falls. Nothing grows. Nothing decays.

That is the rule of the place. You watch a leaf with a bead of water at its tip for a long time and the bead never falls. You watch a flower that must be about to open and it does not. The green is utterly alive — the color is so saturated it hurts — but it is not *growing*. It holds its form perfectly, frozen in a moment of total abundance. If a leaf tears, it does not heal and does not brown. It stays torn. If you push a vine aside to pass, it stays pushed. Slowly, very slowly, it might drift back over decades. The damp never pools, the heat never breaks. The bright light never moves across the sky quite the way it should; the shadows shorten and then stay short.

Because of that, sound is almost absent. The density of the plant mass swallows everything. There is no wind inside the bowl — the hot air is stagnant. There is no creak of growth, no insects humming. The green is mostly quiet, with a quiet that feels pressed. What sound there is is occasional and small: the *tap* of a condensation drop falling three layers down, the soft sigh of your own foot sinking into moss, once in a long while the papery crack of a plate-leaf adjusting to the heat. You hear your own blood.

Fauna is rare to the point of absence. There are no flocks, no herds. You may go a day and see only one living animal — a pale beetle the size of your thumb, moving with impossible slowness across a giant frond, its shell filmed with moisture. Or a thin white lizard pressed flat to a trunk, not moving at all, breathing once a minute. They are there, but they are sparse and still, as if they too have agreed to the stasis. They do not call to each other.

Scattered through this are the built things, though you almost miss them because the green has taken them as part of its structure.

They are not a city. No roads, no machines, no wires — there is no tech here at all, not even the memory of it. What there is are small, low remnants: a terrace wall of stacked, unmortared stone now completely braided through with roots that hold it tighter than mortar ever did; a single hut of sun-baked brick with no roof, whose doorway is now a perfect arch of living vine; a set of shallow steps cut into the lower part of the cliff, ending in mid-air where the plant mat has risen to meet them; a cistern, dry, filled with a cushion of moss. They are pre-tool in their simplicity. They look not ruined but *absorbed*, as if people came here briefly when the Sink was new, tried to live in the bright hot damp, and left, or were gently taken in.

You can stay in the green tunnels for a long time and feel no passage of time at all. The light remains bright. The air remains hot and damp on your skin. The plants do not close around you, nor do they recede. The place is unusual not because it is fantastical, but because it holds contradictions without resolving them: a below-ground place that is blasted with sun; a raw, newborn geology that will never weather; a jungle so dense it is a solid, so vast you can see across it, and so silent you can hear a single drop form. It does not need water because it *is* water, held in leaf. It does not need animals because the plant has done all the work of being alive.

At night — if you stay on the rim and look down — the Sink does not get dark. The heat of the day radiates back from the stone and from the leaves, and the whole bowl glows faintly with trapped light, pale green and white, like a lamp left on under a blanket of leaves. And it is still. Entirely, perfectly still.
