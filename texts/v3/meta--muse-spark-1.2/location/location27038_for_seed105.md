It is not an ocean. Not anymore, or not yet. It is a sea that has climbed a mountain and stayed there.

High up, where the air should be thin and sharp, there is instead weight. A cold, dark, perched ocean sitting in the bowl of a highland. The ridges that hold it are not coastlines - they are true mountains, their upper shoulders still breaking the surface as black, wind-scoured islands, but everything below a certain line is drowned. You are well above where any sea has a right to be; the pressure in your ears and chest tells you you are submerged, the ache in your lungs tells you you are at elevation. Both are true.

The water itself is the first fact and the last. It is not clear tropical water. It is cold — a cold that is close to freezing but never quite freezes, a cold that makes the water thick, viscous, almost glassy. It holds you. It presses on all sides with a quiet, absolute force. From the surface, which is a flat, black mirror far above you, almost no light comes down. What light there is filters as a deep, bruised indigo, a perpetual late dusk that never becomes day or night. At thirty meters down it is already twilight; at a hundred, it is starless dark except for what the place makes for itself.

And still, it is lush.

Without animals to eat it, to thin it, the plant life has become the architecture.

Forests of kelp and kelp-like things rise from the drowned highland floor. Not the familiar green-brown kelp of coasts, but a cold-water flora adapted to darkness. Their stipes are as thick as old tree trunks, pale and smooth as birch bark, rooted not in mud but directly into basalt and schist. They rise a hundred feet, two hundred, swaying with a slow, terrible grace. Their fronds are huge, black-green, translucent at the edges where the faint light catches them. They do not float with bladders; they hang, suspended, as if gravity is lighter here. Between the towers are meadows of a shorter, hair-like weed that carpets the stone in a dense, shivering fur, and fields of sheet-algae that spread like oil slicks, rippling with their own faint, cold luminescence. The plants make their own light - not bright, not warm - a low phosphor of greens and sullen whites that outlines each frond, each drifting spore cloud. In the darkness, the whole valley glows very faintly, like a forest at night seen from a great distance.

It is sparse, for all its lushness. That is the strange balance. The vegetation does not choke the water. Each great kelp-tower stands apart from the next, with a hundred yards of empty, cold, dark water between. You can see from one to the next only as a shadow, then a lit shape, then a shadow again. The water between is profoundly empty. No fish flicker through it. No crustacean scuttles. No eye watches. The absence is total and active. There is nothing with a face, nothing with a fin or a foot or a mouth. Only plant, stone, and water. The spores drift in clouds so thick they look like snow, but they are not eaten. They settle, they decay, they become silt. The forest grows, sheds, regrows, all without a single grazer.

The terrain underneath is old and settled. This is not a raw, catastrophic flood. The stone has lived with water for a very long time. The high valleys between the basalt shoulders are classic U-shapes, carved by glaciers and then softened by millennia of submerged current. The ridges are smooth, weathered, their edges rounded as if rubbed by giant hands. Scree slopes have settled into stable fans. Terraces have been cut into the hillsides - not natural, but so old they look it. Low walls of dry-stacked slate and basalt, now covered in that fur-like weed, contour the slopes in long, patient lines. Someone farmed here when it was air.

The built things are scattered and simple. On a broad, flat spur that would have been a fine summer pasture, there are the remains of a hamlet. Perhaps six structures. Dry-stone, roofless now, the wooden beams long since swollen, blackened, and preserved in the cold like bog wood. Doorways still stand as rectangles of darker water. Inside one, a stone hearth, a low sleeping platform, a niche where a lamp once sat. On another terrace, a single watchtower or granary, circular, its stones fitted without mortar, half-collapsed on its downhill side. Between them, faint marks: cairns that were trail markers, now just piles of stone with fronds growing through them; a line of post-holes where a fence or drying rack stood. No metal. If there ever was metal, it is rusted to nothing or was never there at all. All hand-work, all stone and wood and hand-span. Primitive, but deliberate. The people who made them understood cold and height and scarcity.

They are gone. Whether they left before the water came, or as it rose, or drowned with it, the place does not say. The water is too cold to rot, so it preserves.

The sound is what you notice after the light and the cold. For a dead place it is very loud.

There are no animal calls to fill it, so you hear the world itself moving. The water is not still. Cold currents pour over the ridges from the higher dark above, spill down the terraced walls, move through the kelp forests with a low, continuous roar that you feel in your ribs. The great stipes creak and groan like old masts, rubbing against each other when the surge comes. Bubbles - not from lungs, from the plants themselves, from gases trapped under the weed-mat - release in sudden, startling chains that chime and clatter as they rise. And deeper, less often, the stone itself sounds. The mountain, under the weight of all this cold water, ticks and booms. A low percussive *thud* that travels for miles through the water column, as if a distant door has closed.

Weirdness lives in how the water holds together. In places, especially near the old terraces, the water does not behave quite like water. It layers. You will swim through a band of water that is suddenly clearer and colder, and where dust motes - spores, silt - hang utterly motionless for minutes, as if suspended in glass, before suddenly shearing sideways as a current takes them. In the lee of the tower, kelp fronds sometimes hang at an impossible angle, not drifting, but fixed, as if the water there is briefly solid. Light from the plants does not always go straight; it pools and bends around certain stones. Time seems to thicken with the cold. The phosphor glow brightens slightly when you are not looking directly at it.

The rule of this place is simple: growth without consumption. Light without sun. Weight without depth. It is a high, cold, dark greenhouse under an ocean that forgot to leave. Everything that should be eaten is instead preserved. Everything that should be silent roars with abiotic noise. You move through it slowly, because the water is heavy and cold on your skin, because the forests are spaced so far apart that you must cross great voids of nothing to get from one lit tower to the next, because there is no hurry and nothing to startle.

It extends beyond what you can see. The valley turns, the ridges continue, another drowned hamlet on another spur, another kelp forest swaying in the dark. It has been like this for a long time, and will be for longer.
