On the high peneplain, there is almost nothing left to erode.

This was a mountain range once. You can tell by the way the light lies. Not by peaks - there are no peaks anymore. The whole country was lifted a long time ago, a continent's worth of sandstone and basalt pushed up into the warm air, and then left alone for so long that weather gave up trying to carve it and instead just smoothed it. The horizon is level to inhuman precision. The stone is rounded everywhere, like old bone. Ridges that were once razorbacks are now broad, whale-backed swells you could walk over without noticing you’d climbed anything, except your breath gets a little thinner and the sky gets a little closer and more brutal.

The plain is the color of rust and old paper. The rock itself is porous, pitted, sandblasted to a soft suede. In the shallow pans where soil has collected over epochs, there is a thin, hardy scrub - silver-green cushions and wiry grasses that grow far apart from each other, respecting the dryness. The wind up here never stops. It’s a warm wind, dry as breath, that has had all its moisture wrung out long before it got this high. It carries dust and the smell of hot stone.

And then, without any warning, the ground falls away at your feet.

Not a canyon. Not a valley. A pocket.

About as wide across as a house is long, maybe a little more - you could throw a stone from one rim to the other if you threw well. Perfectly hidden until you are on its lip, because the ancient erosion has beveled its edges into the same gentle swell as everything else. A collapsed bubble. A pothole made by a god's thumb pressed into soft stone when the world was still soft, and then left to weather for a hundred million years until its walls became smooth, inward-leaning bowls of dark, water-stained rock.

The air changes the instant you step over the rim.

Down inside, the wind stops.

### The Bowl

The temperature drops only in the sense that it becomes *wet* heat. The high plain is dry-warm, like an oven. The Bowl is warm-warm, like a mouth, like a greenhouse, like being inside something living. The stone walls, thirty feet high and undercut with age, sweat constantly. Not from a spring, not exactly. There is no river here. There is no lake. The Bowl makes its own water.

In the cool of the high night, the great stone exhales and condensation beads on every surface. By dawn, every curve of rock is slick with dew. It gathers and runs in dark, shining threads down the ancient chutes and runnels cut into the walls - not by rivers, but by a million years of trickle. It collects in the bottom, not as a pool but as a dampness. The floor of the Bowl is not stone. It is a mat.

A mat of vegetation so lush it seems obscene against the sparse plain above. But not a jungle. That’s the strange part. Nothing here crowds.

There are maybe a dozen true plants in the Bowl, and each one is allowed its distance. A tree, for instance - one tree, dead center, not tall but immensely broad, with a trunk like braided, pale-gray rope and leaves the size of dinner plates, waxy and dark green, that angle themselves all day to track the sun. Around it, at respectful intervals, are its companions: a cluster of ferns that grow as big as a man, each frond unfurling from a black, damp hummock; a patch of moss so deep and viridian it looks like poured velvet, humming with moisture; three or four trunkless palms that hold their fronds out like open hands to catch the drip.

Between them is bare, damp, black earth and stone, perfectly tended negative space. Lush, but sparse. Every plant has room to be fully itself.

And the structures are the same way.

You don’t notice them at first, because they aren’t buildings the way we build. Scattered - that’s the word - four or five presences, no more, each set apart near the wall like a respectful observer.

One is a low arch of something that is not stone and not metal, matte white, half-buried in the moss. It does nothing, or nothing visible, but the air immediately around it shivers slightly, like heat off a road, and the condensation on the rock behind it never quite falls - droplets hang suspended, trembling, before sliding sideways and vanishing.

Another is a lattice of black filament, thin as spider silk but rigid, strung between two boulders at chest height. It thrums. If you put your hand near it your teeth ache.

The third is the most obvious and the least understandable: a lens, or a dish, or a mirror - a perfect circle of dark glass three feet across, set into the wall itself, facing inward toward the tree. It is perfectly clean in a place where everything else is damp and faintly algal. It does not reflect. Looking into it you see not yourself but the Bowl from above, as if from a great height, and sometimes, at noon, a second sun passes across its surface that is not in our sky.

No doors. No ruins in the conventional sense. No inscription. Whoever made them built for maintenance, not habitation. They are still working. The Bowl is warm when the plain at night is cold enough to frost. The Bowl is damp when the plain has not seen rain in months. Someone, or something, decided this pocket should stay exactly like this and engineered physics until it obeyed.

### The Inhabitants

That is why the animals are here, and why you hear the Bowl long before you see it.

From the rim on the silent plain, it sounds like a machine left running. A low, incredibly loud, layered drone that rises out of the earth. It is not wind. It is wings.

The fauna is overwhelming not because there are large predators, but because the air itself is thick with life. You have to push through it.

Thousands - tens of thousands - of things like dragonflies but the size of your hand, with bodies of lacquered amber and wings that are not membranes but overlapping glassy feathers, moving with a dry rattle. They patrol the Bowl in ceaseless circuits, grazing on the microscopic algae that blooms on the damp walls, snapping at each other, mating in mid-air in brief, furious spirals. When they pass near the white arch, their flight stutters and they skip a few inches forward instantaneously, as if the space there is folded.

Among them, larger gliders - pale, membranous, almost transparent - launch themselves from the tree’s high leaves and ride the warm, rising, wet air inside the Bowl, never needing to flap, mouths open to filter the dragonfly-things. They are silent, but their shadows flicker constantly over the moss.

On the ground, things that look like pill bugs the size of a loaf of bread move with ponderous slowness through the black earth, turning it over. Their backs are encrusted with the same moss that grows on the rocks, a moving garden. And in the dampest hollow under the ferns, something sings - a creature you never see, with a voice like a struck gong and a wet reed, repeating a three-note phrase so loudly it vibrates your sternum. Another answers it from the opposite wall, then another.

Nothing is still. The ferns slowly track the sun, the condensation constantly forms and falls, the lattice thrums and changes pitch with the heat, the insects wheel and the gliders circle and the gong-singer sings and is hunted by something that moves through the moss like a ripple you can barely see.

It’s an active place, a fast place, despite being ancient. The stone is at the end of its life, tired and smooth, but the life it holds in its palm is frantic, profligate, loud.

The rules here are simple and slightly wrong.

Gravity is a little lighter inside the rim than outside - you feel it in your knees when you step down, a faint springiness, as if the Bowl is cradling you.

Light is normal, clean high-altitude sunlight that pours straight down at midday and turns the wet walls to gold, except near the black glass circle, where shadows fall *toward* the light for a few inches before correcting themselves.

And water, the scarcest thing on the high plain, here is not a resource to be fought over. It is atmosphere. It is the air precipitating itself, the stone weeping, the leaves exhaling, the lattice perhaps pulling it straight out of the thin sky and wringing it into the Bowl like a cloth. You can lick it off your arm. You can watch a droplet form on the white arch, hang for an hour defying gravity, then shoot sideways into the moss where a dozen glass-winged things immediately descend to drink.

If you sit on the moss - and there is plenty of empty moss to sit on, between the ferns - the sound becomes so loud it stops being noise and becomes a pressure. The air smells of iron, green sap, hot rock, and ozone. The tree drops a huge leaf, slowly, spiraling, and before it lands it is already being eaten in mid-air.

You understand after a few minutes why the plain is empty and the Bowl is full. The plain is the world as it ended up after everything eroded away - vast, stable, final, beautiful in its exhaustion. The Bowl is a place that someone refused to let finish. A pocket sewn shut against time, kept warm and wet and loud, with its handful of plants given room to be perfect and its impossible abundance of animals given a place to be deafening, all held together by a few quiet, inexplicable machines that have been humming longer than any mountain has been a mountain.

You don’t stay long. The air is too rich, the sound too close. You climb back up over the beveled lip, and the wind hits you again, dry and thin, and the drone cuts off as if a door has closed. Behind you, there is only the flat, ancient plain again, stretching to the curved horizon. If you didn’t know exactly where to look, you would never find the crack in the world again.

But at night, from your camp on the wind-scoured stone, you can still hear it faintly thrumming - that hidden, warm, dripping room full of wings, singing to itself in the high, dry dark.
