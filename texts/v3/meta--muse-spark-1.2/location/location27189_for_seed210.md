It is a bowl high up.

Not a valley - too small and too complete for that word. A cirque, a room scooped out of the ridge at an altitude where the air itself feels thin and blue. You could walk its circumference in twenty minutes if there were any way to walk it, but there isn't. Its rim is a perfect, unweathered crown of black basalt, sharp as broken teeth, too young to have been softened. The rock was born yesterday, geologically speaking — it still has the sheen of fresh cooling, the knife-edges where it fractured and was lifted. No lichen has had time to dull it, no wind has rounded it, and yet everything inside it is impossibly old and lush.

The light never gets stronger than this. A late dusk that isn't late, isn't dusk. The sky above the rim is a deep, high-altitude indigo, not cloudy and not clear — it is simply dim, as if the sun is perpetually behind a thickness of atmosphere that never moves. There are no shadows that travel. The light is even and cool and diffuse, coming from everywhere and nowhere, enough to see the gloss on every leaf but never enough to warm your skin. The air is cold in the way spring water is cold: clean, biting at the lungs, tasting of stone.

And it weeps.

Every surface inside the bowl weeps water. Not rain — it hasn't rained, it doesn't need to rain. The atmosphere itself wrings itself out against the cold. The basalt walls sweat continuously, a dark varnish of water beading and joining and sheeting down the fresh-cut faces in slow, silent curtains. The floor of the bowl is not a lake and not quite land. It's a thin, saturated mat — sphagnum and liverwort and ferns and a grass that is too green, too broad-leafed for this elevation — all growing on what feels like a soaked sponge of peat and shattered rock. Your foot would sink to the ankle if you stepped, and water would well up around it, clear and shockingly cold. Little rills braid and unbraid without ever cutting a true channel, because nothing here is allowed to erode. The water is always on the surface, always near the surface, condensing on the underside of leaves and falling again six inches.

There are no structures. No cairn, no wall, no path, no cut. Nothing has been placed. And yet it is industrial.

That is the wrongness that takes a moment to resolve.

At first you think the sound is water — the gurgle and hiss of the marsh. Then you understand it is too regular, too loud, too metallic to be water alone. The bowl is a factory that was never built.

The basalt itself is the machine.

Veins of raw iron and something darker run through the fresh rock of the rim, and they move. Slowly, with immense torque, without ever advancing. A slab of wall the size of a house shudders and withdraws an inch with a scream of stone-on-stone, exposing a seam of white steam that vents laterally and instantly condenses on the cold air, raining back down as warm drizzle. A natural piston of columnar basalt, three trunks thick, rises from the bog and sinks again, slick with moss that never tears. Gears — not made, grown — mesh in the cliffside: interlocking basalt teeth twenty feet across turning against each other with a low grinding that you feel in your molars. They do not grind down. They do not wear. Sparks that are not sparks, cold blue light, shear off them and vanish.

There is no fuel, no fire, no chimney. The work is not being done *for* anything. The mountain is simply running through its cycles the way a heart beats, electrified, mechanized, huge and pointless and loud. Thick black cables like roots, like veins, like insulated power lines, but warm and faintly pulsing, burst from the peat and loop over the ferns before diving back into the rock, humming with current. They sweat water, too.

In the dimness, the place should be barren at this height. Instead it is choked with life, and scattered.

The vegetation is lush to the point of excess — not alpine scrub but jungle that has mistaken its altitude. Tree ferns unfurl heads the size of cartwheels, their trunks furred with moss. Something like giant rhubarb spreads leaves as broad as doors, cupping pools of condensed water that reflect the dim sky. Every surface not actively grinding as a gear is upholstered in moss so deep and so wet it looks like animal fur. But it is sparse — there is so much space between presences. One perfect tree fern stands alone in a wide expanse of glittering peat. Ten feet of nothing but wet light. Then a thicket of three. Then nothing again. The bowl is not crowded; it is curated, as if each plant was placed far enough apart to be seen clearly. You can see all the way across the small place to the other wall without your view ever being blocked, and still you feel lost in green.

And the fauna — it is abundant without being dense. You hear them before you see them, because the industrial grinding never stops being loud.

There are midges in a cloud so thick it dims the dim light further, each one hanging perfectly still in the cold air, wings buzzing at a pitch that harmonizes with the 60-cycle hum of the cables. They do not disperse. They do not settle. They just hang.

On the peat, things move: a white hare far too large, its fur dripping, chewing methodically on fern with a jaw that clicks like a relay. A flock of small birds — finches? — but their beaks are too long and needle-bright, and they perch on the humming cables without being electrocuted, opening and closing their mouths in time with the hiss of steam, not singing but venting. Beetles the size of your fist, their wing cases like enameled factory housing, riveted, crawl over the moss and disappear into seams in the rock that open to admit them and close again.

Nothing eats anything else here. Or perhaps it does, but it is frozen in the attempt. A heron-like thing stands one-legged in a rill, neck cocked at an impossible angle, beak open, perfectly still, water beading on its head and never falling. Its eye tracks you though it does not move.

Because this is the final rule of the place, the one that makes the weirdness cohere:

Nothing changes.

The water is always condensing and always falling and never accumulates. The gears turn and never advance. The steam vents and never dissipates. The hare chews and never swallows. The sound — that great, loud chorus of grinding stone, hissing steam, electric hum, insect drone, water trickle — is not a crescendo or a rhythm. It is a single sustained chord, has been that chord, will be that chord. If you watch the bead of water on the basalt face, it will tremble and stretch and hang, trembling, stretching, hanging, for as long as you watch, never quite falling.

Time has not stopped. It has reached equilibrium with itself. The bowl is an engine running at perfect efficiency at high altitude, in the cold, in the wet, in the dim, endlessly performing the exact amount of work needed to remain exactly as it is. Young rock that will never age. Lush life that will never grow or die. A factory with no product, no builders, no workers, only itself — a small, complete, impossible room where the world forgot how to proceed and decided instead to simply, loudly, beautifully, persist.
