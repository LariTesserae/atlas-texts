It is below.

Not deep in the planetary sense, not pressure and mantle and crushing dark. Just below - one level down from the surface world. The kind of below you reach by going down a flight of stairs that keeps going after the air changes.

The place is a bowl. A single, contained hollow, hamlet-sized, with edges you can always see if you turn around. You could walk its circumference in twenty minutes if you didn't have to climb over everything. It was not a cave. It was made. A long time ago someone cut a great rectangular pit into the earth, maybe a quarry, maybe a cistern, maybe the foundation for something that was never finished. The walls are still sheer in places - poured concrete and cut limestone gone soft and pitted with age, rebar showing like rusted bone where the concrete has spalled. But the cut has settled. Nothing is collapsing anymore. The edges have weathered into themselves, chamfered by a century of condensation and root. It is established, in-balance, the way an old garden wall is - leaning, but it will lean like that forever.

The light does not really reach here. High above, the mouth of the bowl is a ragged oval of daylight, half-choked with growth, so the sun comes down as a diffuse, columnated haze. By the time it hits the floor it is dim and green-filtered, like light through river water. That is not enough to work by, so there are work lights - industrial amber and sodium-white strung on catenary wires between the walls, humming on iron poles, some swinging gently, some buzzing with moths. They never go out. In the dimness, everything has a double shadow.

It is warm. Not hot. The perfect, unwelcome warmth of a greenhouse in late summer, or the air behind a running engine. The earth holds heat. The machines hold heat. The vegetation holds heat. The air is damp but not wet - this is not a swamp or a drowned place. Water here is intermittent and secret. It beads. It condenses on every cool pipe and concrete face and drips on its own slow metronome. It gathers in pockets: a shallow rill that cuts down one wall only when it rains up top, a black cistern in the lowest corner slick with algae, the perpetual sweat on the underside of leaves as big as doors. After a rain on the surface, the bowl weeps for two days and then goes back to being merely humid, the air you can taste on your tongue.

And everything that is not air or rust or concrete is plant.

Not planted. Not covering. *Being.* The vegetation is total - substrate and architecture at once. The bowl is filled with it the way a cathedral is filled with air. Giant aerial roots descend from the rim like pillars, as thick as a man, as thick as a house, braiding and fusing as they fall. They hit the floor and become the floor. Leaf mats layer so thick you cannot tell where one tree ends - figs and philodendrons and something that is neither, engineered long ago and now feral, with leaves waxy and veined with light. Vines as thick as industrial hose have learned the shape of infrastructure and now wear it; they follow the catwalks, they lace the girders, they use the factory frames as trellises. The buildings themselves - and there are buildings, this is a town - are not separate from the growth. They are armatures inside it.

Because this is a town. A small, organized, once-planned industrial settlement that was built to fit exactly inside this pit and then was swallowed without being erased.

You can still read the urban logic: a grid of streets, now soft canals of exposed root and packed humus. Two-story workshops of brick and corrugated steel, their windows blown out and replaced with hanging curtains of moss. A central mill - the heart - three stories high with a sawtooth roof that once let in light and now holds soil on its panes, where things grow upside down. The infrastructure is still there and still scaled for production. The electrification never stopped. You can hear it.

This was industrial and still is, but no one is industrializing anymore. The machines run for different reasons.

Along the western wall, the factory line still moves. Big, slow, belt-driven engines - pistons the size of bathtubs, flywheels streaked with grease and green - turn endlessly, not because they are making anything, but because the vines turn them. Or they turn the vines. It's hard to tell. A system of shafts and belts that once drove lathes now drives the gentle opening and closing of stomata-leaves big enough to wrap a person. Condensers clank and hiss, drawing the damp air in and sweating clean water into troughs. Generators thrum behind cages of strangler fig. Every surface is pitted, scaled, weathered, painted and repainted and then grown over. The metal is warm to the touch from friction and sun-heat stored in the earth.

It is sparse, for all its fullness. For a town, there are almost no people. Or rather, there is so much space *between* presences. You can stand on the old main street and see all the way to the far wall with nothing between but air, hanging roots, and a few widely spaced, lit workshops where someone has left a kettle on. The plant mass is total, but it is not dense like a jungle. It is open, vaulted, like the nave of a living church. The town is too big for the few humans who tend it, so it feels tended only incidentally. Tools left on a bench will be there three days later, undisturbed except for a fine dusting of spores.

What is not sparse at all is everything else that lives.

The fauna is overwhelming - not as a mass on the ground but as a saturation of the air itself. This is their city now. Clouds of insects move like weather between the root-pillars, midges and iridescents and beetles with lacquered backs that click against the hot bulbs. Bats the size of your hand pour out of the mill eaves at the dimming hour, and swifts cut through the column of light from above. Things rustle constantly in the canopy. In the troughs, amphibians with translucent throats sing without pause. On the factory belts, lines of ants as long as a finger carry leaf-cuttings in an endless industrial procession of their own, using the human conveyor as a highway. Rats watch from the girders. A heron, impossibly, stands perfectly still in the shallow cistern, because there are fish in there now - blind white fish that came up through the limestone.

And nothing is quiet. That is the rule here. The sound is loud and layered and it never really stops, so you stop hearing it as noise and start hearing it as the place's way of breathing. The base is the low industrial throb - the engines, the sixty-cycle hum of the lights, the clank and sigh of the condensers. Over it is the biological roar: cicadas sawing, frogs belching, birds shrieking territorial claims that echo off the pit walls, the papery rustle of a thousand leaves shifting with the heat. When the wind moves up top, it funnels down and the whole bowl sighs and creaks, vines tightening on metal.

There are strangenesses that you acclimate to too quickly, which is how you know they are not Decoration but Law.

The plants here have learned behaviors from the machines. One species of vine times its flowering to the piston stroke, opening a trumpet flower with each compression with a soft *pff*. The big leaves track the amber work lights instead of the sun. Roots have grown *through* solid flywheels, not around them, and the steel has not broken - it has been incorporated, phloem around iron. In the mill, the air is full of drifting cotton-like seeds that are attracted to static electricity and cling to the generator cages in thick felt, sparking faintly.

And the fauna has learned plant logic. The beetles carry living moss on their backs like a coat. The bats roost not in dark crevices but inside the hollow, humid chambers of the aerial roots, which pulse faintly with warmth.

Nothing is dead. Even the rust is alive - a bacterial sheen that eats iron and excretes a faint, metallic perfume. If you leave a cup of water out, by morning it will have a skin on it.

If you stay long enough - and the bowl invites staying, with its warm dim and its hamlet-scale enclosure - you notice the dynamics, the active cycling. Water condenses, drips, is collected, steams off the engines, condenses again. Leaves fall, are pulped by beetles, become soil on the mill roof, where new leaves sprout. The heron eats the fish, the fish eat the larvae, the larvae eat the leaf litter. The belts turn, the flowers open, the insects pollinate, the seeds drift into the generator. Predator and prey, machine and vine, condensation and evaporation, all at once, all at a pace you can actually watch if you stand still and let the loudness become normal.

At the rim, far above, the surface world is dry and bright and relatively silent. Down here, everything is damp, and dim, and green, and rust, and warm, and overwhelmingly, insistently alive in the shell of a town that learned how to be a forest without ever stopping being a town.

You don't find the edge between built and grown. There isn't one anymore.
