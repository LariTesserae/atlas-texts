It is not a cave. Not really.

A cave suggests a thing hollowed out, a void that was made. This place was *left* — a bubble caught when the world cooled, a single hidden room deep inside the rock, and everything that has ever happened here has happened inside it.

You get to it by going down. Not far in distance, but finally in feeling. The surface is thin, wind-scoured basalt and frost-cracked flats where nothing grows. A fissure barely wide enough for shoulders, a cut that looks like a failed joint in the stone. You have to turn sideways and shuffle. The air changes before the light does — it goes cool, then cold, then damp in a way that has nothing to do with weather. The stone on either side is weeping.

Not wet the way a river is wet. Wet the way a living throat is wet. The rock sweats.

### The Pocket

The fissure opens all at once, without a threshold. One step your hands are brushing tight walls, the next there is space and your hands find nothing.

The pocket is perhaps sixty paces across at its widest. An ovoid, like the inside of an almond or the vesicle in cooling lava where gas was trapped and never escaped. You could hold it in your mind entirely from any one spot, and yet you can't see it all at once because it is so densely full.

It is low. The ceiling is not high — in the center you could raise your arms and almost touch it, near the edges you have to stoop. It is all one continuous surface, floor and walls and ceiling all part of the same dark stone, a dull iron-grey basalt that has been worn until it has no corners left. Everything here is established, settled, in balance with itself. Edges are rounded like river stones. Doorframes, where they have been cut, have softened. Lips of shallow pools have been polished by ten thousand years of overflow. There is no sharpness, no recent break. Even the rubble looks old, as if it fell a very long time ago and decided to stay where it landed.

There is no soil. No plant life at all. Not even lichen. The stone is too cool and too constantly wet for it, and the light too poor, and somehow also — it feels前-life here, as if green things have not yet been invented. The only colors are stone colors: graphite, slate, rust-red where iron leaches, the pale cream of mineral deposits.

And yet it is a city.

### The Dense Town

It fills the pocket edge to edge, not like a ruin but like a town that grew until it could not grow any more and then learned to be content in its own dimensions. Because it cannot expand, it has layered.

Houses — if you can call them that — are made of what the pocket provides. Flat slabs of basalt split with patient wedges and stone hammers, stacked without mortar, chinked with clay that never quite dries. Roofs are more slabs, pitched low. Some dwellings are no more than a lean of stone against the curving wall, a half-circle of wall making the other half. Others are free-standing in the center, two stories where the ceiling allows it, the upper floor a loft of lashed-together drift-bone and black wood that must have been carried down from the surface generations ago and is now kept and mended forever. Nothing is wasted. Ladders are notched poles. Walkways are planks laid from roof to roof so that you can cross the whole settlement without touching the ground. Everything is near-touching. Shoulders brush walls in the alleys, which are not alleys so much as the gaps left between buildings. Doorways face each other across a foot of space.

It is primitive work. You can see the hand in everything. Hammer marks, the chisel wander, the uneven thickness of walls. Simple iron — dark, pitted, precious — exists only as nails, as knife blades, as the hoops on a few small buckets. Most tools are stone, bone, antler. And there are many of them, carefully kept, hung on walls, slotted into niches. For a town this small there are an absurd number of things. Pots and jars and basins, all stone-coiled clay, all sized to catch water. Looms made of sticks. Hammers. Scrapers. A density of objects to match the density of structures. Every surface holds something.

The town is built *for* the water, around the water.

### The Wet

Water is everywhere but not in a body. There is no river here. There is no lake.

The stone condenses it out of the air itself. The pocket breathes cool, and its breath is mist. You see it before you hear the town — a fine, constant condensation forming on the ceiling in beads that swell and fatten and then slip, not quite falling so much as crawling down the curve. The walls are sheeted in it. Every surface glistens. It gathers in the bowls, in the carved channels that run along every wall and roofline, in the shallow lip of a central cistern that is not a well but a wide, flat basin of stone, perhaps ten feet across and only knee-deep, that never fills and never empties.

The sound is the first thing that overwhelms you. And it is loud.

It is not a single sound but a composite so constant you mistake it for silence until you try to speak over it. The drip. Not one drip — thousands. Ceiling to floor, roof to gutter, gutter to pot, pot to channel. A high, arrhythmic patter that, taken together, becomes a roar, like heavy rain heard from inside a shed with a tin roof. Under that, the deeper gurgle of the channels as they move water around the town’s perimeter, and the throatier chuckle of the overflow from the central basin disappearing into a slit in the floor where the stone drinks it back. The acoustics of the ovoid amplify everything and throw it back at you from every direction. Voices are small in it. Even shouting feels padded, swallowed. People here don't shout. They tap stones, they gesture.

The light, what there is, comes from nowhere and everywhere. Dim, sourceless, amber-grey. There are small clay lamps set in wall niches, burning rendered fat — maybe a dozen, their flames steady, smokeless, not flickering at all in the still air. They do not illuminate so much as mark. The main light is mineral: thin veins of something pale in the basalt that give off a faint, cool luminescence when wet, which is always. It is just enough to see by, to make out the sheen on a pot, the edge of a doorway. Shadows are soft and have no true edge. It feels like dusk that has lasted a hundred years.

The air is cool — not freezing, but the kind of cool that lives in stone. About the temperature of spring water. Your breath does not quite fog, but your skin prickles. The damp never leaves your clothes. Everything you touch is cold and slick.

### The Equilibrium

What is unusual here is not that it exists, but that it *persists*.

Water beads, falls, is caught, is channeled, is used, is returned to the slit in the floor, evaporates, condenses on the ceiling again. The level in the central basin has not changed in any memory. The lamps burn but do not seem to consume. The stone does not erode further, or does so at a pace too slow to measure against a human life.

Nothing grows, and almost nothing moves that is alive. Very rarely — maybe once a day, maybe once a week — something pale and eyeless flicks in the central basin, a fish or a salamander-type thing, blind, translucent, the length of a finger. It is gone before you are sure you saw it. A few white isopods cluster under a pot that hasn't been moved. That is the fauna. Rare, incidental, as if life wandered in by accident and found no reason to stay nor to leave.

The people, too, are rare in that pocket-density way — there cannot be many, perhaps thirty or forty, but because the space is so small and so full, they feel numerous. You see the same faces again and again. They move slowly, deliberately, without urgency, performing the same tasks: emptying a pot into a channel, wiping a wall, re-setting a stone that has not actually shifted. Their craft is maintenance, not progress. No one builds new houses; there is no room. They repair the old. No one quarries new stone; they re-dress the old. Tools are re-sharpened, not replaced.

If you stay long enough, you understand the rule of the place, though no one tells you: *here, things have arrived at their final shape*. Water has found its path and will not seek another. Stone has found its smoothness and will not become smoother. Sound has found its volume and will not rise or fall. The town has found its number and will not grow.

It is loud and still. Wet and stony. Crowded and empty. A complete world that fits inside a larger world like a secret kept in a closed hand — urban, ancient, hand-made, and perfectly, permanently, in balance with its own small, weeping sky.
