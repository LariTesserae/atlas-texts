It is not under water. It *is* water that happens to have a bottom.

You drop in from the surface and you never really stop dropping, because even a kilometer down the water is still warm. Bath-warm, blood-warm, a low, dim, amniotic heat that never cools. The light from above doesn't penetrate so much as it gives up. By twenty meters it is the color of cold tea. By fifty it is gone. What light remains is made down here.

This is a low place. A basin, a drowned valley, a continental dish that was once a plain and then subsided all at once. You can tell by how young everything is. The geology hasn't had time to learn how to be soft. The seafloor is not sand and silt but a fresh fracture — black basalt teeth, sharp as poured slag, stair-stepped terraces and columnar cliffs that broke yesterday on a geological clock. No river has rounded them. No tide has smoothed them. They stand in clean, unweathered edges, and from every fault line warm water sighs up in shivering columns. The whole valley is maybe thirty kilometers across, rim to rim — you can almost hold the scale in your head, a single drowned county, a single lost watershed — but you can never see it at once. The water is too thick with things.

It is impossibly dense here. Not empty ocean. Not open water. A jungle that forgot it needed air.

From every hard surface, vegetation has erupted. Not seaweed as you know it, not algae. Something engineered and then left to run. Giant, translucent kelp-trunks as thick as silos that rise a hundred meters and then bend, not toward light, but toward current. Their fronds are broad, waxy, veined with light of their own — a dim amber bioluminescence that pulses slow. Between them, smaller forms pack every inch of available volume: curtains of filamentous green-black hair that hang in the water like tapestries, bladder-ferns that swell and pop and re-inflate, mangrove-like root tangles that do not need soil and instead knit themselves directly into concrete and rebar. It is lush beyond any forest on land, and it is layered vertically, a hundred canopies in the water column, leaves pressed against leaves, frond against frond, so dense you have to push through it, parting it like curtains. And there is nothing moving in it. No fish. No shrimp. No birds above. No insects. Not even a microbe-cloud of krill. The fronds are untouched, uneaten. They grow, they tangle, they die and slough off and become peat in the water, falling like snow upward and downward at once. The water is full of drifting plant snow. It is a green, choking, utterly silent-in-one-way forest. The silence of no animal life is a pressure in itself.

Because everything else is deafening.

The industrial remains are scattered, but the industry is not dead. This is not a ruin. This is a factory floor that flooded and kept working.

Every half a kilometer or so — never clustered into a town, always solitary — there is a structure. A pump house of rust-streaked concrete still humming. A refinery skeleton with its distillation towers now serving as trellises for the kelp. A power station, its turbines turning without pause, its floodlights still burning a sullen sodium-orange that diffuses into the particulate water and makes each frond-cast shadow tremble. Vast pipe organs of intake and outflow, some as wide as subway tunnels, run along the sharp basalt floor, bolted into the fresh rock. They breathe. They *hammer*. The sound is not muffled the way water should muffle. It is amplified, carried in the dense, warm liquid and in the stone. A low, bone-conducted thudding of pistons, the scream of turbines cavitating, the hiss of superheated water venting from pressure valves, the groan of metal expanding and contracting, and underneath it all the constant, deafening roar of water itself being forced to move where it does not want to move. You feel it in your sternum before you hear it. It never stops. You have to open your mouth to equalize the vibration in your jaw.

And the water does not behave.

That is the weirdness. The breakdown.

At first you think it's a current. Then you realize the current is going three directions at once. A few meters ahead of you a perfect cube of water will hang suspended, its surfaces shimmering like glass, rain falling *inside* it sideways while the water around it is still. A wall of rain stands vertical in the middle of the ocean with no wind to hold it. Elsewhere, the ocean has stratification that should be impossible: warm water *below* cold water with a visible, rippling membrane between them that you can push your hand through and feel the temperature invert. Gravity stutters. A plume of plant detritus will fall, then abruptly rise, then hang motionless, as if the idea of down is being debated. Near the largest factory — a cathedral of rust and ceramic that still extrudes something, sheets of white bioplastic that bloom from its vents and are instantly colonized by fern — space itself is folded. You see the far rim of the valley much too close, magnified, then much too far, then duplicated. The kelp growing on that factory leans at an impossible angle, growing sideways along a gravitational contour that only it can feel.

The rules here are not the rules outside.

1. Water can hold an edge.
2. Light is made, not received.
3. Growth does not need consumption. The plants grow without being eaten, without pollinators, without decay by animals. They grow because the warm water is supersaturated with dissolved nutrients pumped endlessly by the machines, a broth.
4. Sound does not decay. It accumulates.
5. The place is unfinished. It is still being made and unmade.

It is turbulent, hyperactive. Nothing is stable. Watch any frond for ten seconds and you will see it grow a hand's breadth. Watch any pipe and you will see it shudder and birth a new joint, a new offshoot, as if the factory is trying to replicate itself vegetatively, too. A kelp trunk will be there and then be ripped sideways by a sudden shear current that wasn't there a moment ago — a current that cuts like a river *within* the ocean — and the trunk will snap, and the two halves will continue growing separately before the water has settled. Bioplastic sheets from the cathedral factory tangle in the canopy and dissolve into fibers that become new leaves. The water itself churns with convection cells the size of houses, warm upwellings colliding with cold intrusions that shouldn't exist, creating perpetual underwater storms with no surface. Vortices spin up, gather a column of leaves and rust flakes, and vanish. It is noisy, crowded, churning, always in the middle of happening.

If you stay still, the forest closes around you. The warm, dim water presses close. The lights of the distant pump houses throb like lighthouses in fog. The forest breathes around you in pulses of amber. The machines hammer against your bones. And through a gap in the fronds, you might see, for a second, the sharp, young basalt of the valley floor drop away into a fresh trench, so clean and black and new it looks poured yesterday, with hot water roaring out of it and vanishing into a wall of vertical rain that hangs in the ocean like a door.
