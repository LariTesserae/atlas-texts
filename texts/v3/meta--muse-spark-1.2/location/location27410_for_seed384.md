It is not under the water. It *is* water.

There is no sky here, only depth. You are low — lower than the sea should allow — in a bowl in the crust where the ocean folded in on itself and decided to stay. The basin is not large. You could walk its rim in an afternoon if you could walk, but you can't. You swim, or you sink.

From above, for a long, long time, there is only cold black water, pressing. The temperature is a constant, even ache, just above freezing. It is not a sharp cold, it is an old cold, a settled cold that has had centuries to soak into everything until stone and steel and bone hold the same temperature as the water itself. Nothing here remembers warmth.

Then, at about nine hundred meters where light has long since given up, the darkness thickens and then — resolves.

A town at the bottom of a cup.

Not a city. Something smaller and more complete than that. A hamlet, a single neighborhood, a factory-canton that was built all at once and then drowned all at once and then kept going. Its boundaries are clear and absolute: where the streets end, the basin wall rises, sheer and black, caked in a century of silt that has smoothed every edge. This place is not ruined. It is weathered, balanced, established in its drowning. Nothing is collapsing. Nothing is new. Erosion has finished its work and made a truce with architecture.

The water holds everything in suspension.

You descend into streets that are still streets. They have names, stamped on corner plaques gone soft with mineral bloom. They are narrow — dense beyond what a surface town would allow, because here density is not about people, it is about things. Pipes cross over pipes. Catwalks stitch buildings together at the second and third story. Conduit, cable, chain, rebar, scaffolding, all layered until there is hardly a cubic meter of water that does not contain something made. It is a thicket, a reef of industry.

The buildings themselves are low, two and three stories, flat-roofed, practical — brick and poured concrete and riveted iron, now all the same color: a deep, rusted umber blackened by cold and coated in a fine, pale silt that never quite settles and never quite clears. The corners are all round now. The water has licked them smooth. Windows remain, but they are not for looking out; they are pressure lenses, thick and clouded, behind which amber work-lights burn perpetually. That is the only light.

Light here is not illumination. It is a rumor. Every few meters a caged bulb, a strip of sodium, a furnace mouth, throws a cone of brown-gold into the black, maybe five feet before the water swallows it. Outside those cones there is perfect, crushing darkness. Your eyes adjust and adjust and never adjust. The dark between lights feels solid, like you could lean on it. The water itself is so clear and so black at once — particulate hanging absolutely still until a current moves through.

The plant is still running. That is the active heart of it.

This was — is — an industrial town, and it never stopped being industrial. On the western edge, sunk against the basin wall, the factory proper exhales. Not smoke — there is no air to carry it. It exhales heat. You can see it, a shivering distortion where superheated water from the deep vents is drawn up through exchangers the size of houses. Turbines turn, slowly, immensely, without hurry. They have been turning for decades. Piston arms the thickness of your body rise and fall with a hydraulic patience, pushing water through water. Everywhere, low engines throb. Not loud. You feel them more than you hear them, a pressure on your sternum, a thrumming in your teeth.

The sound of the place is almost nothing. That is its most unnatural quality. An industrial town should clang and scream. This one is muffled to near-silence by the weight of water and cold. What sounds exist are intimate and close: the soft *tick* of cooling metal, the sigh of a pressure valve equalizing, the creak of a cable contracting, the faint fizz of a bubble that forms on a hot pipe and instantly dissolves. If you hold still, you can hear the water itself moving through the town's throat — drawn in through intakes screened with baleen-like grates, pushed out through louvers in a slow, endless respiration.

Vegetation is sparse to the point of loneliness. Nothing grows fast in this cold and dark. On the leeward sides of buildings, where the current is weakest, there are patches of something that is not quite kelp and not quite mineral — black-red fronds, flat and leathery, that cling to concrete and wave only when the turbines pulse. Down an alley, a whole wall is bearded with pale bacterial mats that glow faintly if you look at them out of the corner of your eye, then go dark when stared at directly. They are the only softness.

Animals are rarer still. Once in a long while, a shape detaches from the darkness above and becomes a fish — blind, white, with a head too large for its body — that drifts through the main street as if down a canyon, utterly indifferent to the town, not touching anything. You might see one an hour. You might see one a day. Something larger sometimes passes beyond the rim of light and you only know it by the displacement, the way the silt briefly comets. The town does not need fauna. It has inhabitants of another kind.

The strangeness is not that a town is underwater. The strangeness is how well the water has learned to be a town.

Here, water does not behave like water on the surface. In the narrowest alleys, where the buildings crowd closest, the water hangs almost solid, viscous as glass, holding silt in perfect, unmoving veils. You can put your hand through it and watch the hole you made stay there for a full minute before it heals. Gravity is polite but negotiable; a dropped wrench falls slowly for a foot, then decides to hover. In the factory courtyard, a perpetual wall of rain falls *sideways* — not rain from above, but a sheet of slightly warmer, slightly fresher water that shears through the colder sea on an endless loop, from an outflow high on the basin wall to an intake low, a transparent curtain that never breaks. The workers who once lived here — if they were workers — built for this. Doorways have no doors, just pressure curtains. Stairs are ladders are ramps, whatever orientation the water required.

Everything is dense, layered, near-touching. You cannot extend your arms without brushing rust, rope, pipe, the frond of that sparse kelp. Yet nothing is chaotic. This is urban logic taken to its conclusion under pressure: every surface used, every void plumbed, every current charted and put to work.

The rule of this place is simple and absolute: nothing ever dries. Nothing ever rusts through. Nothing is ever still.

If you stay long enough — and the cold makes time slow — you stop expecting surface things. You stop listening for wind or voices or footfalls. You feel the town breathe around you, a small, complete, deeply weathered lung at the bottom of the world, pumping cold black water through its dense, dim, humming streets, asking nothing of the surface, needing nothing but the deep heat and the dark, content to keep its lights on forever in the quiet.
