It is not a mountain with a city on it. It is a mountain that *is* a city.

From a distance, across the dry lower air, you would think it was a natural peak — a massive, broad-shouldered mass that shoulders into the sky until its upper terraces disappear into glare. But as you get closer the illusion breaks. There are no slopes. There are steps.

The whole thing has been built.

Generations before anyone now living can remember, the valley floor was leveled and the mountain was begun. Not carved out of — put on. Layer after layer of block and pour and set, brick and poured concrete and cut stone and steel beam, all weathered to almost the same color now. A warm, sun-bleached bone-and-ochre, stained dark in vertical streaks where water has run for centuries. It rises at a grade that would have been impossible for natural rock to hold, a ziggurat the size of a minor range, its faces broken into thousands of terraces, ramps, buttresses and vertical streets. Every surface is made. There is no exposed geological substrate anywhere. If you chip at the ground under your feet, you hit aggregate and rebar and older masonry. If you dig in a garden, you find cistern soil brought up in buckets a hundred years ago and held in place by retaining walls.

You are at extreme elevation. The air is thin enough that everything is a little louder in your own head — your breath, your pulse — and the light is brutal. Bright is an understatement. At this altitude the sun has a white, high-pressure intensity. It flattens shadows to hard black cutouts at noon and makes every metal railing too hot to touch without a wrap of cloth. The sky is a deep, saturated blue that feels close enough to hit. And yet it is warm. Not desert-hot, not lowland-sweltering, but a steady, high-altitude greenhouse warmth. The mass of the built mountain stores heat all day and releases it all night. The stone breathes it back at you. In the sheltered terraces, where the wind is broken by walls three stories thick, the air hangs heavy and calm.

It shouldn't be this green.

The first rule of the place is that anything horizontal eventually becomes a garden. The builders knew it, planned for it, or surrendered to it. Every terrace, every flat roof, every wide stair, every abandoned rail siding — all of them hold soil. Not a dusting, but deep beds, a meter or more, contained by low walls of brick that have softened at the corners from centuries of being brushed by hands and roots. From the valley looking up, the mountain is lush to the point of obscurity. Vines pour over the edges of levels like green water. Trees have taken hold where they were invited and where they were not — figs and jacarandas and flame trees and species with no name that were bred for this thin bright air. Their roots work patiently into mortar joints, but slowly. This is not a ruin being reclaimed. This is a balance that was reached long ago.

The erosion here is established, mature. Water has had a thousand years to learn the shape of what humans made, and what humans made has had a thousand years to learn how to carry water. On every tier there are channels cut — not quite gutters, not quite aqueducts — shallow U-shaped runnels worn smooth by flow, darkened with algae and moss. They all connect. When it rains, and it does rain, but intermittently, in fast, heavy bursts that come mostly in late afternoon, the whole mountain sings and drains at once, water clattering down spillways, stair-cascades, chain gutters, and gargoyles that have been polished to a sheen. In between, it doesn't dry out completely. That is the dampness. The mountain sweats.

Even on clear days the air is never truly dry. Condensation forms overnight on every metal surface and drips down to feed the gutters by morning. Fog pools in the lee of the higher terraces and burns off slowly. Pockets of water remain everywhere — cisterns under plazas, shallow reflecting pools on temple-factory roofs, the slow green tanks where taro and watercress are grown in stepped paddies, the black tanks inside the machine levels where water is kept for boilers. The sound of dripping is constant even when no rain has fallen for a week.

Because it is loud.

Not chaotic-loud, not a city roar. A dense, layered industrial loudness that has settled into a kind of weather. The mountain is full of engines, but old engines, large and slow. On the mid-levels, whole terraces are given over to works — textile mills with looms clacking in sheds open to the air, forges where trip-hammers fall once every three seconds, every second, all day, grain mills turning with a low grinding moan, pumps that lift water one level higher, always one level higher, with piston sighs and chain rattles. Electricity is here, but it is industrial electricity — humming transformers in cages of expanded metal, bare bulbs strung on wires between buildings, the whine of dynamos. The noise stacks vertically. You can stand on a garden terrace full of bees and hear the thump of a stamping mill three levels down through your feet, and the wind carrying the clatter of a higher workshop down to you. No single sound dominates for long. Together they make a thick, warm drone that never really stops, only eases slightly at night.

And it is crowded — not with people, though there are many, but with presences. Density in every sense. The terraces are narrow enough that the canopy of one level brushes the floor of the next. Vines grow up the legs of water towers. Laundry lines cross over alleys where children have planted beans in old engine casings. Every railing has pots. Every wall has a niche with a shrine and a fern. The machine floors are not separate from the green; the green has been threaded through them. Great factory halls have had their clerestory windows pried open and roof beams colonized by epiphytes. Inside, workers tend machines that are themselves half-covered in carefully trained passionflower and gourd vines to keep the sheds cooler. It is not wilderness. It is cultivation taken to an extreme where the distinction is meaningless.

Which is why the animals are everywhere. In a place this dense and this green and this warm, with water always available somewhere within a hundred meters, life fills every available volume. Swifts and swallows in screaming abundance ride the updrafts off the hot faces, nesting by the thousands in holes deliberately left in the masonry for them. Parrots the color of fresh paint argue in the jacarandas. Geckos cover every sun-warmed wall. In the paddies and cisterns, fish and newts and dragonflies. In the air at dusk, bats pour out of expansion joints and eaves, and the night is loud with insects and tree frogs that live in the gutter gardens. People here keep hives on every sunny ledge, and dogs and cats and chickens and small pigs move freely through the stepped streets. None of it feels imported or invasive. It feels like it belongs to the building, like the building was made for it.

Things change slowly here. That is the other rule. Nothing is fast. The engines turn slowly. The vines grow slowly. The erosion that has rounded every corner and smoothed every spillway works on a scale of decades. Terraces are repaired not when they crack but when they have settled, a process watched over years. A family will tend the same paddy and the same loom on the same level for a lifetime, and their children will tend it after them, and the wall behind the loom will be a little more bowed, a little more darkened with lichen, but essentially the same. Even the weather is patient. Clouds form below you in the valley most mornings and rise only by midday, softening that killing light with a veil, then retreat.

If you climb — and climbing is the only way to travel, by stair and ramp and switchback rail — you feel the mountain's logic in your legs. Each level has its own microclimate, its own sound, its own smell: cut oil and hot metal on the machine tiers, then wet earth and frangipani as you cut through a garden tier, then smoke and frying dough around a residential tier where walls are painted bright turquoise and yellow inside to answer the sun. Doorways are low and deep-set to keep heat out. Shutters are propped open with sticks. Water is always moving past you, somewhere to your left, in a channel at ankle height, chattering over pebbles that were placed there to keep it from scouring the concrete.

Near the top, where the air thins further and the wind finally finds you unbroken, the terraces widen into great flat planes that serve as both fields and plazas. The light there is almost painful, and the trees are lower, tougher, wind-pruned. But even there the ground is made — great slabs poured and jointed, with soil carried up and held in. Monks or mechanics or farmer-engineers — the titles blur here — walk the high edges checking the cables that hold shade cloths, checking the mortar, listening to the mountain hum.

You can stand at the parapet and look out and understand the scale. The built mountain does not end abruptly. It grades down through foothill-terraces, outlier platforms, causeways and aqueduct bridges that stalk across the dry plain toward other, smaller built hills in the haze. It is a region, a town the size of a valley, but vertical. And all of it, warm and bright and damp and green and clattering softly to itself, is holding perfectly still and perfectly alive in the thin air, weathering at exactly the rate it was meant to weather.
