It is high up. Not the sharp high of a peak, but the broad high of a table that the mountains forgot to lower.

A glacial valley, a long U scooped out a very long time ago and then left to settle. The walls are not cliffs anymore. They are slumped, softened, shoulders rounded under ten thousand years of weather until they look like the flanks of something sleeping. Scree has stopped falling. The stone beneath is old gneiss and basalt, dark and pitted, but you barely see it. Everything has had time to be covered over.

The floor of the valley is not a floor. It is a mass.

Vegetation here did not colonize the terrain. It *became* the terrain. Cold has made it low and tight and relentless. This is not forest as you understand forest - no trunks to walk between. This is felt. A deep, dense mat of living matter a meter, two meters, three meters thick in places, built up out of itself for centuries. Sphagnum and peat moss in hummocks the size of houses, black-green and russet and frost-white. Lichen that grows in plates like rusted sheet metal. Dwarf willow and birch that never managed to become trees and instead learned to be ground, their branches braided horizontally, interlocked, root-grafted into a single elastic net. Cushion plants - hard, domed, impossibly dense, each one a perfect hemisphere of thousands of tiny leaves pressed together until you could stand on them. Liverworts slick as oil. Haircap moss in deep, sighing polders.

You walk and you do not walk on earth. You walk on plant. It gives under your boot and then returns. In places it is hollow underneath, where the lower layers have died and compressed into peat, leaving dark, breathing cavities. In places it bulges up into ridges and blisters and then splits open to show the wet, black fiber inside, steaming faintly in the cold air before sealing again.

It is cold. A thin, bright cold at elevation, where the light is normal - not dim, not blazing, just the clean, even daylight of a high overcast, a white sky that makes no shadows. The air is thin and smells of iron and sweet rot and tannin. Your breath plumes and does not dissipate quickly, but hangs in the stillness. Condensation is everywhere, but there is no rain. Water exists here as an afterthought of the cold: dew so heavy it runs, rime feathering every leaf-tip with white needles, fog that forms in the hollows between moss hummocks in the afternoon and burns off by evening, meltwater that never pools into lakes but threads through the mass in hidden, braided rivulets. You hear it sometimes before you see it - a glint of water in a crevice between two moss domes, black as tea, moving slowly, then vanishing back under the green. Pockets. Veins. The valley is damp, not wet. As if the whole living carpet wrings itself out every morning and sucks itself dry by night.

And it moves.

This is the strange thing. At first you think it is still. It is silent. Profoundly, unnaturally silent. The wind that should be tearing across a high valley like this seems to be swallowed by the moss. Sound falls into the felt and disappears. No birdsong. No insects at this height and cold. Rarely - very rarely - a ptarmigan ghosting low, a single beetle black against the lichen, but mostly nothing animal at all. The mass is almost purely plant. So the silence feels complete.

Until you watch.

The turbulence is not auditory. It is vegetative. Industrial, but without industry.

Every surface is manufacturing itself. You stare at a cushion plant and you see it pulse - not with wind, but with growth. The mosses extrude. The willow-braid tightens, creeps an inch, throws a runner. In the time it takes to watch, a liverwort colony doubles, crinkles, and collapses in on itself, its edges browning and being immediately overgrown by sphagnum from below. Hummocks swell like lungs, split with a soft, inaudible sigh, exhale a puff of spores that hangs in the cold air like dust in a factory, then subside. Over there, a whole ridge of peat has risen overnight into a low dome, its skin of lichen cracking like drying paint to reveal wet green beneath.

There are no built things here. No walls, no cabins, no ruins, no artifacts at all. But the tech is everywhere, because the plants have become it.

The valley works.

Sap runs under pressure through the dwarf birch network with an audible *tick* you feel more than hear, through clear, lignified pipes as thick as your wrist, jointed at nodes like valves. Resin is rendered and secreted in vats - natural basins in the moss where amber fluid collects, skinning over, reheated by slow fermentation below. The haircap moss grows in perfect, parallel ranks like bristles on a machine brush, all leaning one way, combing condensation out of the air and shuttling it along. Lichen plates lift on hydraulic cushions of stored water, exposing spore-forges underneath that release their product in silent, timed bursts. Everything is conveyor, still, press, loom, refinery, but grown, not made. You realize the weirdness is not that this looks like a factory; it's that a factory, if it were allowed to evolve without people for a million cold years, would look like this. Efficient, silent, dense, endlessly cycling.

The rules here are simple and total:

1.  Everything that can be plant, is plant.
2.  Nothing is wasted. Death is immediate feedstock. A leaf browns and it is already being unmade from below.
3.  Growth is not seasonal but constant. The cold does not stop it, it just slows it to a visible churn. Creation and destruction happen at the same speed, so the valley never gets higher or lower, but it is never the same shape twice.

If you stand on the slope and look across the scale of it - the whole open breadth of the valley, a kilometer or more across, the far wall a smudge of green-black under the white sky - you can see the churn as a faint shivering, like heat haze but cold. A whole sector of moss hummocks near the center is subsiding while another is rising. A dark river-vein opens, runs for an hour, closes. The silence makes it more unsettling. A thing this busy should roar. It doesn't. It respires.

At dusk the light thins just slightly and the condensation blooms. Every tip of moss holds a bead of water that catches the diffuse glow. The industrial plant-mass exhales. The cold deepens. Frost begins to form again, needle by needle, on the living machines, and they do not stop working under it.
