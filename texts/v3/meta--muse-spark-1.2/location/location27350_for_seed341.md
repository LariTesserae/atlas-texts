It is low.

Not just low in the way a plain is low, but sunken. A shallow bowl scooped out of the earth long enough ago that the earth has stopped resenting it. The rim is maybe thirty feet above you at its highest, a soft, weathered lip of dark limestone and clay, all rounded off, all settled. The erosion here isn't dramatic - no raw cliffs, no fresh landslides. Everything has had time to find its angle of repose. The slopes are draped. The stones are pitted and blackened with age, softened by water and heat, and held together by roots.

Because it is hot. Not desert hot. Greenhouse hot.

The air in the basin doesn't move much. It sits. It sweats. It's the kind of heat that has weight, that condenses on the underside of everything before it can rise. The light never really arrives. The sky above the rim is a pale, bleached white, but down in here under the cloud that lives in the bowl, the light is filtered down to a permanent dusk. Like late afternoon during a storm that never breaks. Everything is visible, but nothing casts a hard shadow. The light is diffuse, damp, amber-grey.

And it is wet. Impossibly wet for how hot it is.

Water is not a river here. It doesn't have anywhere to go. It hangs. It seeps. The walls of the basin weep constantly - the limestone sweats cold water even while the air scalds, beading and running in dark tracks down into the town. The ground underfoot is never dry. Not mud, exactly, but a saturated mat - a false floor of iron grating, concrete, and stone that the town has laid down over the marsh that wants to be here, and water pushes up through every joint. It pools in shallow, warm sheets that reflect the dim sky and the orange work lights. It drips from every pipe, every leaf, every roof edge. Condensation forms on the underside of the metal walkways and falls as a steady, warm rain whether it is raining above the rim or not. You hear it before you see anything else.

But the town is loud, so you hear everything at once.

This is a small place. A hamlet, a company town, a single-works settlement. You could walk from one wall of the basin to the other in ten minutes if you could walk in a straight line, which you can't. It was built all at once, with a plan, and then it stayed that size. Maybe a dozen big structures - not houses so much as works. A power house, a pumping house, a low mill with a saw-tooth roof, a processing shed with a stack that no longer quite smokes but breathes. All brick and riveted iron and poured concrete, all stained the same dark mineral color by the water. And all of them are spaced apart. Sparse. There's too much empty space between buildings for a proper city. Between any two structures there's a gap of marsh-garden, or a flooded court, or just open grating with steam rising through it. It feels less like a town and more like a set of machines that have been left with room to breathe, each one occupying its own little territory.

They are still running. All of them.

That's the active part. Nothing here is ruin, even though everything looks weathered. The mill's big wheel - not quite a water wheel, not quite a turbine - turns slowly and endlessly, fed by the sheet of warm water that pours from the upper seep. The pump house thuds. Thuds. Thuds. A deep, chest-felt piston beat every three seconds, pushing water that has just seeped in back up to the rim where it falls again. The power house hums with a transformer whine that makes your teeth ache, and every few minutes it vents with a shrieking hiss of pressure that drowns out everything and leaves your ears ringing in the dripping aftermath. Belts slap. Chains clank in their housings. Water slaps against concrete sumps.

It should be deafening, and it is, but it's a loudness that has become weather.

Between the buildings, in all that sparse, intentional emptiness, it is lush. Obscenely lush. Because there are no animals to eat it, to check it, to move through it.

There are no animals here at all.

No birds call over the basin. No insects stitch the air, even in this heat and wet that should be swarming. No rats in the walls, no gulls. At first you think you're just not hearing them under the machines, but then you notice the stillness on the leaves. No bites taken. No webs. The vegetation grows like it has forgotten it was ever supposed to be afraid.

It grows over everything. Not in a wild, jungle way - in a cultivated, negotiated way. Broad, waxy leaves the size of serving platters, elephant ears and taro and something like giant philodendron that was clearly brought here on purpose and then went happily feral. They crowd the gaps between buildings, rooting in the warm slurry that collects under the grating. Vines as thick as your wrist climb the brick, finding the mortar, but they don't pull the buildings down; they've been trained up trellises of rebar and copper pipe. The town tends them, or they tend the town - water-weed with hollow, bamboo-like stalks grows right up through the cooling outflow channels, and its leaves rattle loudly whenever the venting steam hits them. Creeping mosses and liverworts upholster the weeping walls, dripping. Ferns unfurl from the cracks in the concrete, six feet tall, their fiddleheads beaded with hot condensation.

Everything photosynthesizes in the dimness. The green is almost black in this light, a deep, wet, infrared green. It steams. It grows visibly if you stand and watch - a leaf twitching wider, a runner extending an inch while the pump thuds. The air smells of hot iron, wet earth, crushed green leaves, ozone, and rust.

And that's the strange thing. The rules here are slightly off.

The water doesn't quite obey. It condenses on the *underside* of leaves before it condenses on the top. It runs *up* the coolest pipes for a few inches before gravity remembers to pull it down. In the quiet moments between piston thuds, you can hear the marsh breathing - not a metaphor - the entire mat of root and grating rising and falling a fraction of an inch as water pressure equalizes underneath it. The buildings have settled so completely into the basin that it's hard to tell where the built ends and the grown begins. A brick wall will have a course of bricks that have been replaced by a dense, wood-like root that grew exactly brick-shaped to fill the gap. A factory window glows dim orange and inside there's no floor, just a tangle of vines and warm water with a transformer humming half-submerged, perfectly happy.

No one seems to tend the machines, but they are tended. Tools left on a rail, still warm from a hand. A cup of tea on a catwalk that never cools because the air won't let it, filmed over with a skin of condensation. You get the sense that people were here, are here, but sparse like the buildings - maybe sleeping during the loudest shifts, maybe simply outnumbered by the plants and the works.

You stand on the grating, your shoes already wet, your shirt already sticking to you, and you listen to the loud, wet, hot, dim bowl. The pump thuds. The steam screams and sighs. Water drips from a thousand leaves in a syncopated rain that never stops. The vegetation rustles with growth and steam though no wind moves. And in all that motion, that endless cycling of water up and over and through, that turning and pumping and humming - there is no other movement. Nothing darts. Nothing scurries. Only the plants reaching, the water weeping, and the machines turning over in their established, sweaty, patient circle.
