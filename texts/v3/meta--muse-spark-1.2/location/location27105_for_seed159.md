It is not a cave the way caves are supposed to be.

Not a tunnel, not a throat of dark you have to crawl through. It's a *negative valley* — a place where the surface peeled back while it was still being made and never finished the job.

You are below.

Not deep in the way miners mean deep, with pressure in your ears and stone closing in. Below like a lid lifted. The ceiling is still there, a hundred meters above you, but it is broken. It is raw, embryonic rock — not the smooth, water-carved limestone of an old system, but basalt still sharp from cooling, salt and gypsum still crystalline, shale still flaking off in plates as big as doors. Nothing here has been softened. Every edge is fresh. Every fault is still active. This place is under construction.

The light is wrong for being underground, which is the first thing that tells you how big it is. Normal, day-bright light falls in columns where the roof has foundered — great ragged skylights torn open to the desert above, their rims still smoking with heat. Sun falls straight down through dust and steam and makes the air itself visible. Between those columns, the light carries, reflected and re-reflected off pale stone until even the shadowed places are bright enough to see color. Farther back, where the skylights don't reach, the stone itself leaks light — veins of warm amber fungus or mineral bacteria, not plants, not anything with leaf or root, but a flat, slick biofilm that coats the rock in sheets and pulses faintly when you step near it. Together it's enough to read by, to see for a kilometer down the length of the basin.

The basin is what you have to call it because there is no other word that holds its scale. It runs out past where your eyes can sort detail, a regional wound maybe three or four kilometers long and half that wide, stepping down in raw terraces. You can stand on the highest shelf and see the whole system the way you'd see a town in a valley — except the town is made of movement.

The air is warm. Not comfortably warm. Geothermal warm. The stone radiates it. The water radiates it. It's a wet heat that sits on your skin and in your lungs. 30, 35 degrees, and damp enough that everything sweats. There is no river here. Water 1.4 damp is not a river-world. Instead the whole basin weeps.

Water arrives as condensation. Hot air from below meets cooler air falling through the skylights and wrings itself out on every surface. It beads on the fresh basalt, gathers in hollows, drips. It collects in shallow, warm pans on the terraces — some clear as glass, some milky with dissolved minerals, some only a hand-deep film that shivers with the footsteps of thousands. In places it falls as a real rain for ten minutes when a cloud of steam catches the light and collapses, spattering the stone and everyone on it. Then it stops. The pans steam gently. Within an hour they are half-evaporated, leaving a white crust of salt, then they fill again. Pockets of water in a larger dryness, a place that is never quite dry and never quite wet.

There is no soil. No grass. No trees. Not a single green thing. The world here is mineral — black glass, rust-red iron stone, sulfur yellow, salt white — all of it sharp and unweathered, still learning how to be a landscape. The only thing that softens it is the film, that bacterial varnish. It is dark amber and ochre and sometimes a bruised violet, spreading in damp patches where the water lingers. It smells faintly of iron and brine. It is not vegetation. It does not photosynthesize so much as it *etches*, eating the stone and exhaling that dim light. And it is the base of everything.

Because the place is *crowded*.

Overwhelming is the only word for the fauna, and even that feels thin. This basin should be dead — too new, too raw, too mineral. Instead it is saturated. Dense, layered, near-touching presences in every cubic meter of air and on every surface. You thought at first the stone was moving. It is.

On the rock, the sheets of biofilm ripple with grazers — blind, pale things the size of your hand, like woodlice crossed with shrimp, with dozens of soft legs and translucent carapaces that click and scrape as they feed. They cover the pans in such numbers that the water seems to have a skin. Larger, slower things bulldoze through them — arthropods as long as your forearm, armored in matte black chitin, nosing with shovel heads. They leave furrows that fill with water and are immediately recolonized.

In the air, it is worse. The skylight columns are choked with flight. Not birds. Not bats. Things with four wings of membrane stretched over finger-bone, bodies like darts, heads too small, eyes too large and utterly light-blind despite the brightness. They hunt the smaller flies — and there are clouds of those, gnats the size of rice grains that move like smoke — in screaming, intersecting spirals. They do not seem to see you. They pass so close the wind of their wings hits your face, loud as shuffling cards. On the high terraces, something else watches: hunch-backed, long-limbed climbers with damp, hairless skin and hands that are all fingers, clinging to the raw walls above even the light. They are quiet when they move, but there are so many of them that the rock ticks constantly with their nails.

The sound is loud. Not deafening, but insistent, total. A layered roar made of a million small sounds. Clicking, scraping, chittering, the thin whine of the fly-clouds, the leathery snap of wings, water dripping and sizzling on hot stone, the low groan of the rock itself as it cools and splits. A new crack will open somewhere with a report like a gunshot and a puff of mineral dust, and every animal will go silent for half a second before the noise redoubles, frantic, turbulent.

Nothing here is stable. That is the true rule of this place. Dynamic 2.9 — anthill-energy, bazaar-energy, a system in storm-state. Creation and destruction are happening at the same speed. While you watch, a terrace edge collapses into a lower pan with a splash — and within minutes the grazers are already over it, and the flyers are already hunting above it, and a new salt crust is already forming at its rim. A steam vent opens behind a rock and the bacterial film around it blooms visibly, doubling in an hour, and is eaten back to nothing by evening. Everything breeds, feeds, dies, molts, hatches at an accelerated, almost panicked pace, as if the whole basin knows it has only just come into being and is trying to fill every niche before the stone finishes hardening.

And the built things. You almost miss them at first because they are so small against the chaos, and so primitive.

Scattered. Not a city, not even a village. Faint marks. On a dry ledge where the water doesn't pool, someone has stacked raw basalt slabs into a low windbreak, the cracks daubed with dried mud and salt. Beside it, a circle of stones around a fire pit that is still warm, with charred carapaces cracking in the ash — someone has learned which of the grazers can be eaten. Higher up, tucked under an overhang to avoid the intermittent rain, a shelter of stretched hides and chitin plates lashed together with sinew, weighted with stones. Hand-tools — you find them everywhere if you look: a chipped edge on a piece of glassy black rock, a thigh bone polished to a point, a flat stone with a groove where it was used to grind salt. No metal. No machines. Nothing that will last longer than the rock wants it to.

There are paths, but they are not made. They are worn by feet — many feet, going the same way, over and over, between water pans and shelters and the places where the biofilm is thickest. At a crossing of two such paths, someone has left a marker: three stones balanced vertically, precarious, alien. Maybe a signpost. Maybe a prayer against the constant cracking and boiling and swarming.

The weirdness is not theatrical. It is the quiet way the place does not quite follow. The gravity feels a fraction light near the deepest part of the basin, where the warm air rises fastest — dust hangs a beat too long before falling. The sound sometimes arrives before the motion that makes it. And the shadows under the skylights don't quite line up with the sun you can see through them, as if the light has been folded on its way down.

You stand on warm stone that was liquid a geological instant ago. Condensation beads on your arms. A cloud of flies parts around you. Something shatters in the distance — another slab of newborn rock giving way — and a flock of dart-things screams and wheels. Below you, in a shallow pan no deeper than your ankle, a thousand pale grazers turn as one and recommence their scraping, eating light.

The basin breathes steam. It is too open to be a cave and too enclosed to be a valley, too bright to be underground and too raw to be outside. It is not finished, and because it is not finished, it is absolutely full.
