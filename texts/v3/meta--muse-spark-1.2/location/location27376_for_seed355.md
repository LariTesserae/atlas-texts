It is low. Not low like a hill's foot, low like the world has settled here.

A broad, shallow basin, kilometers across, rimmed by hills so weathered they have lost any edge. They are not mountains anymore; they are the soft, long humps of something that finished eroding a very long time ago and decided to stay that way. Rounded, slumped, dark. The stone is not stone as you first think — it's old alluvium baked and re-baked, salt-caked clay and travertine and basalt smoothed to a river-stone finish even where no river has run in memory. Everything leans slightly inward, toward the center, as if the whole valley were a dish left out in the heat to collect whatever falls.

And what falls is water.

Not in storms. Not in rivers. The air itself is wet. The heat here is heavy and absolute, a hot darkness that never breaks. There is no sun. There has not been a sun for longer than the city can remember being a city. The sky, if you can call it a sky, is a low, close lid of cloud or steam or simply warm, black air saturated past its limit. It sweats.

Water condenses on every surface. It beads on the cool undersides of stone, gathers under every lintel, weeps endlessly out of the rock. The walls weep. The ground weeps. Even in the places where you think you are dry, you put your hand down and it comes away damp. The water table is so high you can see it breathing. In the lower streets it has already arrived — not as a flood, but as a presence. A black mirror an inch deep over flat stone, perfectly still until a drop falls into it from nowhere and rings it. Shallow canals that were once streets, or streets that were always canals, thread between the structures. They do not flow toward anywhere. Some of them flow uphill. Some of them are perfectly level and yet move, slowly, against the slope of the basin, as if they remember a gradient that no longer exists.

The city is here, and it is unmistakably a city — not a ruin, not a village. It has avenues. It has districts. It has density of intent, even if not of crowding.

But it was not built the way cities are built.

There are no machines. No metal. No glass, no wire, no cut timber, no sign that a tool more complex than a hand was ever used. Everything is grown or gathered or coaxed. The buildings are mass — thick-walled, hand-packed, material that was mud and then was pressed and then was left to cure in the hot wet dark until it became something else. Some are like termite mounds swollen to the size of houses, porous limestone and bio-cement, pitted, breathing. Some are like inverted bowls of dark, fired clay, their surfaces rippled with the thumbprints of their making, still showing the layers of their accretion. Their doors are not framed; they are gaps that were left open. Their roofs are domes so low and heavy they seem to sag under the damp air. They are spaced far apart. This is an urban place that learned to leave room.

Between any two structures there is a stretch of empty, wet flat — cracked drying pan, or black water, or steaming silt — fifty, a hundred meters of nothing. You can stand in the center of what is clearly a plaza and see only three or four buildings at its edges, each solitary, each too far to call to. The city is organized, but sparsely. It does not crowd. It knows the water needs somewhere to go, and the heat needs somewhere to rise.

Nothing grows easily here, though the water and heat suggest it should be jungle. It is almost barren. In the dry-ish silts between buildings, a few things have taken hold that do not need light. Pale, thick-stemmed stalks, leafless, the color of wet paper, rising a foot or two and ending in a single closed bud that never opens. Patches of something like black lichen that spreads flat over stone and pulses faintly, exhaling moisture. Near the standing water, clusters of stilt-roots with no tree above them — just the roots, arching up like ribs, holding a damp hollow where a trunk should be. They are not dead. They are not alive the way plants are supposed to be. They simply persist, photosynthesizing heat or darkness or the mineral tang in the condensation. There are no animals to eat them. There are no insects to pollinate them. There are no birds. There is nothing that moves under its own will except the water and the slow growth.

The quiet is what makes the absence clear.

It is not silent. The place is active — constantly. Water drips. Always, everywhere, a tempo of dripping from eaves that never dry, from the invisible sky, from the weeping stones themselves. Plink into canal, plink into puddle, plink onto silt. The buildings sigh. Their thick walls hold heat all day and all night and they expand and contract with a low, infrequent creak, a settling deeper into the silt. Somewhere, water gurgles where it finds a lower place and then, impossibly, climbs again. Condensation ticks as it forms. Mud sighs and releases a bubble of warm air that smells of iron. But there is no rustle, no call, no footfall, no voice. The quiet is not emptiness; it is a held breath. You realize after a while that your own breathing is the loudest animal sound for a valley.

And the strangeness does not announce itself. It accumulates.

At first you think the darkness is just darkness. Then you notice you can see the far side of the basin, faintly, though there is no light source to see by. No moon, no stars, no fire. The buildings themselves are dimly visible not because they are illuminated but because the dark is thinner around them, as if the idea of them repels the night slightly. The water reflects things that are not there — a second set of buildings hanging below the surface, more complete, more crowded, with lights in their windows, though the buildings above have no windows. If you stare at a canal long enough, its surface goes perfectly clear and you see not silt at the bottom but depth, impossible depth, a well that goes down through warm black water for farther than the basin is deep.

Geometry is tired here. A street that looks straight from one end proves to be curved when you walk it. Two buildings that seemed a hundred meters apart are suddenly adjacent when you look over your shoulder, then far again when you turn back. Angles in the clay walls are slightly wrong — a corner that should be ninety degrees holds at a hundred and ten, and yet the dome above it does not collapse. The heat shimmers, but not from heat. The air folds.

The most active thing here is the water cycle, but it is a cycle that has forgotten to be a circle. Steam rises off the warm canals into the lid-sky, thickens, cools just enough to condense on the cool clay, runs back down as beads and rivulets and drips into the canals again — but on the way down some of it never lands. It hangs in mid-air, droplets suspended, trembling, refusing to fall. Some of it falls sideways. Some of it evaporates before it forms. The city is wet because the world is trying to rain and can't quite remember how.

If you stay, you feel the rules: 

No spark will catch here. Fire has no purchase in this wet heat, and there is nothing to burn that is not already damp through.

Water is not to be directed. You can cup it, you can wade it, but you cannot make it go where you want. It goes where it remembers.

Height is not safety. The higher ground near the rim is drier but the dark is thicker there, and the water that weeps from the hills runs uphill to get to you.

You do not build close together. The city knows this. The emptiness is load-bearing.

And nothing that lives moves quickly. Whatever made these buildings is gone, or became the buildings, or never needed to move in the first place. The place is in motion — dripping, creeping, condensing, settling — but it moves at the pace of mineral and heat, not muscle. It will still be doing this long after the hills have smoothed a little more, long after the canals have found a new direction to flow uphill in.

At the center of the basin, where the water is deepest and stillest and blackest, there is no building at all. Just a wide, shallow pool, perfectly circular, though no one circled it. The surface is so still it is not water anymore. It reflects the dark above so completely that looking into it feels like looking up. Drops fall into it without a ripple. The pale stalks around its edge all lean infinitesimally away from it, as if listening outward.
