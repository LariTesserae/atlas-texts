This is a low country, worn down to almost nothing.

Not flat in the way a salt pan is flat — flat in the way very old things are flat. The earth here has been worked on for so long that it has forgotten any other shape. There are no hills to speak of, only the faintest long swells, broad as a sleeping back, rising maybe ten or fifteen feet over a mile and falling again so gradually you wouldn't feel the slope in your legs, only in the way the water lies. The horizon is a perfect, hazy circle in every direction. You can see weather coming for hours before it arrives.

The rock beneath is ancient. Shield rock — granite and gneiss and iron-banded stone polished by eons of rain until all edges are gone. It shows through in places where the thin skin of soil has thinned further, not as cliffs or outcrops but as great smooth domes the color of bone and rust, low and whale-backed, warm to the touch even in shade. They weep. Water finds its way up through the micro-fissures and seams and beads along their flanks, a constant slow sweat. It doesn't run. It gathers, it thickens, it slips down in sheets no thicker than glass and vanishes into the peat at their feet. If you put your hand on the stone you feel it is never dry.

Because water is everywhere and yet nowhere deep.

This is not a lake country and not a river country. The ground itself is wet. The soil — a deep, tired, iron-red clay mixed with pale sand and black organic rot — is saturated to the surface. You step and the ground gives a little and water rises around your footprint instantly, clear and warm. Between the stone domes the land holds itself in shallow pans and braids, a sheet-marsh a few inches to a few feet deep, stretching for miles. The water doesn't flow so much as lean. It moves so slowly that you only know it moves because the algae strings all point one way, southwest, toward a sea you cannot see but can smell faintly on the warm air. Dragonflies hang above it, their shadows sharp on the silt below.

Light is ordinary and full — a late-morning, mid-summer light, high and white, filtered through a thin high humidity haze that takes the hard edge off the sun without dimming it. It makes everything glisten. Every leaf, every stone, every puddle throws back a bright point. The sky is pale blue, almost washed out by the heat, with long mare's tails of cloud that never seem to bring storm, only more moisture condensing and falling again as a brief, warm drizzle that barely ripples the pans. Then steam rises.

The air is warm, consistently warm, the kind of warmth that lives in your chest. Not scorching, not desert. Humid, enveloping, 85 degrees and heavy enough that sound carries. Your skin stays damp whether you sweat or not. Breath condenses even in heat — the air is that close to its limit. At noon the smell is strongest: warm mud and mineral iron and sweet rotting sedge and the clean smell of stone-water.

Vegetation is sparse, and that is what makes the water so visible. This is not a jungle trying to cover everything. The green is scattered, parsimonious, as if the land is too old and too wet to bother with forests.

There are sedges in clumps, stiff and yellow-green, each clump an island a yard across with its roots matted tight above the waterline. There are rushes with black seed heads. Scattered across the plain, solitary and far apart, stand the trees — not many, and not tall. They are broad, flat-topped, with bark like cork, pale grey and deeply furrowed, and leaves that are small and waxy. They look stunted despite their age; their trunks are thick and twisted, their roots sprawling out above the saturated ground like the buttresses of a mangrove, holding them just clear of drowning. Between them is simply open water, open mud, open stone, and sky. You can walk for ten minutes toward the nearest tree and still not reach it. The emptiness between presences is the dominant note — space is respected here. Even the grasses don't carpet; they dot.

And yet for all that sparseness, it is loud. Abundantly, overwhelmingly alive.

The fauna has taken the bargain the plants refused. The water is thick with it.

At first you hear the insects — a wall of sound. Cicadas in the cork-bark trees shave the air with a high, electric whine that rises and falls in waves across the plain, so loud you feel it in your teeth. Underneath that, a lower, continuous trill of crickets and katydids from every sedge clump. At the water's edge, frogs. Hundreds. Thousands. Small brown frogs and larger green bullfrogs bellowing in deep, jug-like calls that overlap until they become a single chorus, pulsing. It never fully stops, even at midday.

Movement is everywhere if you stand still. Water boatmen dimple the surface. Snails leave bright trails on the mud. Brilliant blue dragonflies and copper ones hunt in jerks and hovers. In the clearer pans, minnow-like fish the length of your finger move in sudden silver flocks, and larger, mottled lungfish hold perfectly still in the shadows of stones. Wading birds — white, grey, and pink — stand motionless on stilt legs far out in the shallows, spearing. Their cries are sharp and carry for miles in the warm, dense air. At the base of the stone domes, turtles the size of plates bask, their shells crusted with the same pale lichen that grows on the rock itself.

Nothing is in a hurry. That is the rule of this place.

Everything here operates on slow gradients and patient cycles. Water moves inches per hour. The trees grow a millimeter of bark a year. The stone weeps at the speed of condensation. The frogs inflate their throats, call, deflate. Even the decay is slow — plant matter sinks into the black peat and stays, half-preserved, sour and warm, building ground by fractions. There are no flash floods here, no sudden erosion, because erosion has already finished its work a hundred million years ago. The land has reached its base level, its final accommodation with gravity and rain. All that is left is for water to lie on it, for sun to warm it, for life to fill the wet air with noise.

If you sit on the warm back of one of the weeping stones, your feet in the tepid pan, the water will eventually seep up around your ankles without you noticing when it started. The cicadas will climb to their peak, hold, and fade. A bird will call far away, and another will answer from even farther, the sound taking its time across the open, level world. Nothing has been built here. There is no road, no wall, no cut mark, no piling, no plastic, no glass — not even a cairn. The only lines are those made by roots and ripples. The world is biological, mineral, and wet, exactly as it has been for a very long time, and it will be so for a very long time after.
