It is a low place.

You don't come down to it so much as you realize you have been descending for a while without noticing a slope — the ground softening and giving underfoot, the air getting thicker and warmer, the light going down even though there is nothing overhead to block it. It's not a valley exactly. It's a basin, a shallow bowl in the land maybe three or four hundred meters across, with a rim so low and so weathered you could step over it without thinking it was an edge. From the inside, though, the rim holds. It goes all the way around. Once you're in, the outside is gone.

The whole bowl is stone. Not bedrock - not one thing - but stone upon stone upon stone, packed until there is nowhere that is not stone.

The floor is not earth. It's cobbles and slabs, basalt and limestone and some paler rock that sweats, all fitted by water and time until the gaps between them are only as wide as a finger, only as wide as a fingernail. They are rounded. They have been here so long their edges have given up. You can see where older stones have been split by heat and then healed over with mineral crust, a darker line of precipitate filling the crack. Established, settled. Nothing is sharp here except where a person has made it sharp.

And water is in everything. Not as a lake — there is no open water large enough to call a lake — but as a presence that no surface is without.

It seeps. It weeps from the low walls of the rim where the strata are exposed, darkening the rock in vertical stains. It stands between the packed stones in sheets no deeper than your ankle, clear and warm, so still it looks like glass until you disturb it and it settles again immediately. It beads on every surface and does not evaporate. The air itself is wet. It's a warm dim, not hot enough to burn, warm enough that you loosen your clothes and still feel damp heat on your skin, like the air after rain in late summer when the sun has not come back out.

Breathing here is like breathing in a bathhouse. The light is low and diffuse. There is no direct sun. Either the sky is perpetually overcast — a high, even, pearl-grey cloud that never breaks — or the steam itself catches the light and holds it. You can see everything, but softly. No shadows. Colors are muted except where water makes them dark. The stone goes from grey to black where it is wet, and the pools reflect the grey sky without glare.

Sound is small.

At first you think it is silent. Then you hear how much quiet is shaped by water: the tick… tick… of a seep hitting a pool; the faint, almost inaudible lap when a breeze you cannot feel moves a film of water against a stone lip; the occasional soft shift as a stone settles infinitesimally lower into warm mud beneath. Your own footsteps are muffled — not splashing, just a soft displacement, *shush* as water pushes between tightly packed rocks around your feet. If you stand still for a minute, the quiet comes back and stays.

There are things built here, but not many and not together.

A low wall, dry-stacked, running only five or six meters before it dissolves back into tumbled stone. It was never high; you'd step over it. The stones of it are lichenless — nothing grows here — but stained black-green with mineral. The chipping on them is old, done with a hammerstone, edges now dulled again by condensate.

A little further on, a hollowed slab at ground level, square-cut, about the size of a bed, holding three inches of warmer water. Someone chiseled it out and chiseled a lip for it, and a shallow channel to feed it from the seep-line. The tool marks are still there, softened. Beside it, a cairn no higher than your knee, three flat stones stacked with care, and a fourth fallen.

Another: a simple shelter, three walls of stacked rock with no roof — the roof long rotted or taken — just enough to break wind that hardly ever comes. Inside, the floor is the same packed stone, kept dry-ish by a raised threshold. A fire-blackened stone sits in the middle, but no ash. No wood to burn here.

None of this is recent. None of it is ruined, exactly. It is weathered into the equilibrium of the place. The builders used what was here, moved the smallest amount necessary, and stopped.

There are no plants. Not a blade, not a moss, not even the slick green you expect on wet stone. You keep looking for it, because warm and wet should mean green — but this water is wrong for it, or the stone is wrong for it. Too mineral, too warm, too saturated with dissolved lime and silica that precipitates as a thin, pale crust on every surface at the waterline. You see the crust, brittle as eggshell, and where it has been broken by a footfall it reforms in a day, a quiet mineral growth. If there is life, it is in the film: a faint, translucent slick on the underside of submerged stones, bacterial, almost invisible, that makes the stone slightly slippery and not quite any color. It does not count as vegetation. It does not try to be seen.

Animals are as rare.

You might see one water beetle, black and slow, rowing across a pool that is barely larger than itself. You might see a pale lizard, motionless on a warm dry stone near the rim, watching you for a long time before it blinks. At dusk — though dusk never quite deepens — a few midges rise in a column over the warmest pool and fall again. That is all. No birds call because there is nowhere to perch that isn't wet, and nothing to eat.

And that is the rule of the place, if it has one: nothing leaves and nothing changes. The water does not rise and does not fall. The seep feeds the pools, the pools breath out into the air, the air condenses on the stone and feeds the seep. The warmth is steady, coming up from below, not from the sky. The stones do not erode further because they have already eroded to the shape that water wants them to have. The walls do not fall further because they have settled into their lowest stable form. The crust grows and is dissolved and grows again in the same line.

You can sit on the edge of the chiseled basin and put your hands in water that has been exactly this warm and exactly this deep for longer than you have been alive, for longer than the hands that chiseled it have been dust. The steam lifts in the dim air and beads on your forearms. A drop gathers on the low rim above you and falls, ticking against the surface. The circles spread and vanish.

It does not feel abandoned. It feels occupied in a way that has completed itself. The packed stones, the still water, the scattered, simple work of hands — they have all arrived at an arrangement that holds.
