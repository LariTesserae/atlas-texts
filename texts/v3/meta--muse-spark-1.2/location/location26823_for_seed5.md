You don't find it so much as fall into it.

The plain goes on for a long time first. Flat, low country - not sea-level, but low enough that the sky feels enormous and close. Pale stone and frost-heaved till, scoured clean by old ice, then left alone for long enough that its edges have gone soft. Everything rounded. The boulders are shouldered deep into the ground like they've settled in for sleep, their windward sides polished to a dull sheen. No sharp cuts. This is country that has finished arguing with weather.

And then the ground simply gives way.

Not a canyon. Not a valley. A pocket - an almost perfectly oval sink, perhaps three hundred paces across at the lip and no more than thirty deep. The walls are not cliffs but a continuous, bowl-like slope of weathered limestone and packed earth, old as bone, terraced by time and roots rather than by hands. From the plain you could walk within twenty meters and miss it entirely if the light wasn't right; the sod roofs and snow do that. It's a hidden room in the earth.

The light is what gives it away. It is brutally, cleanly bright in there.

The sky here in the cold is a high, thin white, a sun that never seems to climb or fall, just hangs - pale and wide and without heat. On the plain that light is flat and punishing. Down in the pocket it collects. The walls are pale rock and the rim is crusted year-round with a rime of frost that never quite melts, never quite grows. It reflects. The whole bowl glows, like light poured into a cup. The shadows are faint and blue-edged and they don't really move.

It is cold. A steady, dry, breath-catching cold that makes wood contract and stone hold its chill. Your breath doesn't plume so much as it hangs for a moment and then gently settles. And yet it is not barren. That is the first contradiction you have to let go of.

The bowl is packed. Utterly, impossibly packed.

There is a town in it. If you can call it a town. It fills the floor of the sink from wall to wall, edge to edge, with no waste, no gap, no yard left unclaimed. Primitive - and complete. Low houses of dark, hand-split timber, river stone, and turf, their walls shared, their eaves overlapping. Alleyways not as passages but as narrow seams between buildings where a person must turn sideways. Roofs that are roofs and also gardens and also streets. Ladders, boardwalks, stone steps worn concave in the center by generations of feet. Everything built by hand, with peg and lashing and fitted stone, no nails to speak of, no straight lines that weren't coaxed from crooked wood. Smoke should rise from a place like this, but there are no chimneys smoking. The cold is just lived with. The wood is too precious to burn all at once.

And between, through, over, and inside all of that building is green.

Not the green of summer fields. The green of things that love cold and damp and bright stillness. Moss so thick it rounds the corners of the houses. Sphagnum and reindeer-lichen and liverwort quilted over every shingle, every stone. Dwarf birch and Arctic willow, no higher than your knee, but ancient and woody and knotted, growing sideways out of walls. Ferns in every crack where the condensation gathers - dense, black-green, furled tight against the cold, but abundant, glossy, living. Turf roofs that have become meadows in miniature. On the north-facing inner slope, where water seeps, there is a hanging garden of frost-ferns and white saxifrage that never flowers, just holds itself open. The town is not built *in* vegetation; the vegetation and the town have grown into a single, dense mat. You can't tell where a wall ends and a root begins.

Because there is water. Not much. No lake, no river running through. This is damp country, not wet. In the mornings - if morning means anything where the light never changes - the cold air condenses on the stone walls and drips. It beads on the moss and hangs there, bright as glass. Here and there a spring finds a weakness in the limestone and seeps out in a dark stain, collects in a shallow stone basin cut by hands a hundred years ago, sits still, and is gone again into the moss. Ice forms in clear lenses over the basins and never quite thickens enough to walk on. Under the boardwalks, the earth is always dark and moist to the touch. You could wring water from a handful of moss if you wanted. The rest of the bowl stays dry enough to live in, but never dry enough to dust.

And it is quiet.

That is the strangest thing after the brightness. For a packed town, lush with life, it should be noisy. It isn't. Sound is swallowed whole. The moss, the snow-rim, the close walls, the cold dense air - everything damps it. Footsteps on turf and wood are a soft thud. A door sliding on its wooden groove sighs. People, when you see them - muffled in wool and hide the color of lichen - move without calling out. They nod. They work with quiet hands. Even the fauna respects it.

And there is fauna, everywhere if you are still. The ptarmigan that nest in the turf roofs, pure white save for their eyes and beaks, watching you without alarm. Hares the size of dogs curled in hollows between houses, their ears tipped with frost. Under the boardwalk, lemmings rustle like a secret. Fat, slow bumblebees, black and amber, crawl rather than fly in the cold, moving from fern to fern, humming at a pitch so low you feel it more than hear it. Beetles with bright, hard carapaces click along the stones. None of them cry out. The birds don't sing so much as they make a single, soft *tok* now and then, and fall silent again, as if not to disturb the air.

Nothing seems to change. That is the rule here.

The light stays. The cold stays. The moss grows, but so slowly you'd need to mark a stone to notice in a lifetime. The houses settle a fraction lower each generation as the turf thickens over them. The people mend what needs mending with the same wood, the same stone, the same careful joints. Frost forms and melts and forms again in the same pattern on the same basin. A hare's tracks from yesterday will still be there next week, crisp-edged, because no wind comes into the bowl to erase them and no sun comes to melt them. It is not frozen in the sense of dead - everything is alive, abundant, occupied to bursting - but it is in equilibrium. Weathered into itself. Established. A place that reached its shape a long time ago and decided to keep it.

If you stand on the rim and look down, you get the full, odd perspective - the whole town at once, like a model or a terrarium, lit from within, dense as a honeycomb, green and brown and white under the bright cold glare, absolutely still, absolutely silent, holding its breath. And if you climb down the worn steps cut into the south wall, duck under the low lintel where the willow has grown over the entry, you are immediately inside it - inside the warmth that isn't warmth but closeness, the smell of damp wool and old wood and living moss, the soft give of the ground underfoot, the packed-together life of it pressing in from all sides.

It doesn't open up as you enter. It gets smaller, and more detailed, and more complete, until the plain above might as well not exist.
