It is below.

Not deep in the planetary sense, not cavern-system deep — just below the skin of the world. A wound that never closed. From the surface you would almost miss it: a long, soft collapse in the laterite plain, where the ground gave way in some previous century and settled into a loaf-shaped hollow, maybe two hundred meters long, forty across, twenty down. The edges are not cliffs. They are slopes, established, weathered into their final shape long ago. No raw rock face, no fresh scar. The lip is rounded and crumbling, held together by roots, and the descent is a slow stair of slumped terraces and slick clay. Everything here has had time to find the angle at which it will stay.

The air falls into it and stays there. It is hot — not desert hot, not sun hot, but low, wet, mineral hot. The heat comes up from below as much as down from above. Geothermal. The earth exhales. At noon the sun is a white smear high above the rim and its light comes down in a single, slanted column that never reaches the bottom, diffusing instead in the steam. For the rest of the hollow it is dim, permanently. Not darkness — a brown-green dusk, like late afternoon under a storm that never breaks. The light that exists is secondary: the dull orange safety bulbs still burning on their industrial cages, half-swallowed by vines; the faint, sourceless glow of the condensation itself; the reflected sheen off wet leaves.

It is damp, but not wet. There is no standing water here, no pool, no stream. That is the first strange thing. For all its lushness, for all the heat and the steam, the hollow is not a swamp. Water exists as behavior, not as a body. It beads on every surface and falls again. It sweats out of the small boreholes in the rock where the old geothermal taps still breathe. It condenses on the underside of every leaf — and there are thousands — gathers until it is heavy, and drops. Drip. Drip. Drip onto a lower leaf, onto rust, onto clay. It runs in thin, dark rivulets down the terraced walls that evaporate before they can join anything. The ground underfoot is not mud, but damp humus that never quite dries, never quite saturates. If you press your palm to the wall, your hand comes away wet. If you dig a hole a foot deep, it will be dry again by evening. The water is always passing through.

And everything grows.

The vegetation is impossibly dense for a place with no sun and no topsoil. It is a hanging jungle, a vertical thicket. From the rim, long curtains of pale, heat-loving vines descend thirty feet before finding purchase. On the terraces, broad-leafed things with waxy, black-green leaves overlap each other until no clay is visible. Ferns the size of doors crowd shoulder to shoulder. Fat, trunkless palms push out from the walls themselves. There are no flowers the way you expect flowers — instead the plants make fleshy, closed bulbs that split along a seam and exhale a puff of rust-colored spores, which hang in the hot air for a long time before settling. Everything layers. The density is near-touching. To move through the hollow you do not walk, you part. Leaves brush your face, your shoulders, your knees at once. Roots braid over roots. Even the air is crowded — with steam, with spore dust, with the smell of green rot that never quite completes because there is nothing to complete it.

There is no animal life. None. No insects stitching the air, no birds calling from the rim, no lizards on the pipes. The absence is absolute and, after a minute, loud. You keep listening for the buzz that should be there in this much green and heat, and it never comes. The leaves are perfect, uneaten, untorn except by their own weight. Pollination happens without couriers: the bulbs shudder and split on their own timed heat, the ferns release spores directly into the damp draft that cycles slowly around the hollow. Things fall and decay by fungus and bacteria alone, a slow, chemical composting that smells sweet and metallic. The hollow feels like a greenhouse left after the staff disappeared — the plants kept their bargain, the animals did not.

Because it was built.

Scattered through the green, at the scale of a hamlet or a large room, are the remains of industry. Not a city, not a complex — a few purposeful things left to be taken. A central manifold still stands near the lowest point: a squat, riveted boiler house of black iron and flaking white paint, its four great pipes plunging into the earth where the heat comes from. It still functions, after a fashion, though no one tends it. Pressure builds and releases on a cycle too slow to time without a watch. Catwalks of rusted grating, half-collapsed, thread between the terraces, now serving as trellises. A control booth with its windows gone, its levers grown over with a thick, felt-like moss that has learned to mimic the shape of dials. A chain hoist frozen mid-lift, its hook completely enclosed in a fleshy vine that has grown around it like a fist. A transformer on a concrete pad, humming. These are not ruins. Ruins imply event. This is settlement. The machines and the plants have arrived at an equilibrium: the iron rusts at exactly the rate the roots can absorb the oxide, the steam keeps the leaves from burning, the leaves keep the sun from cracking the iron.

And it is loud.

Not with life, but with the slow loudness of patient systems. The transformer hums at 60 hertz, a deep, chest-filling thrum that makes the damp air vibrate. The manifold breathes — a long inhale of pressure every seven or eight minutes, then a shuddering, steaming exhalation through a relief valve that screams, briefly, like a kettle left too long, and then subsides into ticking. Water drips everywhere, but because the space is enclosed and the walls are close, every drip is amplified, multiplied by a thousand leaves into a constant, arrhythmic patter, like rain on a tin roof that never stops. Clay settles. Iron creaks as it expands in the heat. The vines, growing, make a sound too — a faint, almost inaudible popping as their bladders swell and split. You cannot speak softly here. The hollow fills your ears until you forget what quiet was.

The weirdness is not that any one thing is impossible. It is that the place obeys a slightly offset set of rules and does so consistently.

Light without a source gathers on the undersides of things rather than the tops. Water moves upward as often as down — you will see beads of moisture climb a vine against gravity, toward the heat of a bulb, and vanish into the leaf. The boiler house, though clearly abandoned for decades, is warm to the touch and the gauges inside still tremble faintly, as if the pressure below is being regulated by the roots themselves, which have found the hairline cracks in the pipes and grown *into* the steam, lining the interior with green. If you stay long enough you notice the growth has a direction: everything leans very slightly inward, toward the manifold, even shoots on the far wall. The hollow is a room, and the room has a center.

Time here is slow. Nothing happens quickly and nothing needs to. A leaf unfurls over a week. A pipe rusts through over a decade and is immediately bandaged by a root. Spore clouds hang in the air for hours before the warm draft finally carries them a few feet. The steam cycle is the clock, and it is patient. You get the sense that if you came back in fifty years, the catwalk would be an inch lower, the moss in the control booth a little thicker over the levers, a few more bulbs split, but otherwise the same dim, hot, dripping, humming, crowded green room would be exactly as it is — established, balanced, weirdly and perfectly alive without ever having needed an animal to witness it.
