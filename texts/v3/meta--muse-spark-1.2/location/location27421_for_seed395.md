It is low.

Not low like a valley between higher places — low like the world itself decided to be low, a basin so broad and so level that you only understand its lowness by the behavior of the sky. The air sits heavy here, warm and still. At noon the sun is normal, white-gold, not the bleached hammer of a high desert, not the thin blue of altitude — a temperate, everyday sun that throws short, solid shadows. Because the land does not rise, the sky has to do all the work, and it does; clouds form low and travel far, assembling and disassembling over days, a weather system the size of a sea moving over a place that has no sea.

This is new land.

You can tell by underfoot. It has not been smoothed. There are no rolling hills, no softened ridges, no river meanders cutting back on themselves. The mineral skin of the place is still raw from its making. In some stretches it is basalt, black and ropy, frozen in coils and bubbles where it cooled last year or last thousand years, still sharp enough to skin a knee. In others it is salt and alkaline silt, a pale crust laid down when water left — a void-flat, not white but yellow-white, gray-white, the color of paper left in the sun, cracked into polygons as big as rooms. The edges of those cracks are still crisp, vertical, not yet sanded down by wind or flood. Where the crust has buckled, it has lifted into plates and shards, two or three feet high, like broken pavement. Nothing has been weathered into soil yet; soil is still being invented here, grain by grain, from rock dust and fallen leaf and frass.

And yet it is not barren.

That is the first contradiction that resolves when you look closer. There is almost no water — not in the way we expect water. No rivers. No lakes, no ponds, no creeks cutting the plain. No standing shine anywhere to the horizon. What fell as a shallow inland sea a long age ago evaporated and never returned. The ground is dry to the touch. Salt stings the tongue. If you dig, you find dryness for a long way down. But the air, warm and low, holds a little mercy. Before dawn, it condenses.

Every night, as the vast flat radiates its heat into space, a breath of dew forms and not much more — a beading on every surface, a frosting that never becomes a puddle. By mid-morning it is gone. And that is enough.

Because life here learned to live without drinking.

The vegetation is not sparse, not the lonely scrub you expect on dry mineral. It is packed, lush, and dense from horizon to horizon, a low jungle with no trees as you would picture them. Nothing looks thirsty. Instead everything looks swollen and sealed. The dominant forms are succulent and waxy, but none of them are small. Trunks like fluted columns, pale green and gray, ten or fifteen feet tall, store months of that dawn-mist inside their flesh. Their skin is rubbery, dusted with a fine bloom that keeps the sun off. From them sprout not so many leaves but thick, overlapping tongues — fleshy, upright, the size of doors — that don't droop but stand together, shingling over each other until no ground can be seen. Between the columnar forms are matted species that spread horizontally, forming dense, springy platforms a foot above the salt, their roots never reaching deep water because there is none; instead they are a net of aerial roots that comb the morning air, velvet with hairs that catch dew.

It is a forest that does not need rain. It recycles its own transpiration. The canopy, though only twenty feet high at its highest, is completely closed. Light comes through not in shafts but diffused, green and warm, filtered through translucent flesh. Underneath, the air is warmer still, close, faintly aromatic — resin and salt and the yeasty smell of growth. There is no bare earth anywhere. Every crack between salt plates has been colonized; every basalt rope is pried apart by roots; every vertical wall has something growing flat against it. If a piece of mineral is exposed for more than a week, something finds it.

What finds it fastest are the builders.

This is a city, but no one built it.

The density is not just plant density. It is habitation density. From above you would mistake the canopy for unbroken vegetation, but at ground level you see it is organized. For as far as you can walk — and you could walk for weeks without leaving it — the ground is a lattice of construction, all of it grown or gathered, none of it manufactured.

Colonies overlap colonies. The analogy nearest to hand is termite and ant and weaver and coral, scaled up and knitted together. Towers of carton and mud and resin, six or eight feet high, rise everywhere, not scattered but contiguous, their walls touching. They are not haphazard mounds; they are facades, streets. Between the fleshy trunks, arches of cemented mineral and chewed fiber span the gaps, forming covered avenues just wide enough for traffic. The plants themselves are architecture — many trunks have been hollowed while alive, their living walls forming chambers that are tended from inside, scarred with the smooth marks of mandibles and fingers. In the salty reaches, domes of crystallized salt and bound silt, secreted and mortared grain by grain, cluster like a tight-packed town of igloos, each with a perfect round doorway facing the morning wind to catch dew.

There are strata. On the ground, the primary level of tunnels and galleries runs through the root mat, cool and dark. A foot above, the platform growth forms a second street level, its interwoven stems creating a continuous floor that sags and bounces. Higher, at about head height, the fleshy leaves have been stitched together with silk and fiber to make ceilings and partitions — airy, lit rooms that sway. It is urban in the truest sense: infrastructure, neighborhoods, transit. Narrow, polished runways, kept clear by constant feet and claws, braid between all of it, and at every intersection there is a widening, a plaza where the canopy opens a hand's breadth to let a column of normal daylight in.

No metal. No fired clay. No glass. No tools beyond body. Every wall is piss and spit and wax and mud; every beam is a living branch coaxed sideways over a generation. And it continues for a continent. The scale does not allow for a center or an edge. Over the horizon the pattern repeats, variations in material but not in intensity — basalt districts where towers are darker and pitted, salt districts where everything is paler and crisper. Somewhere far away, the weather being born over this flat is raining its meager dew onto the same arrangement, and the same work continues.

For this is not a quiet place because it is empty. It is quiet because it is full, and busy in a quiet way.

Fauna is everywhere, abundant beyond counting, but you hear almost nothing. The sheer density of soft, fleshy plant mass absorbs sound. There is no echo. Footsteps are muffled by the springy ground. The animals themselves have evolved for silence — they have to, packed so tight.

They are small to medium, mostly, though none are rare: things like scaled woodlice as big as tortoises rolling slowly along the avenues, grazing algae from salt walls; lizards with translucent skin, their bellies visibly full of stored water, clinging to column trunks; birds that don't sing but click, weaving leaf-fiber with their beaks in near-absolute silence; beetles bright as enamel, moving in files up the towers carrying white crumbs of fungus; long, many-legged things that tend the fungal gardens inside the mounds, farming the only true decay in a system that otherwise wastes nothing. You will not hear a chorus or a howl. What you hear is a constant low rustle, like wind in corn, but it is not wind — it is the movement of thousands of bodies on dry fiber, the soft pat of feet on carton, the faint creak of a living trunk adjusting its weight. If you stand still, something will pass within inches of your ankle, then another, then another.

Everything is active. This is not a still diorama. The place is under construction the way a reef is under construction, which is to say perpetually. On the edge of a salt dome, fresh pellets of mortar are still damp where they were placed minutes ago. A platform vine extends its tip a visible fraction of an inch as it seeks the next anchor. A column trunk splits its skin and peels away a papery layer to expose new green beneath. The insects strip, carry, build, prune; the lizards hunt and are hunted; the birds stitch and pull tight. Predation happens without drama, a quick snap and a rustle in the leaves. Growth happens without pause. The dew of this morning is already stored, moved, sweated out and re-caught.

The rule of this world is simple and absolute: nothing leaves. Water is too scarce to waste, material too precious to discard. So everything is looped. Leaves that fall are shredded before they hit the ground. The dead are carried inside and rendered. Salt that crumbles is re-cemented. Even air is passed from chamber to chamber, laden with pheromones instead of noise, messages that tell where to build next, which corridor is blocked, where a new dome should go.

If you stay through dusk, the light goes level and gold and then leaves without ceremony — normal light, normal dusk, no auroras or strange suns. The temperature drops just enough for the air to give up its water again. By midnight a fine glitter has settled on every edge — not frost but dew, beading on wax, on silk, on salt crystals still sharp and new-born. The packed city drinks it in silence, with a million tiny mouths, and swells a little fuller by morning, ready to add another ring, another wall, another street to the vast, low, warm, humming, almost-silent sprawl that is still, geologically speaking, learning how to be ground.
