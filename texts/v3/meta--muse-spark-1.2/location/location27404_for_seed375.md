You come over the lip and the place is already there, complete.

It is not large. You can see its entirety from where you stand, which is part of why it takes a moment to understand what you're seeing — the far wall is no more than three or four hundred meters away, a low, sharp rim that encloses the whole thing like the edge of a bowl that has just been broken out of the ground. The elevation is low, lower than the plain you walked to get here. You have stepped down maybe ten meters and the air got cooler when you did.

It is a young depression. That is the wrong word, but the shape is young. Nothing here has been smoothed. The rim is not worn down but fractured, a black glassy mineral that came up hot and cooled too fast, still holding the stress lines where it cracked. Inside, the floor is not sand but a flat of pale salt and dry clay, pressed hard and white as plaster, with a faint, uneven sheen — not wet, never wet — a kind of mineral oil that sweats out of the ground in the cold and never evaporates, beading in the pores of the rock. The ground is too sharp to be comfortable underfoot; even the salt has edges. Wind has not had time to round anything yet.

The light is dim. Not night, not overcast. The sky above the bowl is a flat, deep violet-grey, as if the sun is far away or behind a translucence you cannot see. The light comes from everywhere and nowhere, diffuse and cool, without shadows that point the same way. Your own shadow falls once, faintly, to your left, but the spires in the center throw theirs to the right. No one corrects it.

Because there is a city here.

That is the second thing that takes a moment, because at first you think the structures in the middle are geology. They are not. They are organized. They are urban in the truest sense — not a ruin, not a scatter, but a dense, intentional settlement scaled for something that walks upright and needs streets and doors and roofs. Only nothing in it was built.

It was grown.

From the salt-flat floor, a thicket of towers and arches and joined dwellings has extruded itself. The material is matte and dry, a chitinous mineral, somewhere between coral and fungus and termite mound — layered, porous, ribbed with growth lines. Cream and ash-grey and rust-pink. The walls are breathing surfaces; if you put your hand close you can feel a faint cool draft being pulled inward through thousands of tiny pores. The whole cluster is maybe the size of a hamlet, fifty or sixty structures fused at their bases, some as high as a cathedral's nave but slender, hollow, leaning very slightly toward each other as if listening. Between them, narrow canyons and plazas open, perfectly proportioned for passage, for gathering. There are openings at regular heights that are unmistakably windows and doorways, but no sills, no frames, no glass. No straight lines at all if you look close — every edge is a curve that resolved into an edge.

No one made it with tools. There are no tools here. No cut marks, no joints, no nails, no wire, no smelting. Even the young rock of the rim is untouched. What technology there is, is biology. The city is biology. It is still active.

You can see it moving if you stay still long enough, which you will, because the place enforces stillness. On the scale of minutes, a buttress on the easternmost tower swells a few millimeters and pales. A thin, translucent membrane extends itself across an alleyway three meters up and then retracts, leaving a faint filament hanging. At the base of the largest form, a low aperture dilates — an exhale of cold, odorless air sighs out and stirs the salt dust at your feet, the only thing that moves quickly here — and then closes, sealed with a fresh, wet-looking film that hardens in seconds to the same matte crust.

The vegetation is sparse and complicit with this. It does not grow *on* the city so much as *with* it. In the cracks of the salt flat, wire-thin black stems hold single, flat leaves perpendicular to the dim light, like solar panels that have forgotten what a sun is. Pale, lichenous sheets, almost the same color as the salt, spread in rosettes a hand's width across; they pulse faintly, lighter and darker, on a slow cycle you can only catch from the corner of your eye. On the leeward sides of the towers, tufts of dry, feathered growths hang without wind, waiting.

There is almost no fauna, and what there is respects the silence. This is a silent place. Not the silence of emptiness but the silence of a sealed room. Sound does not carry. Your footstep on the salt produces a small, deadened *tick* and then is gone, absorbed by the porous walls. The air itself seems to dampen. Far above, a single glider — flat, diamond-shaped, the size of a coat, made of the same translucent membrane as the alley film — circles the rim without ever flapping. It is translucent enough that you see the violet-grey sky through it. It makes no cry. Every few minutes it tilts and drops a little lower to taste the exhalation from the city, then rises again.

The density of the place is strange. For a city, it is hollow. Vast intervals of nothing between each thing. A plaza large enough for a market holds only a single, centered spire as thin as a wrist, vibrating faintly. A tower with a hundred windows faces a wall with no windows at all. The streets are too wide for the sparse life that uses them. You could walk for a minute between structures and not pass anything living. It feels like a city built for a much larger population that never arrived, or that left, or that is still growing into its own size.

And yet it is not abandoned. The rules here are not your rules.

First: nothing evaporates. Moisture does not exist as a cycle. There is no rain, no dew, no river memory. Any liquid is mineral and stays. Things do not dry out; they cure.

Second: growth is construction. The city does not decay so much as it molts. At the foot of several towers you can see collapsed crescents of the same chitinous material, already half-buried in salt, already being undergrown by the same pale lichen that will digest them.

Third: light has weight here, or direction has. That is the weirdest part and the one your eyes keep trying to correct. The dimness is not uniform. In certain alleys the air itself looks thicker, slightly bent, as if a lens were suspended invisibly. If you stand at the precise center of the largest plaza and look up, the rim appears much closer on one side than the other, though you know it is a circle. The city leans into that thickness. Its new growth always favors the side where the light pools.

It is cool. The coolness is not a wind but a property of the salt, which holds the cold of the world below and radiates it upward. Your breath does not fog, the air is too dry — there is no water to fog — but the inside of your nose feels chilled and clean, as if you were breathing through stone.

If you stay until the light dims a fraction more — and it will, on a cycle that has nothing to do with day or night — the pores of the city will begin to close for its resting phase, each tower sealing itself with a faint, almost inaudible series of *clicks*, like tiny doors shutting deep inside. The glider will settle onto the highest spire and fold itself flat until it looks like part of the roof. The black wire plants will turn their single leaves edge-on to conserve whatever they collect.

And then for a long interval, nothing will appear to happen at all. The city will hold its breath. The salt flat will hold its sheen. The silence will become so complete you hear the faint thud of your own heart, and then even that seems to be absorbed.

Until, without sound, a new blister of wall begins to push itself out from an empty plaza where there was only flat salt before, pearl-white and soft, steaming faintly not with heat but with cold, beginning at its tip to differentiate into a doorway that no one has asked for yet.
