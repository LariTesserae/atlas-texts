It is high before it is anything else.

Not a peak, but a table — a broad, unfinished plateau lifted into the thin part of the atmosphere where the air has weight and bite. The rock here hasn't learned to be mountain yet. It was pushed up raw and recent, a black glass plain still warm in its deep seams, cooling by the thousand-year. There has been no time to carve valleys or soften edges. Everything is embryonic: sharp fault-steps like fresh-cut stairs, basalt that broke into columns and never weathered round, fields of cinder and obsidian rubble that haven't settled into soil. The ground is not ground, it is still construction site. Scree hasn't sorted itself. Cliffs end in clean vertical shears that weep.

Because everything weeps here.

The cold is a deep, dry cold that should have locked the water solid, and at first you think it has — the whole plateau glitters with frost, with rime on every angle. But the water does not freeze. Not properly. The air is saturated to wringing. At this height the clouds do not pass over, they live here, pressed against the rock. They condense the moment they touch anything. The city, the stone, your own exposed skin — all of it beads and runs. Water gathers on the undersides of ledges and falls without ever having rained. It seeps out of the raw basalt itself, out of vesicles and cooling cracks, a slow mineral sweat. Braided runnels of near-freezing brine thread the plateau, never deep enough to be rivers, never still enough to be lakes, but everywhere. Shallow pans between the new lava ripples fill and spill into each other. The sound of trickling is everywhere at once, overlaid on everything else, the sound of a world draining very, very slowly off its own back.

There is no green. Not a blade, not a moss. No plant ever took hold on rock this young and cold and dark. What covers the stone instead is mineral and aftermath. A thin, dark biofilm — not plant, not fungus quite, a slick, almost varnish-like sheath of bacteria that drinks the dissolved iron in the seep-water and stains the rock wine-black and rust-orange wherever the water runs longest. And then construction. Because this is not a wilderness. This is a city, or the first draft of one.

It sprawls across the plateau for kilometers, town-sized, valley-sized, but without a valley to contain it. From any rise you can see it extend past the immediate lamplight into the dark, a low, dense lattice. Not built *on* the plateau so much as *with* it. The first foundations were poured directly into the cooling basalt, and the newer structures are still extruding. The tech here is past architecture. Scaffolds assemble themselves. Walls are grown from folded stone and carbon glass in slow, patient accretions. Some buildings have no seams — they were precipitated out of the air by field-looms that hang above them like inverted spiderwebs, humming. Streets are not cut, they are left between growths. Levels stack without planning; walkways bridge gaps that were never intended to be gaps, threading between towers that lean toward each other until they almost touch. Density is the rule. Everything is near-touching, layered, balconied over balconied. You can stand in a plaza that is also a cistern, also a thoroughfare, also the roof of a lower plaza that you can hear through the grates.

It is dark here with a completeness that high places shouldn't have. The sky is there but it gives nothing. Starlight is smothered by the low permanent cloud. There is no sun in this season, or the sun is so far and so oblique behind perpetual overcast that it is a rumor. The only light is made, and it is not made for seeing far. The city runs cold-white along its under-edges — running lights set into curbings, handrails, the lips of water channels, just enough to keep you from stepping into a drainage cut. Most light, though, is animal.

Everything is covered. That is the first thing your hands understand before your eyes do. Every surface that is not moving water is occupied. The fauna here do not live *in* the city. They *are* the city's second skin.

They are not birds, not mammals. At this cold and without plants, nothing with a familiar metabolism should be here in such numbers. They are pale, jointed, slow things — think of isopods scaled up to the size of a hand, or down to a thumbnail, overlapping. Cream-white and translucent, with dark eyes on stalks and feathery gills that flutter in the wet air. Some have thin, almost glassy wings they never use to fly in the thin cold; they use them to shiver and make sound. They carpet the walls in mats three deep, they pack the undersides of every bridge, they line the water channels like living cobbles, filtering the brine. Larger ones, as long as your forearm, haul themselves along the vertical faces with hooked feet, carrying their young in translucent pouches. When you walk, they part around your boots and close again behind you. There are so many that the stone is rarely visible. The city looks soft from a distance, as if upholstered.

And they are loud. That is the contradiction that takes a moment to resolve. For all that each individual moves with glacial patience — a leg lifting, planting, lifting again over minutes — the aggregate is deafening. Millions of chitinous legs clicking on stone. Millions of gill-plates fanning. Stridulation — the winged ones rubbing glass wings together not in song but in constant, atonal chatter, a white-noise hiss that rises and falls like cicadas the size of a district. Beneath it, the deeper knocking of the larger carriers as they tap the basalt to signal, and the endless drip and gurgle of water through the mass of bodies, and beneath that, the low, patient thrum of the field-looms building the city, a sound you feel in your molars more than you hear. It is loud the way a waterfall is loud: not aggressive, just too much to be near without raising your voice, and constant.

The weirdness is not that any of this exists. It is that it all holds together slightly wrong. Gravity here is a suggestion the builders have argued with. Some of the newer towers are too thin to stand, and don't — they stand anyway, held in tension by fields you can see only when condensed water beads along them and hangs in midair in perfect lines and planes, as if the air has edges. Water sometimes runs *up* a channel for a few meters where a field guides it before spilling down again. The dark is not empty dark; it has texture, a faint bending at the periphery where heat exchangers dump waste warmth straight into the upper atmosphere in silent, vertical pillars of shimmer that make the clouds glow from within without ever casting light downward. The cold should kill the swarm, but the swarm clusters around the exchangers' feet where the stone is warm to the touch — the only warmth for leagues — and farms the bacterial varnish where it grows thickest. Nothing eats plants, because there are none. Everything eats film, and eats each other, and eats the city's own shed — the field-looms slough carbon dust that the smallest fauna filter from the condensation like plankton.

Change is slow. That is the final rule. For all the sound and motion, nothing happens fast. The water carves a millimeter of basalt a year. The fauna migrate a few meters a season, following the slow creep of warmth as the plateau cools unevenly. The city grows at the speed of crystal — a new arch buds over a plaza over weeks, a new cistern opens like a blister in the rock over months. Even the sound, loud as it is, is slow — a long, repeating phrase stretched over hours. You can stand on a high walkway, slick with condensation, with the hum in your bones and the clicking enclosing you on every side, look out over the lit, crowded, weeping plain receding into dark and cloud, and feel time itself pooled shallow like the water, not flowing, just present, cold, and breathing very slowly.
