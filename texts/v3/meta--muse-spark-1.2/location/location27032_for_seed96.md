High and bright and cold enough to make your breath visible, even at noon.

You are standing on something that was once a mountain and is now not a mountain at all, though it keeps the shape of one. From a distance it would have looked like geology — a broad, steep mass rising out of a high plateau — but as you get closer the line resolves. It is all edge. It is all step.

The entire slope has been worked.

There is no natural ground left to stand on. Every surface is laid, fitted, cut, or stacked. The mountain has been turned into a stair so vast its top is lost in brightness and its bottom falls away into pale air. Terraces thirty feet deep and hundreds long, retaining walls of dark basalt without mortar, fitted so tightly you cannot slip a fingernail between them. Between the terraces, narrower stairs, ramps, spillways, cisterns, channels no wider than your hand. And then more terraces. It goes on past where you can walk in a day — a whole valley rebuilt as a single structure, a town the size of a watershed but with no houses, only infrastructure.

It is primitive work. You can see it in every stone. No perfect cuts, no glass, no metal to speak of except for the dull iron stains where a wedge split a block. The marks of hammers are still sharp on the faces. Wooden mallets, stone mauls, rope drag-marks, the chipped half-circles where a hand drill was started and abandoned. The quarry is still here, fresh, at the crown — a raw, stepped bite out of the summit where the rock is lighter, unweathered, its edges still knife-sharp. This place is young. Nothing has had time to soften. Corners have not rounded. Dust has not filled the joints. It looks like it was finished last season, or is still being finished, though no workers are visible.

The light is punishing. At this elevation the sky is a thin, hard blue, almost violet at the zenith, and the sun is white and close. It throws hard shadows with razor edges. The stone, where it is dry, is pale grey and almost reflective, so the whole mountain glitters. You squint involuntarily. Colors are bleached out except for the dark wet stone, which is startlingly black.

Because it is wet. Incredibly wet for a place made of bare rock under a bright sun.

Water is everywhere, but not in any body you can name. There are no rivers, no lakes, no marsh plants to frame them — there is nothing living here at all, no green, no lichen, no bird, no insect to break the mineral quiet. Instead the mountain weeps.

The air itself is condensing. Cool — not freezing, but a persistent, high-country cool that sits on the skin — meets that bright sun and pulls moisture straight out of the sky. It beads on every stone. It sheets down every wall in a thin, glassy film. Every horizontal surface holds a shallow mirror, a half-inch of water trembling in the wind. Every vertical joint drips. You hear it before you see the scale of it: a constant, patient ticking, like a thousand clocks out of sync. Drip from a terrace lip into the cistern below. Trickle through a hand-cut runnel. Sigh as overflow finds the next channel.

The builders understood this. The whole mountain is a machine for catching water that was never meant to hold life.

The cisterns are everywhere, packed so densely there is almost no unoccupied plane. Round, square, long and narrow, some no bigger than a basin, some large enough to lie down in, all interlinked by notches and sluices and spill-lips at slightly different heights. They are full to the brim, and then slightly overfull, so water is always moving, but slowly, with almost no gradient. It creeps rather than flows. One cistern sighs a thread of water into the next, which sighs into the next, a chain of infinities working its way down the mountain one inch at a time. If you watch a single channel for a long time you realize the water is moving. If you look away, it seems still.

Where the walls meet, mineral has started to precipitate — thin curtains of pale calcite, like wax, still soft to the touch. In the brightest cisterns, the bottoms are covered in a fine, orange silt that is not soil, just rock dust settled out of the water. No algae grows on it. Nothing grows at all. The water is too cold, too mineral, too new. Even the stains that look like moss at a distance are just water-darkened stone.

There are no animals to drink it. No sound of frogs, no dragonflies. The only sound is water and wind and stone: the faint hiss of wind over a thousand wet edges, the drip and trickle, the occasional tick of a stone contracting in the sun. Your own footsteps are loud on the wet pavers. The air is so thin and so clear that distant terraces look close enough to touch, until you walk toward them and realize there are ten more interleaved levels between you and them.

And there is something quietly wrong about it that you only notice after standing still.

The water does not reflect correctly. In the stillest cisterns, the sky reflected is slightly deeper than the sky above, and the sun's reflection lingers a second too long when a cloud passes. Or maybe it's the other way around — the light is too bright for how cool the air feels, as if the brightness is not heat. The shadows are too sharp for surfaces that are always wet. You expect mist, but there is none, just that impossible clarity, and water appearing from nowhere, endlessly, without rain.

If you place your hand on a wall, it is cold, wet, and thrumming faintly, not with machinery but with the weight of water behind it, seeping through joints that were never meant to be watertight.

The whole place feels like it is waiting for a purpose that never arrived. A reservoir for a city that was never built. An offering to a rain that never needed inviting. Or a tool whose users left, knowing it would keep working without them — slowly, patiently, with no need for tending, because the gradient is so weak it will take centuries for the channels to clog, centuries for the sharp quarry edges to dull, centuries for the sun to evaporate what condenses each night.

You can walk for hours along the spillways and never find an end, never find a natural slope, never find a patch of earth that was not placed there by hands. Everywhere the same packed, hand-made, water-covered geometry, repeating and varying, bright and cool and quiet, extending past the curve of the mountain until distance turns it back into what looks, again, like nature.
