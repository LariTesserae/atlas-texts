It is not a range. It is a room.

You understand that first by the air. Thin enough that the first breath you take at the rim feels shallow in your chest, a little too quick, and then warm. That is the wrongness that tells you where you are. At this height the air should be cold and stripped clean, it should hurt your teeth. Instead it is warm and heavy, soft as lowland air after rain.

You are standing on the lip of a bowl cut high into the spine of the mountains, so high the peaks around you are not peaks anymore but the broken teeth of the world. The sky here is a normal blue, not the dark violet you expect at extreme altitude — close, bright, sun at full strength that makes the colors true — but the horizon drops away on every side into a sheer fall of two, three thousand meters of empty space. Clouds are not above you. They are below you, a white sea lapping at the cliffs. You are on an island.

The bowl itself is young. That is obvious in the rock.

There has been no time to soften it. The last uplift shoved this place up fast and left it raw. The walls are not slopes, they are fresh fracture. Black basalt and pale granite still sharp enough to cut your hand, in slabs and knives and leaning pillars where the mountain split. No talus fans, no rounded shoulders. The eastern wall is a single clean scarp, fifty meters high, still showing the chatter marks where it tore. The stone weeps. Everywhere.

Water is not in a river here. It is in the rock and the air at once.

It condenses out of nothing. The warm updraft from the valley below hits the cold cut of the barren peaks around the bowl and wrings itself out, so the atmosphere itself is always on the verge of rain without ever quite raining. Mist forms on your arms. The scarp sweats in beads that gather and run, darkening the stone in vertical stripes. Higher up where the sun hits, the rock steams faintly. Underfoot, the ground gives.

The floor of the bowl is not floor at all, but a sponge. Maybe thirty meters across, maybe fifty — you can see from one wall to the other without turning your head much, a cathedral nave open to the sky. Peat and shattered stone and moss in layers so thick your boot sinks to the ankle. Water is right at the surface, pressing up with each step. Every depression is a mirror. The sound of your foot is not a crunch but a sigh and a suck. Springs break out at the base of the walls in cold, clear threads and vanish again into the vegetation before they can become streams.

Because it is lush to the point of choking.

At this altitude there should be lichen, a few stubborn cushions. Here there is a jungle in miniature. Not trees — nothing has had time or space to become a tree — but a dense, layered mat of things that grow on top of each other. Giant mosses like ferns, ferns like small trees, bromeliads with their throats full of water, vines as thick as your wrist running horizontally more than vertically, everything flowering in low, waxy reds and yellows that can be seen through mist. Leaves are broad and slick with condensation. The air beneath the canopy is green-filtered and humid. The warmth is trapped here, held in by the walls and by something else. You have to push fronds aside to move. The density is not that there are many species — though there are — it is that there is no empty volume. Root over root over rock over leaf. If you stand still you can feel it growing, slow, patient, but insistent, closing any gap you make.

And it is growing *through*.

The built things are not a village. They are not ruins either. They are scattered and they are working.

There are three of them, maybe four if you count the cistern. All industrial, all too heavy for where they are.

The largest sits against the weeping scarp, built directly into it, using the fresh-cut wall as its back. A pumphouse and generator block in riveted iron and poured concrete, paint gone to rust-red and verdigris. It is small as factories go — you could walk its perimeter in a minute — but it is dense with purpose. A boiler the size of a truck, a diesel that mutters and coughs, a tangle of pipes and pressure vessels and a transformer that hums so loud your teeth buzz. Black insulated cables run from it like vines, stapled into the rock, down into the peat, up to the little assay shed and the ore-crush and the skeletal headframe over a shaft that drops straight down into the mountain.

It should not be here. Nothing should have been able to haul this much iron up this high. And yet there it is, breathing.

The second structure is just a corrugated shed, open on one side, where sheets of perforated steel are stacked and a workbench holds vises and coils of copper wire. The third is the cistern — a concrete trough collecting the weep-water from the scarp, lip thick with green slime, water overflowing constantly in a sheet that the moss has learned to drink.

Nothing is abandoned, but nothing is tended either. No one is here right now. There are signs someone was here this morning — a pair of rubber boots kicked off by the boiler, still warm, a tin cup with tea skin on it, a wrench left on top of the transformer where the vibration is slowly walking it toward the edge. Faint marks everywhere else where people tried to make this place more habitable and gave up: rebar hammered into rock and then bent, a trail of flat stones that the peat has swallowed, a clothesline between two pipe stanchions now claimed by a flowering vine that has grown through the pegs.

This is why it is warm.

The machines never really stop. Even on low idle, the boiler bleeds heat. The diesel's exhaust puts a faint oil slick on the cistern water and a perpetual haze in the lowest meter of air, sulfurous and sweet. The ambient heat of the engines, plus the geothermal breath coming up that shaft — you can feel it when you stand over the headframe, a steady, mineral-hot upwelling that has no business being so close to the surface — is enough to keep the bowl frost-free year round. The plants have learned the shape of the heat. They crowd thickest around the pipes, leaves pressed to the warm metal, roots following condensation lines. The distinction between vegetation and installation is getting vague.

The rules of this place are simple and slow.

Water comes up, not down. It condenses on rock, seeps from the scarp, rises from the shaft as steam, falls as mist, and never quite manages to leave. There is no outlet stream. The bowl holds it until the peat can't and then it percolates down through the broken young stone, back into the mountain's hot interior, to rise again. The cycle takes weeks, months. The rock itself is still settling, making minute ticks and pings as it cools and as roots wedge it further apart. You can hear a flake of basalt spall off the wall every so often and land soft in moss.

It is loud here in a way that does not belong to wilderness.

The transformer sings at 50 hertz, a solid, chest-filling drone. The diesel thuds, the boiler hisses and lets off pressure with a shriek every seven or eight minutes that startles birds that aren't there. Water drips everywhere, hundreds of tiny impacts, and the leaves drip onto leaves, and the overflow from the cistern slaps the peat in a constant patter. Even when the pressure valve is shut, the place is full. Sound is trapped by the walls and held.

There is almost no fauna to make sound back. That's the other absence you notice after the loudness. No insects clouding, no chorus. Once in a while a single high, thin bird call crosses the bowl from rim to rim and then is gone, not answered. A beetle, iridescent green-black, works its way across a pipe, too large for this altitude. In the pools between roots, tadpoles hang motionless — too many of them, with nowhere to go. The system is too wet, too dense, too metallic for most animals. They come, they leave.

The weirdness is quiet.

If you watch the cistern, the water in it never ripples from wind, only from the overflow and from below, as if something is gently breathing under it. If you look at the moss on the north wall, it grows *away* from the sun, toward the heat of the boiler, its fronds all bent in parallel like iron filings toward a magnet. If you put your hand on the fresh scarp, the rock is warm on one side of a hairline fissure and cool on the other, though there is no sun on either.

You can walk the whole thing in five minutes, rim to rim, and still not see it all because you have to look *in* — between leaves, under pipes, into the water held in a bromeliad's cup where the sky is reflected perfectly. It feels larger than it is because it is so packed, so vertical despite its smallness. A hamlet for machines and mosses, high above the clouds, young and sharp and soft at once, steaming gently in the normal light of midday while the white sea breaks silently against cliffs a mile below.

If you stay until the sun goes and the boiler cycles down, the mist thickens until you cannot see the walls, only hear them dripping. The heat remains.
