It is a high country, not a peak but a high world.

You are up where the air thins and cools and still the land does not fall away into snow and stone. Instead it broadens. A plateau so vast it holds weather on its back like a sea holds weather — cloud systems are born here, live and die here, never seeing the lowlands. From any rise you cannot see an edge, only more of itself falling away in soft, terraced steps into blue distance, until distance becomes sky again. The elevation is not sharp. It is settled, ancient, worn into a shape that has agreed with itself.

The air at this height should be thin and dry. It is not. It is cool — a late-September cool that lives in your lungs — but saturated. The atmosphere condenses against itself. You breathe water as much as air. Clouds do not pass overhead so much as form around your shoulders, beading on every surface, dripping. The rocks weep. Not from springs, though there are springs everywhere, but from the stone itself, porous basalt and old limestone sweating cold water that never quite dries. The ground gives under your foot and then springs back, soaked through to depth, a living sponge.

This is not a landscape with ground and then plants on it. There is no room between presences. The vegetation is packed in the way a reef is packed — every inch contested and occupied, layered vertically through itself until light has to negotiate to reach lower. 

On the highest terraces, the wind-sheared meadow is a tight felt of moss and cushion-plant so dense you could lie on it and not touch earth, studded with low, black-barked shrubs that grow outward instead of up, their leaves waxy and dark to hold heat. Below that, where the terraces step down and the water gathers, the forest begins — but not a forest of trunks and empty space. It is a thatch. Alder and iron-bark and a kind of high-altitude bamboo grow so close their branches graft and tangle, their roots braided into mats and dams that hold the marsh in place. Hanging moss drapes from everything in curtains ten feet long, dripping steadily. Fungi the size of tables shelf out from trunks, slick and amber, respiring. Every surface is growing on another surface. If a tree falls — and they do, slowly, sighing into the peat — it is covered within a season, cradled and dissolved by green, never reaching the ground as wood.

There are no structures here. No walls, no roads, no cut stumps, no cairn. Not even the memory of one. The land has never been parceled or paved. The idea of a straight line does not exist except in the veins of leaves.

And yet it is industrial.

Not built, but working. The whole plateau works.

You hear it before you understand it — not loud, but constant and low, underneath the quiet. A deep, patient thrumming, a churning, a sighing of pressure and release. The water does the labor. The plateau is a vast, natural mill with ten thousand levels. Water collects in the high moss and releases in sheets down each terrace, turning in root-wheels and stone sluices that the forest has grown into shape. Where the limestone underplates fracture, cold geysers breathe — not eruptions, but rhythmic exhalations every few minutes, hissing white steam into the cool air that immediately condenses and falls again as warm rain on the mosses nearby. Around each vent the vegetation grows in a ring of furious, almost violent green, fed by heat.

In other places the bog itself is the factory. Peat ten meters deep compresses under its own weight, black and fibrous, exhaling methane in slow bubbles that pop at the surface pools. The pools are perfectly round and dark as ink, and at their edges iron bacteria bloom in orange and rust-red films, laying down metal. The air smells faintly of ozone and hot iron, though nothing has been smelted. At night — though daylight here is ordinary, a pale, high-altitude sun filtered through perpetual thin mist, neither dim nor glaring — you see the other product: faint St. Elmo's fire crawling along the bamboo tips where the constant movement of water and wind up here generates static on a continental scale. The bamboo whistles and crackles, discharging pale violet into the mist. The whole high moor crackles faintly, electrified by its own breathing.

It is all active. This is not a still abundance, it is a busy one.

The fauna is everywhere because the flora leaves nowhere empty, and it has learned to be quiet. The sound is muffled by design — by moss, by mist, by water. The loudest thing is dripping.

There are herds of a low, shaggy grazer — something between elk and musk ox, with wide splayed feet for the sponge-ground — moving in lines of twenty or thirty through the thatch, parting the hanging moss without breaking it, their breath steaming. They do not call. Above them, in the middle story of the forest where the bamboo forms tunnels, flocks of small, slate-grey birds move like a single fluid, turning between trunks without a chirp, their wings making only a soft rush. Insects do the bulk of the work and make bulk of the sound that there is: beetles the size of your palm, iridescent black-green, rolling balls of peat and fungus; dragonflies with wingspans as wide as hawks, hovering over the black pools, their wings glassy and almost silent. And lower, in the water, newts and blind white fish and colonies of things that are not quite ants, not quite bees, building vast, lung-like hives out of chewed fiber inside the peat walls — hives that pulse, that ventilate with tiny, synchronized fanning, that refine oils and waxes at industrial scale without a single tool. Their hives hum, low and even, like dynamos.

Predation here is not a chase but an absorption. A vine that looks like moss will close over a beetle in an hour. A pool will skin over with bacteria and trap a bird that landed to drink, digesting it over days without a splash.

The weirdness is quiet too, and you only notice it after you have been walking a while. Water doesn't always run down. In the densest parts of the moss felt, capillary pressure lifts it sideways and even up, so that rivulets run along the underside of root-arches and drip upward into hanging bogs. The weeping rocks sometimes weep against gravity, the water beading and running up their cold faces toward the moss that drinks it. The light, normal and even, seems to come from slightly too many angles on overcast days, as if the mist itself were faintly luminous after holding all that static.

Everything is weathered into itself. The terraces have been stepping down for so long their edges are not cliffs but deep, rounded lips covered in the same felt as the top. Old lava flows have softened into soil. There is no raw cut, no fresh scar. Even the industrial processes — the venting, the compressing, the generating — feel established, balanced, as if they have run at this pace for epochs, producing peat and iron film and wax and warm rain and never more or less than the plateau can hold.

If you stand still long enough, the cold seeps into your boots and your coat grows heavy with condensation, and the forest stops editing itself around you. The dripping resumes. A grazer passes within arm's length, looking through you. A beetle works at your heel, uninterested. The mist closes a little, and the low hum of the hives and the geysers and the water-wheels made of living root rises and falls, quiet enough that you have to hold your breath to be sure you heard it at all.
