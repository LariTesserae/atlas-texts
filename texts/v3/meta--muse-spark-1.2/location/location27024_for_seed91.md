You hear it long before you see it.

A low, broad roar that resolves, as you get closer, into a layered deafening - not a single noise at all but a composite that fills the air until you feel it in your teeth. The wind over the rim makes a hollow organ note, the ventilation stacks howl, the long-shaft pump house thuds like a heart the size of a building, and somewhere water hammers through corrugated iron sluices. You have to raise your voice to hear yourself think, and then you still can't.

The ground gives out.

Not a cliff, not a cave. A valley that shouldn't be there, cut down into the flat. The plain just stops, its thin snow and frost-stiff grass ending in a clean, sharp lip, and below it the land drops fifty, sixty meters in one sheer step. It's young, that cut. You can see it in the rock. The basalt and shale haven't had time to soften. The walls are faceted and black, blasted and then left, with straight drill-lines still visible and talus fans at the base where the last winter's freeze-thaw has pried off fresh slabs. There is no soil profile, no rounded hills. Everything is edge and angle, a recent wound in the earth kept open.

And the bottom of the wound is impossibly wide.

It runs away from you for kilometers in both directions, a long open trough the size of a town laid on its side, a regional thing with its own weather. From the rim you can see the whole system - headwall to headwall - but you can't take it in at once. The scale forces you to pan your head slowly, the way you would look at a mountain range. The sky up here is a pale, overcast white; down there the sky is a narrow, distant slit.

The light never really arrives. Even at midday in winter the sun clears the rim for only an hour and throws a flat, dim wash straight down the center of the trough, then it's gone again. The rest of the time the place lives in a perpetual blue dusk. What direct light there is has to filter through the low cloud and the steam and the dust, and what artificial light there is - the amber sodium lamps over the catwalks, the bare white worklights on the pump house - only makes the dimness more obvious. They buzz faintly under the roar, throwing long, soft shadows that never sharpen.

It is cold. A dry, still, deep cold that does not bite so much as settle. Your breath hangs and then freezes on your scarf. The air pools down here, heavy. Frost forms on every surface not generating heat, a fine white crystalline fur on railings, on stone, on leaves. Puddles are skimmed with first ice, but they are not many.

Water here is not a river, not a lake. It is dampness as a condition. The walls weep. Condensation beads on every iron girder and cold rock face and falls as a constant, intermittent rain that has no clouds. In the warm pockets around the machinery, the frost melts and refreezes, melts and refreezes, leaving dark damp halos on the stone. There are pockets of it everywhere - a black mirror of a pool under an overhang where groundwater has found a fault, a sluggish creek that gathers only to vanish again into the shattered floor, icicles the length of your arm growing under a sluice gate where the overflow drips once every seven seconds, all day. The place is not wet, it is *damp*, like the inside of a mouth in winter.

And everything damp is covered in living things.

This is not a forest that *grows in* a place. The plant *is* the place.

From the rim it looks like the bottom has been carpeted, but down on the floor you understand. The vegetation is total. Old mine terraces, slag heaps, the floors of abandoned sorting sheds, the very walls themselves - all have become substrate. A cold-climate mass of moss, lichen, dwarf black spruce and larch, hardy fern and liverwort has taken hold so completely that there is no bare ground, only different textures of green and rust and black. The moss is not a covering, it is architecture - a foot thick in places, hummocked and swallowing decommissioned ore carts up to their axles, muffling the bases of transformers. Roots have split the fresh basalt, prying at the blasting seams. Larch trunks rise like pillars, their bark furred with lichen, their branches crusted in rime.

And yet it is sparse.

That is the strange economy of it. For all that biomass, the trees stand far apart. You can see a long way between them, across open glades of moss that no one walks through. Each spruce holds its own space, a perfect cone with ten meters of cold, empty air to the next one, as if they agreed not to crowd. The understory is not dense brush but a low, even, patient lawn of moss and stone. The forest is everywhere and nowhere dense; you could lose a building in it without losing sight of the far wall. The scattering of the plants echoes the scattering of everything else.

Because people were here, and still partly are.

There is no town down here. No streets. Just scattered built things, left in the green like erratics. A brick boiler house with its windows blown out, now a greenhouse for ferns growing on its collapsed roof. A single water tower on skeletal legs, its tank drumming with condensation. A line of concrete footings where a processing mill used to stand, rebar poking up through moss like bones. A still-standing corrugated machine shop, its double doors ajar. They are faint marks, partial remains - not ruins so much as interruptions. The scheme of the place was never residential. It was industrial, and it still is, after a fashion.

The machines never stopped.

That is the source of the deafening. This was never a purely natural pit; it was a cut, a mine, a drainage project. The pumps still run on an automated cycle, vast industrial engines set in concrete blockhouses every half kilometer along the floor, each one electrified, each one fed by the cableway that still hums on pylons overhead. They turn slowly - huge flywheels and pistons moving with that patient, unstoppable slowness of heavy industry in cold - drawing the groundwater that would otherwise fill the trough into the sluices and sending it up to the plain in hammering pipes. The ventilation fans on the walls turn just as slowly, six-meter blades that beat the air into submission and keep the bottom from filling with cold fog. You can see the frost shiver off them. The whole valley is a kept system, mechanized, scaled for production long after the production ended.

You put your hand on a railing and feel the vibration through your gloves.

No one tends it day to day. Perhaps a maintenance crew comes down once a month. The rest of the time the plant and the machine have reached an equilibrium. The moss grows over the warm pipes, steam vents keep a perfect circle of ground thawed around them where the ferns stay green all winter, and the engines - simple, robust, built for another century - keep turning.

Life is rare in the animal sense. You might stand for an hour in that open, echoing, green-bottomed canyon and see nothing move except the fans. Then a raven crosses the slit of sky high above and its call is instantly lost under the industrial roar. A set of fox prints, fresh that morning, cuts a straight line across the snow-dusted moss between two pump houses and disappears. Lichen and spruce have no predators here that you can see. The ecology is plant, stone, iron, and cold water, turning over at its own pace.

Everything here is slow.

This is the rule of the place: cold + damp + dim = patience. The moss adds a millimeter a year. The frost cracks the young rock a flake at a time. The flywheels turn at thirty revolutions per minute and have turned at thirty revolutions per minute for twenty years. Even the sound, for all its deafening volume, is slow - a low throb that never peaks, never breaks. There are no rapid cycles, no floods, no fires. The valley gets drier or wetter over decades as the water table shifts; a spruce that falls will lie for ten winters before it softens enough to become a nurse log for new moss. It is a normal place. No strange physics, no altered light. Stone is stone, iron rusts, water freezes at zero, plants photosynthesize what little light they get. The weirdness would be if anything happened fast.

If you stay until your ears start to adjust and the cold finds every seam in your coat, you begin to understand the trough not as an abandoned mine reclaimed by forest, but as a new kind of forest altogether - one whose canopy is basalt, whose sun is a worklight, whose rain is condensation on a fan housing, whose soil is crushed rock and whose rhythm is set by engines no one remembers programming. It is open enough to be a country, empty enough to be lonely, loud enough to be solitary, and alive enough - totally, stubbornly alive in green - to keep the cold from ever quite winning.
