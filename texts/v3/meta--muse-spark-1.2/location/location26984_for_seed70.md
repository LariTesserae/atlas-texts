On first approach you think it is a cloud.

You have been climbing for days through thin, warm air, up the flank of a range that is too new to have learned to be gentle. The rock is young. It hasn't been smoothed. Every ridge is a knife, every valley is a V cut straight down by water that didn't have time to meander. Black basalt and raw limestone in broken teeth, not yet softened by soil, not yet blunted by wind. Scree slopes still glass-sharp where they shattered. The peaks are not old and rounded and tired — they are abrupt, an upthrust, as if the earth shrugged only yesterday.

And then you crest the last sharp rim and the world in front of you is flat.

Not a valley. Not a plain. A sea, perched.

A bowl the size of a country held up in the sky. The caldera — though caldera is too small a word for it, this is a plateau-ocean, a table that lifted an entire piece of the lowland sea with it when it rose — stretches past the far rim until its water meets the sky without a line. It is so wide you can see weather being born in its middle. It is so high the air above it should be cold and thin, but the water keeps its own heat.

You are looking at water held at elevation. Water that did not drain. Three thousand meters up, and it is still ocean.

The water is warm. Not hot-spring warm, but body-warm, bath-warm, the kind of warmth that makes the boundary between skin and world ambiguous. The air immediately above it is not air at all if you are honest — it is a saturation, a thickness, a permanent low veil of vapor that never breaks. When the wind moves, which is rarely, it pushes not gusts but slow, heavy curtains of rain that hang vertically and do not fall so much as descend like beads on a string. Standing on the rim, the atmosphere feels drinkable. You breathe water and it breathes back.

The light is ordinary daylight, and that is the strangest thing. At this depth it should be gone. In a normal sea, twenty meters down the color is already leaving. Here the sun comes straight down through the warm lens and keeps coming. It shafts through the entire column, green-gold and fractured, all the way to the bottom fifty meters below, without dimming the way water is supposed to dim light. The water is impossibly clear and impossibly full at the same time — you can see for a long way, past pillars and forests, and still the water feels dense, viscous, like thin glass that has decided to flow, very slowly.

Below the surface, the drowned world is not barren. It is choked.

Vegetation does not grow *around* the water here. It grows *in* it, through it, as it. From the rim cliffs and the submerged spires, life has exploded in the warm light and refused to leave when the land was lifted. Every surface that can hold a root does. Kelp-like trees with trunks like braided rope rise from the plateau floor, their bark replaced by mats of felted algae and liverwort. Their leaves are not leaves as you know them — they are broad, translucent ribbons the length of your body, wine-red and amber and deep green, frilled at the edges to catch the slow drift of nutrients. They layer. They stack. They crowd so close that swimming between them — or wading, or falling, because movement here is neither swimming nor walking but something in between, a slow push through a medium that holds you — you have to turn sideways to pass, brush shoulders with fronds that close behind you without a ripple.

It is dense in a way a jungle is dense, but vertical. There is no ground cover and canopy; there is just *volume* filling. Epiphytes grow on epiphytes. White, hair-like root curtains hang from every branch and do not root in soil but drink directly from the water, swaying an inch an hour. Colonies of bright green bubble-algae cling to the rock and exhale perfect, silver spheres that do not rise quickly but ascend as if through honey, taking all afternoon to reach the surface and pop with a soft, almost inaudible *tick*. The rock itself, where you can see it, is still sharp and new, un-eroded into soft shapes, but it is already being pried apart by life — roots splitting the fresh basalt, peat building in impossible mid-water shelves where dead fronds have tangled and made soil without ever touching air.

And it is quiet. That is the rule here.

Water carries sound too well, so the world has learned to make almost none. There is no surf — the rim walls are too high and sheer for waves to form, and the surface barely wrinkles even in wind. There is no birdsong. There are, in fact, almost no animals at all. This lushness is curiously empty. You may spend a long hour pushing through the frond-forest and see nothing move but the plants themselves, slowly reorienting toward light. If you are patient, you will see a pale form: a salamander the length of your forearm, translucent as the leaf-ribbons, with external gills like ferns that beat once, twice a minute. Or a fish with no eyes, white as milk, hanging motionless in a shaft of sun, its fins fanning so slowly you have to watch for a full minute to be sure it is alive. They are rare not because they are hiding, but because there are simply very few of them for so much plant life — as if the system overgrew its consumers and left them behind.

What sound there is is small, close, and muffled. The creak of a giant kelp-trunk adjusting its buoyancy. The faint *plink* of condensation falling from the air-water boundary back into itself. Your own breath, too loud in your head, bubbles rising with unnatural slowness. When a frond brushes another, it doesn't rustle; it sighs.

Nothing happens fast. That is the other rule. The water in this high sea does not circulate with tides. It turns over on the scale of seasons. Sediment does not swirl; it settles as a fine, warm snow that takes days to land. The plants grow, but you would need to mark one with thread to see a day's growth. Even light moves slowly — dust motes hang suspended for hours, lit, unmoving. Erosion, which made the sharp world outside, has almost stopped in here; the water protects the young rock from wind, and the rock cradles the water. They have reached a truce. Things are preserved.

If you stay until late afternoon, when the sun lowers enough to hit the far rim, you see the strangest thing of all. Low light catches the entire atmosphere-water interface at once, and you realize it is not a surface. There is no line where air ends and sea begins. There is only a gradient, a thickening, perhaps ten meters thick, where vapor becomes mist becomes rain becomes sea, all at once and without boundary. Birds — if there were birds — could fly into it and find themselves swimming without ever crossing a surface. You could rise from the bottom, push through warmer and warmer water, find it becoming lighter, more breathable, and step out onto the rim rock still dripping, having never broken through.

It is not built. No boat, no dock, no cut mark, no stone stacked by a hand. Even the idea of building seems absurd here; what would you build that water has not already grown over in a week with felt and frond? It is a place that was made without tools, lifted without intent, flooded without rain.

It will not be here forever in geological time. The rim is sharp and sharp things break. One day a wall will fail and this whole warm, dense, quiet ocean will pour down the young mountains in a flood that will carve canyons in an afternoon. But not today. Not this year. Not in a hundred years. Today it just hangs there, high and full and green, breathing slowly, holding light all the way to its floor, waiting at its own pace.
