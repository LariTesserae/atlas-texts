It is a city on a height that should not hold a city.

You come up to it slowly, because there is only one way up — a long grade cut through basalt that switchbacks until your ears pop. The air thins and then gets heavy again, which is the first strange thing. At  elevation, the atmosphere should be cool and sharp. Here it is hot. Not dry desert hot, but a low, wet, held-in heat that sits on your skin like a damp cloth.

This is a high place. Not a peak, a table. A mesa the size of a county, its edges eroded into soft, stepped cliffs long ago, now weathered into a settled shape. The wind has had time to round every hard corner. The plateau itself is not natural, not entirely. Its top has been leveled and leveled again, poured and cut and poured again, until geology and construction are the same layer. Under the city, the rock is dark, vesicular basalt and older concrete fused together by heat and pressure. Above it, the city.

And it is a city. Not ruins, not outpost. Urban in its logic. The plan is legible even from the approach road: blocks, grids, radial boulevards that make sense from the air, districts of low factories and taller tenement-like stacks, infrastructure layered over infrastructure. Culverts big enough to drive a truck through run under avenues big enough to land a plane on. Power runs on gantries like aqueducts. The waterworks are everywhere.

But it is empty.

That is the second strange thing and the one that stays with you. The scale is regional - you can see the city continue past the haze to where it blurs into the edge of the mesa, a valley's worth of roofs and towers and tanks - and the density is near-void. A town built for millions with almost nothing in it. The avenues are absurdly wide and you are the only thing on them. The buildings stand apart from each other with vast, deliberate intervals, as if they need room to breathe. Some are hollow. You can tell from the open loading bays and the dark windows that the interior volume is just air, unpartitioned. The sound of your footsteps takes too long to come back.

There is no plant life. Not hidden, not dormant. None. The heat and the water should have made a jungle. Instead there is mineral. The architects of this place either removed it or it never took. Every surface that would have been soil is sealed: poured stone, iron plate, ceramic tile glazed to a dull sheen, slag glass. Even the dust is powdered rock and rust, not pollen. In the cracks where weeds should be, there is only a faint discoloration - thin, oily biofilms, black-green and coppery, bacterial mats that live off the condensation itself and lie flat against the concrete like spilled paint. They are not plants. They do not grow upward.

Water is everywhere despite that.

It does not rain so much as it condenses. The light is dim - a permanent overcast, or maybe a permanent smog, a low amber-gray lid that dims the sun to a diffuse coal - but the heat below is intense, geothermal or industrial or both. The city exhales. From every tower, from every vent stack and cooling fin and cracked pipe joint, steam sighs out and hits the cooler air above and falls again. You hear it before you see it: a ticking in the metal as it expands, a faint hiss. Then the weeping starts.

All the vertical surfaces weep. The sides of factories, the retaining walls, the underbellies of elevated pipes sweat beads that gather and run. The city has learned to catch it. Gutters are not afterthoughts here, they are the architecture - deep channels running down the center of every street, half-pipes along every roofline, chains hanging from eaves that guide water down into cisterns without ever letting it touch the ground too fast. At street level, the water collects in shallow, engineered marshes: settling basins the size of plazas, lined with basalt gravel, filled with hot water a few inches deep. They steam. They are perfectly still except for the occasional fat drop falling from a pipe sixty feet overhead and making a ring that expands across the whole basin.

Established. That is the word for how the water has lived with the stone. This is not a flood or a ruin from a storm. This has been happening for a long time, long enough for balance. The mineral water has left its mark: travertine beards under every spillway, rust stalactites under every bridge, smooth scallops worn into concrete where water has run for decades. The edges of the basins are softened with pale crust. Everything looks wet-polished, even when dry.

The tech is industrial, not digital. Electrified, mechanized, scaled for production but not for speed. Huge slow engines turn somewhere - you can feel them more than hear them, a low 60-cycle thrum through the soles of your shoes. Factories that could have made anything - turbines, wire, sheet metal - stand in rows, their forges cold but their vents still breathing heat. Conveyors hang motionless, their belts sagging. Switchyards of ceramic insulators buzz faintly in the damp. It is active, though. This place has not been abandoned; it has been thinned. Cycles continue without anyone to watch them. At intervals a valve somewhere opens on its own, a plume of steam jumps, a basin drains with a sucking gurgle and refills. A lone gantry crane inches along its rails, carrying nothing, from one end of a yard to the other and then stops. Condensation gathers and falls. A pump kicks on. The processes are predatorless, purposeless, but persistent.

Light never gets strong. Even at noon the sky is that bruised, low cloud, filtering everything to a dim, even glow without shadows. Heat shimmers off the shallow pools anyway. In that flat light, distance is hard to judge, and the city seems to bend slightly upward at its edges, as if the mesa were a shallow bowl. It isn't. It's the haze and the heat. But sometimes a building's reflection in a basin is clearer than the building itself, and for a second you think the real city is underneath.

It is mostly quiet. For an urban-industrial complex the size of a small province, it should roar. It doesn't. The damp air swallows sound. The few noises there are - drip, hiss, the distant clang of contracting metal, the sigh of a pressure release - happen far apart and leave a long silence after them. Your own breathing sounds loud.

Fauna is rare to the point of rumor. You walk for an hour along a canal of hot water between two factory walls and see nothing move except the water. Then, at the edge of a settling pool, a single animal drinks - something pale and long-limbed, almost translucent, like a heron made of stretched skin and wire, folding itself down to sip the mineral water. It looks at you without comprehension and is gone between two tanks, taking a long time to disappear because the gap between tanks is so wide. On another wall, a colony of pale, blind insects has built a paper town of their own, small as a hand, and abandoned it.

The rule here seems to be: what was built to contain people now contains weather. The streets are riverbeds. The plazas are lakes. The buildings are cliffs that make their own rain. The heat of the deep rock meets the cool lid of the sky and the city, empty and huge and patient, catches the endless condensation in its hands and lets it fall again.
