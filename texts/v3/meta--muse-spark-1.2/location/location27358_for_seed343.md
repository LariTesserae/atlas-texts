It is high. Higher than weather should allow you to be warm.

You climb for days and the air never really thins into cold. The shape of the land explains it - this is not a peak. It is a remainder.

An ancient massif, so old its mountains have forgotten how to be mountains. The elevation is extreme but the erosion is deeper. What was once a jagged range has been ground down by uncounted ages of wind and soft rain into a single, vast, high table. A continent-lifted plain in the sky. From the lowlands it looks like a wall the color of old bone, sheer in places for a thousand feet where a river finally gave up and cut through, but soft on top. Rounded shoulders, broad backs, valleys that are no longer valleys but shallow dimples miles wide, flowing into each other without edge. The rock itself is tired - sandstone and basalt weathered to a deep, russet loam, so fine it feels like brick dust between the fingers, so settled that nothing slides anymore. The world here has reached its final shape and decided to stay.

And because it has stayed so long, the plants have taken it completely.

Not forest in the way lowlanders understand forest. There is no ground that is not plant. The stone is there, somewhere far underneath, but you will never see it. The substrate *is* vegetation. A living carpet thousands of years thick - mosses and felted lichens and root-mats compressed into a springy, peat-deep soil that covers every horizontal surface, every rolling dome and shallow basin of the plateau. From a distance the whole table looks velvety, a warm green-gold that shifts with the light. Up close you realize the texture is vertical as well as horizontal.

The trees are the architecture. They are not many - that's the first strangeness. For a place so utterly given to plants, it is profoundly empty. Density is a hollow thing here.

There are trees, but you can count them. Massive beyond proper scale, ancients with trunks forty, sixty feet across at the base, bark like folded cliffs, roots that run for hundreds of yards like buttressed walls through the living soil. They rise three hundred feet and more, and between any two of them is a great, open emptiness - a cathedral nave of warm air, a hundred yards of clear space where light falls unbroken to the moss. And then another titan, and another. Their crowns do not touch, not quite, but their upper branches throw out horizontal limbs the size of lesser trees, and from those limbs hangs a secondary world: curtains of epiphytes, ferns the size of doors, flowering bromeliads big as barrels, vines as thick as your waist that never reach the ground but swing free, dripping aerial roots. The canopy is not a roof, it's a scattered archipelago of green islands suspended in open sky, and it fills the space without filling it. You can see for miles through the colonnade of trunks, across the rolling plant-plain, and yet you are never out of the plant. Even the air is vegetative - pollen on the wind like fine dust, seed fluff drifting constantly, pale spores catching the sun.

It is warm. Not hot. A steady, late-summer warmth that lives in the living soil. The extreme height should be cold, but the plateau holds heat - the dark moss, the shelter of the great trunks, the sheer mass of breathing green. The sun is normal here, bright and frank, not dimmed or colored. Light comes straight down around noon and slants endlessly under the crowns in the evening, and the whole place is lit like an open room. No gloom. You can see the far edge of the table where it falls away, ten or twelve miles off, a haze of gold. The scale is regional - one valley would not hold it, one town could be lost in any single shallow bowl between the giants. You can walk for a day and the view changes only in its particulars: a different arrangement of titans, a slightly deeper moss-swale, a new pattern of hanging gardens overhead.

Water is scarce and strange. There are no rivers up here. No lakes. The plateau is too old and too porous; the ancient stone drank everything long ago. What water there is lives as damp. It comes at night as a heavy condensation that beads on every leaf and vine and falls in constant quiet droplets, pattering on the moss like a second, softer rain. It comes in the mists that roll in without warning from the escarpment edge, thick white rivers of fog that pour between the trunks and leave everything gleaming. It collects in pockets - in the cupped leaves of the air-plants, each holding a gallon or more, tea-colored and wriggling with larvae; in clefts where the moss has formed natural basins, dark mirrors a few feet across that persist for weeks after a storm and then slowly vanish; in the deep axils of the great trees where the bark folds into cisterns. If you know how to look, there is always drink within reach. If you don't, you will think the place is dry as dust. Intermittent rain does come - heavy, warm, hammering down for an hour at midday, drumming on the canopy islands and vanishing straight into the sponge-ground with no runoff, leaving the air steaming.

What lives on that damp, in that warm light, in that emptiness, is overwhelming.

You do not see the fauna at first because you are listening to it. The sound is deafening. That is not a figure of speech.

Every surface that is plant is also animal. The moss thrums with insects. The air between the great trunks is never empty - it is filled, stitched, crowded with wings. Beetles the size of fists with iridescent backs drone past like small cargo. Clouds of white moths spiral in the shafts of light. Wasps and bees in colors that have no name. And birds - flocks beyond counting, small swift things and large gliders with translucent membranes, parrots and crow-kin and things with no lowland analogue, all calling at once. They do not take turns. At dawn the chorus is a physical pressure, a wall of trills and clicks and hoots and whistles and buzzes so dense it makes your teeth hurt. You have to raise your voice to be heard by someone two feet away. By midday it thins only slightly, shifts in pitch as cicadas the length of your hand take over, sawing from every trunk. Even at night it never stops - tree frogs in the cistern-pools, thousands per tree, bellowing in overlapping waves.

And they move. The dynamism is constant but not violent. No storms that reshape. Instead, endless small motions: a troop of long-armed gliding mammals launching from a high bromeliad, flaring skin-wings to coast the hundred yards to the next titan; ants in rivers wide as roads ferrying cut leaves; bright lizards flicking across moss; a slow, browsing giant - something like a sloth but large as a house - moving inches per hour along a horizontal limb high above, stripping ferns, trailing smaller commensals. Predation happens in the open air - hawk-things stooping through the empty naves - but it does not disturb the whole. The system breathes, eats, sings, breeds, rots and regrows all at once, visible at every scale.

People are here, but thinly. Scattered. You find their marks if you walk long enough. A trail worn not into dirt but *through* moss, compressed to a darker line that slowly rebounds if abandoned. A platform woven between two buttress roots, thatched with living fern that continues to grow. A small hut on stilts where the moss is deepest, its walls made of cured vine and bark slabs, empty now, with a cold ash pit. Higher up, roped ladders hanging from a limb-city, swaying. Carvings on a trunk - hand deep, spirals marking a water pocket or a fruiting season. No roads. No villages large enough to be called such. Three or four shelters that share a cistern-tree might be the most you will find together.

Their tech is hand-made, held together by cordage and ingenuity. Nothing that needs a mine or a forge beyond a small bloomery for simple iron - adzes, hooks, knives. The ingenuity is botanical. They have learned to coax the epiphytes into producing bowls, to splice a vine to grow into a bridge, to hollow a bromeliad without killing it so it keeps filling with water for them. They wear bark cloth and insect silk, carry gourds, speak loudly to be heard over the deafening song of the day, and at night they go quiet and listen to the frogs.

There is a slight unusualness to the rules, the kind you only notice after a few days. The plants here are too coordinated. When one of the great trees flowers - a once-a-decade event that turns its entire crown into a blaze of red - every epiphyte on it flowers at the same time, as if on signal. When the mists come, the hanging vines all curl slightly inward at the same moment, wringing themselves out. And the sound, deafening as it is, has a pattern. If you stand still long enough, you can hear it pulse - not an echo, but a wave, passing from the escarpment inward, a ripple of calling that circumnavigates the whole plateau and returns a few minutes later from the other side, endless.

No one built this place. It grew itself, wore itself down, lifted itself up, and then filled the resulting quiet, warm, empty height with a single, continuous, singing green that knows how to hold water in its cupped hands and space enough between its pillars for the wind and wings and voices to travel forever.
