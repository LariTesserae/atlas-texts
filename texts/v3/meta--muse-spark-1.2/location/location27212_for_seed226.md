It is a town on a table that shouldn't exist yet.

You climb to it and your lungs tell you how high you are before your eyes do — cool, thin air that makes every breath feel rinsed. The switchback trail ends not in a pass or a peak, but in a cut. A clean, vertical lip of stone, still sharp enough to cut your hand. Above that lip, the world flattens.

The mesa is small. You could walk its circumference before lunch and still have time to sit. It's not much larger than the town it holds, and the town is built right to the edge as if it knew exactly how much room it had and decided to use it all at once. From below it must look like a crown. From on top, it feels like a room with no roof.

The geology is wrong. Or rather, too new.

This whole highland was lifted recently — in the slow time of stone, yesterday. There has been no time for rain to carve valleys, for rivers to learn where to go, for wind to soften anything. The basalt is raw, close-grained and dark blue-black where it is dry, almost glassy on the fresh breaks. The edges of the mesa are sheer, ninety degrees and unweathered, with the faint stair-step of cooling columns still visible like stacked organ pipes. The surface is not soil so much as shattered rock that hasn't decided to become soil yet. Blocks tilted at birth, plates that rang when they were pushed up. No talus slopes. No scree fans. Just the clean fracture and then the flat.

Because there is no drainage. That is the first strange thing you notice after the cold.

Water has nowhere to go. It seeps and weeps instead of flowing. The stone sweats. Every vertical face is beaded with condensation, dark with it. The low cloud that lives at this altitude — not above you, *at* you — brushes the mesa all day, condensing on every leaf and lintel and cold rock edge. It never quite rains so much as the air precipitates itself. You hear it before you see it: a constant, loud ticking and pattering of drops falling from one leaf to the next to the next. From the weeping cliffs at the town's edge, thin curtains of water spill without channels, just sheeting down the new rock in a hundred braided threads, pooling anywhere flat.

And yet it is lush beyond any reason for this height and cold.

Because the water stays, and the light is good — full, normal, high-mountain daylight, white and unfiltered, but tempered by that perpetual mist — everything that can hold a root does. Moss does most of the work. It is not a covering so much as the actual surface of the town. Thick, springy mats of it, saturated and brilliant, emerald and jade and yellow-green, swallowing the basalt. It climbs the walls, thatches the gaps between stone blocks, smothers the flat roofs until the houses look like they grew. Ferns as big as your arm unfurl from every seam. Lichen crusts the stone in pale gray-green scales. In the shallow pans where water stands for days, sphagnum and cotton-grass form miniature bogs no larger than a doorway, quaking when you step near. Trees — if you can call them that — are stunted, wind-trained mountain alders and rowans, but their trunks are furred three inches deep in moss and their branches drip.

It is a cloud-forest in miniature, compressed onto a plate.

And in the middle of that wet, green plate is the town.

It is unmistakably a town, not a village. It was made with a plan. Stone foundations, streets that cross at right angles, terraces that step with the slight unevenness of the fresh basalt, drains that were clearly meant to carry water — now all overwhelmed and greenly choked. Low walls of fitted, undressed stone, the blocks still showing quarry marks from hand tools, the mortar pale lime that hasn't had time to stain. Timber frames of split alder, joinery pegged not nailed, roofs of overlapping slate shingles so new their edges are still sharp. Walkways of plank that bridge the boggy flats. A small plaza, paved, with a cistern in the center that is always full to the lip and spilling. Two-story houses in rows, shutters carved with simple patterns. A smokehouse, a weaving shed, a granary raised on stilts against the damp. Infrastructure for hundreds.

There are perhaps thirty people here.

That is the second strange thing. The density is wrong. The town is almost empty. Vast space between presences. Not ruined — not abandoned — just under-inhabited, as if it were built in anticipation of a population that never arrived, or that hasn't been born yet. Houses stand finished and empty, doors open to show clean-swept floors with a single sleeping mat. In the plaza, laundry lines hang with three shirts where there could be thirty. The streets are too wide for the few feet that use them. Sound carries strangely in all that unused space.

No one seems bothered by it. The few inhabitants move slowly. That is the rule here: everything is slow. The water seeps rather than rushes. The moss advances a millimeter a year. The stone is still settling from its uplift with a faint, once-a-season creak you feel through your feet more than hear. People speak softly and work with hand tools — stone mauls, antler picks, wooden adzes, polished slate blades — and the work takes a long time and is done well. A wall being built at the eastern edge has been going up for months; each block is levered, dressed, and set by hand. No haste. The cold discourages it and the town's emptiness does not demand it.

Because there is almost no fauna to mark time. Once in a while a raven cuts across the white sky and its croak is huge in the quiet between water-drops. Mountain midges hang in a still cloud over the bog pans and then are gone. You might see the broad, three-toed print of a high grazer in the moss and never see the animal itself. No chorus, no flocks. The loudness here is not animal at all.

It is water and stone. The whole mesa rings.

The sharp new cliffs and the hard, flat stone and the empty paved streets make an amphitheater. Every drip is amplified. Every trickle down a wall becomes a bright, percussive line. The wind, when it comes over the edge, hits the plaza and scours it with a low roar that vibrates the shutters. When the cloud thickens, the humidity condenses on every surface at once and then releases in a sudden, rattling shower from a thousand leaves with no rain having fallen. At night it is louder still — you hear the mesa breathing, water finding its first tentative route off the edge, drop by patient drop carving what will, in ten thousand years, be a canyon. Right now it is only a damp stain.

You can walk to the edge and look down a thousand feet of untouched fall to the forested lowlands, hazy and warm below, and then look back at the town behind you — compact, organized, green-roofed, half empty, steaming faintly in the cool light as the sun breaks through the cloud for an hour and turns every bead of water into glass.

It feels embryonic, as if the world is still deciding what this place is for. The mountain came up new. The water came immediately after. The plants came impossibly fast, seizing the wet. The people came and built a city sized for the future, with hand-tools, and are now waiting, slowly and without anxiety, for the rest of the world — the erosion, the valleys, the animals, the crowds — to catch up.
