It is not a cave.

It is a going-down that forgot it was supposed to end.

You descend to it, which is the first thing wrong. The surface up there is a high, cool desert of pale stone — wind-scoured, almost featureless, the kind of ancient that has stopped trying to be dramatic. The rock has been worked by time until time got bored and left it. No peaks, no ridges. Just a vast, faintly convex plain of limestone and basalt, pitted with shallow pans where the wind has polished the stone to a soft sheen, like old bone. There is no soil. There is no green. And then, without a rim or a warning cliff, the ground simply isn't there anymore.

A sink. A collapse. An unroofing.

Maybe three kilometers across at its widest and longer than that north to south, an oval bowl sunk perhaps four hundred meters below the level of the plain. The walls are not sheer. Nothing here has been sheer for a very long time. They slope inward in great, worn steps and slumps, terraces as wide as fields, all of it smoothed by an erosion so old it has become a kind of architecture itself. You can read the water in them, even though the water is mostly gone — great curving grooves where rivers ran for a million years and then didn't, potholes the size of houses, scalloped undercuts. The whole basin is primordial in the way a river stone is primordial; every edge has been rubbed until it is kind.

The light should not reach here.

It does. Brutally.

There is no roof, and the sky above the sink is a hard, high, cloudless blue-white. The stone of the plain is so pale it throws the sun back up, and the stone of the basin throws it back again. At noon the light in the hollow is almost doubled, a bright, cool illumination with no shadows quite dark enough to hide in. It bounces off the damp sheen on the rock and off the white mineral crusts, until even the undersides of ledges are lit. It is a cold brightness, not a warm one. The air down here stays cool year-round — cool enough that your breath ghosts a little in the morning, cool enough that the stone never quite warms under your hand. That chill is what keeps the place alive.

Because for all its dryness, the hollow is damp.

Not wet. Damp, in the way an old cellar is damp. Water lives here intermittently, pocketed, shy. It is not a lake or a river. It seeps from the ancient walls in dark vertical stains and gathers in shallow pans on the terraces. It beads as condensation on the undersides of stone lintels and on the cool shafts of the buildings themselves, dripping once every few minutes with a tick. After a rain up on the plain — rare, sudden, gone in an hour — the whole basin weeps. For a day, thin sheets of water will run down those enormous, gentle slopes in braided silver threads, collecting in the lowest part of the city before vanishing again into the stone. The inhabitants build for this. Every street is slightly cambered, every foundation trenched with a little runnel. Even when it looks dry, if you press your palm to the rock in shadow you feel the cold sweat of it.

And there is a city in it.

Not ruins. An inhabited, ongoing, cluttered, noisy-looking but strangely quiet city that has spread itself across the terraced floor and lower slopes of the sink, sparse enough that you can always see stone between structures. Nothing is crowded. Scattered is the word. A house here, a cluster of three there, a long low wall that runs for a hundred meters and then stops for no reason you can see, a yard full of stacked stone. From above it looks like someone dropped a handful of dice into a bowl. But when you are down in it, you understand it is urban — not a village. There are streets, there are quarters, there are thoroughfares worn smooth by feet. The buildings are all mineral. Limestone block and shale slab and basalt, cut and stacked by hand. Primitive work, but not crude. Everything is hand-tool made: hammer-dressed stone, timber poles brought down from — somewhere else? there is no wood here — lashed with cordage, rope lifts, scaffolds of lashed-together struts that creak. No metal, or very little, and what there is is dark iron, hammered thin for brackets and nails. You see chisel marks on every block, and the marks are fresh. This is not an abandoned place.

It is turbulent.

That is the contradiction that takes you a while to hold in your head. The city is sparse and it is silent, but it never stops moving.

At any given moment, a third of the structures are being taken apart while another third is being put up. No single building looks finished. Scaffolds go up around a low, thick-walled house and come down two days later to reveal the house is now lower, or wider, or has moved ten feet to the left. A wall is built, left for a week, then carefully unbuilt and its stones carried elsewhere on people's backs and on sledges. Piles of sorted stone appear and disappear. People — you see them everywhere once your eyes adjust to their stone-colored clothing — are always carrying, hauling, chipping, pointing, re-measuring with knotted cords. There is no sense of progress toward a final plan. There is only constant, restless reconfiguration, as if the city were trying out shapes, or as if staying still would be a kind of death. An anthill energy, but without the hurry. No one shouts. No one runs. The bustle is methodical, almost meditative in its endless unmaking.

And it is quiet.

For all that motion, the sound in the hollow is nothing. The cool, damp air and the immense, porous bowl swallow noise. Stone dust dampens footfall. The work itself is muffled — the tap-tap-tap of a stone hammer doesn't carry more than a few meters before the vast open air drinks it. The loudest things are natural: the occasional plink of a condensation drop into a pool, the papery whisper of wind over the rim far above, a thin trickle finding a new course down a terrace. People speak quietly or not at all, using gestures. Even when a large block is set down, it lands with a soft, deep *thunk* rather than a crash.

What you hear most, if you stand still, is fauna.

There are no plants here to rustle. No grass, no moss, no lichen even — the bright light and the cool dryness and the constant disturbance of stone seem to have forbidden anything photosynthetic, or perhaps the rock itself never allowed it. The mineral world is bare, except for pale crusts of salt and calcite that bloom like frost where water has evaporated. White, faintly glittering, sometimes pink with trapped minerals.

But the animal life is everywhere, abundant in a way that feels impossible without plants to eat. You realize after a few minutes that you have been seeing motion at the edge of your vision the whole time and dismissing it as people.

Low, blind, pale arthropods the size of your hand scuttle in lines along the runnels, harvesting mineral film. Herds — if you can call them herds — of translucent, slug-like grazers as long as your forearm move slowly across the salt crusts, leaving a faint shine where they have fed on whatever bacterial mat grows in the damp. Things with too many legs and things with no legs at all. On the higher terraces, where the seep is stronger, colonies of soft, anemone-like filterers open and close in the trickles, pale pink and yellow. Above, the air is full of wings. Not birds. Leathery, silent fliers, from sparrow-sized to as wide as a doorway, circling the bright column of air over the sink, or clinging to the sloping walls in great quiet roosts, folding and unfolding. They make no call. At night — if you stay — you see they glow faintly from their bellies, and the walls themselves faintly answer. Something in the stone, some reactive lode exposed by the collapse, holds light a little too long, so that after sunset the entire basin glows for an hour with a cool, sourceless luminescence before truly going dark.

And lizards, and scuttling things like lizards, and beetles with shells like polished basalt. None of them are afraid of people. A line of workers carrying a stone lintel will step carefully over a slow-moving grazer without breaking rhythm. A child sits on a low wall and watches a cluster of white insects dismantle a crumb of something — fungus? fallen skin? — with quiet industry that mirrors the humans'.

There is the unusualness to it, present but not shouting. The weirdness is low-grade, accumulative. Way up on the rim, the horizon looks slightly too close, as if the sink is deeper than geometry allows. If you pour water on the flattest stone, it hesitates a second too long before finding a downhill. The glowing after-light has no source. The fauna, for all its abundance, seems to be eating nothing that you can see, and to be eaten by nothing — there are no predators that chase, only things that wait with open mouths. And the people — their endless building and unbuilding never produces rubble. Every chip, every flake of stone is gathered, sorted, reused. Nothing is wasted, nothing accumulates, as if the city were breathing stone in and out.

The rules here are simple and not spoken:

Stone is plentiful and therefore precious. Take it, use it, return it.
Water is occasional and therefore sacred. Direct it, do not own it.
Light is free. So is space.
Be still and you will be built around.

You stand on a terrace halfway down, your hand on a cool, damp block someone set yesterday and might move tomorrow, and you look out over the open, bright, mineral town veined with intermittent silver, busy with countless quiet lives, human and not, all of it unsettled and settled at once. The wind above the rim makes no sound down here. A drop falls somewhere behind you. A scaffold creaks. A pale wing passes overhead without a shadow.

The hollow has been here since before any map. It will be here after the map wears away. In between, it keeps rearranging itself, stone by stone, in the cool bright silence.
