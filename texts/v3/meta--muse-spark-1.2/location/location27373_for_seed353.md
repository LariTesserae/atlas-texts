It is high up and it has been high up for a very long time.

Not a mountain. A mesa, a table, a shield. The land here was lifted in some earlier version of the world and then left to weather until weather itself gave up. The stone underneath — if there is stone underneath — is smooth in the way only incredibly old things are smooth. Not sharp, not dramatic. Beveled, rounded, pitted, polished by wind that has had eons to work. The edges of this plateau fall away on all sides into darkness, and the darkness below is not a valley but simply more night. You can't see the lowlands. The air is thin and very hot and does not move the way air should.

Nothing here is natural and nothing here has been natural for a very long time.

The entire top of the mesa, as far as you can see — which is not far, because the light is poor — is occupied. Not built upon. Occupied in the sense that there is no gap between what was built and what grows. The distinction has failed.

Someone — something — built a city-factory the size of a small county up here. Tiered frames, gantries, scaffolds, arches, tanks, pipes, catwalks, silos, lattices, all iron and basalt-concrete and heat-blackened steel. The scaffolding was meant to hold something. Now it *is* held. By plants.

Not plants as you know them.

The vegetation here is total. It is the substrate. It is not growing *on* the built environment; it has replaced the function of walls and floors and roofs while leaving the shape of them. Every girder is sleeved in bark that is not bark. Every cable is a vine that is not a vine. Every flat surface is a mat, a felt, a thatch, a weave of living tissue so dense you cannot put your hand between fronds.

It is a dry jungle. A desert jungle.

There is almost no water. The air you breathe will crack your lips in minutes. The hot wind, when it does move, carries fine salt and mineral dust that tastes like hot iron on the tongue. These plants do not want water. Their leaves — if you can call them leaves — are black-green, almost black, thick as leather, waxy, curled inward to seal themselves. Many have no leaves at all. They are succulent columns, barrel-trunks, tangled air-roots that hang like charred wiring, puffed bladders of resin. When you brush against one it does not feel wet or sappy, it feels warm and slightly oily. They sweat a thin, clear hydrocarbon instead of water. It beads on their surfaces and evaporates instantly in the heat, leaving a faint varnish smell, like hot plastic and cloves.

They do not photosynthesize, not here. There is almost no light to do it with.

The sky is dark. Not night-dark, but deep, enclosed darkness. No sun, no moon. Perhaps the mesa is under a dome, perhaps the atmosphere itself has become opaque with age and heat, perhaps this is just what high elevation looks like when the world has grown old. The only light is industrial and biological together: a low, sullen amber glow from furnaces and pipe seams, the dull cherry-red of metal that never cools, and the even duller, intermittent phosphor of the plants themselves — not bright bioluminescence, but a tired, chemical glint along the veins of the larger fronds, as if they remember light and are trying to make a little of their own. It is enough to see silhouettes by, to see the steam, to see the next arch receding into gloom. It is not enough to see color. Everything is shades of hot black.

The heat is the point. The heat is everywhere, soaking from below, radiating from the structures.  The stone of the plateau itself is warm through the soles of your shoes. The air at chest height is 45, 50 degrees Celsius and dry as an oven. The plants like it. They were made — or made themselves — for this.

Because this is still an industrial place. The machines never stopped.

Deeper in the mat, you hear them. Not loud — the sound here is strangely muffled, as if the dense weave of fiber and trunk absorbs it. But it is there: a low, pervasive thrum of engines turning over slowly, far apart. The thud of a press stamping. The sigh of a pressure valve releasing. The tick and creak of metal expanding and contracting in the heat. Conveyor chains moving somewhere out of sight, carrying not ore but bales of fibrous growth, culled and shredded and pulped and extruded again as struts and panels on which new growth is seeded.

The forest is farmed by itself. The factory tends the forest that *is* the factory.

And it is turbulent. Violently, silently turbulent. This is not a stable jungle. This is not a climax forest that has found equilibrium. The growth is hyperactive. If you stand still and watch one section of wall — a lattice of steel choked with those black, fleshy vines — you will see it move. A shoot extends an inch, then another inch, curling around a pipe. A pod swells and splits with a soft pop you feel more than hear, releasing a puff of cinnamon-smelling spores that blacken and root even as they drift. On the next level up, a section is dying, sloughing off in dry sheets that curl and rattle and are immediately gathered by clanking, spindly harvester arms that descend from the gantries on oiled tracks. Cut, bale, pulp, re-seed. Cut, bale, pulp, re-seed. The whole valley-sized mat shivers with this creation and destruction, an anthill's energy but performed by plants and machines in near-silence, at the speed of time-lapse made real.

There are no animals. That absence is profound once you notice it. No insects buzzing, no birds rustling the canopy, no rats in the pipes. The pollination, the pruning, the carrying — all of that is done by wind, by gravity, by the machines, by the plants themselves which have learned to twitch and snap and throw. You see a tendril that ends in a claw-like hook, opening and closing with no prey to catch, a leftover gesture from some engineered purpose. You see flowers the size of dinner plates that open in the dark to no pollinator, exhaling heat and that varnish perfume to empty air, then closing again. The whole ecosystem is plant and machine, locked together, humming.

The rules here are simple and strange:

1. Nothing is allowed to be empty. Any gap, any interval of air between two beams, will be filled within hours by a seeking root or a pneumatically sprayed seed-foam.

2. Nothing is allowed to be wet. Water is poison, or at least, it is irrelevant. Condensers on the highest gantries strain the thin air and yield a few liters a day, which are immediately cracked in hot catalytic tanks and fed to the plants as something else.

3. Everything ages but nothing falls down. The original built forms are ancient — pitted, sagging, worn down to their gentlest possible shape by erosion that happened before the plants arrived — but the living sheath holds them up past the point of collapse. The mesa should have eroded flat by now. Instead it is held in its final, flattened, stabilized shape by roots as thick as pillars, by wood that has become as hard as the stone it clutches.

If you walk — and you can walk, on narrow maintenance paths that the forest has politely left open, or re-opened after you push through — the town-valley extends beyond what your dark-adjusted eyes can resolve. To your left, a cathedral-like tangle of columnar cactiforms holding up a roof of interwoven broadleaf that has itself become a ceiling, studded with the dull red eyes of furnaces. To your right, terraces stepping down toward the plateau's eroded rim, each terrace a monoculture of a different dry-phyte: one of black wire-grass that hums when the wind does move, one of puffball trunks that split and regenerate daily. Above you, more levels, more lattices, disappearing into hot gloom.

It is packed, saturated, claustrophobic in its density, and yet it feels open because you can sense its repetition continuing beyond sight. There is no wilderness beyond its edge, only the long, smooth fall into darkness. There is no center either. Just more of itself, churning quietly, growing furiously in the hot dark, waiting for no one.

And it is mostly quiet. The machines thrum, the pods pop, the dry fronds whisper against steel. But the heat and the darkness and the sheer mass of fiber swallow sound. You hear your own breathing too loud. You hear the faint tick of a tendril growing past your ear.
