It is not a valley. It is not a mountain. It is a room the mountain kept for itself.

High up where the air goes thin and cool enough to make your breath hang, the ridge has folded in on itself and formed a cirque — a bowl cut from old stone, perhaps forty paces across, perhaps sixty. You could walk its perimeter in a few minutes if there were anywhere to walk. There isn't. The walls are close and complete, a ring of weeping rock some three or four times taller than a man, weathered into softness. Nothing jagged remains here. Everything has been rounded, pitted, scooped, and smoothed by a long patience of water and wind and ice until the basalt and granite hold the shape of water even when they are dry. Which they never are.

The light never really arrives here.

The bowl faces north and the rim is high enough to cut the sky. What sky there is is usually low cloud that sits on the rim like a lid, or at night is a narrow coin of deep blue-black powdered with stars that never seem to turn. Even at noon it is a dusk. A blue, cool dusk that lives permanently in the stone. Your eyes adjust and then you realize there is more light than you thought, but it is not from above. It condenses.

The air in the cirque is saturated. It is wet in a way that is not rain. Moisture beads on every surface and never falls. It gathers on the overhangs in droplets fat as berries that hang and hang and never quite drop. It sweats out of the rock itself — the walls weep in dark vertical streaks where water finds its way through invisible seams and slides down over cushions of moss without ever making a stream. The ground is not ground. It is a sponge, a mat, a deep plate of peat and living moss over cold black water that is always an inch below the surface. Step — if you could step — and water would well up around your foot without a splash, filling the depression you made, clear and still and ice-cold. There are small pools, no larger than a basin, no deeper than a hand, black and perfectly still, reflecting the dark walls without a ripple. They never overflow. They never dry. They are exactly full.

It is cool enough that everything is slow. Not cold enough to freeze. Just cool enough that decay has almost stopped. The temperature holds in that narrow band where breath mists and stone stays clammy and the water never wants to evaporate. You feel it on your cheeks, on the back of your hands — a constant, damp coolness like the air inside a cave mouth.

And everything is occupied.

There is no bare rock. There is no empty water. The moss that lines the walls is not a covering, it is a building. Layer upon layer of it — emerald and rust and pale, almost-white green, star moss and cushion moss and the flat liverworts that lie like wet leather. It hangs in curtains from the rim where the weeping lines are, it bulges from cracks in pillows, it forms small terraces and domes on the floor of the bowl that rise and fall like breathing. Dwarf willow, no higher than your ankle, knots itself through the moss, its tiny leaves glossy with condensation. Alpine saxifrage clenches in rosettes, flowering white even in this dimness with flowers that never seem to open or close, just are. Lichen crusts the only places moss will not grow and even the lichen is thick, textured like coral.

And packed into that lushness, in it, under it, over it, is animal life at a density that stops feeling like a population and starts feeling like a texture.

You notice it slowly because it is so quiet.

The pools are crowded with salamanders. Not a few — dozens to a pool, black with gold flecks, each as long as your finger, layered over each other in the cold water, their gills fanned out like ferns, perfectly still. They do not swim. They rest, gill-frills pulsing once, twice a minute. On the moss between the pools, beetles — black, iridescent green — move with such slowness they appear motionless until you see that their position has changed. Midges hang in the air in columns, thousands of them in a single shaft of non-light, hovering without humming. Moths the color of stone cling to the walls in such numbers the rock looks patterned. Small frogs the brown of peat sit half-submerged with their eyes just above the water. Higher, in the willow tangle, nests — not built, but found — hold clutches of eggs that never hatch and never spoil, tended by dun-colored birds that watch without calling. Everywhere you look, something is looking back, or sleeping with its eyes open. It is overwhelming, not because it moves, but because it is there, shoulder to shoulder, leaf to leaf, body to body, with no space left unclaimed. The bowl is full. It is saturated with living.

You expect noise from such a crowd and there is none. This is a mostly quiet place the way a library is quiet — full and hushed. The midges do not whine at this temperature. The frogs do not croak. The birds make no song. The loudest sound is the occasional soft *tick* as a too-heavy droplet finally loses its hold on the moss and lands on a leaf below, or the faint sigh of your own breath fogging. Even the water makes no sound as it seeps. If you hold still long enough you hear the faintest clicking and rustling, the micro-sounds of many small bodies shifting a millimeter in their sleep.

There is nothing made here. No path, no wall, no cairn, no cut branch. No human has ever needed to mark this place because nothing ever changes enough to need marking. And yet there are tools.

You have to look for them among the packed life. A thrush — or something like a thrush, puffed against the cool — hops to the edge of a pool with a sharp flake of stone in its beak, holds it, and uses the edge to pry the casing off a caddisfly larva. It leaves the flake where it drops it, among a small scatter of other flakes at that one favored stone. Some of the salamanders have hollowed little cups in the peat to sit more perfectly in the water. The beetles roll and tuck fragments of lichen. Nothing is built up; everything is used as it is found, picked up and set down again. Simple, hand — or claw, or beak — work, and then left.

There is a slight wrongness here that you feel more than see, and that keeps the bowl from feeling merely like an alpine tarn. The water is a little too still. The reflection in the pools is a little too perfect, holding the stars even when cloud covers the sky. The moss glows very faintly — not bioluminescence, not really light, but a memory of green that persists after you look away. And nothing ever changes. A droplet that was about to fall when you arrived is still about to fall an hour later. The thrush lifts its stone tool and does not set it down. The midges hold their places in the air like dust in amber. The flowers do not wilt. The moisture does not rise or lower. It is not frozen. It is not dead. It is in balance so complete it has stepped out of time. The weathered walls, the peat, the crowded, quiet animals, the weeping water, the dim — they have negotiated an equilibrium so stable that dynamics have ceased. It persists.

The cirque does not invite you in. It does not keep you out. It simply is, a small, packed, wet, dark, lush room high in the cold where life has filled every available inch and then learned how to be perfectly still together. You stand at its rim and look down into its dusk and feel the cool, wet air condensing on your skin, and understand that you are looking at a place that finished becoming itself a very long time ago and has seen no reason to become anything else.
