You don't see it at first for what it is.

From the rim of the basin, it looks like a lowland rainforest after a hot rain. A broad, warm flat between young hills - the land here hasn't been smoothed yet. The uplift is recent. The ridges that cup the valley are sharp, raw, their stone still angular where it has broken through the soil, not yet worn to hills. Black basalt and red laterite cut in clean steps and ledges, as if the earth was quarried yesterday and then abandoned. But you can barely see the rock for what grows on it.

Then your eyes adjust and you realize the forest *is* the valley.

There is no forest *in* the valley. The vegetation has become the terrain. 

Everything below the rim is plant mass, dense to the point of architecture. Not trees with space between them — a single, continuous, interwoven organism that has built itself into a city without ever having been built. Canopy, trunk, root, vine, leaf, flower have differentiated into streets and walls and roofs and aqueducts, but none of it was made. It was grown. It is still growing.

The light is ordinary daylight, high and white-gold and warm — this is a low place, near sea-level though the sea is nowhere in sight, and the air sits heavy and damp and still. Not wet. Not a swamp. The water here is intermittent, pocketed. It doesn't flow in a river. It arrives as condensation. As sudden warm rain that hammers for twenty minutes at noon and vanishes into leaves. As dew that beads overnight into droplets the size of your fist, hanging from the underside of every leaf, dripping on its own slow clock. As vapor caught in curtains of aerial roots, as shallow pans of water held in the cupped leaves the size of courtyards, as dark cisterns inside hollow trunks. The whole city drinks the air. You can feel the damp on your skin constantly, the air at 30 degrees and sweet with transpiration, your shirt sticking to you, but your feet are on dry, springy mat — woven root-floor — not mud.

You walk down from the rock rim and the ground gives slightly under you. The slope is a ramp of living wood, braided roots as thick as your torso that have flattened and fused into a road. On either side, walls rise. They are not walls. They are colonnades of figs and ironwoods that have grown together, their trunks fluted and buttressed, their bark smooth and warm to the touch like skin. Where a window should be, a membrane of translucent leaves has stretched thin, letting light through green. Where a door should be, two great aerial roots have arched and not quite met, leaving a breathing gap. Higher up, second and third stories are not stacked but *grown* — limbs that reached out horizontally and then thickened, flattened, became floors. Balconies are brackets of hard wood exuded by the trunk. Streets are not cut; they are the negative space the plants agreed to leave.

It is urban. Unmistakably. This is not wilderness. It has the density and intent of a town that holds tens of thousands, only its citizens are all plant. Neighborhoods have character. Near the basin edge where the basalt juts up, the forms are hard, squat, defensive — lignin-heavy, bark like armor, small hard leaves. Toward the center they open into a vast, airy vaulted district where the canopy lifts thirty, forty meters on pillar trunks and interlaces into a roof that is also a sky. Light comes down in shafts, normal daylight filtered through chlorophyll but still bright enough to read by, to see dust motes and pollen hanging. Beneath that vault, avenues run straight for half a kilometer — not because anyone planned them straight, but because the figs on either side grew straight in competition for light and left a corridor between them. Markets would have been here, if there were market-goers. Instead the avenue is lined with open pods the size of houses, their interiors ribbed, empty, waiting.

And it is dense. Layered. Nothing is single-use. Above you, below you, beside you, there is always another surface of life. Vines as thick as cables run like plumbing and powerlines, but they carry water and sugar. Epiphytes the size of blankets hang and make walls within walls. The floor of one level is the ceiling of the next. Roots descend through open air to become pillars. Leaves overlap shingles, ten deep, so that from within you are always inside several shades of green. You have to turn sideways to pass in places where two growths have nearly converged. The air is close with the smell of green sap and warm wood and crushed flowers — cloying, peppery.

There are no animals here. That is the first silence you notice inside the noise. No birds. No insects, not even gnats. No rustle of anything with muscle. Nothing darts. The pollen hangs uncollected. Fallen petals lie where they fell and wither without being eaten. It should feel dead. It feels instead like the plants have expanded to fill every niche an animal would have occupied. They move enough for all of them.

Because it is not silent. It is deafening.

The city is unbearably loud, not with voices but with vegetation in turbulent motion. You expect a forest to creak. This roars. Wood groans under its own accelerated growth like a ship hull in a storm. Bark splits with rifle-cracks as trunks expand a handspan in a minute. Vines whip and slide against each other with a scream of wet fiber. Leaves unfurl with a papery snap that runs in waves down an entire street. Flowers detonate — literally burst open, spilling heat and scent — with a soft concussive *thump* that you feel in your chest. And underneath it all, a constant low hydraulic hiss, the sound of water being hauled up ten thousand trunks at impossible speed. You have to raise your voice to be heard to someone two meters away, and there is no one to speak to. If you stand still for too long, a runner will creep over your foot.

Nothing is stable. This is the rule you learn fastest.

Weirdness is not an accent here; it is the substrate. The place does not obey like a forest should. On a normal day you can see space misbehave. An avenue that was a straight shot to the central vault when you looked down it will be a closed loop when you look again, the far end grafted to the near. A chamber that held three pillars now holds five, the extra two having budded from the ceiling while your back was turned. Time dilates with growth — you watch a bud swell, hesitate, then decide to become a staircase and become one before you can step back, its steps still soft and green and damp under your feet. Gravity is negotiable where the growth is fast enough; roots grow up as readily as down, and water runs along the underside of leaves for a long time before it remembers to drip.

You try to map it. You can't. The young terrain underneath — those sharp, fresh-cut ridges — suggested a place that would erode slowly, over millennia. The plants have inverted that. They erode and build at the speed of thought, tearing themselves down as fast as they create. A block collapses inward as its supporting trunks decide to flower all at once, becoming soft and pulpy, and by the time the dust of pollen clears, a new lattice has already woven itself across the gap, tighter than before. Creation and destruction are not phases but the same motion, like breathing. The city is an anthill, a bazaar, a storm-system made of wood. If you stay an hour, the street where you entered will not be there when you try to leave.

And yet it holds as a whole. For all the churn, it remains recognizably a valley-city, open and regional in scale — you can stand on one of the high interwoven platforms near the vault and see from rim to rim, a few kilometers across, a whole low, warm, steaming basin of green infrastructure, humming and cracking and blooming under a normal sun. You can see neighborhoods, districts, thoroughfares, plazas of flat leaf-mosaic. You can never see it all at once; there is always another layer behind the one you are looking at.

The ecology has no predator, no prey. Only plants competing and cooperating with themselves. Reproduction happens by air — the detonating flowers fire clouds of heavy, warm spores that hang visible, then settle and immediately sprout on any damp surface, including your skin if you stand still, sending out thread-thin white roots that tickle before you brush them off. There is no rot, or rather rot is instant and repurposed; a limb that dies is hollowed and inhabited by seedlings within minutes, its matter never touching the ground.

The rules, if you stay, are simple and physical:

1. Do not expect ground to stay ground. Floors become walls.
2. Do not wait for quiet. The sound is the metabolism; if it stops, the air is about to change.
3. Drink where water collects — cup-leaves, trunk cisterns — but never from the same one twice. It will be gone or it will be a mouth.
4. Keep moving. The city is not hostile. It simply does not have a concept of *animal* to leave room for. If you are still, you become substrate.

By mid-afternoon the daily rain comes without clouds forming the way they should — the canopy itself transpires so much that it makes its own weather, and warm drops the size of grapes hammer down through the green vaults, drumming deafeningly on ten thousand leaves, collecting, channeling, vanishing into gutters of living wood. For twenty minutes the whole basin steams, light fracturing through water and leaf, everything dripping and shining. Then it stops mid-drop, as if turned off, and the splitting and groaning resumes louder than before, the city stretching in the heat, growing toward the normal light with a sound like a crowd shouting with wooden throats.

You came looking for a place. You found a building that is also a weather system that is also a forest that is trying, rapidly and loudly and without any tool or hand to guide it, to be a city — and succeeding.
