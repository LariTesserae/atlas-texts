This is the Low City. Not low as in humble — low as in pressed down.

You see it first from a distance because there is nothing to block the view. The land here is still new. A vast, flat plain that was under a kilometer of ice until very recently, geologically speaking, and hasn't learned how to be land yet. No valleys. No gorges. No river canyons. The glaciers scraped it clean down to raw basalt and till and then left, and what they left is smooth, dark, unfinished stone, still bearing the long striations of the ice like claw marks. It is embryonic geology - a place before erosion has had time to write on it. Water doesn't know where to go yet. It sheets and braids and pools in wide, shallow fans instead of cutting channels. You can walk for a mile and never gain or lose more than ten feet of elevation. The horizon is a perfect, cold circle.

And across that entire circle, packed edge to edge, is the city.

It was not built *on* the plain so much as it was extruded *across* it, all at once, in a single frantic generation after the thaw. There is no countryside bleeding into suburbs. There is just the plain and then, abruptly, the beginning of walls and roofs, and then wall-to-wall, roof-to-roof, block after block after block until the other side of the plain where the ice is still receding. A regional organism. A valley-sized town if the valley were a flat plate. From above it would look like a circuit board or a mold culture, dense beyond planning — no gaps, no parks set aside, no empty lots left fallow. Every inch is occupied. Buildings lean against buildings, factories shoulder up against tenements that shoulder up against greenhouses that shoulder up against refineries. Streets are not streets so much as the narrow gaps left between structures that grew too close. Courtyards are lightwells the size of a kitchen table.

The materials are what you'd expect for a city built fast and cold and meant to stay hot inside: poured concrete gone black with moisture, riveted iron, corrugated steel, brick that has never quite dried. Everything sweats. The air itself is cold — not a killing, high-peak cold, but a persistent, lowland, maritime cold that gets into the joints of everything. Your breath hangs. Your fingertips ache minutes after you put on gloves. And yet the light is perfectly ordinary, a flat, white, northern daylight filtered through a high, thin overcast, bright enough to read by, bright enough to cast soft shadows, with none of the gloom you would expect. It is a normal day, endlessly.

It should be arid in this cold. It isn't. This is a damp place, but not a wet one. There are no lakes, no proper rivers. The braided melt-sheets that wander the plain mostly vanish under the city and reappear on the other side, if they reappear at all. What water there is here is second-hand. It comes as condensation. As intermittent rain that freezes halfway into sleet and then melts again. As the endless exhalation of the factories themselves. Every chimney, every heat-exchange tower, every kilometer of poorly insulated steam piping that stitches the city together weeps water. It beads on the underside of every pipe, drips from every gantry, collects in the seams of the concrete, freezes into ferns of frost overnight and liquefies by noon and drips again. You never get your feet soaked wading, but nothing ever fully dries. Your cuffs are always a little damp. The iron is always rusting at the edges and being painted over again.

Because of that damp and because of the heat leaking from below, the city is impossibly lush.

This far north, this cold, you would expect lichen and stunted spruce. Instead everything that can hold soil does. Moss carpets the rooftops three inches thick, so saturated it looks like turf. Vines the thickness of your wrist — not ivy, something faster, paler, with leaves like translucent hands — have colonized every south-facing wall and are making aggressive progress on the north-facing ones. They grow over windows and are cut back weekly. Ferns sprout from gutters. Shelf fungi the size of dinner plates grow on the shady sides of factory walls where the condensation runs constantly. In the gaps between buildings where sun never reaches, little greenhouse alleys glow amber, where residents have wedged in planter boxes and grow kale and mutated tomatoes and tubers that taste of iron, all of it fed by waste heat and pipe-water. The city smells of coal smoke and wet earth at the same time. A living industrial forest.

It has to grow so fast because it is being eaten so fast.

The fauna are everywhere, but you have to recalibrate what fauna means here. Not wilderness animals - urban animals, industrial animals. Pigeons, of course, in clouds that move like smoke between the smokestacks. Rats along the steam culverts, fat and fearless in the warmth. Sparrows nesting by the thousand in the insulation of warehouses. But also stranger things that came with the lushness or followed the heat: pale moths as big as your hand that roost on the hot pipes and leave soot-like scales; long, centipede-like grazers that eat the wall-moss and are in turn eaten by the crows; quick, feral cats that have gone nearly white. In the cisterns under the dye works live blind white fish that were never stocked by anyone. Nobody remembers seeing them introduced. They just appeared where water stood long enough. The gulls that circle inland here have learned to ride the thermal updrafts from the rolling mills.

And none of it makes quite the right noise, which is the strangest thing of all.

This should be loud. It *looks* loud. Cranes swing constantly overhead carrying new panels to slap onto half-finished buildings while two streets over a building is being gutted down to its rebar skeleton for parts. Factories run three shifts; forges hammer, presses stamp, looms clatter, generators hum. The whole plain vibrates with anthill energy — a turbulent, bazaar-like, Solaris-like state of constant making and unmaking. Nothing stays the same for a month. A block that was housing is a machine shop by winter, a greenhouse by spring. Scaffoldings rise and are absorbed into the structure they were meant to repair. The city is not finished. It will never be finished. Its rule, unspoken but obeyed by everyone, is: keep adding, keep patching, keep moving, or the cold comes back in.

But the sound doesn't carry. The moss eats it. The snow that never quite melts into water eats it. The low pressure and the damp air and the close-packed walls swallow almost everything. What reaches you is muffled, as if heard through wool. A distant, subdued thudding instead of a clang. The murmur of engines like a heart through a chest wall. People here speak quietly without knowing why. Even the gulls cry thinly and then seem embarrassed. You can stand on a walkway between two roaring fabrication halls — sparks cascading inside, workers shouting inside their masks — and hear, primarily, the drip of condensation from the pipe over your head and your own breath.

The weirdness is not that any one thing is impossible. It is that they all insist on happening at once and in the wrong way.

The vegetation should not be able to be this lush in this cold, on this raw, un-weathered rock, with so little real water. But it is, because the city sweats for it.

The city should not be able to be this dense and still function — streets should be choked, collapse should have happened — but it does, because the buildings have learned to share walls, to grow into each other, to use each other's heat.

The industry should be deafening and soot-choked, but it is almost quiet, and the air, despite the smoke, is sharp and clean from the cold.

The plain should, by now, have rivers. It doesn't. The water still hesitates, spreading in sheets across basalt, evaporating or being drunk by the city before it ever learns to carve.

And if you stand still long enough on one of those high maintenance gantries that cross over the whole roiling, dripping, steaming, moss-swallowed, quietly hammering expanse, you will feel the other rule of this place, the one no one writes down: nothing here is stable except the turbulence itself. Constructions that look solid will be gone next week. A patch of brilliant green moss will blacken and slough off overnight when the pipe beneath it cools, only to reappear two buildings over. The ground beneath is still soft, still being made. The city knows it. So it keeps building itself over itself, a forest of iron and brick and leaf in the cold white light, packed tight and shivering and growing at the speed of rumor, all of it strangely, impossibly hushed.
