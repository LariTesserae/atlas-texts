This is a low place.

Not low like a pit or a canyon, low like the world itself has sagged here. A broad, shallow basin the size of a former sea, ringed by hills so worn down they are little more than a rise in the haze. You can see the whole of it if you stand on the lip - ten, fifteen miles across - and still not see it all at once because the air is thick. Warm air. Not desert-hot, not jungle-hot, but the steady, close heat of a greenhouse, or of a room where a boiler has been running for a very long time.

The light is ordinary. White-yellow sun at a normal height, filtered through a thin, high veil that bleaches no color but softens every shadow. There is no drama to the light. It simply is, and has been.

Nothing was ever built here.

There is no ruin, no road, no piling, no brick, no rebar, no cut stone. No straight line that was made by a hand. And yet - from the rim, before you even descend - you hear it.

You hear industry.

A low, wide, relentless factory sound. Not the sound of one machine, but of a district of them heard from a mile away: the huff and sigh of great pistons, the shriek of steam through a pressure valve, the clank of metal cooling, the deep electrical thrum of dynamos under load, the clatter of something heavy being stamped again and again. It is LOUD. It carries in the warm air and seems to vibrate in your sternum. You look for the source and for a long moment your brain insists there must be sheds, chimneys, towers down there in the basin, hidden in mist.

There are none.

The basin floor is mineral.

That is the first thing you understand as you walk down the long, gentle slope. The rock here is old. Not jagged or raw, but settled. Established. Everything has been weathered to its equilibrium shape - hummocks and domes and shallow bowls worn smooth as river stones the size of houses, channels cut and then cut no deeper, salt pans polished to a matte gloss. This is a landscape that finished eroding a long time ago and now simply persists. The slopes are mantled in a fine, rust-dark grit that has not moved in centuries, bound by its own weight. Boulders sit half-buried, their windward sides scooped into perfect spoons. Nothing is sharp. Nothing is new.

And nothing green grows. Not a blade, not a weed, not a tree. At 0.3 on any scale of vegetation, this is a world that has declined plant life entirely. What color there is comes from mineral alone - ochres and umbers, sulfur yellows, the deep iron red of hematite, the pale, almost-white of evaporite salts, the oil-slick iridescence of manganese crusts. In places a dark, bacterial varnish coats the rock like spilled varnish, but it is not plant, not lichen - it is a thin, inert film, a chemical stain that does not grow or spread. It just is.

Water is here, but not as a sea or a lake. This is a damp place, not a wet one. The air itself holds it. You feel it on your skin before you see it - condensation beading on every cool surface, on the underside of every stone, in the shadow of every dome. Occasional rain must come, because there are shallow washes that hold the memory of flow, and shallow pans that hold an inch of brine so saturated it barely ripples. But mostly the water is interstitial. Seeps that never quite become springs, that darken the rock and then vanish. Pockets in hollow stones that hold a cupful of warm, clear, faintly sulfurous water for years without evaporating or overflowing. Riverine veins that run for a few hundred yards as a thin sheet over smooth stone, making no channel, then sink back into the ground. The ground exhales. If you sit still, you can watch droplets form on a stone in front of you, fatten, and then - instead of falling - be drawn back into the stone's pores.

The sound - the impossible industrial sound - comes from the stones themselves.

As you walk the basin floor, you find the engines.

They are not built. They are grown, or rather, they precipitated. Geothermal chimneys, five or six feet tall or thirty feet tall, scattered sparsely across the plain so that you can walk minutes between them and always see three or four smoking in the distance. Each is a natural factory. At the base, a throat breathes. Superheated water and steam, under pressure from far below, forces its way through constrictions in layered silica and iron, and the rock shudders and huffs. *Chuff. Chuff. Chuff.* Perfectly regular, like a steam engine at work, without speeding or slowing. Higher up, narrower vents whistle - a clean, piercing factory whistle that holds one note for a long ten seconds, cuts off, holds again. Some chimneys are wet and muttering, their sides weeping boiling water that runs down fluted channels, turning turbines made of nothing but the shape of the rock itself - eddies that spin and spin in little potholes, perpetual, frictionless. Others are dry and hot, and the wind through their hollow spires makes a dynamo hum, an electrified drone you feel in your teeth. The air around them shimmers with heat and smells of hot iron and wet salt.

There are no belts, no wheels, no manufactured parts. But the processes are unmistakably industrial in scale and organization. Condensers drip. Presses seem to press. One low, wide formation repeats a deep, resonant *clang* every 47 seconds as a slab of thermally stressed basalt expands and contracts and slaps against itself. It has been doing so, at that interval, for longer than any measurement could matter. 

And none of it changes.

That is the strangest part, the quietly unusual thing that settles on you after the loudness stops being startling. This place is in perfect, frozen equilibrium. The water that seeps is exactly balanced by the steam that vents. The erosion that smooths is balanced by the mineral that precipitates. The heat coming up is the heat radiating away. You can watch a chimney huff and whistle for an hour and realize you have not seen a single thing alter - no pile of silica grows higher, no pool gets deeper, no sound gets louder or softer. It is a loud that has become silence through constancy. A factory that has been running at the same load forever, producing nothing, consuming nothing, needing no fuel and leaving no slag beyond what is already here. If you came back in a thousand years, that same valve would whistle, that same stone would clang, the same warm droplets would bead on the same stone.

Life, where it exists, has learned to be rare and quiet within that equilibrium.

You might walk an hour between sightings. A pale, eyeless isopod the size of your thumb, the color of salt, moving with extreme slowness across a damp film, feeding on the bacterial varnish. A flock - if you can call three of them a flock - of thin, translucent things like dragonflies but wrong, with wings that do not beat but vibrate like heated foil, that hover near a steam vent for the warmth and never land. They make no sound you can hear over the engines. They do not disturb the water. They are so sparse they feel like an afterthought the landscape tolerates.

There is a rule here, unspoken but absolute: nothing accumulates, nothing depletes. The world is not dead, it is not alive the way green places are. It is *working*. Running. The mineral world has found a way to be mechanized without manufacture, to be industrial without industry, to be loud without event, to be damp without drowning, and to go on doing so without ever moving forward in time. The basin does not age. It endures.

If you stay until evening, the light does not really fade so much as thin, and the normal light becomes a normal dusk, and the engines keep their same rhythm in the dark, huffing and whistling and humming under a sky full of ordinary stars, warming the damp stones for no one.
