It is high up.

At first you think it is a mountain, and then you realize the mountain is gone. What is left is the high place itself — a table of stone lifted so far into the warm air that the sky has thinned and darkened around it. Not a peak. A final, flat place. A mesa that erosion forgot how to eat any further. The wind has had millions of years to work on it and has given up, leaving it rounded at the corners like a bar of soap held too long, its basalt edges softened and slumped, its top planed smooth. It is ancient in the way a beach pebble is ancient; not jagged, not dramatic, just settled into the last shape it will ever have.

And that last shape is entirely covered.

You expect a ruin, or a town on a hill. This is not that. There is no natural substrate left visible. Every square hand of the tableland has been made into something. From the crumbling lip of the cliff to the cliff on the other side — a walk of perhaps three hundred paces if you could walk straight — the surface is a single, continuous, packed artifact.

It was built by hand. You can see it in everything. No saws, no precision. Stone broken with stone, mud brick dried in a warm sun that never seems to get brighter, timber long since turned to mineral-hard black lumps. Walls of unglazed terracotta and salt-caked limestone share walls share walls share walls. There are no streets as you understand them, only the negative space left when thousands of small rooms were pressed against one another until there was no room left to press. Alleyways that are not alleys but cracks between houses, barely shoulder-wide, roofed over by second stories and third stories leaning into each other. Courtyards the size of a sleeping mat. Stairs cut directly into the roof of the room below you. Doors that open onto someone else's wall a foot away. The whole hamlet — for it is hamlet-small, a single cathedral's footprint, a grove's size — is a dense, saturated hive. You could stand on any rooftop and see all its boundaries at once: the sheer drop on every side, the darkening sky beyond. And you could not find an empty place in it to sit that was not already a floor, a ledge, a lintel.

There is nothing else here to live with. The air is warm — not hot, but a kiln-warm that radiates back from the stone itself, a dry-baked heat that lives in the mass of the walls and breathes out at you all day and night. It is a dry heat without sweat, because there is no water to sweat with. No spring, no cistern, no well. The mineral around the mesa's edge is furred with salt and alkaline crust, white and pale yellow, where any damp would have been centuries ago. The chemistry of the place is not for water. Mortar is made of dust and ash and rendered fat that has long since desiccated. The few wooden beams are like iron from the dryness. If anything lived here it brought its water with it, and then it left or it dried.

Nothing grows. Not a lichen, not a weed in a crack. For growth there would need to be soil that was not brick, and rain that never comes. The only textures are made textures: thumbprints in sun-baked brick, chisel scores, the smooth wear of countless hands on a sill, the pitting where wind-blown grit has sanded the same corner for ten thousand years. No plant, no fungus, no animal. Not even insects in the cracks. The place is too packed and too dry and too high to host them. If something moves, it is dust.

The light is dim despite the warmth. The sun is not seen directly. Above the mesa hangs a permanent haze — high, thin, yellowish dust held in the warm, unmoving air, or perhaps a thin veil of volcanic ash from a world that finished its volcanoes long ago and forgot to clear the sky. It diffuses everything into a late-afternoon amber that never becomes afternoon and never becomes evening. There are no shadows with edges. The light comes from everywhere and nowhere and is never enough to read by, but enough to see the endless close walls, the muted reds and browns of the brick. The sky beyond the cliff edge is a deep, warm violet, and the stars are visible even at noon, unmoving.

And the sound.

You heard it long before you understood you were hearing the place itself. It is deafening. Not loud in the way of a crash or a shouting. Loud in the way of being inside a bell as it is struck. The wind at this elevation never stops — a high, constant pressure moving over the table — and the town that covers the table is an instrument for it. Every narrow crack, every chimney pot, every doorway that faces no door, every jar set into a wall by a primitive hand for storage and then left empty, is a flute. The wind forces itself through the saturated, porous mass of the hamlet and screams.

It is not a howl that rises and falls. That would be change, and nothing changes here. It is a single, total, saturated chord held forever at the same volume. A roar so full it stops being sound and becomes a pressure on your chest, in your teeth. It is the sound of air being shredded through a thousand stone throats at once, low and high braided together, a drone that makes the brick hum. You shout and cannot hear yourself. You cover your ears and hear it through your bones. Primitive shutters carved from slab-stone rattle in their frames without ever shifting position, vibrating in place. Salt crystals on the leeward walls tremble.

But nothing moves.

That is the rule of this place. For all its noise, for all its warmth, it is in equilibrium. The wind does not gust. The light does not shift. The heat does not cool. No brick falls, no dust settles, because the dust is already suspended in a perfect balance. The erosion that made this mesa flat and soft has reached its end; the building that filled it has reached its end; the sound that fills it has reached its end. Things are packed too tightly to collapse and too dry to rot. Hands built this place room by room, generation after generation, with simple tools — hammerstones, copper chisels green with age, mud and straw — until there was no room left to build, and then they stopped, but the place did not empty. It just remained, full.

There is a weirdness to it that you feel more than see: the scale is wrong. Sounds should fade with distance but here a whisper in a crack three houses away is the same volume as the wind at the cliff. The walls are warm to the touch even in the dimness, and they hold that warmth without a source. If you place your palm on a brick you can feel the faintest tremor of the deafening chord, and the brick itself gives slightly, not like stone but like dense, fired clay still curing — as if the whole hamlet is not quite stone, not quite pottery, but something in between that was made to be inhabited and then was inhabited by the wind alone.

If you stand at the very edge, where the last wall is also the cliff face, you can see that the outer bricks are fused by age and heat into a single mass, smoothed by time until you cannot tell where geology ends and craft begins. Below is nothing — a depth of warm haze that has no bottom you can see. Above is the dim, star-pricked vault. Around you, pressing in, is the total, unbroken, hand-made world, no larger than a village, no emptier than a lung full of air, roaring without moving, warm without sun, high and final and absolutely, permanently occupied by its own empty rooms.
