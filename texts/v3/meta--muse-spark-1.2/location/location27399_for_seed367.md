On the top of the world, there is a plain where the world has finished becoming.

It is not a peak. There are no peaks left up here. This is high country, 1.8 on any honest scale — high enough that the air is a little thinner and the sun is a little closer and the horizon runs away from you in a clean, uninterrupted circle. But it is not rugged. It is ancient. Erosion has had so long to work here that it has sanded everything down to its final shape. The plateau is a peneplain, a sea of stone that was once mountains and is now only their memory: long, low swells of rust-red and bone-white sandstone and exposed granite, rounded like the backs of sleeping animals, separated by shallow pans of pale dust. No cliffs. No talus. The stone itself is polished by wind, by heat, by time. When you walk it, your boots make a soft hush on powder-fine grit that has been grinding itself finer for a million years. Nothing sharp remains.

There is no water here. That is the first rule of the high plain.

Not low water. *No* water. No riverbeds, not even dry ones. No salt pans where water once was. No wells. The rock is too old and too porous and the air too warm and too hungry — anything liquid that ever pooled here was drunk by the sky a geologic age ago. The atmosphere is warm, a dry, kiln-warmth that radiates back out of the stone even after sunset. By midday the air shivers over the swells. Your mouth dries in minutes. Your sweat vanishes before it beads. It is a desert in the truest sense: a mineral world, sealed against water.

And then the ground drops out from under you.

You would walk right past it if you didn't know where to look. Between two of those low swells, where the wind has found a slightly softer layer of stone and worried at it for eons, the plain has collapsed inward. Not a canyon. Not a valley. A pocket. A single feature, hidden and contained and intimate as a room in a house the size of a continent. Perhaps fifty meters across, perhaps thirty deep — a roughly oval bowl with smooth, undercut walls, like a giant's kettle or a molar hollowed by decay. From the rim you can't see the bottom until you are right on the lip, because the walls lean in. The light doesn't quite reach it properly. The whole plain is bright, bleached, open to the sky, but down in the Kettle the light is dim, filtered, late-afternoon even at noon.

That dimness is the reason everything lives.

The air in the bowl is still warm — warmer, even, trapped and held by the stone — but it is sheltered from the scouring wind up top. At night, when the high plain radiates its heat furiously into the black sky and the stone goes cold, the warm air held in the bowl meets the cold stone and weeps.

Every night without fail, dew.

Not rain. Never rain. But a heavy, silent condensation that beads on every surface, runs in silver threads down the bowl's curved walls, and vanishes by mid-morning. For the two hours after dawn, the Kettle glitters.

And that is enough.

The bowl is lush. Impossibly, densely lush for a place with no water. Because nothing here drinks the way you expect. The vegetation is all succulent and aerial and waxy, evolved not for rain but for dew. The walls are draped. Thick mats of grey-green felt-lichen cling to the stone and swell at night like sponges. Rosette succulents the size of dinner plates stack themselves in terraces, their leaves fat and turgid, coated in a powdery bloom that seals moisture for weeks. From the rim hang curtains of tillandsia-like air plants, rootless, silver-bearded, that never touch soil at all — they live entirely on the nightly mist. On the floor of the bowl, where dust has collected and been held by roots for centuries, there is a crowded, jostling thicket of bottle-trunk shrubs and paddle cactus and a low, creeping fig that spreads over rock like a rug. The trunks are swollen, fibrous cisterns. Cut one open and it is not wet inside but slick with a clear, bitter sap that the people here drink in emergencies. The leaves are leathery, furred, or waxed to a shine. Everything is built to catch, hold, and keep. No leaf is wasted. No drop is spared.

Because of that density, the bowl feels larger inside than it is. It is crowded, layered, near-touching. Vegetation grows on vegetation — vines on succulents, moss on vines, fungi in the moss. You cannot see bare rock on the lower walls anymore. The green has taken the stone and made it soft.

And where there is that much plant, even without standing water, there is animal life beyond reason.

It is overwhelming. That is the word. Not abundant — *overwhelming*. The Kettle thrums with it. A cloud of fauna so dense the air itself seems to have weight.

In the dim, warm light, everything moves. Glossy black beetles the size of your thumb roll balls of dust and lichen. Clouds of tiny, pale moths — so many they look like dust motes — rise and settle on the succulent flowers that open only for an hour after dew. Lizards in turquoise and ash-grey do push-ups on every sun-warmed rock, throats flashing. Swifts and swallows and a dozen kinds of small, loud finch have built nests in every crevice of the wall, and at any moment half of them are in the air, stitching the space above the bowl with fast shadows. Geckos call. Crickets saw. The succulents themselves rustle with the passage of stick insects as long as your forearm, perfectly mimicking the cactus spines until they move.

It is loud. Not with water — there is no trickle, no lap — but with life. A constant, layered, active chorus. The high plain above is silent as bone. Down here, the sound is pressed in by the walls and held. You hear the buzz, the chirp, the flutter, the chirring of a thousand wings, the soft *plip* of dew falling from leaf to leaf long after sunrise, the chatter of people.

Because people live here. Of course they do. Where else would they live?

The town is the bowl. Or the bowl is the town — there is no distinction left. It is urban in the way a beehive or a cliff dwelling is urban: organized, layered, infrastructured, but scaled to the pocket that holds it. Primitive, hand-made, and utterly complete.

No metal worth naming. No machines. The stone is too soft for smelting and there is nothing to burn long enough to forge it anyway. Everything is stone-on-stone, fiber, wood, and clay. The inhabitants — a few hundred, three or four generations deep — have built with what the bowl provides. Low, round houses of stacked, dry-fit sandstone, chinked with dust-clay, tucked into the natural terraces of the wall like swallow nests. Roofs are woven tight from the dried beards of the air plants, thatched and re-thatched every season, dark and springy. Between houses, narrow stairways have been worn into the rock itself over centuries, polished to a sheen by bare feet. Cisterns are everywhere — shallow stone pans, leaf-lined basins, hollowed gourds from the bottle shrubs — set out each night to catch dew, brought in each morning, covered with woven lids. Children are tasked with wiping lichen with cloths and wringing them out. No drop is allowed to evaporate unwatched.

Walkways are strung with drying racks where strips of succulent leaf and insect larvae and small lizards are cured in the warm, dry air. The walls are patched with murals of handprint and ochre. Ladders of knotted fiber hang where the wall is too steep. On the very bottom, where the soil is deepest, they keep the gardens: not rows, but a dense, companion-planted tangle of edible succulents, beans that grow on cactus, and the one precious grain that stores water in its seeds.

The rules of the world here are simple and non-negotiable and understood by every child before they can talk:

1. Nothing leaves the bowl during the day without a water cloth.
2. Dew is gathered before speech.
3. No plant is cut without planting two.
4. No fire larger than a cook-pot, because smoke kills the moths that pollinate the air plants that feed you.

It is a normal world. No strange physics. No other chemistry. Just a very old, very high, very dry place that found one trick and held onto it for dear life.

If you stay until evening, you will see why they never leave. The sun drops, the plain above goes cold and violet, and the air in the Kettle turns still. The dim light deepens to dusk. Then — all at once, as the stone exhales — the succulents open. Pale, waxy flowers the size of plates unfurl with an audible soft pop. The moths rise in a blizzard. The birds go quiet, and the crickets and the geckos take over, louder and louder, until the bowl rings like a drum. The scent is overwhelming — warm sap, dust, honey, and the faint iron tang of condensation on stone. The people come out onto their roofs and terraces, small figures in that dense, green, stone room, and talk quietly, and wring out their cloths, and listen to the sound of their pocket world being alive — active, layered, humming, crowded — while all around them, for a hundred miles in every direction, there is nothing but warm wind over empty, ancient stone under an enormous, dry sky.
