You are high up. That is the first fact of this place and it does not stop being true.

The air is thin enough that you notice your lungs before you notice anything else, a faint work in every breath, but it is warm air. Not alpine cold, not the clean knife of snow-peaks. This is a tropical height, an old height. A plateau that rose a long time ago and never came down. The world fell away around it and left it there.

Because it is ancient. That is the second fact, and it explains the shape of the first.

These are not young mountains. There is no jaggedness here, no fresh uplift, no teeth. The stone has been rained on for so many million years that it has forgotten how to be a mountain at all. Everything is rounded, bellied, hollowed. The basalt and sandstone, red-black and bone-white in layers, has been weathered into soft domes and broad bowls, into fins as thin as paper and then further, into stumps. Into mesas so wide their tops are their own countries. Into chasms where the wind has found a weakness and worried at it until a canyon opened like a slow mouth. The erosion is not a process anymore; it is a finished state. The rock looks poured. It looks like wax that melted and re-hardened. You can put your hand on an escarpment a thousand feet high and feel no edge, only a long, warm curve under lichen.

And yet it is not flat. The scale tricks you. From a distance the plateau looks level, near-flattened, a table left after dinner. Up close it is a broken labyrinth at a regional scale — one valley, then another, then a third, each a day's walk across, each different. One holds a forest. One holds nothing but stone rippled like water. One holds a hidden river that you can hear for miles before you see it. You cannot take it in at once. It extends past the immediate, always past the next ridge, open and multi-featured and indifferent to you.

It is empty. That is the rule that governs everything living here.

Not barren — lush. Aggressively, almost unreasonably lush. Warmth and normal daylight and enough moisture have done their work. Wherever soil has been allowed to collect — in the wind-scoured bowls, on the flat tops, in the cracks that split the mesas — there is green, and then more green. Not the tidy green of temperate woods. Matted, waxy, fern-thick, vine-bound. Cloud-forest trees stunted and gnarled by wind but dense with epiphytes, their branches sagging with curtains of moss and staghorn and white orchids. Grasses as tall as a person that grow in swaying seas on the high flats. Succulents that store water for months swelling fat in stone pockets. In the sheltered basins, the jungle closes overhead completely, humid and dripping and whispering with growth.

But the green does not fill the space. It clings to the places that will have it and leaves the rest bare. For every acre of dripping thicket there is a half-mile of naked, polished rock where nothing grows but a crust of lichen. The emptiness is not absence of life, but interval. Vast space between presences. You can walk for an hour across warm, bare stone with the sky huge above you, seeing nothing, and then come abruptly to a wall of vegetation so thick you cannot push into it without a blade.

Water is why.

This is not a wet world. There are no great lakes, no permanent rains. It is a damp world. Intermittent. Condensation and pocket-water. Most days a white sea of cloud builds from below in the late morning, breaks warmly and violently against the plateau's sides in the afternoon, and wrings itself out in a short, furious rain that vanishes almost as quickly as it came. For ten minutes every surface streams. Then the sun returns and the stone steams. Water collects where the ancient erosion has allowed it to — in kettles and potholes polished to perfect bowls, in the deep clefts where a spring still sighs out of the rock, in the braided, shallow river that appears and disappears across the plateau, sinking underground for miles to re-emerge as a waterfall over a blind cliff. In the forests, everything is wet for hours after. On the open rock, everything is dry again in twenty minutes. Life here is expert at waiting and at holding. The trees have roots that crawl for fifty feet across bare stone to find a crack. The bromeliads hold a cupful of rain in their centers for weeks. You learn to look for water not as a landscape but as a secret.

There are almost no animals. That is strange, given how much plant there is to eat. You will go long stretches seeing nothing move except leaves. No flocks. No herds. But rarely, and therefore with disproportionate weight — a flash of iridescent beetle the size of your fist, drilling into bark. A gliding lizard with skin like bark that opens like a kite between trees and is gone. High above, a single raptor turning and turning on thermals coming up the cliffs, never flapping. In the pothole pools, blind white fish turn slowly. The absence makes each encounter feel like an event, a visitation.

People have been here, but lightly, and a long time, and not many.

You find their marks scattered, never clustered into anything you could call a town. A terrace wall of fitted dry-stone holding back soil on a slope where some old attempt at gardening has now gone wild with vines. A hut — or the idea of a hut — four posts of hard, resinous wood still standing, its thatch long ago blown away, its floor now a mat of moss. A cairn on a high dome, carefully stacked, each stone lichen-spotted, oriented to a gap in the distant mesas that frames the solstice sunrise. Shards of coarse, hand-coiled pottery the color of the rock itself, half-buried in silt near a spring. Rope bridges of fiber that sag across narrow chasms, some broken, some still holding, swaying slightly though no one crosses.

Their technology was and is primitive, manual. No metal that you find, or only simple beaten copper green with age, used for a knife, a needle. Tools of stone and fire-hardened wood, of woven reed and cured hide. Everything is made to be carried, repaired by hand, left behind without poisoning the place. The people who left these things understood emptiness and did not fight it.

If they are still here, they are as rare as the fauna and as quiet.

What is not quiet is the place itself.

It is deafening.

At elevation the wind never stops. It comes off the lowlands warm and fast and hits this ancient, fluted rock and is forced to sing. The mesas are pocked with holes and tubes and organ-pipes carved by water over epochs; the wind plays them all at once, a low, vast humming that you feel in your sternum more than hear, overlaid with whistling, with howling through the canyons, with a sudden percussive booming when a gust finds a big enough chamber. When the afternoon storm comes, the sound doubles, quadruples — rain hammering stone like shot, thunder moving sideways along the cliffs so close you can smell the ozone, water roaring briefly in every channel at once. Even when it is still, the forest is loud. The grasses rush and hiss like surf. The succulents pop and creak as they swell. The vines, growing fast in the warm damp — impossibly fast, turbulent, hyperactive — groan as they tighten around stone and tree. It is a place of chaotic change despite its ancient bones. Nothing looks like it stays still. Clouds boil up from nothing over the lip of the plateau and tear themselves apart in minutes. A mat of flowers opens in an hour after rain and is dead by evening. Landslides happen slowly, then all at once, a whole skin of moss and soil slipping off polished rock in a green avalanche that is over before you can step back, leaving raw stone that will be green again in a week.

And there is a strangeness to it that is not alien, just slightly out of true. The light is normal — warm, yellow-white, casting ordinary shadows — but the colors are too saturated, as if the stone and leaf are more themselves than they should be. Some plants grow like stone: shelf fungi as hard as sandstone, ferns with fronds that clink. Some stone grows like plant: basalt that peels in layers like bark, quartz veins that branch like roots. The horizon seems too close and too far at once. Time feels quick and deep simultaneously — the frantic, bazaar-like turnover of growth and storm laid over rock that has been sitting here since before any forest existed. You have the distinct and unsettling feeling that the plateau is not eroding anymore but being tended, that the lushness is not covering the emptiness but farming it, that the wind is not weathering the flutes but tuning them.

Stay still long enough and the turbulence resolves into a kind of order. Pockets of water, pockets of green, pockets of sound and stillness, scattered the way the old huts are scattered. Hollow, vast, warm, and loud — an old high place that has settled into its final form and then decided, perversely, to become frantically, deafeningly alive on top of it.
