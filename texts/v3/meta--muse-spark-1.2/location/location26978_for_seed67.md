It is low. That is the first thing.

Not low like a valley - there are no mountains to make a valley. Low like the world was poured flat and left to settle, a continent-wide pan with a lip so distant you will never see it, so low that the sky has weight on it. The air sits on it. The light does not have to travel far to reach the ground because the ground is already as far down as anything goes.

And the ground is not ground.

Everything is made.

There is no soil under it, no bedrock under that. If you lift a plate - and there are plates, millions of them - you will find only more making: struts, serviceways, aggregate poured over rebar, the underside of the plate glazed with condensation. If you lift that, more. It goes down through levels of construction until the concept of "natural" loses meaning. This place was not built *on* a landscape. It is the landscape. A low, warm, endless built plain that replaced whatever planet was here before, or was grown in one piece, fresh.

You can tell it is fresh. That is the second thing you learn when you walk it.

For all its vastness, nothing is worn. The edges are still sharp. The vast stepped terraces that fall away in increments of a hand's breadth - each terrace a shallow basin a kilometer across - have lips that could still cut you. The casting seams on the concrete are still present, thin ridges like the memory of a mold. The bolts on the catwalks have burrs. The sluices and spillways and gutters have no smooth bellies from centuries of water; they are angular, newly crisp, as if the water has only just been turned on. And it has, in a sense. This world is young. It has not had time to be softened. Even the rust is young - a thin, even orange blush over iron, not yet flaking, not yet deep.

It is warm. The light is normal, open daylight, a white-yellow sun high and diffused only slightly by humidity. Not glaring, not dim, just the light of a late morning that never ends. The heat it brings does not rise and fall. It stays. The air is warm on your skin like breath, like the inside of a machine that has been running long enough to be kind. And because it is warm and because it is low and because everything is made of surfaces that do not drink, the air is wet.

Not submerged. Not an ocean. Wet in the way a bathroom is wet after everyone has showered and left. Wet in the way stone weeps.

Water is everywhere but not deep anywhere. It condenses out of the atmosphere itself - the plain makes its own weather because it is large enough to need to. Clouds form a few hundred meters up from evaporation off the warm basins and then precipitate again as a fine, warm drizzle that never quite becomes rain. It beads on every rail, sheets down every vertical face, gathers in the shallow terraces in films ankle-deep and perfectly clear. It moves constantly but slowly, sheeting from one basin to the next over those sharp lips in glassy, silent veils. It seeps from joints. It sweats from the undersides of overpasses. The concrete itself seems to perspire. In the low places, where the terrace floors are slightly dished, it stands as broad, warm mirrors that reflect the sun and the undersides of gantries, trembling only when something moves through them.

There should be plants. With this much water and warmth and light, there should be choking green. There isn't.

There is nothing green here. No reeds at the marsh-edge of the pools, no algae even, or nothing you would recognize as algae. The mineral world won. The water is too clean, or too dosed, or the surfaces too recently cured to allow colonization. The basins are pale. The uprights are pale. The kilometers of pipe and duct and factory hall are painted in faded industrial enamels - cream, rust-red, a dull institutional blue - and where the paint has not reached, the bare material is basalt-dark aggregate and iron. It is a world of stone and ceramic and steel and poured composite, and the only color that grows is the thin mineral stain where water has run for a little while and left a trail of white salts.

And yet it is not dead. It is overwhelmingly, impossibly alive.

The fauna.

You notice the sound first, or rather the lack of it. For a place that is industrial and alive, it should be deafening. It is mostly quiet. The engines - and there are engines everywhere, low blocky buildings with vent stacks, pump houses, transformer yards humming behind chainlink that is itself part of the floor - run with a muffled, well-oiled thrum so even and so low it becomes a pressure in your ears rather than a noise. The water moves without roar, because the drops are short. And the animals - there are so many animals - make almost no sound at all.

They are dense. Layered. Near-touching. In the shallow water, crustaceans the size of your hand, flat and pale as the concrete, move in sheets, thousands per pool, filtering the mineral water, clicking legs almost inaudibly. Over them, on stilt legs, wading birds with too many joints stand shoulder to shoulder along every spillway, dipping needle beaks. On the rails, on the pipes, on the vertical walls, colonies of dark, beetle-like things with shells like enameled steel crowd so thickly the surface underneath disappears, each one twitching antennae that collect condensation. Things like bats but hairless, with translucent wings, roost in dense, breathing clusters under every overhang, layered three and four deep. Things like rats but smoother, built for concrete, run in quiet streams along cable trays. Insects - or insect-analogues - cloud the air in slow, heavy veils that part around you without buzzing.

There are larger things too, in the middle distance, wading through the basins: tall, silent grazer-forms on thin legs that drink the warm film and leave precise, three-toed prints in the soft silt of precipitated minerals. Predators among them, low and wide, so still you think they are part of the machinery until they flow forward a foot and stop again.

Nothing eats plants, because there are no plants. The whole dense, teeming, planet-scale marsh functions without them. The base of the food chain is the building itself and the water and the air. Biofilms - not green, but beige and pink - precipitate out of the mineral-rich condensation on steel. The filter-feeders harvest that. The beetle-things graze rust and salts. The birds eat the filter-feeders. The bat-things drink the humid air itself, straining it. Some things gnaw directly on the softer composites, and their droppings are the same composite, re-deposited. It is a closed loop, an industrial ecology where the factory is both habitat and food.

And it does not change.

That is the strange rule of this place, the quiet weirdness that settles over you after you have walked for an hour and realized the view has not changed and also has no reason to change.

In any other wet, warm world, water would carve. Life would overgrow. Machines would break or choke or evolve. Here, nothing accumulates, nothing erodes. The water sheets over the sharp lips and does not round them. The rust does not deepen past its first blush. The beetle-things crowd the wall but never completely cover it; their numbers stay exactly at the density the wall can support. A bird dies and is taken apart in minutes by small cleaners and its minerals go back into the film, and another bird takes its exact place on the spillway. The factories produce, pump, condense, drain - but the reservoirs never fill and never empty. The heat stays the same. The light stays the same.

It is not frozen like ice. It is frozen like equilibrium. A dynamic stasis where every inflow is matched by an outflow, every birth by a death, every drip by an evaporation, so perfectly balanced that time passes without leaving a mark. You can walk for what feels like days across the repeating terraces, past the same pump house repeated with minor variations every half-kilometer, through the same warm drizzle that condenses on your shoulders and runs off, and the scuff marks your boots leave in the mineral silt are erased by the next sheet of water within minutes.

The strangeness is not that it is alien. It is that it is so familiar - concrete, rebar, puddles, pigeons, factory hum - but scaled up to a weather system and then held perfectly, impossibly still. The sky is low because the world is low. The air is thick. The light is ordinary. The water keeps weeping from the sharp, young edges of everything, and the dense, silent multitudes drink it and filter it and run over it, maintaining the quiet, warm, wet machine that maintains them.

It has no history yet, despite its size. Or its history is happening all at once, continuously, without ever becoming the past.
