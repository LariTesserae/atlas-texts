It is not a valley. It is not a desert. It is a pocket.

You find it by accident on the salt flat, where the ancient plain has been sanded down to almost nothing. For a hundred kilometers in every direction the world is a low, cool table — elevation so slight it feels like sea level without a sea. The wind has had ten million years to work it over, and it has. There are no cliffs, no dunes, no sharpness left. Only a faint undulation in the white-grey crust, like the memory of topography. Erosion here is finished. The world is done eroding. Everything that could be taken has been taken, and what remains is smooth, pitted, and patient.

And then your foot breaks through.

Not far — six inches, a foot. A hollow sound. You kneel and brush away the salt and the cemented dust, and find an edge. A lip of iron, rounded to a softness like sea-glass by age, but still holding its shape. A slit, maybe two meters long and half a meter wide at its widest, where the crust has sagged and split over something buried. Cool air breathes out of it. Dry as paper. It smells of cold iron and dust and something else, something animal.

You have to turn sideways to fit.

Inside, the world opens sideways, not down. A single room, if you can call it a room — a lenticular blister in the earth, maybe thirty meters across and four meters high at the crown, a bubble trapped under the flat. The light in here is normal, which is strange. It shouldn't be. But the crack in the roof lets in a clean, flat white daylight, a shaft that moves slowly across the hollow from morning to afternoon. No shadows like a cave. It illuminates like a workshop with a skylight. Dust hangs in it, never settling.

The terrain of the pocket is not ground. It is a floor, but barely. The base is the original bedrock ground down until it is almost polished — a dark basalt that has been wind-lapped inside the hollow until it feels like the worn sole of a boot. Over that, a crust has formed: salt, gypsum, fine iron scale that has flaked from above, all cemented into a brittle, pale pan. It crunches and then yields under your knee. And nothing grows. Not a speck of green, not a lichen, not a mold you would recognize as plant. The chemistry is wrong, the air too dry. Any water that ever was here has been gone for geological ages. If you breathe through your mouth your tongue dries in minutes. Your lips chap. There is a faint sting of alkaline dust on any exposed skin. This is a mineral world.

And it is absolutely covered in life.

You notice sound first, before you understand what you're seeing. It is loud. Not loud like an engine — loud like a room full of people whispering and clicking and rustling all at once until it becomes a roar. A dry, chattering, ticking susurrus that makes the air vibrate. The walls seem to buzz.

Then your eyes adjust and the floor moves.

Density is the wrong word. Saturation is better. Every surface — floor, walls, low ceiling — is occupied. Not sparsely. Completely. Layer upon layer, bodies nearly touching, shifting, crawling over each other in a restless mat. Fauna, overwhelming. You have never seen biomass like this without water or plants to support it.

They are not insects, not exactly, but your brain calls them that for lack of better filing. Pale, translucent things the size of your thumb, like pill-bugs made of smoked glass and thin iron wire. Their backs are domed plates of nacreous mineral they secrete themselves, each one a little scratched mirror. Underneath, dozens of needle legs, and mouths like tiny rasp-files. They move by rippling. They eat the pan itself — the salt, the rust, the concrete dust — scraping it with those mouths and leaving behind a faintly darker trail of compressed pellets. Lithovores. Stone-eaters.

And things eat them.

Through the mat of grazers dart faster shapes — long, centipede-thin hunters the color of oil, a handspan in length, so fast they blur. They drive their heads into the grazers and pull them wriggling from the carpet, crunching the mineral shells with an audible pop. The grazers scatter, flow around the disturbance like water, and close again. On the ceiling, hanging like stalactites, are larger, sessile things — leathery sacs that pulse slowly, opening umbrella mouths to wait for the jumpers that misjudge their leap. Nothing is still. Even when an individual is still, the mass moves. It is an active ecology, predator and prey and scavenger, running at full speed on nothing but dust and rust and each other. The rules are simple and total: eat the rock, eat the eater, become rock again for the next eater. Water is not part of the contract.

Scattered through this living carpet are the structures. Not a settlement — just traces. Human, once. Three or four low pump housings, industrial, riveted iron and poured concrete, each no larger than a shed, spaced out across the hollow like islands. Their paint is long gone, their corners worn to radii by the same ancient abrasion that smoothed the bedrock. Their windows are blind with salt. But they are not dead.

One of them still runs.

In the center of the pocket, sunk half into the floor, is a machine that must have kept this blister dry when the world still had water to keep out. A sump engine, two cylinders tall as a man, flywheel, belt-drive, a soot-blackened diesel that chugs without fuel you can see. Perhaps it drinks the same thin methane that seeps from the pan. It turns over slowly, thud-thud... thud-thud, shaking the floor. A piston rod, pitted but still oiled by condensation from the cool air, slides in and out with a sigh. A loose belt slaps. It is not high technology — it is heavy, electrified industrial brute force, the kind of thing that was built to run for a century and then was left to run for ten thousand. It is cool in here, that particular machine-cool of metal and shadow, maybe 12 or 13 degrees Celsius. The air that it pushes is cool on your face, and carries the clicking mat-smell of the fauna with the sharp tang of ozone from its ancient generator, which still hums somewhere behind a panel and throws a pale, normal-white work light across the far wall. That light, with the daylight from the slit, keeps the whole pocket in a clear, even illumination — you can see every detail, every leg, every flake of rust. Nothing hides.

The whole place is unusual in a way that takes you a minute to place. It's not alien. It's just... inverted. A desert that is not empty but too full. A room the size of a house that contains a prairie's worth of animals, all living on stone, sealed under a salt flat that should be lifeless. An engine with nothing to pump that pumps anyway, because to stop would be to let the weight of the plain above settle.

If you stand still, the grazers begin to explore your boots. Their file-mouths test the leather, the rubber. They are not afraid. You are just another mineral surface, briefly warm. The hunters pause and taste the air with feathery antennae, then flow on.

If you lie on your back, you can see the underside of the salt crust that roofs you — laminated like pages, each year a white line — and through the slit, a narrow bar of the pale, high sky. The sound rises and falls around you, the engine thudding under the chitinous rain. You understand that this pocket has been like this for a very long time, and will be like this for a very long time after you seal the slit and leave — a hidden, dense, loud, cool little world running on dryness and iron, endlessly busy, asking nothing from the outside.
