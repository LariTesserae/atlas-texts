The air thins before you get there.

You climb for a long time through switchbacks that shouldn't still exist — this range is too old for switchbacks, too old for peaks. It has been wearing itself down since before anything had a name for wearing. What was once a thrust of jagged mountains is now a high, broad back of stone, a plateau that forgot it was a mountain range. The elevation is still there, three thousand meters up where the light goes clean and the sky presses close, but the shape is gone. Everything has been rounded off, softened, flattened into long whaleback ridges and shallow, saucer-wide valleys that hold the sky like bowls. Ancient erosion — not dramatic, not carved canyons, but the patient, settling final-form of rock that has decided to be low and smooth again. You can walk for a day and gain or lose only a hundred meters, even though you are higher than most birds fly.

And it is completely, impossibly green. Not forested. Not covered. *Made* of plant.

The vegetation didn't colonize the city. The vegetation *is* the city, or the city learned to be vegetation to survive the altitude and the cold.

From the lip of the plateau you first think you're looking at a ruined industrial town in a valley, because that is the shape your brain offers: streets, blocks, towers, factories with sawtooth roofs and brick stacks. Then the wind shifts and the whole valley moves.

Trunks as thick as apartment buildings rise out of the soft stone, but they aren't trunks. They are braids of trunks, of root and stem and vine and moss and leaf fused into load-bearing columns a hundred meters across. Their bark is layered like shingles, like slate, like corrugated iron rusted to a deep green-black. Canopies interlock so densely that they form ground — secondary ground, tertiary ground — suspended avenues and plazas of living wood fifty, a hundred meters above the actual stone. You walk on leaves the size of doors, on limbs that have flattened and broadened into boulevards, on moss that has grown so thick and dense it has the give of carpet and the resilience of concrete.

And built into it, through it, with it, is the town.

It is unmistakably urban, settlement-scale, organized. Not a village. A city that was meant to house tens of thousands. You see the grid if you squint, the way the great bough-avenues intersect at right angles where no tree would ever intersect. Factories still stand — or grow — with walls of woven wattle that has lignified into something harder than brick, with windows that are just gaps the plant left open, edged with amber sap. Water-towers are swollen figs the size of houses, half wood, half riveted steel, the steel plating now grown over with bark that holds the rivets like jewelry. Conveyor belts of chain and belt run between structures, creaking along catenaries of vine, carrying not coal but seed-pods the size of cars, dripping condensation.

It is industrial here, but an industry that has been absorbed. Smokestacks exhale, but what they exhale is cool steam that instantly beads on every leaf. Engines throb somewhere deep in the root-buildings, low diesel thuds and electric whines, turning massive wooden gears as tall as houses. Looms the size of city blocks knit fibers pulled from aerial roots. You can hear it long before you see it — the sound is loud and unrelenting, not because any one machine is deafening, but because everything resonates. The wind through the high canopy is a roar. The creak of living timber under its own weight is a constant chorus of groans and pops. The factories beat their pulse against it all. There is no quiet place on the plateau; even when the machines cycle down, the plants themselves click and sigh as they grow, audible as joints popping.

And yet it is sparse. For all its scale — this is a valley town, open, you can see from one end to the other across the kilometers of green infrastructure — it feels empty. The density is wrong. A city this big should be crowded, but there are too many vast, empty platforms of leaf-street with nothing on them. Storefronts carved into living bark stand open and hollow. Assembly lines move, but no one tends them. Most of the buildings are just shape, architecture without inhabitants, holding their form because the plants remember the shape they were told to hold. You walk a boulevard that could hold a parade and you are the only thing moving on it except the slow crawl of vines tightening around a lamppost. Presence is scattered, rare, like a few lights left on in a hundred windows.

That is true of everything living. The biomass is total, overwhelming, plant-as-air, plant-as-ground, plant-as-wall, but almost nothing *moves* in it. Fauna is rare to the point of superstition. No birds call. No insects cloud. Once in a while you see something — a pale, long-limbed grazer the size of a deer picking its way across a moss field, its coat so mottled with lichen it flickers in and out of visibility — and then it is gone and you aren't sure it was ever there. The ecosystem is dynamic though, actively so. Not through predation, but through plant processes accelerated to animal speed. You watch a runner vine swell and throw itself across a gap in ten minutes, bridging a street you just crossed. Sap rivers pulse visibly under translucent bark. Leaves unfold, yellow, drop, rot, and are reabsorbed in a single afternoon cycle. Mist forms, drips, is drunk.

Water is scarce for how green this is. There are no rivers here, no lakes — too high, too old, the stone no longer channels water that way. The damp comes sideways. Every morning and every evening the temperature, which never gets warm, drops from cool to cold and the whole plateau sweats. Condensation forms on every leaf, every steel plate, every gear tooth — heavy, cold dew that coalesces and runs in braided rivulets down trunks and down the sides of buildings, collecting in cisterns that are just cupped leaves or hollowed-out factory basements. It rains maybe once a week, a brief, thin, cold rain that the canopy catches and passes hand-to-hand for hours before any of it reaches the stone. The rest of the time the city glistens as if it just rained. Your clothes never quite dry. Your breath always smokes.

Light is normal, mercilessly so. This high, the sun is bright and direct at midday, neither dim nor colored. It falls straight down through the gaps in the canopy and makes hard, white pools on the leaf-ground. And that is where the weirdness starts, because the light does not behave after it lands.

Shadows fall the wrong way. Sometimes they fall toward the sun. Sometimes a shadow cast by a vine-column will pool on the ground and then, while you watch, slide sideways off the leaf and up the side of a factory without its object moving. Geometry is tired here. Walk down a boulevard of root-pavement and count the intersections to the steam-mill, and on the way back you will count a different number. Buildings you leave behind you are not always there when you look over your shoulder; sometimes there is just smooth bark where a doorway was, and if you wait with your eyes averted it will grow back, slightly different — a window where there was none, a chimney that exhales a different color of steam. The plants are not hallucinating. The plateau itself has been here so long that reality has worn thin along with the rock. Places that old don't have to be consistent anymore.

The rule here seems to be: growth remembers form, but not precisely. The original engineers taught the trees to grow into factories, into apartments, into power stations, and the trees learned the *idea* of a town. Now they keep iterating it, even empty. A new leaf-building buds overnight with perfect stairwells that lead nowhere, with smokestacks that smoke cold mist because that is what smokestacks do. You can open a door that is still soft and green at the edges and find inside a room that is too large on the inside, or that has a floor made of overlapping leaves that ripple under your weight like water.

You stand on a high limb that serves as an overlook, the wind loud in your ears, the cold biting at your damp sleeves, and you look out over the green city. It extends valley-wide, a sparse, loud, cool, industrial forest that is also a town, humming and creaking and growing even as you watch, its grid dissolving at the edges into pure, ancient, plant-mass where street becomes branch becomes ridge becomes sky. No birds pass. The steam from a hundred stacks rises straight up and then, all at once, bends sideways as if blown by a wind that isn't there.

It is not abandoned. It was just never built for you.
