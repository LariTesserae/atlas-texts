It is low. Not low like a valley between mountains, low like the bottom of the world where everything has settled.

You come down to it through air that gets thicker and warmer with every step, down a slope that isn't really a slope but the long, weathered root of something that has been here long enough to forget it was ever growing. The stone underneath is there, but you can't see it anymore. It has been smoothed and pocketed and given up. Established. Everything here is in its final shape, not eroding anymore but *eroded* — rounded, hollowed, held.

The Town is small. You can see its edges from anywhere inside it, which should not be possible.

From the outside it is a single thing — a hamlet-sized knot of green, a cathedral-grove about as wide as three old houses and as tall as a church steeple, sitting in the damp heat of the lowlands like a single breath held. There is no skyline to it. It is one plant, or a colony so entangled it has become one plant. A banyan, a fig, a mangrove and a vine and a flower all at once, braiding itself into architecture.

From the inside it is empty.

That is the first wrongness. You duck through the outer curtain — great leaves the size of doors, waxy and warm to the touch, sweating condensation that never quite becomes rain — and the space opens. Not a forest interior, choked and close. A hollow.

The vegetation is total. It is the substrate, the walls, the floor, the ceiling, the air itself. But it has grown itself hollow the way a ribcage is hollow. Vast vaulted chambers where the trunks have pulled apart from each other and become pillars. Streets that are not streets but the gaps between living walls. Houses that are not houses but living chambers, curled leaves and inflated pitcher-structures big enough to stand in, pneumatically open and closed, connected by vine-arteries and bark bridges and aerial roots as thick as your waist that hang and do not touch the ground.

It is urban. Unmistakably a town. There are avenues, intersections, second stories, balconies, courtyards, even something like a square at the center where six massive trunks lean away from each other to make a plaza. But nothing was built. Everything was *grown*. There are no tools, no cut marks, no nails, no stonework. The hinges are living joints. The doors are membranes that learn your weight and part. The gutters are the grooved backs of leaves. The whole settlement is pre-tool, biological-only, and infinitely more complex than any carpentry.

And it is warm. Not hot. A low, mammalian warmth that comes off the plant mass itself. The air is damp but not wet. Water here is intermittent, intimate. There is no river, no lake. At 1.4/3, water remembers itself in other forms. Every surface beads with condensation at all hours, fat droplets running down the undersides of leaves and collecting in cupped bromeliads the size of bathtubs. The ground — which is not ground but a mat of interwoven root and moss and humus a foot deep — sighs when you step on it and releases a shallow puddle that soaks into your ankle and is gone. In the late afternoon, which is always late afternoon here, a soft rain falls for seven minutes without clouds. It comes from the canopy itself, exhaled. Then it stops. Pockets of water hang in the air for hours after, suspended in spider silk or in the pitcher-houses, quivering.

The light is normal. That is what makes the rest of it so unsettling. No endless twilight, no alien sun. Just warm, ordinary daylight filtered through three layers of canopy until it falls in mottled gold across the root-floor. Dust motes — which are actually pollen and tiny white seed parachutes — hang in those shafts. You can see perfectly well. You wish you couldn't, briefly, because--

It is deafening.

The sound has weight.

At first you think it's cicadas. Then you realize there are no cicadas, there are a hundred things each louder than cicadas layered on top of each other until the air itself vibrates. The Town groans. The wood creaks as it actively grows, an audible stretching, a wet popping of new cells inflating. Sap rushes behind walls with the roar of a culvert. Leaves the size of sails shudder and clap. And the fauna —

The fauna is abundant to the point of being the atmosphere. The hollow is empty of large presences but absolutely crammed with small, frantic life. Not empty meaning lifeless; empty meaning *spacious*. The scale of the architecture is for something much larger than what lives here, so the inhabitants move like motes in a cathedral.

Clouds of beetles with backs like polished jade shellac, hummingbirds that are not birds but moths the size of birds, glass frogs that live in the condensation pockets and sing with voices like struck bells. Ants run highways along the vine-bridges in ribbons so thick the vine looks black. Things with too many legs and too few joints flicker through the leaf-chambers. Everything is calling, chewing, drumming, mating, warning, all at once, at full volume. You have to open your mouth to equalize the pressure. When the pitcher-houses contract — which they do, breathing — they exhale a low organ note that you feel in your molars.

And the motion never stops. This is not a still place. Dynamic, active, predator-prey and growth and weather all happening at biological pace but accelerated, visible. You watch a vine inch forward across an alleyway in the time it takes you to watch it. A flower the size of a doorway opens as you look at it, petal by petal, with a soft tearing sound, and immediately a flock of needle-beaked things darts inside, and immediately a mantis-thing the color of the flower snaps one of them out of the air. The condensation on a leaf swells, falls, is caught by another leaf below. The whole Town transpires, drips, opens, closes.

Then the weirdness folds it.

You walk from the square to what should be the edge — maybe fifty paces — and you are back in the square, but from a different angle, and the pitcher-house that was closed is now open and has someone else's footprints in its moss, even though you have seen no one else. The geometry is wrong. The interior is larger than the exterior. Low elevation, yes, but inside, down is not always down; some root-floors slope gently *up* towards the center, and water still runs outward. If you stand very still and listen past the deafening chorus, you can hear the Town listening back. The walls adjust. A balcony that was not there before has grown while you were looking at the floor.

Gravity goes soft near the center. Pollen and little frogs hang a fraction too long in the air before falling. Light pools in corners where no shaft reaches. Time does not pool, it stretches — a drop of condensation will take an hour to fall, then fall all at once.

The rule here is not that plants imitate a city. The rule is that when a plant is allowed to become total, when it is the only material available in a warm, low, damp hollow that has had a very long time to settle, it *becomes* a city because a city is just what dense, organized living looks like when it has nowhere else to go. And when it becomes a city without any mind to plan it, it becomes a city that does not need people, only occupants. So it hollows itself for occupants who never came, and the local fauna — abundant, shrieking, ecstatic — moves in to fill an architecture that was waiting for them.

You find a chamber where the walls are thin enough to see light through, like skin, and you put your hand on it. It is warm. It pulses, slow. Outside, beyond the leaf-doors, the seven-minute rain starts again, exactly on time, drumming on a thousand leaves at once until the roar is so loud you can't hear your own breathing.
