It is not a city on a mountain. It is a city *in* a mountain, but only if you still call this a mountain.

Far below, there is a dry world. You can see it only once, on the way up, and then you don't see it again. A plain of salt and cracked basalt that exhales heat in visible sheets. The air shimmers and sticks. From that plain, without foothills, without warning, a single needle of rock has been pushed up.

Not folded. Pushed. Like something pressed its thumb up through the crust from below while the rock was still soft.

It is young. That is the first thing you understand when you get close enough to see texture. There is no softness to it. No sand has had time to round it. The outside is black glass and scabbed basalt, stair-stepped with cooling fractures, feathered with razor ridges where the lava quenched too fast. It is still outgassing. Thin white fumaroles sigh from its flanks and vanish instantly in the dry wind. If you put your hand on it, it is warm. Not sun-warm. *Body-warm*, planet-warm. This thing has not finished being born.

It goes up and up until the sky changes color. At its base you were in white-yellow scorching light. At its tip you are in indigo. The elevation is obscene. It is the kind of height where the air should be cold enough to kill you, but it isn't - the stone carries its own heat with it, a column of geothermal fever rising eight kilometers straight up. The wind up here is thin and fast and hot as breath from an oven, and it screams over the glass outside. Inside, though, is something else.

Because the needle is not solid.

About two thirds of the way up, where the pressure inside the spire equalized as it cooled, a bubble was trapped. A vertical throat. Maybe three hundred meters tall, forty across at its widest — not a cavern, a *pocket*. An omission. From the outside you'd never know it was there; there is only a fissure, a hairline crack just wide enough for a person to slip through sideways if they knew where to look. Inside, the scale collapses. The world becomes intimate, hidden, contained. A single room that thinks it's a city.

Someone built here.

Not on the rock, *with* it. The settlement is organized on rings. When the bubble cooled, the walls left behind smooth, draped ledges of black glass like frozen tar. The builders did not cut into them so much as coaxed them. They grew balconies out of the wall itself — sintered ceramic and basalt re-knit at the molecular level, seams you can only see when the light hits at an angle. Narrow streets spiral down the interior of the throat, one continuous street that loops and loops, lined with doorways and shallow dwellings and conduit channels. Not sprawling. Compact, dense in organization but not in population. A whole urban logic compressed into a single vertical cylinder. You could walk from the topmost door to the bottom in ten minutes if you didn't stop.

But you will stop. Because it is empty.

Not ruined. Not abandoned in the way that implies chaos. It is *hollow*. The density is wrong for a town. There are hundreds of rooms and none of them are full. A cup left on a ledge. A folded thermal blanket. But vast amounts of nothing between each presence. The air itself feels over-proportioned, as if the architecture was made for a crowd that never arrived, or that left politely and took its noise with it. Your footsteps don't echo the way they should in a stone chamber; the space eats sound.

It is dim. The light does not come from the fissure entrance — that crack is too small and the glass too thick to let the savage outside sun in except as a single, thin, dust-filled blade at mid-height that never reaches the floor. The light here is made. Or found.

Low along the walls, in the channels that braid between the dwellings, a dull amber glow clings to ceramic veins. Not bulbs. Not fire. Something that lives in the stone when current is passed through it, a field-effect luminescence, the color of cooling iron. It is enough to see by but not enough to read by, and it makes the edges of everything soft. Shadows pool deep under the balconies. Far above, the throat narrows to a point where you cannot tell if it ends in rock or in dark air. Far below, it bottoms out not in a floor but in a slow taper that disappears into heat-haze.

It is hot. Consistently, stiflingly hot, but not a dry desert heat. A damp heat.

This is the contradiction that makes the place active.

Outside, there is almost no water. Inside, the rock sweats.

Because the spire is hot and the outside air at this elevation is thin and cold compared to the interior, condensation never stops. Water is not present as a lake or a river. It is present as behavior. It beads on the cooler undersides of balconies, gathers into fat droplets, falls. It mists off the warmer glass and immediately precipitates again on the next surface. The engineered channels — shallow troughs that run along every street-edge — are always dark with moisture, always carrying a slow, silent trickle downward. Not quite a stream. More like the building is perspiring.

In places the builders have helped it. Where troughs intersect, they have hung lattices of black filament that shouldn't be able to support their own weight, let alone water. But the water doesn't fall through. It hangs. Spheres of water the size of fists hover in the lattice, perfectly still, quivering occasionally when the temperature shifts. Some are clear. Some have grown a skin of dissolved silica and look like smoky glass marbles. If you touch one, it breaks and re-forms around your finger, cold for an instant before the ambient heat warms it. The tech here does not announce itself with chrome. It is quiet, post-mechanical. It holds gravity lightly, as if it has negotiated with it.

There is no soil. No plant. The 200-degree sweep of glass and ceramic has never hosted anything green, and never will. What looks like lichen from a distance is just mineral bloom — sulfur yellows and iron oxide reds fractaling out where the damp has leached salts from the stone. What looks like fungus in the darkest corners is cooled silica foam, brittle as sugar. The world here is purely mineral. It has skipped biology altogether and gone straight to architecture.

And by the same token, nothing lives here. No birds could reach it; the air outside is too thin and the entrance too hidden. No insects buzz. No rats. The only things that move are the water and the air and the occasional soft tick of the stone contracting.

The sound is mostly absence. The Howl of the high wind outside does not penetrate except as a very low, felt pressure. Inside you hear: drip. Drip... drip. The faint ping as a droplet hits a ceramic channel. The whisper of the trickle finding its way down. The almost inaudible hum of the lattices holding their water-spheres. Your own breathing, too loud. If you stand still for a long time, you hear the rock itself — a deep, irregular creak as the spire settles, still adjusting to its own height.

But for all its quiet and emptiness, the throat is not static. It is busy.

The convection drives a slow, continuous cycle. Hot air rises up the center of the void, cool moist air slides down the walls. Where they meet in the middle, a perpetual, nearly invisible cloud forms and dissolves, forms and dissolves. A weather system for one room. At intervals — maybe every twenty minutes, maybe every hour, time gets strange in dim heat — the cloud gets heavy enough to release a soft, warm rain that falls straight down the hollow center without ever touching the sides, evaporating before it hits the bottom taper. Then it starts again. Drip. Trickle. Gather. Hover. Rain. The city washes itself.

And there are stranger dynamics. In the center of the void, where nothing should be, dust motes sometimes stop falling and hang, suspended not by the lattices but by something else. Light bends around them slightly wrong, as if the air is thicker there. If you toss a small stone into the open middle, it does not fall straight. It drifts, slows, takes a beat too long to land. The builders learned how to make weight less insistent. They built their balconies too thin, their spans too wide. The whole place has a feeling of being slightly unmoored from the planet that made it — as if the spire is so tall, so hot, so new, that the rules are still negotiable here.

Stay long enough and you understand the rule of this place: *water remembers, stone forgets, and engineering mediates.*

You came up through the heat, through the glass, through the crack, into a pocket of dim, damp, urban quiet at the top of the world — a town built for no one, inside a rock that is still warm from its birth, where it rains indoors and gravity is polite, and where every surface is waiting, patiently, to be touched by condensation and then dried again.
