It is not a valley. It is not a plain. It is a room.

High up where the mountain has not yet decided to be a mountain — raw, black, and just-born — the rock split while it was still hot and never healed. The split didn't erode. Erosion hasn't had time. The edges are still as sharp as when they broke, glassy basalt with conchoidal fractures that catch light like spilled oil, unsoftened by wind or water because there is no water here to soften anything.

You find it by climbing until the air goes thin and hot at the same time, which should be impossible, and then noticing the mountain is breathing.

The pocket is perhaps forty meters across. Maybe sixty. Scale slips when you try to hold it. From the ridge it looks like a shadow between two shoulders of stone, nothing. From the lip, it is suddenly interior - a contained world, a geode left open to the sky. The sky above is high-altitude brutal, a normal sun at noon but too close, bleached blue-white and buzzing with heat. The light falls straight down into the pocket and stays there, pooled. It doesn't bounce the way light should. It accumulates in the corners like dust.

The air inside is hotter than the air outside by twenty degrees. It doesn't move. It hangs, dense with mineral vapor, with the smell of hot iron and ozone and salt without sea. There is no water here. Not a trickle, not a film, not even damp in the shade. The stone is utterly dry, a kiln-dryness that pulls moisture from your lips in minutes. The floor of the pocket is not soil but a cooling slag - a porous, rust-red and black scoria still warm to the touch, faintly giving under your boots, ticking as it contracts. A century from now this floor might be ground down to sand. Now it is still the shape it was when it foamed.

And yet it is lush.

Not in the way a jungle is lush. Sparse. That is the word that takes a while to understand. Each thing is lush individually, extravagantly alive, and then there is a long, empty interval of hot stone before the next thing. Five meters of nothing, then a single plant that shouldn't exist.

They are not green. Or not only green. They root directly into the hot slag without soil, without water, their roots like bare copper wire splayed and fused into the rock. From a central knuckle they throw up fronds that are too perfect, too manufactured — broad, waxy blades of a deep malachite and verdigris, edged with what looks like verdigris rust, translucent when the sun hits them. Some are taller than you. They are heavy with themselves, drooping slightly under their own fluid, which is not water. When wind brushes them — rarely — you can hear the fluid inside move, viscous, a slow slosh like oil in a drum. A cut would not bleed sap; it would bleed warm brine, or molten salt, clear and stinging. They transpire not water vapor but a faint metallic mist that hangs around each plant for a foot before vanishing, leaving a fine crust of white salt on the stone leeward of it.

There are maybe a dozen of them in the whole pocket. Maybe more if you count the young ones, which are just knuckles of folded ceramic-looking leaves hissing quietly as they assemble themselves from air.

No animals have found this place, or if they did they left no sign. No insects in the fronds. No birds over the lip. The absence is complete. The plants do not need pollinators. They shiver and reproduce by heat.

Because the place is a factory without anything built.

That is the wrong sentence but it's the closest.

There are no walls, no machines, no artifacts, no straight lines made by hands. And yet everything hums with industrial logic. Along the eastern wall — the rawest wall, still showing the hackles where the mountain tore — a series of basalt columns have formed as natural pistons. They are part of the rock, but they move. Slowly. So slowly you think you imagined it until you look away and look back and a seam that was closed is now open a finger's width, exhaling a breath of sulfurous heat that makes the nearest frond flutter. Below the floor, deep, something turns over with a low pressure sigh, a geothermal engine the size of the pocket itself. You can feel it through your soles more than hear it: a subsonic thud... thud... thud... with a minute between thuds. When it thuds, the light in the pocket wavers. The shadows lag a half-second behind the objects that cast them.

The fronds are oriented toward that thud, not toward the sun.

Pipes that are not pipes — smooth, hollow stalactites of black glass — run down the walls and drip not water but a bead of molten mineral every few hours. The bead hangs, glowing amber, stretches, falls onto a ledge, and hardens into a blister of slag. The plants nearest to those drips are the largest, their leaves stained black with the fallout.

The rules here are close to normal but not sealed shut.

Gravity is slightly too strong near the floor and slightly too weak near the lip, so dust takes a long time to fall and then hits hard. Sound is mostly absorbed by the heat and the porous stone. It is very quiet. The quiet has weight. What little sound there is — the tick of cooling rock, the slow sigh of the piston wall, the creak of a frond expanding a millimeter — seems to come from inside your own ear.

If you stay long enough you see the weirdness not as weird but as the law: the plants are not growing. They are being extruded. The rock is not still. It is being manufactured from below, fresh, embryonic geology pushing up so slowly it looks static. The pocket is getting smaller, very slightly, as the walls swell inward. In a thousand years it will seal over and become a hot, dark inclusion inside the mountain again, a pocket of pressed leaves and salt. Now, for this brief moment at high elevation, it is open, hot, dry, and lit like any ordinary afternoon, holding its sparse, impossible garden in the palm of stone that behaves like a machine that grew itself.

It does not want anything from you. It does not notice you, except that the air around you, for a breath after you pass, holds your shape in heat-haze before dissolving.
