It is very high, and it is very old.

That is the first thing you understand when you get above the cloud sea. The range you are in was once something savage - folded, thrust, Himalayan - but that was a long time ago. So long that the earth has forgotten how to be sharp here. What is left at nearly four thousand meters is not peaks but the memory of peaks: vast, bald domes of stone worn down to the patience of old bone. The shoulders of the mountains have been rounded and rounded until they are like the backs of sleeping animals. Between them, the valleys are impossibly broad and smooth - U-shaped, scooped out by ice that has been gone for a million years, their floors flattened, their walls burnished to a gentle slope. Nothing juts. Even the cliffs, where they do occur, are not jagged but undercut and weeping, dark with age, softened at the edges like wet paper.

The air is thin and cool. Not bitter, not alpine-death cold, but a steady, deep cool that lives in the stone. 8 or 10 degrees, even at noon. You breathe a little harder than you expect, and every smell is clean and metallic. Light never quite arrives here. The sun is a diffuse pearl behind a permanent high overcast - not a storm, just a veil of stratus that catches on the ancient domes and never leaves. It makes a dim, even day without shadows. Twilight lasts for hours. The stone and the sky become the same grey for long stretches, and distance goes soft.

This is not a dry height. There is not much standing water - no great lakes, no roaring river through the main valley - but nothing is ever truly dry either. The world sweats.

Condensation is the weather. Clouds push up against the domes and condense on every surface. In the mornings the whole valley drips. Rivulets form overnight on the undersides of leaves and on steel girders alike and run down in cold beads. The rock itself weeps - seep springs appear from nowhere on those smooth slopes, darkening the stone, feeding narrow, fast rills that cut sudden, mossy gutters before sinking again. Cisterns and catch-basins are everywhere because you have to catch the water when it gives itself to you. You hear ticking, not rushing. The sound of a single drop falling from a pipe onto a slate roof, repeating. The sound of a thousand such drops at once, muted.

Because where there is seep and cool and dim light, there is green. An unreasonable green for this altitude.

It should be lichen and scrub and nothing else up here, but the valley has become lush. Not jungle-lush; boreal, temperate, relentless lush. A deep, mat-like lushness that has learned to live low and dense. Carpets of moss a foot thick smother every horizontal surface that isn't actively scraped clean. Dark ferns - hardy, leathery, black-green - erupt from every crack in the masonry. Dwarf rowan and stunted birch, wind-pruned and gnarled, grow right out of walls, their roots splitting concrete, their crowns flat as tables from the constant wind. On the old domes outside town, the tundra is not patchy but complete: a springy, saturated meadow of moss and sedge and woolly alpine flowers that quivers when you walk on it. And inside, the town itself is half-buried in it. Vines as thick as your wrist. Ivy that has swallowed entire factory wings. Roofs that are not tiled but thatched with living sedum, humming with seeping water.

The town occupies the whole floor of one of those broad, ancient valleys, and the lower slopes of the domes on either side. It is not sprawling, it is layered. This is its strangeness.

From a distance, in that shadowless light, you might not at first see where geology ends and construction begins, because there is so little difference in color or texture. The builders used what was here: the same grey, feldspar-flecked stone, now cut into blocks and already re-softened by a century of weather. Houses are stacked almost touching, stone to stone, wall sharing wall, alleyways so narrow the moss bridges them overhead and turns them into tunnels. Then on top of houses, more houses. Factories braced against hillsides on iron stilts that have gone orange-black with rust, their brick sides streaked with mineral tears. Above them, walkways and pipe-bridges, water-towers and gantries, all riveted and bolted and then immediately colonized by green. Nothing is planned on a grid; the settlement grew like lichen itself, following the seep lines, following the easiest curve of that ancient, softened topography.

That is the industrial heart: not belching, not modern. Industrial in the way of looms and forges and water-power. Everything here runs on fall and weight and condensation. The rills that seep from the dome are gathered into flumes, into penstocks of bolted iron that scream faintly with pressure. They drive wheels, they drive pistons, they drive whole mills where shuttles still clack. There are soot-blackened brick chimneys but they rarely smoke now; they breathe a thin, pale steam that vanishes instantly in the cool air. You can see the belts turning through soot-grimed windows, endless loops of leather and chain moving without hurry. Hydroelectric dynamos hum in stone sheds, and the whole town is laced with black cable that sags under the weight of moss and drips. At night, bare bulbs burn amber behind frosted panes, the only warm color in all that grey-green.

And it is quiet. That is the odd thing for a place so dense and so active. The stone and the moss eat sound. The cool air deadens it. There is no loud commerce, no shouting that carries. The factories are muffled, as if heard through wool - a low, pervasive thrum you feel in your teeth more than your ears, the sigh of steam valves, the tick-tick-tick of water on metal. Footsteps are softened by moss. People move through those near-touching alleys with their shoulders almost brushing walls green with growth, speaking quietly because the thin air makes you conserve breath. You hear, instead, the small active things: the wind combing through the dwarf birches, the creak of a gantry expanding in the cool, the sudden flutter of a wet canvas tarp, the fizz of water hitting a hot pipe and vanishing.

Life is busy but not animal. That is the other quiet. For all its lushness, for all its motion, the valley is almost empty of fauna. No flocks. No herds. The insects are small and dark. The birds are rare - a high raven that circles the domes on wide, slow wings and cries once, hoarse, and is gone. Occasionally a marmot whistles from a stone pile and slips away. The mosses and ferns have outcompeted everything else; the ecosystem is botanical and mechanical more than zoological. The things that move most are the plants themselves - visibly, over days, you can see a vine find a new handhold on a factory wall - and the machines, endlessly cycling water and weight.

The rules here are simple and old:

Stone softens, it does not break. Water is always moving but never pools for long unless you hold it. Keep your roof clear or it will become a meadow. Keep your flume clear or it will become a stream. Air is thin; work slower. Light is dim; colors are a luxury. Rust is a kind of soil.

If you stay, you learn to live densely, layered like the moss, your building touching the next, your life touching others, because the valley is only so wide and the rest of the world is steep, smooth, and ancient in every direction. Beyond the last street, the meadow resumes immediately, thick and springing, climbing the great, soft domes until the cloud eats it. You can walk fifteen minutes from a clattering mill floor and be in a place where the only signs that anyone ever lived are the faint, straight lines on the horizon where the domes were once quarried, now themselves furred over in green.
