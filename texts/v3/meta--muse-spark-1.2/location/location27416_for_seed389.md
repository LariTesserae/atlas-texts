It is high. Higher than any city should be.

You feel it first in the lungs before you see it properly — the air is thin and hot at the same time, which should be a contradiction. At this elevation the sky should be cold, brittle, blue-black at the edges. Instead the air hangs thick and warm, shimmering a little over every surface, as if the mountain itself is exhaling. Sunlight is normal, white-yellow and direct, not dimmed or doubled or colored strange. It falls straight down at noon and makes hard, short shadows. But the heat does not come from the sun alone.

The land is still being made.

That is the second thing you understand. This is not a mountain that has been weathered down. This is a mountain that has just arrived.

A plateau torn upward — a continent's knuckle pushed up five or six kilometers in one raw, recent gesture. The rock is young. Black and red basalt, still angular, still broken in clean conchoidal curves where it cooled. There are no softened ridges, no river valleys cut deep, no scree slopes sorted by age. The edge of the plateau drops away in a single, staggering basalt wall, columnar in places, shattered in others, like the side of a mold that hasn't been knocked away yet. The top is not flat but bubbled, blistered, folded while still semi-plastic. You can see flow lines frozen mid-ripple. Erosion has barely started its work. Wind has not yet learned the shape of the place. Where rain does fall, it doesn't know where to go — it hesitates in shallow pans, in unconnected basins, in thumbprint depressions in the cooling stone, then steams away.

There is no soil. No green. Nothing that could be called a plant has ever taken hold here, or if it tried, the heat and the mineral air boiled it off. The world is mineral. The colors are mineral: graphite black, iron oxide red, sulfur yellow where the vents breathe, ash grey, and the dull white of salt crusts left behind when the damp evaporates. In places a thin, dark varnish grows on the rock — not moss, not lichen — a sheen of iron-eating bacteria or something simpler that precipitates metal in faint, concentric rainbows. It glints when wet and is otherwise invisible. That is the only vegetation this place permits.

And yet it is dense. Impossibly dense.

Because the city is here.

It does not sit *on* the plateau so much as it *is* the plateau's upper crust, extruded and organized. A settlement the size of a valley, but compressed vertically and laterally until every gap is filled. From the lip of the escarpment to the central uplift, structure touches structure. Not a sprawl — a stacking. Terraces cut directly into still-warm stone, then faced with more stone, then built over with frames of black iron and heat-stained brick. Scaffolds that became streets that became load-bearing walls. Factories and dwellings and cisterns and switching yards all layered together without a plan you can see, but with a logic you can feel — the logic of using the heat and the height itself.

This is an industrial city, but not a roaring one. That is its strangest quality.

You expect the clamor of industry at this density — hammers, presses, sirens, voices. Instead it is mostly quiet. The machines here are large, slow, and patient. Geothermal engines the size of houses breathe once every few minutes, pistons rising on a column of superheated steam drawn from deep below the new rock, then sighing down again. Rolling mills turn basalt foam into rebar at the speed of cooling lava. Cooling towers do not roar; they tick. The whole plateau pings and pops as it contracts, a continent-sized sheet of glass settling. The dominant sounds are mineral: the faint hiss of a pressure valve, the wind threading through the dense alleys and making the hanging cables hum at a single low note, the distant, dull thump of a forge hammer that you feel in your molars more than your ears, once, then a long silence, then again.

People are here, many of them — the density demands it — but they move slowly, conserving breath in the thin-hot air, conserving motion. You see them on the high catwalks in heat-mirrored coveralls, tending to things that mostly tend to themselves.

Water is what makes it habitable at all, and water is scarce in the way that makes every drop intentional. There is no permanent river, no lake. The plateau is too new and too porous and too hot to hold one. Instead there is dampness. At dawn, condensation beads on every cold metal surface — on the undersides of pipes, on the shade side of basalt fins — and is channeled by grooves cut into the rock into covered cisterns. Three times a day, the automated misters on the upper terraces fire for eight minutes, not for plants but for the machines and the lungs, laying a fine, cool veil that evaporates before it can puddle. When rain does come, which is intermittent and violent, it hits the hot stone and half of it flashes directly to steam, filling the dense streets with a sudden, blind, scalding fog that smells of iron. The rest collects in the embryonic basins — shallow, perfectly round pans a few meters across, none connected to any other — where it sits for a day, dark and still and reflecting the sky, before the heat drinks it again. Around each pan, the salt crust is white and cracked like dead skin. The city maps itself by these temporary mirrors.

Fauna, like water, is pocketed and rare. Nothing lives here in numbers. No flocks, no swarms. Once in a while you see a single glider — a thing like a dragonfly but the size of a hawk, with wings of translucent silica that don't beat so much as ride the thermals rising off the plateau wall. It lands on a cooling tower, folds its glass wings, and is gone. In the cisterns, something pale and eyeless turns slowly. The workers leave it alone. It is not food. It is not pest. It is just the one other thing that learned how to live on intermittent damp and stone.

The rules of this place are not the rules of a normal mountain town.

1.  **Heat rises from below, not above.** The sun is normal, but the ground is warmer than the air. At night the rock glows faintly in the infrared, and you can warm your hands by holding them near a fresh terrace cut. Buildings are insulated *against* the ground.
2.  **The land is still soft.** In the deepest cuttings, the basalt is not fully solid. It moves on a timescale of weeks. Doorframes go out of square and must be re-cut. A street laid last year now has a two-degree tilt that no one bothers to fix. The city has learned to build with joints, with slip-planes, with flexible iron staples, because to build rigidly here is to be broken.
3.  **Height is power.** The plateau wall is a 4,000-meter drop. The city drops cargo not on roads but on controlled falls — heat-resistant pallets on long cables that sing as they descend through the quiet air to the lowlands no one lives in anymore. What comes up comes up on the slow, counterweighted lifts powered by the plateau's own cooling. Everything moves vertically before it moves horizontally.
4.  **Quiet is required.** Sound carries too well in the thin air and the dense stone canyons. Loud noise triggers rockfalls from the still-unstable rim. The city enforces quiet the way other cities enforce traffic laws. Even the factory whistles are tuned low, to that molar-rattling thump that does not echo.

If you stand at the edge where the dense city thins just enough to see out — on a basalt balcony still warm through your boots — you can see the scale of it. Not infinite. Not a planet-wide ecumenopolis. A region, a town the size of a small country, clustered and layered on its hot anvil of new earth, open to a sky that is too close. Beyond the rim, the world falls away into hazy distance, lower, older, eroded lands that look soft by comparison, like wax. Behind you, the city clicks and sighs, dense as a honeycomb, slow as cooling rock, strange in its patience.

It is not finished. It was never finished. It is still in the act of cooling into the shape it will keep for the next million years, and the city cooling with it, learning not to fight the heat or the height but to use them, one condensation droplet and one slow piston stroke at a time.
