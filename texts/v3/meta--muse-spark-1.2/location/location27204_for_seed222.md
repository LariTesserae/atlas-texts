It is high.

Not mountain-high, not peak-high — table high. The land rose a long time ago and decided to stay there. A plateau the size of a small country, lifted two kilometers above where the sea should be, but there's no sea anymore, or not here. The air is thin and bright and warm. That shouldn't go together — high should be cold — but here the sun is closer, or stronger, or the atmosphere is just slightly wrong in a way that lets light pour down without being filtered. Light has weight here at midday. It presses on your shoulders.

You stand on weathered stone. Not jagged. Not new. This place has been wind-shaped for a very, very long time until it found its equilibrium. Established erosion — the cliffs are not crumbling, they are *resolved*. The plateau is cut by broad, shallow basins and soft, U-shaped valleys where the wind has planed everything down to long curves. The rock underneath is a warm, rust-dark basalt, but it's almost entirely veneered in something else: a pale, pitted limestone, wind-honed into yardangs and waves, like frozen dunes. Everything is smoothed. Edges have been sanded off for millennia. Even the boulders look like sleeping animals. In the late afternoon, their shadows are long and perfectly sharp because there is so little dust in the air.

The sky is too large. Pale at the zenith, bleached almost white-blue with brightness, then deepening suddenly at the edges where the plateau drops away into hazy air. You can see the entire bowl of the sky. At noon the shadows shrink to black puddles directly under things, and the stone glitters. Not with mica — with a fine salt.

Water exists in the negative spaces. There is no river. There is no lake that would show up on a map. This is damp in the way a desert is damp when it remembers water. Every morning before the heat comes, the air exhales. Condensation beads on every surface, runs down the fluted sides of the limestone into runnels and seeps, gathers in the low places. By ten, it is gone again — boiled off into a brief, low mist that hugs the hollows, then vanished.

But it leaves its mark. Follow any shallow valley to its center and you will find the damp line. A dark seam of soil, almost black against the pale stone. Pockets. Small, stubborn oases strung like beads on a thread that is only visible for two hours a day. Where the water lingers longest, life holds on.

Vegetation is sparse and it knows how to wait. It is not a forest. It is not even a grassland. It is a scattered calligraphy. Low, silver-green cushion plants cling to the lee sides of rocks, tight and waxy to seal in overnight dew. Out in the flats, things grow far apart, as if respecting each other's need for that condensation. There are wiry shrubs with leaves like needles that point straight up to reduce sun, and between them, in the damp seams, sudden riot: short, brilliant green grasses, mats of moss that look soft from a distance and turn out to be tough as felt, and once every few hundred meters, a tree. Not tall. Flat-topped, umbrella-crowned, with bark like elephant skin and roots that spider out across bare rock for twenty meters to drink the morning. They are old. You can tell because the wind has combed them all in the same direction, permanently leaning slightly east.

And then the fauna, which makes no sense until you watch for a while.

It shouldn't be abundant. There's not enough plant to feed it. But the air is *full* — not with big things, but with many things, moving quietly. This is not a noisy jungle. This is a high, quiet abundance.

At first you only see the stone. Then the stone moves.

Lizards the color of salt and shadow flicker over rock, pausing to do push-ups in the sun. Big, slow tortoises like ambulatory boulders graze the moss seams, their shells patched with the same lichen that grows on the rocks. Insects — but not as you know them: swarms of glass-winged things like flying seeds that drift without buzzing, and heavier, iridescent beetles that click from shrub to shrub. And above all, the fliers.

They ride the thermals that come screaming up off the plateau edge. Ribbons. That's the best word. Six-foot ribbons of membrane and hollow bone, translucent amber in the bright light, undulating through the air without a single flap for minutes at a time. They are not birds. They have no feathers, no beaks — just a wide, manta-like head with a fringe of sensory filaments that taste the damp in the air. There are dozens of them at any given moment, turning slow circles high above, occasionally dipping to skim a damp hollow and sip. Smaller fliers dart beneath them, flocks of needle-swifts that move so precisely they look like thrown stitches.

Everything is quiet. That is the strangest thing. With so much life, there should be calls, songs, roars. But the thin, warm air swallows sound. The ribbons make no cry. The insects have no buzz — or their buzz is at a pitch above hearing. Even your own footsteps are muffled by the salt-crusted stone. The only sounds that carry are small, close sounds: the tick of a beetle's legs on bark, the sigh of a tortoise breathing, the faint *tink* as condensation drips from a leaf onto stone after dawn. When the wind moves, it moves without howling. It just passes over the smoothed rock like a hand over a head.

The built things are almost not there. If you weren't looking for them you would think they were natural.

Scattered across the plateau, kilometers apart, are structures. They are not ruins in the ordinary sense. They haven't fallen. They have just... stopped being noticed.

One near the central basin is a pylon, maybe thirty meters tall, but so thin it looks like a scratch in the air until the light catches it. It doesn't cast a proper shadow. Instead, light bends slightly around it, pooling at its base like spilled mercury. Up close, it isn't solid. It's a lattice of something that isn't quite matter — matte black filaments that phase in and out of visibility, holding a shape that is more like a held note than a building. It hums, but not in sound; you feel it in your molars. The air near it is cooler by a few degrees, and the condensation never evaporates. Moss grows thick there, and the tortoises gather, and the ribbons circle lower, as if they know.

Another, half-buried on a ridge, is a low dome like a blister in the rock, opalescent. You can see through it sometimes. Inside, there is no machinery, no furniture, just a space that seems larger than its outside and a faint geometry of light suspended in mid-air — slow-moving, self-writing angles that rearrange when you look away. Animals avoid it. Or maybe they respect it.

There are more. Small plates set flush with the stone that are warm to the touch at midnight. A long, straight scar in the plateau where stone has turned to glass for three kilometers, perfectly smooth, reflecting the sky. None of them connect. None of them seem to do anything that you can name. They are not a city. They are artifacts left by something that understood engineering on a scale where gravity and light are just materials to be woven. Post-modern is too small a word. They are not technology sitting *on* the landscape; they are a second set of rules laid gently over the first, and the landscape has grown around them.

That's the weirdness, not loud but persistent. The light is wrong near the structures. Shadows fall slightly toward them, even when the sun is on the other side. If you stay still long enough at noon, you see afterimages that don't fade, but slowly drift upward like dust motes. The night sky — and the nights are warm, windless, and impossibly clear — shows stars that stutter. Just a frame skip, as if the sky needed to refresh. The ribbons seem to know how to use this. They fly in straight lines between pylons that are beyond human sight, surfing something you can't feel.

And the place moves. It is not static. While you watch, a distant dust devil, thin as a pencil, wanders across a flat for an hour and then dissolves. The cushion plants open their pores at dawn with an audible, collective soft *patter* as they drink. A predator you never see — something that casts a fast, narrow shadow — makes the needle-swifts suddenly coalesce into a tight ball and drop. The damp line migrates inch by inch over weeks as the season turns a fraction warmer.

It is open country. You can see to the edge where the plateau falls away in cliffs so weathered they look like staircases for giants. You can walk for a day and not get anywhere that looks different, and then come over a rise and find a hidden basin, perfectly circular, filled with dark soil and buzzing quietly with life that exists nowhere else. Scale here is human and not human at once — a valley you could cross before lunch, under a sky that makes you feel planetary.

Weird, bright, high, warm, sparse and full. The wind moves. The water hides until morning. The old, smooth stone holds the heat of the day long into the evening, and the quiet animals come out to lie on it, and the thin black pylons stand and do whatever they have been doing since before the stone was smooth.
