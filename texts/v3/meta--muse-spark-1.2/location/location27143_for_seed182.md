It is not a valley. It is a cut.

From the plain you wouldn't know it was there until you are at the lip. The high savanna goes flat and cracked and yellow for kilometers, and then the ground simply ends — a clean, rectangular absence, as if a god pressed a thumbprint into still-wet stone and lifted. 

About four hundred meters long, half that wide, and a hundred and fifty deep. Small enough that from any point inside you can see the whole of it, rim to rim. The scale makes it feel like a room with the sky for a ceiling, a cathedral sunk into the earth.

And the walls are wrong. That is the first thing you notice on the descent. They are too sharp.

This place is young. The basalt and red sandstone where the earth was quarried — or collapsed, or opened, no one agrees — has not yet learned how to be weathered. The edges are still knife-clean, stair-stepped where the cutters or the quake left them. No soft slopes, no talus fans. Where water has tried to cut gullies, it has only just started, shallow black teeth running two or three meters down before vanishing. The chutes and ledges still hold drill marks and heat stress fractures, fresh as a wound. You can see where the rock sheared, glassy in places, glittering with unoxidized minerals that haven't had time to dull.

You go down by the switchback road cut into the eastern wall, or by the cage elevator that runs on a shuddering industrial chain, or — if you live here — by the ladders and rebar staircases that have been welded to every vertical surface since.

Below the rim, the temperature changes immediately. On the surface it was hot and dry and open. Here, the heat is trapped. The pit holds the day's sun like a kiln holds fire. By mid-afternoon the air at the bottom is thick, damp, 38 degrees and not moving. Your shirt sticks to you before you are halfway down.

Light barely reaches here. The sun, for most of the day, is only a bright bar far above, reflected once, twice off the sheer walls before it dies. At noon, for about twenty minutes, a solid column of white light falls straight down and strikes the central cistern and for a moment everything glitters — wet leaves, tin roofs, oil puddles. The rest of the time it is a permanent dim, a smoky amber dusk supplied by strings of bare bulbs, sodium worklamps, and the forge-glow from the factory tier. Shadows are long and vertical. You learn to see by the glint off wet stone.

It should not be lush here. It is. 

That is the strangeness.

Because the pit sweats. The hot air above the plain cools as it sinks into the shadowed depth, and the stone itself exhales. Every surface drips. Not a river — there is no river — but condensation in a thousand places. Beads form on the basalt and run together into trickles, into pipes, into gutters hammered from scrap. Pockets of water collect wherever someone has thought to put a barrel or a tarp. The central cistern, a rusted industrial tank sunk into the lowest point, is always half-full and always rippling from drips that fall sixty meters from moss on the upper ledges. Between rains — which on the surface are brief and violent — this is the only water, and it is everywhere and nowhere. Damp stone, damp cloth, damp lungs. Everything smells of wet iron and green.

And where there is damp and heat and dim light, things grow as if they have been ordered to.

The city is packed. That is not an exaggeration. There is no empty ground left at the bottom. Urban in the truest, most literal sense: three and four stories of layered construction crammed wall to wall, built from what was here and what was brought down. Concrete block, corrugated tin, reclaimed scaffolding, brick fired from the pit clay, all stitched together with industrial cable and steam pipe. Walkways overhang walkways. Balconies become streets for the level above. Factories and homes are the same building — a lathe on the ground floor, a kitchen on the second, a sleeping loft under a roof of living vine.

Because the vegetation did not wait for permission. In this heat, in this wet, it grows through everything. Not trees — there is no room or light for trees — but a dense, furious mat of it. Elephant ear and hanging fern spill from every gutter. Creeping fig has found the rebar and uses it as a trellis, splitting concrete as it goes. Moss carpets the north-facing walls ten centimeters thick and glows faintly green-black in the lamplight. Air plants hang from power lines like laundry. Flowering vines — waxy, pale, night-blooming — push out of factory vents and open only in the dim, filling the hot air with a heavy, sweet rot perfume that almost covers the smell of coal smoke and hot oil. People do not clear it. They train it. They let gourds grow across their roofs for shade, let beans climb the sides of generators to keep them cool. The city is as much grown as built.

It is industrial, but not as you expect industry to sound. The lower tier along the western wall is all forges and stamping presses, textile looms, a small foundry that recasts scrap into new sheet metal, a power plant that burns whatever comes down the elevator — diesel, charcoal, dried vine. There are engines everywhere, big flywheels and pistons, belts turning. But they are throttled. They run slow. 

Everything here runs slow.

The air is too thick and too still for speed. The flywheels turn with a patient, unstoppable *thud... thud... thud*, one revolution every few seconds. Looms clack at a walking pace. Water wheels fed by the cistern overflow creep rather than spin. Even people move slowly, conserving water, conserving cool. The dynamic of the place is long-cycle: rust forms over months, vines advance an inch a day, a building settles a degree every year as its foundations sink into damp clay. Change happens, but you have to live here to see it. A visitor thinks nothing moves. A resident knows the third house on the left is two bricks lower than last summer.

And it is quiet. For an urban, industrial place, impossibly quiet. The thick air and the enclosing walls dampen everything. The thud of the engines is muffled as if heard through water. Voices do not carry. There is no wind at the bottom, only the occasional sigh when hot air spills over the rim and drops. What you hear most is water: the tap-tap-tap of condensation on tin, the hiss of steam being released in small, careful bursts, the rustle of leaves shifting in heat. Once in a while a work song, hummed low, because singing loudly makes you sweat.

There is almost no fauna. The pit is too enclosed, too hot, too human. No birdsnest here except the soot pigeons that ride the elevator up and down and roost on the rim at night. No insects beyond roaches and a few pale moths that live their whole lives under the bulbs. Rats, of course, but even they are rare — the cats, thin and vine-scratched, keep them down, and there is no waste ground for them to breed in. The ecology is plant and human and machine, a closed loop. People pollinate the gourds by hand.

The rules of the pit are unwritten but absolute:

1. Do not block a drip line. Water belongs to whoever catches it, but the path of water belongs to everyone below you.
2. Do not run a machine faster than its idle. Speed makes heat, heat makes stillness unbearable, and heat brings the bad air that pools and will not leave.
3. Build up, not out. There is no out.
4. Tend your green. A house without a living roof is a house that will cook you in the afternoon column of sun.

If you stand on the highest catwalk at dusk, when the worklamps have come on and the forges are banked low, you can see the whole thing at once — the entire hamlet-city, glowing, dripping, breathing. The far wall is right there, close enough to see the individual ferns trembling in the updraft. Smoke rises straight up for fifty meters before it hits the cooler air at the rim and finally, reluctantly, spills out into the world above. Above that, a square of deepening blue, first stars appearing.

It feels less like a town that was built and more like something that grew to fill a fresh wound in the earth before the earth could scab over — packed, damp, dim, patient, strange, and completely, perfectly suited to its hole.
