It is high. And it is drowned.

Not high like a mountain peak above clouds, and not drowned like a coast after a storm. Both at once, completely. A sea perched where a sea should not be.

Far below, past the sheer drop of the world-rim, there is said to be dry air and lowland and the old ocean, but no one goes down there. The world that matters is the Table - a vast, young plateau lifted kilometers into the sky in one raw, unfinished heave of basalt. The stone is still sharp. It hasn't learned to be smooth yet. The uplift is so recent, geologically speaking, that the rock hasn't been weathered or carved. It broke and it held.

And then the water came and never left.

It doesn't drain. The rim of the Table is a jagged, unbroken lip - a black escarpment of columnar basalt and glassy fault scarps that circles the horizon entirely, like the wall of a quarry made for gods. Inside that wall, warm water has gathered to the brim. Not a lake. A shallow ocean held in the sky. From edge to edge it is perhaps a day's slow paddle across - wide enough to have weather, to have an opposite shore lost in haze - but shallow enough that the drowned land is always there beneath you. Ten meters, twenty meters, sometimes fifty where the old valleys dip. You can see the bottom everywhere if the silt settles, which it rarely does.

The water is warm. Body-warm, bath-warm, womb-warm. It holds its heat from the stone itself; the plateau is still cooling from its birth and the basalt exhales a low, constant geothermal breath. The water never chills. It hangs dim and translucent, not blue but a deep, greenish amber, thick with suspended life. Light comes down diffuse and late, as if filtered through parchment. The sun is a pale smear behind permanent high haze - not clouds precisely, but the exhalation of the perched sea itself, a low, luminous mist that never quite becomes rain and never quite clears. At noon it is dusk. At night it is a deeper dusk. The light is always enough to see by, never enough to cast a hard shadow.

You live submerged. That is the rule of the Table.

There was never a surface to build on, so the town was grown down into the water. It is not a city of streets but of vertical layers, dense and close and nearly touching. The founders did not have metal to speak of, only what they could carry up and knap and weave. Everything is built from what the water gives and what the raw stone provides.

The foundations are the bones of the old highland itself - the basalt pillars and ridges that stuck up highest when the flood began. Upon and between them, they have Lashed a second topography. Platforms of woven living reed and black mangrove root, cultivated to grow in braids, form broad, springy floors suspended in mid-water, anchored with cordage made from kelp fiber and weighted with bored stones. Houses are not houses but inverted bowls and bells, great thatched domes of sealed reed and resin and stretched kelp skin, each holding a stolen bubble of air underneath. They hang in the water like jellyfish, dark inside, breathing slowly. People move between them by swimming, by pulling along ropeways, by drifting.

It is intensely, bewilderingly dense. Nothing is isolated. The kelp forest is a city and the city is a kelp forest. Lush beyond any normal measure, the vegetation has taken the warm dim water as an invitation to fill every cubic meter. There are no empty stretches. Giant kelps with trunks as thick as a man's torso rise from the basalt floor, their fronds spreading at the water's top to form a continuous, undulating canopy that further dims the light. Beneath that, in the mid-water, gardens of luminous feather-weed and red brush-algae are tended on trellises, pruned and harvested daily with flint and shell knives. Freshwater hydrangea-like bushes grow impossibly underwater, their roots dangling free, their pale flowers opening slow and huge, the size of plates, translucent in the gloom. Vines connect everything to everything else. If you reach out an arm you will touch something living - leaf, wall, rope, frond, neighbor's dwelling, a curtain of roots.

And still, it is quiet. And still, almost nothing moves fast.

The water itself is strange in its stillness. It does not churn or tide. The Table has no rivers in, no rivers out. The only circulation is the slow, convective roll of warm water rising from the hot stone and cool water sinking from the mist - a breath that takes hours to complete a cycle. Silt hangs motionless for a long time before settling. A dropped stone falls as if through honey. Sound is swallowed. The loudest thing is often your own exhalation bubbling up to catch under a house-bell with a soft *tok*. Distant voices travel strangely, seeming to come from inside your ear rather than across the water, because the dense weave of kelp and cordage and water breaks all echo. The town communicates in taps on rope, in tugs, in hand-signs made luminous with pale fungal paint on the forearms.

Animals are rare, and that absence makes the lushness feel stranger. No schools of fish silver the water. No birds. Once in a long while a great, slow, blind salamander-thing the size of a canoe will undulate between the basalt pillars, pale as milk, filtering feather-weed with frilled gills, utterly indifferent to the people who pause and watch it pass. Tiny shrimp like grains of rice pick at the algae. Otherwise, the water is plant and human and stone. The ecology feels incomplete, as if it was assembled in a hurry and is still waiting for its animals to evolve and arrive.

That is the weirdness that sits under everything: the water does not behave like water should. At certain depths, especially near the cold seeps where the basalt exhales, the water separates into layers that do not mix. You can swim down and feel a distinct skin between warm amber water and a colder, clearer lens beneath it, and for a moment your body is half in one world and half in another, with a perfect, trembling meniscus cutting across your chest. Bubbles, sometimes, do not rise. They hang suspended for minutes, wobbling in place like glass beads, before reluctantly remembering to go up. If you stay very still you can see faint vertical columns in the water - warmer currents - that stay locked in place for days, like invisible pillars, and the kelp leans permanently around them.

Everything is embryonic. The geology is raw and unsmoothed. The basalt at the bottom is still sharp enough to cut bare feet, all fresh fracture planes and hexagonal columns that have not yet been rounded by current. There are no beaches, no sand, no soil except what the mangroves and kelp have made and trapped in their root-mats. The escarpment rim is still scree-less; it sheared clean. You can see where the world is still coming into being, still deciding what shape it will be when it finally erodes. The town itself looks the same way - provisional, grown rather than built, lashed and retied and added onto generationally, patient. No one hammers quickly here. A new platform is woven over months, a new air-bell is sealed over a season, the resin cured in the warm dark water.

To live here is to learn the slow rules. Hold your breath and kick gently - never thrash, the water resists you if you hurry. Keep to the ropeways at mid-depth where the light is best for the gardens. Return to your bell before your chest burns; duck under the lip and breathe the stale, warm, human air inside, which smells of smoke from little stone oil lamps and of damp thatch. Sleep with the gentle sway of the whole town, which moves not with waves but with the slow heave of the thermal breath. Speak softly - sound carries too well and too strangely to waste.

From the top of the tallest kelp canopy, if you pull yourself up and push your head for a moment into the thin, misty air above the waterline, you can see the whole thing at once: the dim amber sea to the black, unweathered rim and the haze beyond, the dark heads of thousands of kelp crowns, the pale flowers open just under the surface like stars, the constellation of air-bells below glowing faintly with lamp-light, the heat-shimmer of the water itself. It is a town the size of a valley, a forest the size of a town, an ocean the size of a table, held impossibly high, warm and dim and quiet and crowded and green, waiting - not decaying - waiting very, very slowly to become whatever it will be next.
