High up, the air should be thin and cold. Here it isn't.

You climb and climb and the mountain doesn't come to a point. It comes to a rim.

The last hundred meters of basalt have been cut away and replaced. Not terraced, not built *upon* — replaced. A perfect circle of concrete and riveted steel set into the peak like a crown, or a mouth. From the outside it looks like a silo, or a cooling tower that never got its roof. From below, in the valleys, you can see it catching the sun long after the slopes have gone dark. From the trail you think it is empty.

It isn't empty. It is full.

The basin is maybe sixty meters across. You could walk its circumference in a few minutes if there were anywhere to walk. A hamlet, a single room the size of a cathedral nave, bounded on all sides by a wall you could touch. That is the whole place. There is nowhere else to it. The world beyond the rim is only distant blue and cloud. Inside the rim, the world is complete.

Because it is full of water.

Not a lake surface you look down upon. The water goes right to the top and keeps going. Warm, clear to a depth and then green with suspended life, it fills the basin to the lip and is held there. There is no spill, no roar over the edge. At the rim the surface tenses and holds, meniscus-still, as if the air itself has a skin. Stand at the parapet and you look *across* water at the opposite wall, not down into it. The light of late afternoon comes in sideways, normal daylight, unfiltered by depth, and it refracts through kilometers of warm water as if through a windowpane. Dust motes hang in the sunbeams below the surface and do not settle.

You have to go in by the service stair, a sharp-edged cage of galvanized steel that was put here recently enough that its corners still bite. Nothing here is weathered. Every edge is as-cast, every weld bead still proud, every bolt head still square. This is a young place. The mountain made it and then hands made everything else, and there hasn't been time for rain or wind to soften any of it. The concrete still shows the grain of its formwork. The spillways are crisp as paper cuts.

Below the waterline, everything is built.

There is no mountain left inside. The natural substrate is gone. What you thought was ground is a lattice: perforated steel decks, poured retaining basins, ceramic troughs, glass channels, vertical pipe-runs as thick as tree trunks and horizontal ones as thin as veins, all bolted and slotted together. The architecture is not buildings; it is infrastructure exposed. A factory with no facade. Four or five levels of catwalks and tanks and turbine housings stacked to the bottom you can barely see, and between every surface and every other surface there is no empty space.

It is packed.

That is the word that hits you when your eyes adjust to the refracted light. Not crowded. *Saturated.* The vegetation leaves no room.

It grows out of everything because there is no soil for it to be limited to. It is industrial-vegetal, hydroponic, epiphytic, engineered to grow on made things. From every beam, every pipe seam, every bolt, something green is rooting. Sheets of broad-leafed water velvet hang from the undersides of walkways like curtains. Pillars of concrete are furred completely in moss so thick you could sink your hand to the wrist. Ferns the size of doors unfurl from vertical walls and lean into the slow current. Flowering vines with waxy, translucent leaves as big as plates thread themselves through the railings, through the turbine grilles, through the mesh floors. Above you — or what feels like above, though you are submerged to the chest now on the stair — lily pads larger than tables float at mid-water, not on the surface, held up by nothing but their own buoyancy in the thermal column, their undersides tangled with white root beards that drift like hair.

It is warm. Not hot. Bath-warm, air-warm, blood-warm. The water is the temperature of the air trapped against the skin, and the air above the meniscus is thick with it, a greenhouse humidity that beads on the steel and runs without ever falling. You breathe and the air tastes green and mineral, faintly of iron and ozone from the machines.

Because the machines are running. All of them, always, but slowly and very quietly.

This was built to be industrial. You can see the purpose in the scale of it: the low drums of generators, the heat exchangers hissing steadily, the great slow screws turning behind grates to circulate the basin, the rows of grow-lights — unneeded now in daylight — waiting like eyes. Electrified, mechanized, productive. But nothing slams or shudders. The tempo is glacial. The pumps push warm water from the bottom to the top in a cycle so slow that a leaf takes a full minute to turn over. The turbines turn once every ten seconds, a lazy, silent sweep that you feel as pressure on your eardrums more than hear. The system has no need to be fast.

It is quiet enough that you hear your own breath bubbling. No hum of insects. No dart of fish. No birds. No animal life at all — that absence becomes a weight after a moment. In a place this lush there should be riot, but there is only plant and machine. No pollinators, so the flowers — and there are many, pale, bell-shaped things that open underwater and release a puff of white pollen that hangs in the water like smoke — are left to self-fertilize or to be brushed by the current, by the sway of a pipe exhaling. No grazers, so the growth is unchecked, lapping over itself, folding, dying and composting in place, feeding the next layer. It has the strange, uncanny patience of a place that grows without being eaten.

And it is strange in other ways that accumulate the longer you stay still.

The water doesn't stratify properly. Warm water should rise, but here coils of colder and warmer water braid around each other and stay, distinct as smoke ribbons, for minutes before diffusing. The light behaves as if the basin is deeper than it is; looking down through the walkway grating you get vertigo, as if the bottom recedes many times farther than sixty meters, though you know the mountain's peak cannot contain that. Some of the vines are growing downward, roots up and leaves down, oriented to a gravity that is not yours. In one corner the water hangs in a vertical sheet, a wall of rain that never falls, suspended between two charged plates, and ferns grow *inside* that sheet, their fronds fluttering in a precipitation that never lands.

You realize the rule of this place: it is a sky ocean that learned to be a garden and a factory at the same time. The mountain was cut open high enough to touch the weather, and instead of a temple or a mine, someone — or something — poured a complete, bounded world into the wound. A cistern for growing in the clouds. The water is held not by walls alone but by whatever field keeps that meniscus from breaking. The plants are kept without any natural substrate because they were never meant to have one; they are part of the machinery, filters and indicators and products. The machines are kept quiet and slow because there is nothing here that needs force; everything needs circulation and warmth and light.

You go down one more rung on the stair and the water closes over your shoulders. It is still warm. A sheet of moss beneath the handrail brushes your wrist with impossible softness. A turbine below you completes another silent revolution, pushing a cloud of pollen gently past your face. The light beyond the water, beyond the rim, is still normal afternoon light, gold on the far peaks, but in here it is green and diffused and held. The rim contains it all — the packed, humming, dripping, silent greenhouse-sea — like a bowl in the sky, small enough to hold in your mind all at once, and too strange and too full to ever fully understand.
