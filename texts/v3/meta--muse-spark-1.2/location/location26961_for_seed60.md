It is low. Not valley-low, not carved - just the kind of low that stays.

A shallow pan in the land where warm air settles and does not leave. You can feel the rim of it if you try to walk out in any direction: a low, eroded lip of ground about shoulder-high when you're standing in the middle, not a hill, just the place where the pan stops. Weathered sandstone and old concrete aggregate fused by time, softened until you can't tell which was laid and which was there. Everything has been here long enough to agree on a shape. Nothing is being cut or collapsing. It has settled into itself.

The air is warm and wet without being a swamp. It's a wet that lives on surfaces.

Everything weeps.

### The Mass

There is no forest here. There is no ground with plants on it. The plants *are* the ground, the walls, the roof, the filler. At 2.9 you don't get trees, you get a totality - a living substrate that has decided to occupy every cubic inch of available volume inside that shallow pan.

From the lip you look down into what seems like a single organism the size of a hamlet, maybe a city block, maybe the nave of a huge cathedral sunken into the earth. But as you get closer you see it is thousands of species locked together so tightly they have become architecture.

The base is a mat. Not soil - a woven mattress of roots, rhizomes, moss, liverwort, water-root and black fiber that is springy underfoot and holds your weight like a wet trampoline. It quivers. If you kneel and press your palm you leave a handprint that fills slowly with tea-colored water seeping up from below. The water is always near. Not flooding. Just there, a hand's breadth under the mat, breathing. Walk and water sighs around your ankles from the sponge you've compressed.

Out of that mat rise trunks, but not as individuals. Huge, pale-barked buttress columns - something between banyan and mangrove and fig - that have fused as they grew. Leaves the size of doors overlap leaves the size of hands overlap vines as thick as your thigh overlap curtains of aerial root that hang like rope and combed hair. There is no empty air. Even the air gaps are threaded with hair-fine runners, with cloud-moss, with the pale green lace of some epiphyte that never touches the ground. Light comes in normal and full - not dim, not gloom - white daylight straight overhead - but it has to fight for every foot. It comes down shafted and green, cutting through steam.

Because it's warm. Not hot. Incubator-warm. The kind of warmth that makes sap run and metal sweat. Your skin beads instantly. Every leaf is beaded too, tipped with a constant drop that swells, hangs, falls. The rocks - where you can find a rock under the mat, those softened sandstone lips, the stray curb - weep continuously, cold seep condensing on them and running in sheets. The atmosphere condenses on itself. You can see your breath even though it's warm, little puffs that vanish into the general mist that never quite becomes fog.

It is packed. Pathologically packed. There is no room between presences. If you want to move horizontally you have to turn sideways and let the vegetation accept you, which it does slowly. Vines indent around your shoulder. Fronds brush your face and leave water on your cheek. To stand still is to be held. After a minute you feel tendrils settling against your back, not gripping, just finding the shape of you as another surface to follow.

### The Machines Inside It

And through all of that, mostly swallowed - the scattered built things. The industrial.

It was not a city. It was some kind of lowland processing station. Perhaps a pumping house, a juice mill, a fiber works. You can still read it in fragments. The scale was always small: one brick boiler house, now with its roof gone and a tree growing straight through its center, bark pressed flush to brick. One iron smokestack, waist-thick, choked and bridged over with the glossy leaves of a climber that has learned to make a perfect cylinder up its length. A gantry of rusted I-beams that the mat has grown over, so you walk *on* the gantry without knowing, until you feel the hard line under the moss.

The machines are still there, and more strangely, some are still going, though no person tends them.

Down near the lowest point, where the water gathers, there is a pump engine the size of a truck, painted once in industrial green, now mottled black-green with algae and copper verdigris. Its flywheel, eight feet across, turns - slowly. One revolution every twenty seconds or so. So slow you can watch it and doubt that it's moving, but you can hear it. And the plant has entered into negotiation with it.

Roots have grown deliberately around the axle, not jamming it, but oiled with sap. Vines have trained themselves along the piston rods like insulation. The engine draws water up from under the mat and breathes it out as low, hot steam through a cracked pipe that the leaves lean toward. The leaves nearest the pipe have become translucent, veined like lungs, swelling with the condensate and circulating it. In return, the plant keeps the engine from seizing. Fleshy nodules press against grease nipples. A curtain of aerial roots drips water directly onto the flywheel bearing with metronomic patience.

There are two or three more like it. Electrified, too - all humming low-voltage. A string of frosted bulbs on a sagging wire still burns amber in the daytime, caged in vine, buzzing with insects that never come. A concrete settling tank, now a clear, warm pool filled with lily pads as big as tables, each pad supporting a perfect, shallow puddle of water on its upper surface, like a dish. The water in the tank turns over, slowly, filtered by roots that hang in it like curtains.

Nothing here was built to be big. Everything built was hamlet-scale, single-building, small-batch production. And now everything built is only the hard seed inside the fruit of the plant. Scattered remains. Partial. But not dead.

### The Rules and The Sound

It is loud here and empty.

That is the strangest thing. It is not quiet like a jungle should be. With fauna this rare - almost none - there are no birds calling, no monkeys, no chorus of frogs. Once an hour you might see a single glossy black beetle the length of your thumb trundling along a leaf midrib, or hear the brief, startled whirr of one dragonfly, huge and glassy, and then nothing. The absence of animals makes the place feel untenable, like a body with no blood.

So the loudness is not biological. It's water and plant and machine.

The mat drips everywhere, a thousand points of percussion. Leaf-tip to leaf-tip to mat, a constant patter like rain even when the sky is clear. The engine flywheel makes a heavy *huh... huh... huh* with each slow turn, and the steam pipe hisses and sighs. The living wood creaks - a deep, slow groan as the mass adjusts, as a buttress swells with water and leans a millimeter. Somewhere a tension cable of vine, stretched between two trunks, vibrates in the warm draft and hums a low, mournful note. In the density, sound has nowhere to go. It stays and folds over itself. You speak and the mass gives your voice back, close and leaf-muffled.

And it is strange.

Weirdness is not a costume here, it is a behavior. The plants have learned industrial logic. You see leaves that have grown flat and rectangular to sheath the exact dimensions of a fuse box. You see a patch of moss that pulses faint green light with the flicker of the bulbs it covers. Sap runs through aerial roots that have become indistinguishable from copper pipe, joining at right angles, with little valve-like nodes that open and close. Some vines carry water *up* against gravity, cold and fast, like pumps, shivering as they do. If you sit long enough and watch the big leaves, you see them all slowly turning, not toward the sun, but toward the steam plume, offering themselves to be beaded.

The light at midday is normal, honest daylight, but filtered through so many green lenses it feels deliberate, like the place is lighting itself. Dusts of pollen and mist hang and do not drift, because there is no room for wind. Everything moves slow. The flywheel. The drip. The growth, which you can almost see if you are patient - a runner inching along a beam over the course of an afternoon, a flower opening over hours with an audible sigh, exhaling warm sweet-yeasty air.

You don't belong here, but neither are you intruding. The place is in balance. Long-established, long-weathered, settled-form. It has solved itself: plant needs water and heat, machine needs water and motion, mat needs filtering. You can walk its boundaries in ten minutes and still feel you have been inside a cathedral, because the interior is so much bigger than its footprint. It is a room built of living things around a few old engines that never quite stopped, weeping, breathing, dripping, humming loudly to an empty congregation of leaves.
