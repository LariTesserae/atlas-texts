It is a room that was made to hold water, and now it does.

You find it by going down. Not deep in the geological sense — no cavern, no abyss — just below the surface, below the level where the ground should be. A stair cut straight into the earth, each step hand-hewn and still sharp at the edge, where the chisel has not yet been softened by feet. The stone is pale, almost white — limestone blocks set without mortar, or with a lime plaster that is still curing, still faintly pink in the seams. Everything here is placed. There is no exposed earth, no bedrock, no natural floor. Even the dust is stone dust.

At the bottom of the stair is a chamber. A single chamber, intimate and complete, like a pocket sewn into the world. Four walls, a flat floor, a low vaulted ceiling — all built. You could touch every surface in a few minutes if you swam it. It was not quarried. It was assembled, block by block, timber by timber, with rope and wooden scaffolding that still leans against one wall, dark with damp, lashed together with fiber cord. The tool marks are everywhere, fresh and parallel, the stone still crisp where it was split. Nothing here has had time to round or crumble. It is young.

And it is full.

Warm water fills it to the brim and then keeps coming. It is not a pool so much as a held piece of sea, or a flood that has been given architecture. The water is clear, bright, and warm as skin — sun-heated from above, holding the heat through the stone. When you stand on the top step, the water line shivers at your ankles. One more step and you are in it, submerged to the knees, then the waist, then entirely. The chamber is drowned and yet perfectly visible, because the light is total.

High in the vault there is an oculus — a perfect circle, maybe a meter across, also built, its rim lined with cut stone. It is open to the sky. Midday sun falls straight down through it in a solid column, and the water catches it and throws it. The whole room glows. Light refracts off the pale floor, off the plaster, off the suspended motes of lime in the water, and scatters until there are no shadows except the brief, hard shadows of the scaffold poles. The water itself is light. You can see every chisel score on the bottom, every joint between blocks, even though you are looking through three meters of liquid. Near the surface the light breaks into a trembling lattice that crawls across the walls.

It is loud.

The water is not still. It enters on one side through a sluice — a narrow, steeply cut channel, also lined in stone, where water comes from somewhere higher, a rain catchment or a diverted spring or an aqueduct you cannot see. It pours in without interruption, a white, muscular chute of water that hits the chamber's surface and drums. The sound is amplified by the vault, by the water itself. It slaps and echoes, a constant low roar and the higher clatter of overflow spilling out the opposite wall through a lip of notched stone, where it disappears into a darker drain. You hear the pour, the slap, the gurgle of the drain, the faint hiss of water against lime, and your own breathing made strange and close inside the wet stone box. It is too loud to be restful, too enclosed to ignore.

There is almost no life in it.

No fish turn in the column of light. No insects skate the surface up where the air meets the sun. Even the expected green scum of a warm cistern is reluctant here — perhaps because the stone is too new, the water too active, the light too scouring. Only in the seams where the plaster has chipped, and on the north wall where the sun does not directly strike, there is a sparse, brave vegetation: a few strands of hair-thin algae waving like green thread, a clump of pale reeds that has rooted in a crack near the overflow, its stems thin and translucent. They do not fill the space. They punctuate the emptiness, making it emptier. The chamber is mostly stone and water and light, balanced with a great deal of unoccupied volume. A single wooden bucket, half-sunk, drifts slowly in the circulation. A coil of rope rests on a submerged ledge. That is all.

Because it is built, it has rules.

Water must come in, water must go out, or the chamber drowns the stair. The level holds itself at the lip of the drain, trembling but constant, and if you block it even for a moment the whole room would rise and spill upward into the light. The stone holds heat but also leaches it; the water stays warm only because the sun keeps pouring in and the flow keeps renewing. The plaster is still softening; if you press a thumb to the wall underwater, it leaves a print that clouds and slowly dissolves. The edges are all still sharp enough to cut — the lip of the sluice, the corner of each step — because weathering has not yet had its turn. Whoever made this made it recently, with hands and simple tools, and for a plain purpose: to hold water where there was no water, to keep it bright and clean and warm below the ground.

If you stay in it long enough to let your eyes adjust, you see that the chamber breathes. The inflow pulses when wind moves over the catchment above. The lattice of light on the walls shifts with the smallest ripple. A fine silt of stone dust lifts and settles, lifts and settles in the current. It is active, not stagnant — a small, vigorous weather system contained in a room — and yet it is utterly still in the other sense: no birds, no creatures, no wildness at all. Only the work of hands, and water, and sun, loud and bright in a perfect, handmade pocket under the earth.
