It never quite drains here.

This is a low place. Not low like a valley between mountains — there are no mountains left to frame it — low like the bottom of a bowl the size of a continent, low like the world sagged here and decided to stay sagged. The air is heavier for it, a warm, pressing weight at sea-level-plus-a-little-less, thick enough that you feel it on your skin before you feel the heat. Warm, not hot. A late-summer warmth that never breaks, that holds water in the air until the air can't hold it anymore.

And it can't, very often.

Everything weeps.

The city — because it is a city, continuously, without break or border — was not built *on* the land so much as *with* the water in mind. It occupies a basin so vast you can't see its edge, and the curvature of the planet is lost under the curvature of construction. From any high point, and there are many high points but none of them very high, you see only more: a packed, dense, saturated tessellation of structure that goes to a hazy horizon and then goes further. It makes its own weather. Clouds form over it from its own exhalation, pile up in the warm air, and drop again as a short, hard rain that drums on ten million roofs, fills ten million gutters, and vanishes into the stone.

The terrain underneath is still there, if you dig. Or rather, the terrain *is* the city now. The bedrock is a dark, warm basalt and compacted silt, smoothed and settled, old. This is not a sharp, young, jagged place. Erosion here has had time to do its work. Edges are rounded. Canyons that were once streets have been worn by a century of runoff into soft U-shapes, their curbs licked smooth. The foundations have settled into equilibrium with the water table, which is so high it is practically the surface. You don't dig a hole here without it filling. You don't lay a foundation without it weeping. Water finds its way up through the seams in the mineral world, cold and clear, beading on the undersides of overpasses, sweating out of retaining walls, condensing on every metal surface in the early morning until the whole city glistens.

There are no plants. Not in any way you would recognize as a plant. No trees, no grass, no weeds cracking the pavement — the pavement does not crack, or if it does it seals itself before a seed could find it. What lives on the mineral is thinner and older: a dark patina, a slick of mineral-fed biofilm in the places where water runs constantly, rust-colored and olive-black, matte where it dries. It is not vegetation. It does not photosynthesize so much as stain. The world is stone, metal, ceramic, glass, and the endless composite the city extrudes for itself — a warm, slightly yielding basalt-like material that is poured and grown and printed at once.

And yet it is not dead.

It is, in fact, impossibly crowded with animal life — not hidden, but *packed*. Abundant to the point of saturation, shoulder-to-shoulder with the built density.

The water brings them, the warmth keeps them. In the permanent shallows between structures, where the streets dip and become canals by design, where plazas are engineered to hold three inches of rain forever, there are clouds of small silver things — fish, shrimp, amphibian somethings — turning in tight, synchronized spirals. The air above the water is never empty: flocks of white and grey birds the size of your hand move like a single fabric, snapping and folding between towers, screaming. Insects — or things that have taken that niche, bright, hard-shelled, humming — form living smoke over every warm vent. On the vertical faces, colonies of lizard-like runners and soft, many-legged cleaners swarm, eating the biofilm, eating each other, eating the flecks of protein that flake off the city's skin. Nothing here eats plants, because there are none to eat. The food chain starts with the water and the film and the city itself: detritus, cultured fats, the endless microscopic rain of skin and chaff from so many bodies, and the engineered yeasts that bloom on condensation pipes.

You hear them all at once, and that is part of why you cannot hear yourself think.

Rain hammering on awnings a mile away and right above your head at the same time. The grinding hymn of the city fabricating itself — low, bone-conducted thrums from matter-looms in the underlayers, the high whine of field-cohesion as a new span knits itself out of air. The chittering, clicking, cawing, chirping of fauna in numbers too large to be a chorus, become a static. Voices — human and otherwise — shouting, bargaining, singing, arguing in a bazaar that never closes because there is no night quiet enough to close it. You feel it in your molars before you hear it in your ears. If you stand still, your ribs vibrate.

The built aspect is not "ruins" or "remnants." This is urban in the most intentional sense: organized, dense, layered, infrastructural. But it is urban beyond any modern planning.

The structures are packed with no interval wasted. Buildings lean into each other and share walls and share loads and share air. A street is rarely a street; it is a slot canyon with a roof of bridges, walkways, pipes, balconies, gardens that contain no plants but contain shallow pools and mineral sculptures. The lower levels are submerged or semi-submerged, lit from within by a normal, white daylight that is piped and bounced and re-emitted until even the deepest service trench has the quality of an overcast noon. Light here is ordinary — not dim, not blinding — it just comes from everywhere, scattered by humidity and by the active haze of the city's own fields.

Technology is not visible as machines so much as it is visible as behavior of matter. Walls heal. A handrail, worn smooth by erosion and use, will thicken slightly overnight where it was thinning. A plaza that was flooded at noon will be dry and slightly re-graded by evening, its stone having flowed like slow wax to a new inclination. Construction is not done by cranes but by slow, turbulent growth: scaffolds of light hang in the air, and dust within them organizes itself into structure. Demolition is the same as construction, just in reverse, and both happen constantly. Nothing is stable in detail, though the whole is stable in form.

That is the rule here: turbulence as a steady state.

This place is in chaotic change at every scale. Not decaying, not growing toward a finish line — churning. A block will be disassembled by its own inhabitants and reassembled two streets over because the water flow demanded it. A market that was on the ground floor yesterday is on the fifth today, having walked there on temporary legs. Fauna migrations cut through traffic like weather fronts; for twenty minutes a street is impassable with a tide of black chitin, then it is clear again and the cleaners come out to eat what was left behind. Storms form within the city-basin, rain falls hard for an hour on one district while a mile away washing hangs and dries. And through it all the stone persists — weathered, settled, patient — letting the frenzy happen on its back.

It smells like warm rain on hot stone, and like brine, and like ozone, and like a crowded body.

If you stand on one of the broad, low terraces — the closest thing to a hill here, a hundred feet of textured composite stepping down to a sheet of shallow water that reflects the sky and the buildings equally — you will see the horizon shimmer not with heat but with condensation and wings. You will hear the roar become so complete it inverts into a kind of silence, a pressure. You will feel water beading on your forearms that did not fall from the sky but formed right there out of the air. And you will understand that this place is not a city *in* a landscape. It is a landscape that *is* a city, that learned to weep and swarm and rebuild forever, vast enough to have weather, old enough to have smoothed, packed so tight there is no such thing as outside, and warm enough to never need to stop.

A little unusual, only a little — in the way a puddle is deeper than it should be, in the way the light sometimes catches on nothing and throws a shadow anyway, in the way water sometimes runs uphill for a few feet before remembering itself. Not enough to name. Just enough to keep you looking down, to make sure the ground is still there.

It always is. Wet, warm, thrumming, crowded, and waiting for the next rain that is already forming over your head.
