The valley is not a valley yet.

It is too young for that name - a fresh cut in a high basalt plateau, a long, straight trough where the earth has split and not yet learned how to soften its edges. From above, from the lip of the plateau, it looks like someone took a chisel to the world when the rock was still cooling. The walls are not slopes but steps and sudden faces, black rock and grey rock, columnar joints still showing their hexagonal crystal where the cut is cleanest. No talus has had time to gather at their feet. No river has had time to grind them smooth. The rims are sharp enough to cut cloud.

You are high up. High enough that the air is thin and bright to the point of pain. The sun is a white coin in a colorless sky, and the light has no filter. It comes down hard, vertical, unbuffered by haze. Everything is outlined. Every leaf-edge throws a razor shadow. The light makes the cool air feel stranger - it should be warm under this much sun, but it isn't. The temperature holds at a constant early-autumn, a breath that never quite warms. Stone stays cold to the touch even at noon. If you put your palm to the basalt, the cold comes through first, then, after a long time, a faint sun-heat.

And yet it is green. Unreasonably, chokingly green.

Because the water here does not run. There is no river in the trough, no lake. On a map you would mark it arid. But the plateau sweats.

Condensation is the hydrology. At night the cold stone exhales, at day the bright sun forces the air to release it again. Dawn comes with a heavy dew that does not so much fall as materialize - on every surface a sheet of beads, on the underside of every leaf a trembling lens. By ten it has gathered and run in brief, silver threads down the column walls, into pockets. There are pockets everywhere: potholes in the basalt not larger than a bathtub, cisterns carved by the same sharp fracturing that made the valley, shaded overhangs where last week's dew still sits, cold and perfectly clear, cupped in stone and moss. You walk and you hear no trickle; you just find water suddenly at your feet, unexpectedly, and then ten meters later the moss is dry again, crisp. Intermittent moisture. The plants have learned to be opportunists and reservoirs at once.

The vegetation is not forest. It is infestation, it is tapestry, it is upholstery. In the bright, cold, damp, the green has become dense beyond any reasonable ecology. Moss first, feet thick, a mattress over every horizontal surface - and then fern, and then vine, and then small, waxy alpine trees that grow not up but out, sideways from cracks, tangling into each other until there is no ground, only layers of living mat. You cannot see stone except where it is vertical. The horizontal is all green, and the green is layered three, four deep: moss under fern under vine under shrub, all touching, all competing for that hard light, all holding dew in their interstices like sponges. It is lush the way a neglected greenhouse is lush - overabundant, airless, pressing in. When you push through it your shoulders brush wet leaves on both sides even on what should be a wide boulevard. Pollen and spores make the bright air visible, a slow gold dust that never quite settles.

Scattered through this press are the buildings. They are not a town. They were never a town.

They are industrial, but an industry that forgot to be loud.

On terraces cut into the young rock, or half-swallowed by the moss-mat on the valley floor, sit machines and the sheds that house them. Not ruins, not exactly - they are too intact, too cared-for in some minimal way. Low, long structures of riveted steel and poured concrete, paint gone to a flat oxide red and green, windows intact but opaque with condensation on the inside. Outside each one, a gantry, a conveyor belt, a sorting drum, a turbine housing large enough to stand in, all still. Not rusted dead - oiled, but not moving. Or moving so slowly you have to stare to see it. A great wheel on the side of one shed turns once every twenty minutes, lifting a chain of empty buckets from a cistern that is now mostly moss up to a hopper that never fills. Inside, through a window, you can see a line of looms or presses or dynamos, their belts still tensioned, their electric bulbs burning a steady amber even at midday, too dim to compete with the sun but clearly, impossibly on. There is power here. Humming transformers on poles stitched together with vines. But there is no smoke. No clatter. The density of the vegetation has soundproofed everything.

That is the strangest thing, before you even get to the truly strange things. The silence.

The valley should ring. Metal, stone, high altitude - you expect wind to howl through the cut, expect birds, expect the creak of those slow machines. Instead the sound has been taken out. The moss absorbs it. The thin, cold air carries poorly. Your own footsteps on the mat are a dull thud, not a crunch. You can hear your blood. You can hear the faint click as a dewdrop swells and falls from a leaf-tip onto a steel drum, and then nothing after. When the big wheel turns, you don't hear the axle; you see only that its position has changed when you look back. People who worked here - if people worked here - must have spoken in whispers and still felt too loud.

Because there are almost no animals to make sound either. No birds. No insects beyond a few pale, slow moths that sit on leaves and look like more moss. Once in a long time you see a track in the dew: three-toed, too large, pressed into the moss and then gone, with no creature attached to it. The greenery grows untroubled, unpruned, un-eaten. It has no predators but time.

And time is behaving a little strangely here, which is the other silence.

Light pools wrong. In a few places, especially near the machinery, the brightness does not seem to come from the sun alone. The undersides of the terraces, which should be in hard shadow, glow faintly green, as if the moss itself were holding light after its source is gone. The shadows of the gantry cranes fall one direction at 9am and a slightly different direction at 9:01, jittering as if the sun were flickering. If you leave a steel cup of cistern water in the sun, it does not evaporate; it thickens, becomes slightly viscous, and clings to the cup. If you come back the next day the water is gone and the cup is full of fine, dark seedlings that should have taken weeks to germinate. Things happen, but on a delayed, patient calendar. Rust forms in geometric patterns. Vines grow visibly only if you compare yesterday's photograph to today's. The industry is not abandoned. It is operating on a cycle so slow you cannot hear its rhythm - a factory that makes one thing per season, perhaps, or per year, and does not need workers to attend it minute by minute. The rules here are damp, bright, cold, slow. Conserve water. Conserve motion. Conserve sound.

You can walk the length of the valley in an afternoon, and you will never be out of sight of both walls, and you will never be out of reach of something green touching your elbow, and you will pass fifteen or twenty of those low sheds, each humming at the edge of hearing, each lit from within, each doing its imperceptible, unhurried work while the moss slowly, inevitably, pulls it down into the soft, bright, silent green.
