It is not a valley, not really. Valleys are made by water over time, softened and sorted. This place hasn't had time.

Think of a wound in the flat earth, recent enough that the edges are still sharp.

On the surface, the land is a high, cool moorland — thin soil over flatbed limestone, wind-scoured, the grass short and brown-green and sparse. It stretches far enough that you can't see its end, only a pale horizon under a wide, normal sky. Clouds move quickly and throw solid shadows. Then the ground simply stops.

The Scar runs for miles, north to south, more or less straight. It is not wide — at most four or five hundred paces across — but it is deep, perhaps a hundred feet down at its deepest, and its walls are vertical. Not weathered vertical, not slumped and vegetated, but sheer and fresh, as if a great block had dropped. The stone is still raw where it broke: pale grey limestone in flat strata, each layer as clean as a quarried face, with darker bands of chert glittering when the light hits. There is almost no scree at the foot of the walls. There has not been time for the freeze-thaw to pry much loose. A few sharp blocks lie where they fell, their edges still un-dulled, sitting on the flat floor of the chasm like they were left there yesterday. In places you can see the tool marks where the stone has been worked since the opening — but for the most part, it is untouched, embryonic geology, new rock exposed to air for the first time in millions of years.

And it is full.

The floor of the Scar is a city, but not a city that was planned. It is a city that grew to fill every available inch of the floor and then grew upward. From the moor above, you might not even know it is there until you are at the lip, because the smoke does not rise high and the sound does not carry. Then you look down and the entire bottom is occupied.

Houses, workshops, pens, storerooms, kilns — all built wall-to-wall, roof-to-roof, leaning against each other and against the chasm walls themselves. There is no street that is not also an alley, no courtyard that is not also a roof for something below. Timber is the main material, because timber can be lowered down. Dark, cool-weather wood — pine and a stunted oak from the moor's edge — cut with adze and saw, joined with wooden pegs and a few precious copper nails, weathered grey by the damp. Foundations are the limestone itself, sometimes leveled with a hammer, sometimes simply built around a fallen block that was too heavy to move and is now a wall of a kitchen. Upper stories are half-timbered and cantilevered, so that from above the whole settlement looks like a solid, uneven lid of thatch and split shingles, broken only by narrow, crooked fissures that serve as streets. Ladders and stairways climb the cliff faces; dwellings are carved shallowly into the stone where a family has had time, a square-cut doorway, a soot-blackened window.

It is an urban place without being a hard city. No stone avenues, no great architecture. Primitive, manual work. Everything shows the hand that made it. Beams are still axe-marked. Rope, not chain. Clay plaster mixed with straw and hair over wattle. The infrastructure is what can be maintained by hand: a central drainage channel, stone-lined, running down the middle of the Scar where water wants to go anyway, with smaller wooden gutters feeding it. Wells that are not deep but seep-fed. Terraces cut into the walls wherever a little sun can be caught.

Light is normal here, which is strange for a place below the surface. Because the Scar is open to the sky, the same clouds pass over it as pass over the moor. Around midday the sun reaches straight to the bottom, throwing short, hard shadows. In morning and late afternoon the walls cast one side or the other into deep, cool shade and the other wall glows. There is no gloom of a cave, only the particular quality of light at the bottom of a deep cut — a little bluer, a little softer, as if the sky has been narrowed into a river.

It is cool always. The stone holds cold. In the summer the Scar is a relief from the wind above; in winter it traps it. Your breath mists in the early hours, even in late spring. The air is damp in the way of stone places. Not wet. There is no river. There is intermittent moisture — seepage that beads on the limestone and runs in dark streaks, condensation that forms on the underside of every roof at night and drips through the thatch by morning, puddles that collect in the foot-worn hollows of the central channel after rain and are gone two days later. After a storm on the moor, water pours over the lip in a hundred temporary falls, threads of white that vanish almost as quickly as they appear, feeding the channel to a brief rush before subsiding. People collect it zealously. Every house has a barrel, a cistern cut into rock, a stretched skin to catch drip. Water is not scarce, exactly, but it is never standing. It appears, it is used, it sinks or evaporates.

Because of that damp, and because of the cool, there is life where you wouldn't expect it, but sparse. No trees down here; they would not get enough wind or spread. Instead: lichens on the north-facing walls in crusts of pale green and orange, moss thick on the shadier roofs, a few hardier grasses finding footing in the cracks of the limestone. People cultivate where they can — narrow beds of barley and cold-root vegetables on the few ledges that get sun, beans trained up ladder-frames, pots of herbs on every sill. The green is deliberate, not wild, a thin horticulture stitched into the dense wood and stone.

Animals are rare. The moor above has its birds and hares, but few come down into the packed, human world. You hear a pigeon sometimes, echoing off the walls. Rats, of course, but even they are fewer because there is little to waste; everything is eaten. In the drainage channel, small black beetles and the occasional lizard. Once in a while a hawk passes high overhead, a shadow crossing the thatch, but it does not descend.

For all that it is packed — saturated, with no gap between presences, shoulder-to-shoulder living — the Scar is quiet. The limestone absorbs sound. The overhangs and thatch muffle it. There is no wind at the bottom, only a faint, cool draft that moves north to south along the trench, carrying smells of smoke and wet stone and cooking. The work is constant and active: the thud of mallets, the scrape of adzes, the creak of a laden pulley lowering timber from the moor, women beating laundry and the water slapping stone, children running on roofs, looms clacking in low rooms, the kiln firing. But none of it is loud. People speak low here; habit, perhaps, from living in a place where everyone is within hearing. Even a shout does not go far before the walls take it.

The rules of the place are dictated by the geometry and the geology.

First, you do not waste vertical space. If you have a wall, you build against it or into it. If you have a roof, someone lives or dries beans on top of it.

Second, you do not block water. The central channel must run clear, and no house may bridge it fully. After a rain, the person whose house floods because they dammed a gutter will get no help moving their stores.

Third, you respect the fresh stone. The walls are still settling. Every few months a slab calves off with a clean crack like a gunshot and everyone freezes, listening for the rumble and the dust. No one builds too heavy directly beneath an unbraced overhang. The stone-cutters who work the walls with hammer and wedge do so slowly, tapping, listening.

It is a young place. You can feel it in the unfinished edges, the way the settlement hasn't yet been smoothed into its environment but sits raw inside it, like a filling. The moor will in time weather the lip, the rains will round the corners, soil will gather and trees will find a way down. But not yet. For now, the town in the crack is active and crowded and damp and cool, busy with hand-work under an ordinary sky that has been cut into a long, narrow strip — a whole compact world, open and extensive along its length, yet entirely below the level of the ordinary ground.
