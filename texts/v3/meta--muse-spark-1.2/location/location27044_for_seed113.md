It is not a cave. It is a wound.

You go down to get to it, and the air changes before the light does. The surface above is dry and ordinary - cracked clay and low wind - but there is a lip in the ground you almost miss until you are at its edge. A perfect oval has opened there, maybe two hundred meters across, its rim as sharp as broken pottery. It hasn't been here long. The geology hasn't learned to soften it yet.

The walls drop straight down for forty, fifty meters, striated basalt and poured aggregate and raw earth still holding the memory of the shear. It's young, this hole. Fresh. No talus slope of centuries, no smoothed shoulders. The cut is vertical and blocky, stair-stepped where the strata broke, and water is bleeding from every layer.

This is why the air changes. It's warm down there.

Not hot-spring warm, but held, humid, greenhouse warm. Three degrees warmer than the surface, five degrees warmer, enough that when the cooler air of evening slips over the rim it condenses instantly. By mid-afternoon every day, the whole basin fills with low cloud that doesn't rain so much as *unmakes* itself. Beads form on every surface.

And it is built.

That is the first disorientation. You expect a sinkhole to be empty or wild. This one has a town in it. Or the skeleton of a town. Or the *idea* of a town waiting for people who never arrived.

The structures don't climb the walls. They are suspended in the void, and they are anchored to the bottom.

At the floor of the basin there is not a lake but a marsh that decided to be an engineering project. Shallow water, black and warm, covers the entire bottom in a sheet no deeper than your knees, braided through with causeways and root-islands and the stilts of buildings. The water never stills. It is fed from everywhere at once - seeps weeping from the basalt halfway down the walls in dark vertical stains, condensation running down the undersides of every platform and falling as a constant second rain, springs that pulse up through grates in the floor. You hear it before you see anything else. The whole basin is loud with water in a way that a forest stream is not. It's architectural water. Dripping from a hundred edges. Splashing on metal. Gurgling through culverts under walkways. Hissing where it hits warm stone and returns to vapor.

The town is built over it, in it, with it.

Seven or eight major structures - that is all - and the word "urban" feels wrong until you look closer. Each one is dense. Not houses but vertical hamlets, layered blocks of residence and workshop and cistern and greenhouse stacked forty meters high on stilts that disappear into the water. Streets run *through* the buildings, not between them. Balconies are farms. Walls are not walls but scaffolds for other things. If you counted doors and windows there would be enough for a thousand people.

There are no people. Or almost none. That is the second disorientation. The density of construction says city; the emptiness says abandoned cathedral.

You can stand on the central causeway - a spine of dark, grown concrete that was not poured but coaxed - and see from one wall of the sinkhole to the other in a single glance. One grove. One room. One vaulted hall with a sky for a ceiling that is just the oval mouth of the world above, dimming to indigo. Sound carries too well. Your footsteps slap and echo off the sharp young rock on the far side a full second later. A caretaker drone, if it passes, you hear its wake long before and after.

For empty, it is not still.

The vegetation makes sure of it. There should not be this much green this far under a dim light. The light itself never gets bright - even at noon the sun is a hard white coin straight overhead that throws a single column down and paints the rest in permanent late-afternoon. But the green is everywhere and it is lush in the way a swamp is lush: indiscriminate, opportunistic, layered. Engineered moss thick as carpet over every walkway where foot traffic hasn't worn it away. Water-ferns choking the canals between buildings. Vine-things with translucent leaves the size of tables that climb the stilts and spread into umbrellas to catch the dimness. Fruiting bodies like pale lanterns that open at dusk and exhale a sweet, yeasty air.

Nothing eats them. That is the quiet horror and the quiet relief. No insects. No birds. No fish in the water, though the water is full of life in the botanical sense. No hum of flies. When a frond drops into the water it just floats until it dissolves. The whole basin should be buzzing and it is instead *hollow*. Only the water and the buildings make sound.

The buildings make sound because they are not inert.

This is not modern tech left to rust. The concrete breathes. If you put your hand on a wall, it is warm - not from the sun, from whatever slow computation is running through it. Hairline cracks seal themselves while you watch, exuding a bead of mineral milk that hardens in minutes. Light is not delivered by bulbs but by filaments grown into the undersides of every overhang, a dim amber bioluminescence that brightens when you approach and dims when you leave, as if the town is conserving attention. The causeways shed water with a slight electrostatic shiver, so the surface is never slick though it is always wet. High up, near the rim, you can see the tether-fields: empty hexagonal frames where rain pauses, hanging in mid-air for half a second before it is allowed to fall, part of some atmospheric management system that no longer has anyone to manage for.

It keeps managing anyway. That is the dynamic. Clouds form at 3 p.m. and dissolve at 4. The water rises two centimeters after midnight and drops by dawn, pumped somewhere, filtered through the root-mats, and returned. The vines grow a visible inch a day. The empty buildings cycle air, open and close shutters with no one inside, hum at an uneven 60-cycle that vibrates in your teeth.

The rules here are slightly off-register:

1. Water does not pool where it should. It clings to vertical surfaces and creeps sideways along the undersides of platforms until it finds a designated drip point.
2. Light without a source grows more useful the less you look directly at it.
3. Things built for many are kept perfect for none.

You understand after an hour that the basin is not abandoned. Abandoned would be decay. This is *tended*. By systems that do not need use to find purpose. The town waits in its warm, dripping, overgrown room - sharp-edged, small enough to hold in one look, vast enough to feel empty - breathing water in and out, growing its green lungs fuller in the dimness, loud with its own maintenance, patient in a way only a place that has outlived its need for animals can be.

If you stay until night, the oval of sky above goes completely dark and the filaments under every eave brighten just a notch, and the reflection of the whole empty, lush, humming city doubles itself in the black water, and for a moment there are two of it, one right-side up and one inverted, and neither one has anyone in it.
