It is a plateau that should not exist.

Kilometers above the lowlands, a table of land has been lifted whole into the warm air — not a jagged peak, not a range, but a continent cut flat and raised. You could walk for weeks and not find its edge, only suspect it when the weather changes. The rock is old enough to have forgotten it was ever molten. Basalt and limestone in deep, patient strata, weathered not into drama but into accommodation — broad, shallow bowls, long soft ridges, cliffs that have leaned back on themselves until they are more like steps for giants. This is not young land being torn down. This is land that has settled into its shape and agreed to keep it.

The light is normal. High sun, white and honest, falling straight down at noon and laying hard shadows that do not hide much. The air up here should be thin and cold. It isn't. It is warm — shirt-sleeve warm — humid and heavy, the kind of warmth that stays in stone long after sunset. The lowlands below are cooler in theory than this height, an inversion that everyone who lives here stops questioning. Perhaps the stone itself exhales heat. Perhaps the altitude was never about cold.

There is water everywhere and nowhere. No rivers worth naming, no lakes. Instead the plateau weeps.

The stone is porous. The atmosphere condenses against it. Morning brings a low, slow sea of cloud that does not rise so much as arrive, dragging its belly across the flat. It catches on every edge of the City and precipitates without ever properly raining — beading on cold iron, on basalt, on every vertical face until it runs. The rocks weep continuously. The walls weep. The undersides of bridges and overpasses drip at a rate you could set a watch to. Water stands in shallow sheets on every flat roof, in trays and basins and old industrial catchments, reflecting the sky in a broken mirror that is never still because of wind. You can hear it constantly, a quieter percussion under everything else: ticking, seeping, rilling through gutters cut centuries ago and worn smooth.

There are no plants here. Not in any way you would recognize.

No trees soften the horizon. No grass holds the dust. No weeds pry at the concrete. Where soil should be there is only fine mineral dust, dark and damp, and where life should be green there is black rock, pale efflorescence, rust. If you dig down you find no roots. If you look closely you find other things instead — slicks of bacterial film, orange and rust-red and sulfur-yellow, painting the weeping walls; mats of fungal leather stretched over sheltered stone like damp paper; a faint mineral fuzz on the windward sides of buildings where nothing should grow but something does, thin and flat and not-quite-alive by old definitions. It does not photosynthesize. It drinks condensation and exhales metallic breath. It is food.

Because the fauna is overwhelming.

This is a world made of animals living off stone and steam and each other and the City itself.

They are not exotic. That would be a kind of weirdness and this place is only slightly unusual. They are the logical animals for a warm, wet, treeless height with infinite ledges. In their millions, in their billions.

The air is fauna before it is air. At any moment, a third of what you think is haze is wings. Pale cliff moths the size of your hand, soot-colored swifts and needletails that never land except to cling vertically, pigeons and gulls and things between pigeon and gull that have been bred by the City's ledges into a hundred niche forms — all in constant circulation, all crying. The sound is not a birdsong but a weather. A high, tearing, kiting chorus that dopplers and folds over itself. They nest in every cornice, every broken window, every culvert. Their guano has become a geology, white and grey strata on the leeward walls, harvested by machines and by smaller animals.

Below them, the crawling density. On every damp surface there is a crust of movement. Pill beetles with iron-black carapaces that drink from the weeping films and scrape the bacterial paint with rasping mouths. White lice that farm the fungal mats. Long, flat centipedes that hunt them. Lizards — not green, but stone-colored, heat-loving, throats pulsing — that hunt the hunters. And in the City proper, rats, or the high-plateau equivalent of rats, dense as cobblestones in the underlayers, moving through pipes and service tunnels in rivers of brown and grey.

You cannot set a plate down outside for ten seconds without it being visited. You cannot leave a doorway open without something coming in to see if you are edible, or warm, or simply a new ledge. Density here does not mean crowded like a street. It means *layered*. The City and the animals occupy the same volume. Every beam has a nest, every pipe has a roost, every warm exhaust vent has a dozen bodies pressed around it, shoulder to shoulder, wing to wing, shell to shell. It is not a plague. It is the baseline. An equilibrium that has been negotiated over a very long time.

And the City is vast enough to hold it.

It is not a city placed on a plateau. It *is* the plateau, or as much of it as matters. Urban as far as you can see and then further, not in a modern skyline but in an industrial sprawl that has had time to become as weathered as the rock it sits on. Low, heavy buildings of poured concrete and brick gone dark with moisture, block after block after block intersected by rail yards, by waterworks, by manufactories that do not make anything that needs to be advertised. They make parts, and power, and more City. Their windows are small. Their roofs are flat to catch water. Their walls are stained vertically where the weeping has run for a hundred years. In between them are the infrastructures that make a settlement work at continental scale: aqueducts that are not Roman arches but long, humming concrete troughs on stilts, carrying grey water from one district's weep-catch to another's cisterns; switching yards where trains shunt forever at walking speed; power stations that breathe warm steam into the already warm air, their turbines turning with a patient, low thrum you feel in your sternum.

The technology is industrial, not futuristic. Everything has a weight. You can see how things work. Belt drives, rivets, porcelain insulators on power lines that sag between pylons, the smell of hot oil and ozone when a transformer throws a spark in the damp. Electrified, mechanized, scaled for production without being scaled for speed. Smokestacks do smoke, but the smoke is pale and wet and quickly becomes cloud. At night the City glows not with neon but with amber work-light, sodium lamps along every elevated causeway, furnace light behind corrugated walls. Nothing flashes. Everything hums.

That hum is the other half of the loudness. The City drones — turbines, looms, stamping presses, ventilation fans the size of houses moving warm wet air through districts — and the animals shriek and chitter and clatter, and the water ticks, and the sum is loud without being chaotic. It is a loudness you can live inside, the way you live inside surf. You have to raise your voice on a street corner. You stop noticing after a month.

Nothing happens quickly here. That is the rule.

Water moves slowly, seeping more than flowing. Rust deepens by a shade a year. The buildings subside by millimeters a decade and are jacked and patched without comment. The flocks wheel in the same gyres their great-grandparents wheeled in, following pressure gradients that repeat every nine days when the plateau makes its own weather and the cloud sea comes in from the same quarter. Even the factories work on slow cycles — curing, soaking, pressing, drying — processes that take days and cannot be rushed because the damp will not be rushed.

Erosion, at 2.5, is not an event. It is a condition that has been met. The wind has done its work. The water has found its runnels. The corners of everything are rounded. Rebar shows like bone where a wall has been wept on for too long, but the wall still stands. The cliff edge of the plateau, when you finally reach it after weeks of concrete and beetles and pigeons, is not crumbling. It is polished. Water spills over it in a thousand thin veils into the white below, evaporates halfway down, and returns as cloud by evening. The cycle is closed and patient.

If you stand there at the edge, with the warm wind coming up the scarp face carrying the faint metallic smell of the bacterial fields and the distant throb of a power plant behind you, and watch the gulls hang motionless in the updraft, you understand the place. A warm high desert that refused to be a desert because the air itself condenses. A city that refused to be separate from the rock because it had too much time and too much water. A world with no plants because it never needed them — fungal films and mineral waters feed the insects, the insects feed the birds, the birds feed the rats and the City, the City feeds the films with its heat and its runoff, and everything is wept on, and everything is weathered, and everything is in balance, loudly, densely, slowly, under a normal sun.

There is nowhere to be alone here. Even the stone has something living on it. Especially the stone.
