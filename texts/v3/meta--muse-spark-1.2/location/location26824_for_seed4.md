It's not on any map, not because it's secret, but because it's too small to map.

You find it by dropping.

The town of Nizhny-Kol is a low town, a wet town, laid flat on a plain so low you can feel the weight of the sky on it. In summer the plain is a marsh, in winter it is a marsh pretending to be solid. The town itself is built on stilts and peat-rafts and causeways of split larch — a proper town, with a street grid and a well and a smokehouse and fences that mean something, primitive and insistent, all hand-work: adze marks on every beam, rope lashing black with tar, stone set without mortar. From above, it looks sparse. Houses set far apart, lots of black water and white sky between them. A sparse, quiet northern town that minds its own business.

But you are not above. You have slipped.

Between two houses where the boardwalk had rotted, where the water never quite froze even in deep cold, there is a slump. The ground gave way last spring — young ground, fresh ground. The frost heaved and then dropped, cracking the basalt shelf underneath like a fresh plate, leaving a sharp, raw pocket. Not a cave. A room. A hollow maybe twenty paces across and the height of two men, sunk entirely below the causeway level, with a lip of torn roots holding it open. You have to turn sideways to get in.

Inside, the scale inverts.

The darkness is the first thing. It is not night-darkness. It is the darkness of being inside something. Light comes down in one thin, cold shaft from the crack you entered through — grey winter light, filtered through board gaps and falling snow — and it dies about halfway down. After that your eyes do the rest. After ten minutes you see that the darkness is not empty.

The second thing is the cold. A wet, near-freezing cold that never bites, just holds. Your breath hangs and does not leave. The air itself is wet, condensing on every surface and beading and falling again without ever raining. The walls weep. The basalt that cracked to make this place is young, its edges still sharp and unsoftened, but you can hardly see the stone anymore.

Because everything is plant.

Not plants growing *on* things. Plants *as* things. The walls, the floor, the ceiling of this pocket are living mass. Sphagnum and liverwort and cold-thread moss grown so thick it has become architecture. Old growth and new growth braided together into pillars, curtains, shelves. The floor gives under your boot and springs back, a mattress of peat a foot deep, saturated, cold water rising around your ankle and then sinking away again as the moss drinks it. From the ceiling, rootlets as thick as your wrist have come down looking for this wet air, and they have hung themselves with moss until they are columns. The town above drove piles through this — you can see the bottoms of them here, the tarred points of the larch pilings that hold the houses up, and each one is now a totem, furred completely in green-black moss, in white lichen that glows faintly, in tiny ferns that uncurl from cracks in the wood itself.

The builders didn't build this place. They just left a frame for it to grow around.

And it is empty in the way a town can be empty — sparse, scattered. There are only four structures down here, built by hand in the dark. Not houses for living so much as *attempts*: a low storage platform on cross-beams to keep salt fish off the peat, a ladder that goes nowhere now that the wall has grown over its top rungs, a shrine of stacked flat stones with a wooden bowl on it, and the old, half-collapsed wall of a root cellar that has become an arbor. They are primitive, careful, human-scale carpentry, and there is so much space between them. You could lie down between any two and see nothing but moss.

But the space is not empty.

It moves.

The fauna is total. This is their city, and the human town is the sparse interruption. You thought at first the moss was still, and then you realized the moss *is* moving because everything living on it is moving and there is no gap between them. Woodlice the size of thumbnail and the size of your thumb, pale and translucent, turning over the peat. Cold-water salamanders, ash-grey with orange bellies, hanging from the ceiling roots like fruit, blinking slowly. Clouds of midges that do not bite, that hang in the cold shaft of light and do not hum. Spiders — dozens of kinds — that have strung the pillars together with sheets of web that collect condensation and glitter when you breathe on them. And beetles. Black, iridescent, flat-backed beetles that carry tiny gardens of moss on their backs, so that when they stop you lose them.

Mostly, overwhelmingly — isopods and hoppers and pale crickets. They carpet the walls until a sound or a shadow startles them and the wall seems to ripple and rearrange. There are so many that the green mass has a second texture, a living, shifting exoskeleton.

And it is *quiet*. That is the strange part with so much life. There is no bird call, no wind down here. The sound is the water cycle of a single room: the *tit... tit... tit* of condensation falling from root tip to peat, the soft sigh of the moss taking it back up, the occasional *plip* of a salamander dropping into a shallow pool that has formed in a footprint and never drained. Your own boots sound too loud. Even the insects move mostly in silence, their legs muffled by the wet felt. When you stand still long enough, you hear the town above as if from very far away — a door, a dog, an axe — filtered through water and plant.

The rules here are strange, but simple, and they assert themselves the longer you stand.

**1. Light is food and light is forbidden.** Anything that glows is eaten. The lichen glows faintly, but only just enough to see by. If you bring a lantern down, the midges and moths will cover it in seconds and smother it, not out of hunger but out of a kind of urgent tending. The pocket wants to stay dark. The plants grow *toward* the dark, not the crack.

**2. Things grow before you make them.** On the eastern wall, the moss has formed a perfect, empty shelf at waist height, the size of a crate, with a lip. No one built it. A week later, someone will find that a crate fits there exactly. The shrine stones have a soft green replicate growing next to them, a moss cairn that was there before the stones were stacked. The place prototypes human building in plant.

**3. Nothing decays, nothing erodes — or rather, it does, but slowly and then all at once.** The cold holds everything. A fish left on the platform three winters ago is still there, freeze-dried and then moss-wrapped, its eye a beetle's nest. The basalt edges are still knife-sharp because the water that weeps over them is too cold to carry silt. This is young terrain held in stasis. But when the thaw comes, even for a day, everything moves an inch. Roots tighten. Peat sighs and settles. A ladder rung snaps and is immediately bridged by new growth.

You crouch because there is nowhere comfortable to stand. The air smells of peat and cold iron and green. A salamander watches you from eye level on a root-column, its throat pulsing. On the peat floor, a train of ants — white in the near-dark — carries flecks of larch bark from one piling to another, building something you can't understand yet.

Above you, through the sharp-lipped crack, the town continues its sparse, organized life, thinking it is on solid ground. Below, in this hidden, single room that contains a whole ecosystem, the ground is thinking around it — wet, total, cold, actively and quietly alive, waiting to see what you will build to fill the green shelf it has already made for you.
