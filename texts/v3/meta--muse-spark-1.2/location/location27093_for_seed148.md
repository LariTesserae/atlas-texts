It is a low place.

Not a valley, exactly — it hasn't had time to become a valley. The land is young. Raw. It sits in a shallow bowl of dark basalt that has only recently cooled, or been laid down, or grown — the rock is still deciding what it is. There are no riverbeds cut deep, no cliffs worn smooth. The ground is unsorted, embryonic: slabs of vesicular black stone tipped at odd angles against each other, with soft seams of ash and cold clay still settling between them. If you put your hand to it, it feels gritty, not yet sanded by weather. The earth hasn't been eroded so much as it is still being assembled. You can see the pour-lines where the ground flowed.

And everything is cold.

Not winter-cold, not killing cold — a persistent, late-autumn cool that lives in the stone. The air is cool enough that your breath makes a small cloud and then vanishes. Cool enough that condensation never quite evaporates. It beads.

Because this place is damp the way a breath is damp. There is not a lake here, not a sea. There is a pocketed water, an intermittent water. At night — which is almost always — water gathers out of the air itself. It pearls on every surface, runs in slow, bright rivulets down the undersides of leaves, collects in cupped fronds and basalt hollows, drips. Once or twice a day a soft rain comes without clouds, a vertical mist that hangs for twenty minutes and then is gone. There is a single, lazy watercourse that threads the bottom of the bowl, more of a chain of pools than a river, each pool connected to the next by a trickle over moss-slick stone. The sound of it is not rushing, but ticking — drip, spill, drip.

Nothing would prepare you for the green.

You expect a forest. This is not a forest. A forest has soil and then trees in it. Here the plant is the ground, the wall, the building, the road. Vegetation at total saturation. Leaves have become architecture.

In the center of the shallow bowl, where the stone is lowest, a town has grown. Or been grown. Or built and then been swallowed until the distinction stopped mattering.

From a distance, in the dark, you might mistake it for an industrial quarter — the silhouette is urban. There are verticals, there are levels, there are rectilinear masses with windows, there are towers, there are the sharp angles of infrastructure. Roads or streets or runways, but narrow, precise. It has the density of plan, the logic of a settlement meant for thousands.

And then you get closer and you see every wall is alive.

The buildings are not covered in vines. They *are* vines. Interwoven, lignified, breathing. Great trunks rise like columns, flatten into a canopy that becomes a roof, split into ribs that become walls. The factories — because they are factories — are made of plant. Their walls are cambium and bark, inches thick, warm faintly to the touch from the metabolism inside. The conveyor belts are not rubber but living runners, pale flat leaves that ripple and carry pods along their midribs. Vats are hollow gourds the size of rooms, filled with slow-fermenting sap that churns and steams. Chimneys are hollow fig-trees exhaling cool white vapor that condenses instantly and falls as rain on the streets below. Copper piping, old iron, and ceramic insulators are still there, bolted into the living wood, absorbed at the edges, half-swallowed, humming with a low industrial current. You can see where the original town was brick and steel, and where the plant has extruded itself around and through it, replacing it piece by piece without ever demolishing it. An electrical box buzzes inside a knot. A streetlamp, still burning amber behind a lens of living membrane, swings gently because the branch it hangs from is growing.

It is a small place. You could walk its perimeter in half an hour if you didn't stop. A hamlet, a single precinct, a cathedral complex. It has a boundary — the basalt rim of the bowl, and above that, the interlocked canopy that closes over it like a dome. Not sky — a roof of leaves, thousands of layers deep, so dense that almost no true light penetrates. That's why it is dark here.

The darkness is not absolute. It is a greenhouse darkness, a depth darkness. What light there is is made inside. The factory-vats glow faintly green from within, illuminating the sap. Fungal seams along the streets give off a cool, bluish light when stepped near. The condensation itself seems to carry light for a moment as it falls. And deeper in the plant-buildings, there are strings of old industrial bulbs, caged and flickering, that the vines have kept lit for no one, powered by something — a waterwheel turned by drip, a root-tapped geothermal seam. So you move in a dusk that never lifts, lit from below and within, not above. Shadows are soft and very close.

And it is empty.

That is the first thing that feels wrong. An urban place should be dense, crowded. This one was built for density but is hollowed. The streets are absurdly wide for a town this size, the avenues between the plant-factories are vast open gaps where nothing stands. The factories themselves are mostly air — huge vaulted chambers inside the living walls where a single machine might turn silently, or where a single table sits with a single chair overgrown into it. There is space everywhere, a near-void between presences. You can stand in the middle of a four-story intersection made of living bark and feel like you are in a deserted opera house. Your voice does not echo the way it should.

Because it is quiet.

For a place so active, it is almost silent. The industrial processes do not clank. The leaf-belts move without motors. The sap vats breathe rather than boil. Water ticks. The air is so still you can hear condensation forming — a faint tightening on the skin of a leaf — before it falls. When wind moves through the canopy-dome far above, it comes down filtered to a sigh.

But quiet does not mean still. This place is furiously, invisibly active. It is dynamic on every scale at once.

You see it if you stand still long enough to let your eyes adjust to the dark. The walls are growing. Not imperceptibly — visibly, a millimeter a minute, a curl of new bark unfurling, a buttress root thickening around a steel beam. The leaves overhead shift to track a light that isn't there. The runner-conveyors pulse pods forward, and the pods burst in transit, scattering seeds that sprout mid-air into small hovering ferns before landing.

And the fauna —

There should not be this much life in a cold, dark, empty industrial bowl. But life fills the negative space.

The air between the buildings is crowded, though you only notice it in glimpses when something passes through a shaft of fungal light. Moths the size of your hands, with bark-patterned wings that are exactly the pattern of the factory walls. Beetles with iron-colored elytra that cling to the copper pipes and drink condensation. Small birds, or something like birds, made almost entirely of thistledown and bone, nesting in the light fixtures. Things that rustle inside the walls. Things with too many joints that tend the sap vats, harvesting, not quite insects, not quite workers — they pay you no attention. On the ground, in the chain-pools, translucent salamanders and flat, blind fish stir the water, and every puddle holds clouds of larvae. The plant itself hums with mites, aphids, ants farming them in living corridors you mistake for electrical conduits. Abundant, layered, oblivious to the urban geometry, using it.

And then the wrongness deepens, because this is not a place that holds together.

The weirdness is not decoration here. It is the rule.

Distances are not stable. You can walk down a street that should take thirty seconds and find it takes ten minutes because the street grew longer while you weren't looking at its end. A factory interior that looks small from outside opens into a hollow far larger than its walls should contain — you step through a door of woven roots and the ceiling arches away thirty meters up into darkness that smells like canopy. If you turn around quickly, the door is higher than it was, or on the other side of the room. Gravity is polite but not committed; sap rises faster than it should, water droplets sometimes fall sideways to be caught by a leaf that wanted them. Time pools. In one gallery, the rain is still falling from an hour ago, frozen mid-air in bright beads, while in the next the wood is already rotting and re-sprouting.

The town remembers being a town, and the plant remembers being a plant, and both memories are running at once and overwriting each other. You will find a brick wall inside a trunk that has growth rings that predate the brick. You will find a sign stenciled on living bark in fresh paint that says SUB-STATION 3 and underneath, in a rougher hand, PLEASE DO NOT PRUNE. You will find a set of stairs that goes up and arrives slightly before you started climbing it, so you meet yourself coming down, or rather the condensation of yourself.

No one lives here anymore, or perhaps everyone does and they have become indistinguishable from the fauna. The machines keep making whatever it was they make — perhaps more plant, perhaps more machine, perhaps a slow conversion of basalt into leaf into light into water into air — without need for operators. The town does not need you to see it. It continues to construct itself, embryonic, damp, cool, dark, empty and utterly full, a small urban-industrial ecosystem sealed under its own living sky, working quietly and without pause toward a form it will never finish becoming.
