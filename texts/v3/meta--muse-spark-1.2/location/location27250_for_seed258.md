It is high. High enough that the air should be thin and cold, but it isn't.

You are on a plateau that has not yet learned to be a plateau. It is a raw thing, new-made. The edges where the world cuts away are not weathered smooth but are fresh and abrupt, like something torn. Sheer faces of dark, unlicked stone fall away in clean, vertical slabs, unworn by wind or water. There is no talus at their feet, no softening of scree. The rock is embryonic, just-extruded, still holding the heat of its making under a skin of moss that has rushed to cover it. From a distance the whole mass looks like it was lifted yesterday. It was.

And nothing here is rock, except the original lift. Everything else is built. Everything.

Not built *on*. Built *of*.

The vegetation did not colonize the plateau. It *is* the plateau. There is no soil left visible, no exposed ground that is not also a living surface. The warm air - a steady, humid, late-summer warmth that never quite tips into heat, never cools - has allowed for a total architecture of plant.

Below your feet is not earth but a mat, a braid, a floor woven from living wood. Roots as thick as your body run like buttresses and roadways, interlaced with secondary roots as thick as your arm, then as thick as your wrist, then as fine as hair, until the weave becomes watertight. You can feel it give slightly when you step, a deep, structural flex, and then hold. In places the root-floor is polished by its own growth into a dark, ruddy smoothness you could sit on. In other places it bristles with young, soft moss that swallows your footsteps whole.

And from that floor, the buildings grow.

For lack of a better word. They are not houses, not ruins, not temples, though they have the geometry of all three. Hollow trunks rise fifty, eighty feet, but they are not trees. They are columns, arches, vaulted halls, grown hollow from the start. Their walls are living bark on the outside and ribbed, chambered wood on the inside, still sapped and breathing. Leaves the size of doors form roofs, overlapping like tiles, shingling themselves mid-air, angling to catch normal, white-yellow sun. Vines as thick as cables have thrown themselves in perfect catenaries between the trunks, and from those cables hang curtains of aerial roots, gardens, nests of epiphyte and flower that never touch the ground.

It is a town, a valley-wide town, but sparsely populated. That is the strangeness of the scale. You can see for a long way - an open, regional vista across the woven plain - and you understand there was meant to be density here, there *could* be density here, but there isn't. The great plant-halls stand far apart from one another, each a solitary cathedral with immense, empty space between. The emptiness is not absence; it is part of the design. The wind moves uninterrupted between the structures. Light falls unbroken onto the root-floor between them. It would feel abandoned if it didn't feel so intentional, like a city built for giants who never arrived, or for a single inhabitant who likes a lot of air.

Water is everywhere, but not in rivers. There are no rivers yet. The geology is too new to have cut them.

Instead the plateau weeps.

The warm air condenses as it lifts over the high edge and moves inland, and it never stops condensing. Water beads on every leaf-tip and does not fall so much as it *exhales*. The great leaves tip and pour in steady, glassy threads. The bark sweats. The hanging root-curtains drip in a thousand parallel metronomes. Low places in the root-floor have become shallow, clear pans - not ponds, but living basins held watertight by interlocked grain, their bottoms made of smooth, pale root. You wade through them ankle-deep and the water is warm as skin. Where the raw stone of the plateau's edge is still exposed, water comes straight out of the rock face in cold seeps, drawing dark vertical stripes down the cliff that glitter even in shade.

The sound is loud.

You expected high quiet. Instead the plateau is a single, continuous, low chord. It never varies. The creak of living wood under tension - a deep, organ-like groan as the vast woven floor expands infinitesimally in the warmth and holds. The unending drip and trickle of water from a million leaf-points onto root and into basin, a hiss that becomes a roar when you listen too long. The rustle of the door-sized leaves shifting against each other, not with wind but with growth, with the slow hydraulic push of water through their veins. It is not noise. It is tone. It has no beginning, no crescendo, no ending. When you first arrived it seemed deafening. After an hour you realize you have stopped hearing it and have started hearing *through* it, as if it were air.

Little moves. That is the other law of this place.

For all its dripping and creaking, nothing changes. The growth here is in perfect, frozen equilibrium. A leaf unfurls at the exact rate another curls and drops and is absorbed. A root thickens as another thins. The water that condenses and pours is the water that is taken up again by the aerial roots before it can ever carve a channel. You can watch a droplet form at a leaf tip, stretch, fall, and vanish into moss, and watch another droplet form in the exact same spot a moment later. The plateau is under construction and has always been under construction and will always be under construction, but the construction never advances. The raw, new stone never weathers further. The plant-cathedrals never get taller or fall. It is embryonic and total at once, a newborn world held in warm stasis.

Life, as in animal life, is scarce to the point of rarity. There are no flocks, no swarms. You might see a single, pale insect the length of your hand moving with impossible slowness along the underside of a vine, tending fungus. You might hear a solitary, deep-throated call from inside one of the hollow trunks - not a bird, something that lives in the wood itself and only calls once an hour, exactly once. If the plant is the city, the animal is the rare citizen who walks its empty avenues alone and is not lonely.

And the weirdness - because it is weird, gently and totally weird - is that you understand after a while that no hand made this. No tool. No tech. No cut, no nail, no plan. The built world here was not assembled. It was *grown into being built*. The plants did not evolve to be plants and then get used as material. They *are* material that learned to be plant-shaped, or plant that learned to be city-shaped. The distinction has no meaning here. The columns have bark because bark is what a column is, here. The arches leaf because that is how an arch breathes.

You stand in the warm, loud, wet light on a new high world that is entirely a house that grew itself, spaced itself sparsely to let the water and sound travel, and then stopped time so it could remain perfect.

You can walk for miles across the springy root-plain, from one empty cathedral to the next, your feet wet, your ears full, and the plateau will not get any older under you.
