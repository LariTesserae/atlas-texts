It is not a mountain range anymore. It is what is left after a mountain range has finished being a mountain range.

The elevation is extreme — not peaked, not jagged. That kind of youth is gone. This is a high country that has been lifted and then left alone for so long that the earth has settled into its final shape. A peneplain at the roof of the world. Basalt and old granite worn down until it is almost flat, rounded into broad, whale-backed domes and shallow, immense bowls — a tableland four kilometers up where the air is thin and cool enough that your breath ghosts even in summer. The rock itself is ancient, polished by wind and water and time until it feels soft under the hand. No cliffs. No scree. Just long, slow slopes that go for kilometers before they fall away at the edges in smooth, undercut lips, and everywhere the stone is covered in a deep, black, peaty soil that has had a million years to accumulate.

And that flat high world is drowned.

The sea came up here and never left. Not a lake, not in any honest sense — too big, too deep, too restless for that word. A perched ocean held in the shallow saucer of the plateau. Post-flood water, wall-to-wall, sky-water that sits on top of the sky. Cold, clear, and utterly covering. From any of the higher domes you can see it stretch to the rim of the world, a slate-gray inland sea at an altitude where there should only be wind. In the deeper bowls it is fathoms deep, dark green-black down where the light doesn't reach. On the shallows it is only waist or chest high, so clear you can see the drowned meadows underneath, the stone still there under the water, furred with algae.

The water is cool — 0.8 cold, not freezing but permanently cool, snow-melt cold, high-altitude cold. It never really warms. When the wind comes off it, it carries a fine, soaking mist that beads on everything and never quite dries. You are damp within minutes of stepping outside. The light is normal, flat northern light, overcast more often than not, filtered through a thick, fast-moving cloud deck that scrapes just above the water's surface. Not dim, not glaring. Just even, diffuse, revealing.

Nothing is still here. The system is turbulent, hyperactive, loud.

Wind funnels up over the plateau rim and hits the water and never stops. Waves chop and slap against the smooth stone domes even though there is no tide, driven entirely by storm. Rain comes in curtains that hammer for an hour, vanish, then return. The water itself moves — currents pull between the deeper bowls, spillways roar where one flooded basin pours into the next over smooth stone sills worn into perfect spill-steps. And over it all is the industrial sound.

Because people did build here, though lightly and long ago. Scattered — 0.6 scattered. No cities. No sprawl. Just what you need to live and work on a drowned roof.

Steel walkways on stilts stitch dome to dome, half-submerged and rust-red. Pump houses and turbine halls, hydromills and sluice gates, all electrified, all clanking, all scaled for work rather than habitation. This place was — is — industrial. Not digital, not sleek. Rivets, diesel, poured concrete stained with mineral seep, cableways strung between domes with ore buckets still creaking along them though many dump nothing now. Steam sighs from pressure valves. Generators thrum day and night to keep the water where it is, to keep it oxygenated, to keep the spillways from undercutting the rim and draining the whole sky-sea in a single catastrophic waterfall to the lowlands. The sound is layered and dense and loud — water roar plus wind plus the thud and whine of machinery, plus life.

And life is dense, saturated. 2.0 dense. 2.2 lush.

Everything that can grow does, crowded shoulder to shoulder in the thin cool air. Where the stone breaks the surface, it is carpeted in moss so thick it feels like walking on soaked mattresses, in lichen, in dwarf alpine willow and birch that have adapted to having their roots permanently wet. Out in the water, the vegetation is wilder: mats of floating peat and sphagnum big enough to stand on, drifting kelp-like forests of cold-water weed that sway just under the surface, their fronds broad and dark green. Reeds choke the shallows, so thick you have to push a boat through with a pole. The water itself is green with suspended life, phytoplankton blooming in the normal light.

Fauna is abundant, noisy, unafraid. Not monstrous — this is a normal world, physics as usual, weirdness low. Just life filling every niche that water and altitude will allow. Clouds of midges and stoneflies. Trout and char fat in the cold, flashing silver when they turn. Geese and cranes in the thousands lifting off the water at dusk in a deafening clatter, their calls echoing off the turbine halls. Amphibians everywhere — small dark newts clinging to the walkway pilings, chorus frogs that make the reeds vibrate at night. On the domes, shaggy highland cattle and musk-sheep, feral now, graze the moss, their coats dripping.

The rule of this place is simple: the water wants to leave. Gravity wants the sea to fall off the plateau. Everything that lives here — plant, animal, human, machine — is engaged in the same constant, turbulent negotiation to keep it here a little longer. The ancient, worn-flat stone wants to be still. The water wants to move. The wind and the engines compromise between them, endlessly.

If you stand on one of the taller domes — really just a low hill now, smoothed to a hump — you can see the whole regional scale at once, a town-or-valley sized piece of the perched ocean with its islands of stone and steel. Waves, walkways, drifting green islands, a turbine hall belching steam on the next rise, a flock of birds turning as one, and beyond that, the faint gray line where water meets cloud meets the drop-off at the world's edge. Close up you can hear everything at once: the slap of water on stone, the creak of a cableway, the putter of a pump, the frogs, the wind in the reeds. It smells like cold iron, peat, and rain.

It is not beautiful in a pristine way. It is working, crowded, damp, and very old — and completely, stubbornly alive on top of its own drowned height.
