This place will kill you politely. It won't freeze you, it won't storm on you, it won't chase you. It will just let you make every mistake a warm, dark, quiet place invites you to make.

You need to prepare for a location that *feels* survivable and is not.

Think of it as: High-altitude desert + fresh volcano + newborn planet + low-pressure physics bug. Here's how an ordinary, baseline human survives a visit.

### 1. Understand What Is Trying To Kill You

It is not the cold or the dark. It's the combination of three lies:

**Lie 1: "Warm means safe."** The air is 30-35C, body-temperature warm. Your brain will tell you that you don't need protection. You do. That warmth is coming from the rock under your feet, not the air. The air itself is thin, dead, and chemically wrong.

**Lie 2: "I can see, so I can navigate."** The light is dim, diffuse, and comes from the *ground*. Your depth perception, your shadow-reading, your circadian clock - all evolved for light from above. Here they will fail.

**Lie 3: "Quiet means stable."** This mountain range is being born while you stand on it. The ground is not ground yet.

If you go in thinking "warm desert night," you will be dehydrated, hypoxic, lost, and bleeding within an hour.

### 2. Your Body - The Non-Negotiables

This is a high-altitude, hyper-arid, hypoxic environment. Treat it like climbing at 5,000-6,000 meters without the cold to remind you to be careful.

**Oxygen is mandatory, not optional.**
The air is thin enough that "each breath feels deliberate." That is altitude sickness territory. An ordinary human cannot acclimatize to this, because the partial pressure of oxygen is too low and there is no time to adapt on a short visit. Bring supplemental oxygen. Not just a can - a proper system:

*   A closed or semi-closed rebreather or at minimum a demand-flow supplemental O2 with nasal cannula + full-face backup. You will need it the entire time you are there.
*   A pulse oximeter on your finger. If you drop below 85% SpO2, you leave. No arguments. You will feel fine right up until you can't think straight.
*   Practice deliberate breathing *before* you go. Slow, deep, diaphragmatic. Hyperventilating in dry thin air will dump your CO2 and make you dizzy faster.

**Water and salt, more than you think.**
There is zero environmental water. No rivers, no frost, no humidity. Your sweat evaporates before you feel it. Your lips will crack in minutes, your nose will bleed, your eyes will dry out.

*   Carry 1 liter per hour you plan to stay, *plus* 2 liters emergency. In a sealed hydration bladder with a tube, so you can drink without taking your mask off.
*   Electrolytes. Plain water will kill you almost as fast as no water. Salt tablets or oral rehydration salts. You are losing salt with every invisible exhalation.
*   Prepare your skin: Heavy lip balm, nasal saline gel, artificial tears, moisturizer on every exposed surface. Cover everything. Your skin is not meant for anhydrous air.
*   Chapstick and skin barrier will feel silly until 20 minutes in when your eyelids feel like paper.

**Gas, not just thin air.**
That forge smell - hot iron, ozone, clean stone - is vaporized metal, sulfur, carbon dioxide, maybe carbon monoxide and sulfur dioxide pooling in the valleys. The air is still. There is no wind to clear it.

*   Bring a personal 4-gas monitor: O2, CO2, CO, H2S/SO2. Clip it to your chest. If it alarms in a low spot, get high immediately. CO2 is heavier than the rest of the air and will collect invisibly in those broad valleys where the rock is still plastic.
*   Your supplemental O2 system should be sealed enough that you are not breathing ambient for long stretches. A simple dust mask is not enough; you want a half-face respirator with acid gas cartridges if you can't do a full rebreather.

### 3. Your Gear - For Ground That Hasn't Learned To Be Ground

**Feet and Hands:**
Everything is sharp and hot. This is not scree, this is broken glass the size of houses.

*   Boots: High-ankle, stiff mountaineering boots with a *thick, heat-insulated sole* - Vibram with a ceramic shank. The rock is warm to hot through the sole. Thin hiking shoes will cook your feet and be sliced open. Break them in beforehand.
*   Gaiters: To keep obsidian splinters out.
*   Gloves: Heavy leather, heat-resistant work gloves. You *will* catch yourself on a razor edge. Bare hands will be filleted.
*   Trekking pole or collapsible probe: Tap every slab before you put weight on it. If it sounds hollow, glows, or gives, you don't step there. The "semi-plastic" valleys with the sighing cherry-red cracks are no-go zones. They are crust over magma. They can take your weight for three seconds and then not.

**Eyes and Light:**
Your eyes will never fully adjust. Don't try to force them.

*   Goggles, not sunglasses: Clear, sealed, anti-fog. Against flying glass shards when a spire pops or a cliff whisper-avalanches. And against the bone-dry air.
*   Bring your own light, but use it wrong on purpose. A bright white headlamp will blind you and kill the faint vein-glow and phosphorescence that are your only natural landmarks. Bring two lights:
    1. A dim red-light headlamp for walking and preserving night vision. Use this 90% of the time.
    2. A strong, focused white handheld for inspecting ground, checking route, signaling. Use it in bursts.
*   A headlamp with a low UV mode is useful - those thallium salts and other minerals will pop under UV, giving you better edge definition on cliffs than white light.

**Clothing:**
You need to trap moisture in and keep heat and cuts out, while not overheating at 35C.

*   Light-colored, loose, full-coverage layers: Merino or synthetic base layer + wind shell. You want to create a microclimate. Exposed skin loses water instantly.
*   No shorts, no short sleeves. Ever.
*   A wide-brim hat with neck drape or hood. There is no sun to burn you, but the rock radiates IR from below. You will burn from the ground up if you lie down.

### 4. How To Move

This is the hardest part. The physics is slightly off.

**Move like you are on the moon with an illness.**

*   Things fall too slowly, arcs hang too long, shadows lean the wrong way. Your inner ear will feel seasick. Your proprioception will be off. You will misjudge jumps and throws by 10-20%. *Do not jump across gaps.* Do not trust your ability to catch a tossed object. Walk. Step deliberately. Keep your center of gravity low.
*   Test gravity. Before you need to rely on it, toss a small stone and watch the arc. Remind your brain: this is not Earth.
*   Silence swallows sound. You cannot judge distance by sound. That collapse that looked "a few valleys over" might be 400 meters away or 4 kilometers. Shout and your voice will go nowhere. Bring a whistle or personal locator beacon. Do not rely on hearing your partner.
*   The landscape moves. Never camp, sit, or loiter in:
    - Valley bottoms where new upwellings bulge
    - The base of those leaning house-sized plates of black glass
    - Under needle-spires or top-heavy fused spires
    Pick a broad, solid, already-cooled basalt plate, check it for warmth with the back of your hand, and treat it as your island. Be ready to leave it.
*   Plan a short stay. 45-90 minutes max for a first visit. Your body is on a timer with dehydration and hypoxia. Set a literal alarm. When it rings, you walk back to your exit point even if you just saw the most beautiful glowing vein you've ever seen.

**Navigation:**
Do not trust the fake buildings.

The basalt colonnades, sulfur domes, fused-spire "towns" - your brain is hardwired to see them as meaning. They are pareidolia, but very, very good pareidolia in low light. You will *want* to go investigate the temple. That is how you get lost.

*   Drop breadcrumbs that don't rely on landscape memory: Luminous trail markers, GPS waypoint if your system works cross-universe, or a spool of high-visibility cord back to your entry point.
*   Compass may be unreliable if the magnetic field is young or anomalous. Bring inertial navigation - count steps, note bearings, watch your back-trail constantly.
*   Watch the phosphorescent edges. They are the most honest map of where a cliff is. Mark them mentally.

### 5. Your Mind

This place is psychologically brutal in a way that doesn't show up on a gear list.

*   **Endless warm night:** Without a sun or stars, your circadian rhythm will dissolve in hours. You will feel like you've been there for both 10 minutes and 10 hours. Bring a watch with an illuminated face and trust it, not your feeling of time.
*   **High, silent, empty:** The ringing in your ears in that profound silence can become panic. Some people get vertigo from the thin air + wrong gravity + horizon jitter. Have a grounding routine: name 5 things you see, slow your breathing, check your O2.
*   **Loneliness of pre-life:** There is nothing alive. No microbe, no lichen. That registers on a deep level. People often feel watched or feel a pressure to be quiet, or conversely feel an urge to be loud to fill the void. Expect it. It's normal. It doesn't mean something is there. But don't ignore real danger signs because you wrote them off as "just the weird feeling."

Go with a rule: If you feel a strong emotional pull to keep going toward a beautiful light or a perfect ruin, stop, drink water, check O2, check your exit bearing. Beauty is a hazard here.

### A Minimal Checklist For An Ordinary Human

**On your body:**
[ ] Sealed hydration system + 3L+ water + electrolytes
[ ] Supplemental O2 + pulse oximeter + gas monitor
[ ] Clear sealed goggles + red/white headlamp
[ ] Heat-insulated stiff boots + gaiters + leather gloves
[ ] Full skin coverage, loose light layers, hat
[ ] Lip balm, nasal gel, eye drops in pocket

**In your pack:**
[ ] Trekking pole / ground probe
[ ] Heat-resistant sleeping pad to sit on - never sit directly on rock
[ ] Whistle, PLB/beacon, luminous markers/cord
[ ] Basic first aid + blister care + burn gel + field splint
[ ] Watch/timer

**In your head:**
[ ] Hard time limit. Hard turn-back O2 and water limit.
[ ] "The ground is alive" mindset.
[ ] "The buildings are not buildings" mantra.
[ ] Exit rehearsed before entry.

You stand on warm rock under a sky that never changes, surrounded by a quiet so deep it hurts, watching a planet learn how to be a planet. It is stunning. It will also try, without malice, to mummify you, trip you, slice you, and quietly asphyxiate you while you stare at the glow.

Go prepared to be a respectful, well-hydrated, well-oxygenated guest who leaves no footprints because the rock will erase them anyway - and who leaves quickly enough to tell someone what it smelled like.
