This is not a dangerous place. It will not try to kill you. That's what makes it hard to prepare for.

Most hostile locations announce themselves. This one will try to absorb you. It is a slow, warm, wet, working equilibrium that has been stable for a hundred years, and your job as a visitor is to not break it, and to not let it break you slowly in the 6-12 hours it takes to start.

Think of it less as a swamp and less as a factory. Think of it as a single greenhouse-workshop the size of a few city blocks that has learned to breathe.

Here is how an ordinary, unaugmented human should prepare to walk into that dish and walk back out again.

### 1. Understand What Your Body Will Actually Experience

You will be wet before you understand why. You won't fall in the canal. You will just... never be dry again until you leave.

**The Air:** 28-30C / 82-86F and at or near 100% humidity. The air is physically heavy. Breathing feels like inhaling warm broth. If you have asthma, exercise-induced bronchospasm, or are very sensitive to mold, this place will test you hard. Even if you don't, you will sweat and it will not evaporate. Your sweat will sit on you. Your core temperature will creep up without you noticing because the cooling system doesn't work. Heat exhaustion here doesn't feel like desert heat exhaustion - it feels like becoming slowly waterlogged, tired, and headachy.

**The Ground:** You will not walk on stone or concrete. You will walk on living root-mat, wet brick, and slick wood, all of it beading with seepage. Every foundation weeps at knee height. Every leaf drips. After the daily afternoon rain - short, hard, clockwork - the whole hamlet curtains water for hours from living gutters and roof-thatch. There is no puddle to avoid. The water is *in* the walls.

**The Sound & Light:** There is no silence. The engine house in the center thumps like a slow heart 24 hours a day - you feel it in your teeth. Line shafts whine, belts slap, water ticks constantly on metal and leaf and canal. It's not loud enough to damage hearing in a short visit, but it's saturating. You won't be able to think clearly at first.

Light is fractured. The canopy is pruned open over the saw-tooth mill roof, so you get hard, bright bars of sun that make you squint, then five steps later you are in deep green shade where the bare bulbs are burning at noon. Your eyes will be constantly adjusting.

**Time:** Nothing happens fast here. The figs add one ring a year. A root swells around a pipe over a decade. The mill runs at the speed it ran 20 years ago. If you are an impatient traveler you will feel a very strong, itchy urge to *do something* or hurry. Don't. The place punishes hurry with a twisted ankle on a root or a hand caught on a belt.

### 2. Physical Preparation & Gear

Pack as if you are going to work in a botanical garden's boiler room, not hike a jungle.

**Clothing - Assume Permanent Damp:**
*   **Do NOT bring waterproof membranes like Gore-Tex.** They will trap your sweat inside and you will stew. They also never dry.
*   Do bring: Light, loose, quick-dry synthetics or thin merino wool. Long sleeves and long pants - not for warmth, but to keep constant damp leaf-contact and rusty iron off your skin. Everything will be brushed by wet fern and moss.
*   Bring 3x more socks than you think you need. Merino or synthetic. Your feet will be wet all day. Trench foot / immersion foot and fungal rot is the single most common injury for ordinary humans here, not falling.
*   Footwear: Knee-high rubber boots are what the locals wear for a reason, but for a visitor, closed, draining trail shoes or jungle boots with good grip are better. Something you can submerge and keep walking in. No leather hiking boots - they will waterlog and never dry. No sandals - root-mat will tear your feet up and rusty metal is everywhere.
*   A wide-brimmed, vented hat. Not to keep rain off - nothing will - but to keep sun bars out of your eyes and drips out of your eyes.

**Skin and Lungs:**
*   Before you go: Make sure your tetanus vaccine is current. You *will* scrape yourself on rusted iron, brick, or root. Every surface is abrasive and wet.
*   Pack: Antifungal powder/cream, zinc oxide barrier cream, adhesive bandages that actually stick when wet, small bottle of povidone-iodine. Antihistamines and a basic inhaler if you use one, even if you rarely need it - the spore load from moss, thatch, and constantly damp brick is very high. An N95 mask is worth having in your bag if you are mold-sensitive. You won't wear it the whole time, but you will want it if you have to go deep into the mill under the trellis.
*   Bring earplugs or low-profile ear defenders. Not because it's deafening, but so you can sleep or reset your brain for 20 minutes. The constant drip-thump-whine is exhausting.
*   You will chafe. Everywhere. Apply barrier balm before you enter. Bring it.

**Tech Will Suffer:**
*   Condensation will form *inside* everything. Camera lenses, phones, watches, glasses. Bring everything in dry-bags with silica gel packets. Bring a microfiber cloth you keep in a ziplock and accept your glasses will fog constantly.
*   Bring an analog or cheap digital watch you don't care about. Batteries and contacts corrode.
*   Do not rely on electronics for navigation or return. The dish is only 300-400 meters across and a meter deep - you won't get lost, you will be seen from the moment you cross the levee - but your multiverse anchor/beacon/return marker should be set **outside** the dish on the dry farmland beyond the levee ring. Inside, water and living mass and iron attenuate everything. Think of it like trying to get a signal in a greenhouse made of wet earth.
*   A small, powerful headlamp is still useful despite the bare bulbs everywhere. The aisles between buildings are dim even at noon.

### 3. How to Behave Inside

This is the most important preparation. This hamlet is not a ruin. It is a maintained equilibrium. The plants are not weeds. They are walls, pillars, plumbing, and roof. The locals - machinists, pruners, weavers, kids - are not living *in spite* of the swamp. They are tending it like a machine.

**You are a guest in a workshop that is alive.**

1.  **Do not fight the water.** Don't try to stay dry. Don't wipe every surface before you sit. Let your feet get wet and keep moving. The locals let water seep, collect, boil for steam, and seep again. If you try to dam or divert a trickle, you are breaking a system.

2.  **Do not touch, pick, prune, or lean.** That vine on the wall is holding up the wall. That aerial root as thick as your arm banded with iron is a load-bearing column that has been trained for 30 years. Don't swing on it, carve it, or pull a flower off it to smell. Ask before you touch any plant that is part of a building. If an old woman is trimming the wall-vine with shears so it doesn't eat the power line, she is not gardening. She is doing electrical maintenance.

3.  **Respect the moving parts.** The line shafts, belts, looms, lathes - they are old, electrified with thick rubber cable stapled to living wood, and they have no modern guards. Loose clothing, long hair, dangling bag straps will be caught. Keep everything tied close. Don't reach over a belt. Don't touch a pipe that is weeping condensation - it might be steam. The thin heat shimmer from the foundry chimney is the only warning you get.

4.  **Drink and eat carefully.** The canal is tea-brown seep, not a river. Do not drink it. The cisterns that catch overflow from the living gutters are boiled for the boilers - locals know which cisterns are potable. Unless someone hands you water and you see it poured from a kettle or sealed bottle, purify it yourself. Bring your own water in and bring purification tablets as backup. Food offered will be safe, but it will be damp and warm.

5.  **Be visible and be polite.** You cannot sneak into a place this small and this open. Everyone will see you cross the levee. Greet people. Ask where you can walk. The root-streets are swept every morning - treat them like someone's living room floor. Don't drop trash - it will never dry and never decompose the way you expect, it will just mold into the moss. Carry it out to the dry fields.

6.  **Don't expect fauna.** Don't bring birdseed, don't look for monkeys. The absence of animals is the point. The human tending and machine noise has made a plant-and-people-only ecology. The only insects are small and under leaves. If you swat loudly and thrash, you are just making noise in a place that is already full of it.

### 4. Psychological Preparation

This place is profoundly comfortable and profoundly uncanny at once, and that combination is disorienting.

It is warm, dim-green, thrumming like a womb. The air holds you. The drip becomes hypnotic. People are calm and focused on slow tasks. After two hours, your traveler brain - which is used to scanning for danger, for novelty, for a way out - will start to down-regulate. You will feel sleepy, content, and slightly unreal, as if you've always been there.

That is the hazard.

**For an ordinary human, the risk is not panic, but acquiescence.** You will stop wanting to check your feet, drink water, or leave. You will want to sit on a mossy balcony and watch water bead on brick for a long time.

Prepare by:
*   Setting a hard time limit before you enter. "I will be out in 4 hours." Tell someone or set a watch alarm outside the dish.
*   Planning a deliberate drying point. Know where you will go *after* to become completely dry, warm, and quiet for 12 hours. Your skin and lungs need to reset. Don't plan to go from this dish directly into another wet or cold location.
*   Bringing quiet. Even foam earplugs for 10 minutes in a corner will feel like surfacing for air. You need to give your brain a break from the saturated sound.

### 5. Minimal Checklist for the Ordinary Traveler

**On your body going in:** Long quick-dry pants/shirt, hat, draining shoes, fresh socks in ziplock, barrier cream on, tetanus up to date.

**In a small dry-bag:**
- 2 extra pairs socks + small towel in a ziplock
- Water bottle + purification
- Antifungal powder, plasters, iodine, antihistamines
- Foam earplugs
- Headlamp + silica gel
- Microfiber cloth for glasses/lens
- Small offering/gesture of respect: a pair of good work gloves or a small folding pruning saw is valued far more than money here. You are entering a place where maintenance is prayer.

**Leave outside the levee:** Your return anchor, your dry camp, any leather, paper, or electronics you aren't willing to get damp.

**When you leave:** Don't just walk out and keep walking. Stop on the dry dirt road beyond the levee. Take your shoes and socks off, even if it's just for five minutes. Dry your feet. Drink clean water. Look back at the dark green mound that from here again looks like just a single dense grove. Notice how you couldn't see the dish until you were in it, and now you can feel the air get lighter as you moved out of it.

You will carry the smell of hot oil and crushed leaves with you for a day no matter how much you wash. Check between your toes, behind your ears, and in any fold of skin for redness or maceration the next morning.

If you do it right, you will leave with the memory of a place that is neither wild nor tame, but perfectly tended - a town that decided not to drain the swamp and not to drown in it, but to weave itself into it until you can't tell where brick ends and root begins. And you will have left it exactly as you found it, still thumping, still dripping, still slowly growing around its pipes.

That's the best way to visit a place that intends to be exactly itself for a long time more.
