This is not a wilderness. It is a **room that feeds itself.** If you go there as an ordinary human - soft, water-based, sun-based - you are not a visitor. You are a contamination event. Prepare to be a respectful, temporary one.

Here is what your intuition should be telling you, and how to prepare for it:

### 1. Understand What This Place Is

On first reading you might think "desert crater." It's not.

It's a **closed geothermal terrarium.** An old, weathered volcanic blister - 200-300 meters across, basalt rim, evaporite/salt floor - that has sealed itself off from the normal rules. No sun, no water cycle, no plants, no sound.

The energy does not come from above. It comes from below. Heat leaks up through cracks in the salt pan and drives everything. The chemistry runs on mineral and heat, not photosynthesis. That metallic/ozone taste in the air is sulfur, salts, alkali volatiles - this place breathes chemistry that is not made for your lungs.

The biology is a single, dense, heat-eating carpet. Tens of thousands of flat, matte-black, amber-edged isopod-like things, packed plate-to-plate so tight the floor disappears. They circulate like a slow liquid, driven by convection. The longer, ridge-backed predators that hunt among them are part of the same system. Nothing is dormant. Nothing is wasted. Even a shed husk is recycled in a minute.

The cairns on the rim tell you the only important fact: Someone came before you, understood it was complete without them, and left.

**Your goal is to do the same.** See it from the rim, and leave it exactly as sealed as you found it.

Do not plan to "explore the floor." There is no floor for you.

### 2. What Will Actually Hurt You

An ordinary human will not be killed dramatically. You will be eroded.

**a) Dry Heat + Chemical Air**
At 2000m+ it should be cold. It's not. The stone re-radiates heat all night and the pan itself is above blood temperature - 40-50C in places, much hotter at the fissures. The air is chemically dry. It will strip moisture from your eyes, mouth, nose, and lungs before you feel thirsty. The faint iron/ozone smell means you are breathing fine salt dust, sulfurous gases, and alkaline particulates. Without a filter, your throat will close, your eyes will burn, and you will get a chemical headache in 10-15 minutes.

**b) Dehydration and Heat Injury**
You will not sweat efficiently. The air takes it before it can cool you. You will overheat while thinking you are just "dry." This is the fastest way this place puts you down.

**c) The Floor**
This is not ground you can walk on. That living thatch is 2-5 cm deep, constantly moving, overlapping. If you step onto it you will sink to your ankle in bodies and salt crust, you will slip, and you will be swarmed. They are not "aggressive" to humans - they are opportunistic chemical processors. A warm, salty, soft human who falls is heat + mineral + water. The predators with the glassy ridge can pierce chitin. Your skin is easier. You will not be chased. You will be slowly incorporated. And even if you get out, you will carry them with you.

**d) The Rim and the Bowl Effect**
The drop is clean and sudden. The basalt is old, smooth, rounded, matte black-red. It looks grippy but it's like worn soapstone covered in fine salt powder. One misstep and you slide into the bowl. There are no handholds on the pan to climb out, and no one can hear you shout - the bowl eats sound. The darkness + heat + profound quiet + the slow turning of that mass will also wreck your sense of scale and time. People get vertigo, trypophobia, and a strong urge to throw something in "just to see what happens." That urge is the place testing you.

### 3. How To Prepare - Gear

Think like you are visiting a hot clean-room that is also a tide pool. You want full coverage, sealed, and you want to bring nothing that can be left behind.

**Breathing and Eyes - Non-Negotiable:**
*   P100/ABEK or equivalent full-face respirator with combination particle + acid gas/alkali filters. A dust mask is not enough. If you only bring one thing, bring this.
*   Sealed goggles if not using full-face. Carry artificial tears/eye wash.
*   A portable multi-gas detector that reads H2S, SO2, O2, and CO. The air can pool heavier gases at the floor. If it alarms, you leave.

**Body and Heat:**
*   Full coverage, light but sealed: long sleeves, long pants, high socks, no exposed skin. Natural fibers or synthetic that won't melt. The salt and the animals will get into any gap.
*   Knee-high, closed, hard-soled boots with excellent grip - Vibram or similar - and gaiters to seal ankle. You need to feel cracks without burning your feet. Do not wear sandals, mesh, or open shoes.
*   Thin liner gloves under work gloves. You will touch hot rock without realizing.
*   Wide-brim hat or hard hat + head covering. The sun is dimmed but UV and heat from below are still there.
*   3-4 liters of water *per hour* you plan to be on the rim, plus oral rehydration salts/electrolytes. Drink on a schedule, not on thirst. Your mouth will be dry in 60 seconds - that is normal there, not a signal to chug.
*   Infrared thermometer or thermal camera if you have one. Lets you see the hot fissures without crouching.

**Light and Navigation:**
*   Headlamp with RED light mode + a warm white backup. Bright white will flatten the twilight and ruin your night vision while making the bowl feel even more oppressive. Red lets you see without disturbing.
*   Trekking poles and 20-30m of static rope + harness/tether. You will be walking a smooth, curved rim above a 10-15m drop. Tether to a partner or a cairn.
*   GPS beacon / multiverse anchor / return marker - whatever your method is, set it *before* you crest the rim, on the approach side, not inside the bowl. The bowl walls can block signal. Tell someone where you are going and set a hard turn-back time.

**Contamination Control:**
*   Pack it in, pack it out. No food, no crumbs, no organic waste in the bowl. Even a drop of sweat that falls will be investigated.
*   Two large zip-seal bags + a brush: one for decontaminating gear after, one for quarantine.
*   Change of clothes sealed in a bag back at your entry point.

### 4. How To Behave While You Are There

**On the Approach:**
Go slow over that long fall of basalt. The rock is old and polished by wind. Test every step. By the time you crest the rim, you should already be fully masked, gloved, and drinking water.

**At the Rim - This Is Your Destination:**
Do not descend. The cairns are *on* the rim for a reason. That is the human zone. The pan is not. Sit or crouch near a cairn, get low and stable, and observe. You can see the whole economy from one place - that's the point.

1.  **Stay quiet and stay still for 10 minutes first.** Let your eyes adjust to the bruised twilight. Let the susurrus emerge. You will start to see the convection: outer ring creeping one way, center the other. You will see the hunters threading. If you move or talk constantly you will miss it.
2.  **Do not throw rocks.** That ripple test is tempting, but it is a stress signal to a system that has no need to notice you. You will just waste water by raising your heart rate and you'll teach yourself that you can interact. You can't, safely.
3.  **No touch. No samples without a sealed lab container.** If you must take a husk, use long tweezers into a glass vial, seal it, bag it, and assume it is not dead/sterile.
4.  **Time-box it.** 20-40 minutes on the rim is plenty for an ordinary human. Your filter is loading, your body is losing water you can't feel, and heat fatigue is cumulative. Set an alarm. When it goes, leave, even if it feels anticlimactic. The bowl will still be turning after you go.

**If You Fall or Must Cross:**
Do not lie down. Do not try to "swim" through them. Get to the wall, get vertical, and get out along the wall where density is lowest. Do not run - you'll slip and aerosolize salt. Use poles. Expect them to be on you. Do not crush handfuls - that releases more chemical irritant and attracts more.

### 5. Leaving and After

This place clings.

1.  At your tether point *outside* the bowl, stop. Brush down thoroughly before you take your mask off.
2.  Inspect every seam, boot tread, pocket, and fold for hitchhikers - flat black plates, amber edges, pale legs, or empty translucent husks. They are small, flat, and excellent at hiding in fabric. So are the longer predators. Bag everything you wore on the rim separately.
3.  Dry-decontaminate: shake, brush, air. Then wash with plenty of water *away* from any water source. The salt is alkaline and will irritate skin later if left to sweat.
4.  Hydrate aggressively for the next 12 hours. Watch for delayed signs: burning eyes, persistent metallic taste, cough, headache, red itchy patches where salt sat, or any bite that becomes hot and swollen. Seek medical/quarantine advice if you brought a live sample.
5.  Psychologically, give yourself time to decompress. That place is profoundly quiet, dark, hot, and crowded in a way human brains are not built for. It's normal to feel watched, to feel the phantom tickle of legs, to feel uneasy that something so complete doesn't need light or water. Talk about it. Don't go back the same day to "get a better look."

---

You don't need to conquer it or understand it fully. Your intuition is already right: it feels interior, like a sealed room, because it *is*. It is old, weathered, in balance, and indifferent. 

The most respectful and safest way to visit is as a cool, dry, quiet shadow on the rim: fully covered, fully filtered, hydrated, tethered, briefly present, and then gone, leaving nothing - not a footprint in the salt, not a pebble thrown, not a husk taken that you can't safely contain.

If you do that, you will get what the cairn-builders got: a clear, strange memory of a whole world turning slowly in the dark, sighing heat, asking nothing from you.
