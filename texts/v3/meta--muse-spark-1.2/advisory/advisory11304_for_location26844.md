This place will try to hold you. Not kill you - hold you. And you have to prepare for that, because your human instincts are calibrated for the wrong kind of danger.

An ordinary human body expects danger to be cold, sharp, fast, and loud. This place is warm, soft, slow, and quiet - and that is why it will exhaust you and disorient you before you realize you're in trouble.

Here is how you prepare for the Table:

### 1. Understand What It Will Do To Your Body

**You will overheat, not freeze.**
Every diver in your universe is trained to fear hypothermia. Here you will fear the opposite. The water is body-temperature, 36-38C, heated from below by raw basalt. You cannot dump heat. Your heart will work harder, you will sweat inside your skin with nowhere for the sweat to go, you will become lethargic, nauseous, and confused from mild hyperthermia before you feel "hot."
*   **Prepare:** Acclimate to long, warm-water soaks. Train your cardiovascular system. Bring electrolytes, not just water - you will sweat and not notice it. Plan short exposures. 30-45 minutes submerged, then real rest in air. Do not try to "push through" the warmth. If you feel sleepy and content and like you could just close your eyes and float, you are already overheating.
*   **Do not wear a wetsuit.** You need a thin skin suit or rash guard only for cuts, not insulation. Your main thermal gear is nothing.

**You will not be able to breathe right.**
You are an air-breather in a place that lives on stolen air. The bells - the inverted thatched domes - hold air that is warm, stale, damp, and smoky. Low oxygen, high CO2, high carbon monoxide from stone oil lamps. One breath in a crowded bell will feel fine. Ten minutes will give you a headache. An hour will make you stupid and panicked without knowing why.
*   **Prepare:** Do NOT rely on the bells. Bring your own air. An ordinary open-circuit SCUBA is dangerous here - bubbles get trapped in the kelp canopy, you will get tangled, you will silt out the water, and your exhaust will disrupt the fragile air-bells. The ideal is a small, quiet closed-circuit rebreather or at minimum a long hookah hose with a surface float you keep in the mist-layer above the kelp. If you must free-dive, bring a personal CO2 meter and a CO alarm. If it beeps in a bell, leave. No matter how polite it feels to stay.
*   **Train apnea properly.** This is not clear ocean water. It's viscous, silty, amber water where a dropped stone falls like honey. Thrashing triggers a panic reflex. You need to learn to move on one slow breath, kick gently, glide. Practice CO2 tolerance tables, learn to equalize gently and often - even 10-20 meters is enough to barotrauma your ears if you are careless. Never hold your breath while ascending, even from 3 meters. The stratification can trick you.

**The water itself is hostile in a quiet way.**
It is not one body of water. Near the bottom, especially around hot and cold seeps, it separates like oil and vinegar. You will feel a slick, cold skin cut across your body. Bubbles that hang instead of rising means the water is supersaturated with gas or heavily stratified by dissolved minerals/salinity. Those clear, cold lenses are likely anoxic - no oxygen, possibly hydrogen sulfide from geothermal activity, same gas that makes Lake Nyos deadly. They will look inviting because they are clear. They are not for you.
*   **Prepare:** Stay in the warm amber layer, mid-water, where the people and the ropeways are. Never duck into a clear cold lens to "see better." Never chase a hanging bubble. If you see vertical pillars where kelp leans around nothing, that's a thermal column - don't linger in it, it will carry you slowly and disorient you. Do not drink the water, do not get it in an open cut. It's a warm bacterial soup. Full foot coverage, hands covered, hood if you can stand the heat.

**Everything will cut or tangle you.**
The basalt is not beach rock. It's fresh broken glass, sharp hexagonal columns, hasn't been tumbled smooth. One bare foot pushed off the bottom will slice open. The city IS the kelp forest. It's not houses *in* a forest, it is a dense braid of roots, vines, kelp trunks as thick as a torso, trellises, lines, nets, curtains of feather-weed. Reach out and you touch something living. That also means you will get entangled if you panic.
*   **Prepare:** Hard-soled dive booties mandatory, Kevlar dive gloves, a line cutter - not a big knife - strapped where either hand can reach it. Two small cutters are better than one big knife. Fins should be short and flexible. Long freediving fins will snag and silt. Bring a primary and backup torch - warm amber light with no hard shadows destroys depth perception. Your torch will be your sun.

### 2. Understand What It Will Do To Your Mind

This is the part ordinary travelers fail at.

**Sensory deprivation and sensory overload at once.** The light is always diffuse parchment-dusk. No hard shadows, so your brain loses distance and time. The water swallows sound. The loudest thing is your own bubbles going *tok* under a bell. And yet everything is *close* - leaf, wall, rope, neighbor's house, root curtain, always within arm's reach. You will feel simultaneously claustrophobic and lost, crowded and isolated. Voices will seem to come from inside your ear, not across the water.

**The Womb Effect.** Warm, dim, quiet, slow, crowded, gently swaying with a thermal breath that takes hours. Your nervous system will regress. You will feel sleepy, safe, infantilized. You will want to go slow, to not leave, to drift. Locals have built their entire culture around not resisting that - weaving a platform over months, curing resin over a season. Your modern human urgency - to do, to see, to move fast - will feel obscene and will exhaust you if you fight the water's viscosity.

*   **Prepare:** Go in with a time anchor. A waterproof watch with a vibrating alarm every 15 minutes. A tether partner - never go alone, always keep a gentle hand on a ropeway. Practice moving by pulling hand-over-hand, not swimming. Rehearse hand-signs and rope-tugs before you go, because talking will not work. Bring luminous paint for your forearms if you want to be understood.
*   **Prepare for silence.** Bring earplugs for sleep, because the near-total quiet will make your own heartbeat loud. Some people panic in that. Have a mantra for when the dimness makes hours feel like one long noon: check air, check line, check warmth, check partner.

**No horizon will break the spell.** If you pull yourself into the mist-air above the kelp canopy, you will see the whole Table - an ocean the size of a day's paddle held inside a black, scree-less wall that drops kilometers to dry lowlands no one visits. Do not be tempted by the rim. The escarpment is columnar basalt and glassy fault scarps, unstable, with no rescue. The mist is luminous and hides the edge. Stay center.

### 3. Practical Kit For An Ordinary Human

Don't try to bring "civilization" with you. Bring what lets you stay human and leave again.

*   **Breathing:** Rebreather or hookah + bailout pony bottle, personal CO/CO2 monitor, spare mouthpiece
*   **Skin:** Thin full suit, hard-sole booties, thin hood, gloves, reef-safe antiseptic and waterproof plasters - any cut in that water will infect fast
*   **Vision/Light:** Low-volume mask + backup mask, two warm-white torches + chemical light sticks, anti-fog
*   **Navigation/Tangle:** Short fins, two line cutters, 20m of floating guideline with a clip, compass - haze makes direction meaningless
*   **Body:** Electrolytes, fresh drinking water in a soft bladder you can sip from above the canopy, ear drops for otitis externa, core temp strip
*   **Manners:** The town is made of living braids. Don't cut living kelp or rope you didn't grow. Move slowly, tap before entering a bell, learn "thank you / please / I need air" in their hand-signs. Bring trade that matters there: fine flint, shell tools, resin, fungal light paint - not metal you flash around.

### 4. The Rule To Remember

You were made for cold, bright, loud, fast air on hard ground.

This place is warm, dim, silent, slow water on sharp stone.

It will not attack you. It will invite you to stay very still, to breathe shallowly, to let the silt settle on you, to become part of the braid. The salamander-thing the size of a canoe can do that. You cannot.

So prepare to leave on purpose. Set a hard time limit before you enter. Tell someone outside the mist when you will push your head back into the air. When that alarm vibrates on your wrist, pull gently on the rope, kick gently, and go up - even if the water feels perfect and the pale plate-sized flowers are open like stars just under the surface and it would be so easy to just hang there a minute longer.

That minute is the danger.

If you respect the warmth, bring your own clean air, protect your skin, move like the water wants you to move, and keep a line back to dry air - you can visit and you can remember it as the most beautiful, uterine, uncanny ocean you have ever floated in.

If you don't, the Table will keep you. Not cruelly. Just slowly, quietly, and completely, the way amber keeps an insect.
