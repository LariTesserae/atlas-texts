For an ordinary human, this is not a hike. It's an immersion. You are not going *into* a place, you are going *inside* an organism that has learned to use buildings as organs. It is not hostile. It is not predatory. It is worse for a baseline human - it is *accommodating*. It will make a place for you if you stand still long enough.

This is how you prepare to visit the Pan and come back human.

### 1. Understand What It Will Do To You

Before you pack anything, understand the physics of the place so you don't misread it:

**It's Low and Closed.** That shallow pan is a bowl with a lip. Warm, wet air is heavier than the air outside. It pools there and doesn't exchange. There is no wind. Mist, steam, pollen, spores, and your own exhaled breath hang in place. Sound doesn't leave. Heat doesn't leave. You will be in a terrarium the size of a city block that has been running for decades on its own heat and water cycle. It feels like being inside a breathing lung.

**2.9 is Thin.** You noted the number yourself. Whether you call it Hume level, narrative pressure, or just local reality - this place is *low*. Things that are settled elsewhere are soft here. Form is negotiable. The plants have learned to grow in right angles and copy fuse boxes because at 2.9, *intention* is a growth nutrient. In there, if you believe a vine is a pipe, for a little while, it will be. Don't bring strong expectations, don't bring strong desires, and absolutely don't bring rituals, reality-bending tech, or active anomalies with you. An ordinary human is actually safer here than a mage or a reality-bender, because you are dense and boring. Stay that way.

**It Weeps, It Doesn't Flow.** The water isn't a river or a swamp. It's interstitial. It's *on* everything and *just under* everything. Every surface condenses. Every leaf drips. The mat under your feet is a saturated sponge with water a hand's breadth below. That means three things: You will never be dry, you will never be sure where the solid ground is, and everything electrical is still wet and still live.

### 2. Your Body Is The Weak Point

You will overheat before you realize you're hot. Incubator-warm + 95-100% humidity means your sweat does not evaporate. You bead and drip like the leaves, but you don't cool.

**Pre-conditioning:**
* Be fully hydrated for 48 hours before. Bring electrolytes, not just water. You will lose salt fast.
* No open wounds, no eczema flare-ups, no fresh tattoos. Everything weeps there, including any cut you bring. The tea-colored water under the mat is warm, tannic, bacterial, and full of dissolved plant compounds. It will find any break in your skin.
* Shave or trim body hair. Hair holds spores and gives runners something to follow.
* Take an antihistamine and discuss a prophylactic antifungal with a doctor if you have access to one. This is a fungal paradise. You will inhale spores even through a mask.
* Do not go if you are immunocompromised, asthmatic, or claustrophobic. The air is heavy, the space is packed, and the absence of animal life should tell you the air is not kindly to animal lungs long-term.

**What it feels like, minute by minute:** Within 60 seconds your shirt will be plastered to you. Your glasses, if you wear them, will fog and never clear. Your breath will be visible as little white puffs even though you're sweating. Within 5 minutes your skin will feel pruny. Within 15 minutes, if you stand still, you will feel the place *holding* you - fronds settling on your shoulders, a vine lightly touching your calf and not moving away. It's not grabbing you. It's measuring you as a surface. That feeling is psychologically much harder than fear.

### 3. Kit - What To Wear and Carry

Think caver + lab tech, not hiker.

**Skin and Lungs:**
* **P100 / FFP3 respirator with organic vapor relief, plus sealed goggles.** Not a paper mask. You need protection from spores, pollen clouds, steam, and aerosolized leaf compounds. The mist that never becomes fog is full of particulates. Bring spare filters in a dry bag.
* **Light, long, slick coverage.** A cheap, disposable Tyvek coverall or a lightweight waterproof rain suit is better than "breathable" hiking gear. Breathable will just saturate. You want a smooth surface that vines slide off and that you can strip off and burn later. Under it, thin synthetic base layer. No cotton. Cotton stays wet and rots.
* **Rubber boots, knee-high, with aggressive tread.** Not hiking boots - they will be waterlogged in 10 steps and never dry. You need to be able to stand in 2 inches of sighed-up brown water. Bring gaiters to seal the top.
* **Nitrile gloves, long cuff, two pairs.** You will want to touch things. Don't with bare skin. The sap that oils the flywheel and the water that drips on the bearings are not water.
* **Wide-brim hat or hard hat.** Drip is constant. Having it hit bare head and neck for an hour is maddening and disorienting.

**Movement and Safety:**
* **Snowshoe principle:** The mat is a trampoline. Your weight is distributed on roots and moss, with water underneath. If you stand on one foot, you will punch through. Bring trekking poles or a single staff to spread weight, test depth, and push vegetation aside without using your hands. If you go with a partner, bring a 10-meter tether line between you.
* **Cord and markers.** GPS and compass will be sluggish and confused in a metal-rich, living bowl. The interior is bigger-feeling than it is. Bring 30m of bright, non-biodegradable cord to tie at the lip where you entered. Mark it. And bring 10-15 retroreflective or bright trail clips. The light is white and shafted but directionless once you're inside the mass - you can get turned around in 6 feet.
* **Light.** Even though it's daylight, bring a small headlamp. The mass creates deep pockets of green shadow. You need to see rust under moss before you step on it.
* **No loose straps, no dangling hair, no jewelry.** Anything that hangs will be found by a runner in 20 minutes. Tuck everything tight.
* **A dry bag with:** 1L of *your* water + electrolytes, high-calorie, sealed food you don't need to chew much, a personal locator beacon or whistle, and a knife with a blunt tip for cutting yourself free if a vine or root snags.

**What NOT to bring:** Heavy electronics you can't afford to lose to condensation. Anything with a strong scent. Anything you consider "lucky" or "anchored" - at 2.9, a significant object can become a template the place copies.

### 4. How To Move Inside

This is the core protocol. Wandering travelers die here by treating it like a forest.

**1. Timebox Ruthlessly.** Plan for 20-40 minutes maximum inside the lip. Set a vibrating timer on your wrist. You will lose time perception because drip, flywheel *huh...huh...*, wood creak, and vine-hum create a trance rhythm. An outside anchor person is non-negotiable for an ordinary human. Someone *stays on the lip* in dry air, watches the cord, and pulls you out if you don't respond.

**2. Never Stand Still.** Keep slow, deliberate motion. Shift your weight every 30 seconds. Roll your shoulders. Brush off settling tendrils. If you must stop to look at the pump engine or the lily-tank, do not lean on anything. The mass's politeness is literal - it will grow around stillness. Sitting to watch a flower open over hours is how you become architecture.

**3. Walk Like You Are Being Let Through.** Turn sideways. Let vines indent around you. Don't snap, rip, or cut unless necessary. Tearing the mass open releases a burst of yeasty, warm air and spores, and the mat will weep more aggressively at the wound. If you must make room, push gently and wait. It *does* accept you, slowly.

**4. Do Not Interfere With The Machines.** This is the most important rule. The flywheel turning once per 20 seconds, the hissing steam pipe, the amber bulbs, the water turning over in the settling tank - they are not ruins. They are organs. The plant keeps the engine from seizing and the engine moves water for the plant. Do not touch the flywheel, do not block the drip onto the bearing, do not try to turn off the bulbs, do not drink from the lily pads. They are electrified, they are scalding, and interrupting them breaks a balance that has held for a very long time. Watch from a foot away.

**5. Talk Little, Observe Much.** Sound folds over itself. If you panic and shout, the mass gives it back leaf-muffled, which is more disorienting. Use hand signals with your anchor. Breathe through your nose. The visible breath you see is condensation - your lungs are adding moisture to already saturated air. Slow, shallow breathing reduces spore intake and fogging.

**6. The Exit Is The Rim.** No matter how cathedral-like the interior feels, the footprint is hamlet-scale. Any direction is out within 10 minutes. If you feel dizzy, nauseous, too hot, or if your goggles are completely beaded and you can't clear them, follow your cord back to the lip and *step up*. The lip is only shoulder-high, but it is a climate boundary. Two steps up and the air will suddenly be dry and moving again. Sit there. Do not look back down immediately.

### 5. After You Leave - Decontamination

You are not done when you are out.

* Strip the coverall, gloves, boot covers *outside* the pan, bag them. Do not bring them into your ship/tent/home. The moss that pulses with bulb-light will continue to do so on your sleeve.
* Shake and check your body thoroughly - hairline, behind ears, between toes, waistband. Look for: tiny green root hairs stuck to skin, flat seeds, leeches you didn't feel, or a faint rectangular imprint where a leaf tried to become a fuse box on your forearm.
* Rinse with clean water and soap, irrigate eyes and nose. Wash your throat with gargle.
* Dry everything completely. The weeping spreads through damp fabric.
* Quarantine your gear for 48 hours in dry, bright, moving air. If anything continues to weep, hum, or grow, leave it.
* Monitor yourself for 10-14 days: persistent cough, skin rash that is geometrically rectangular, fever, or dreams of slow humming and green light. That's not superstition - that's fungal/bacterial colonization. Seek medical help and tell them you were in a hot, saturated, stagnant biofilm environment with industrial contaminants.

### 6. The Intuition You Asked For

If you go, you will not feel like an explorer. You will feel like a warm object that has been briefly placed inside a body that is already complete without you. It is loud with dripping and creaking and hissing, but it is empty of the gossip of animals, so the loudness feels like a machine talking to itself.

It doesn't want you. It doesn't reject you. It will simply *include* you for as long as you remain available to be included. Leaves will turn toward your heat as if you were a little steam pipe. The mat will remember your handprint longer than you will.

An ordinary human can visit this, pay respect, and leave. But you must be ordinary on purpose: covered, filtered, timed, tethered, and unwilling to be a surface.

Go light, go brief, go with someone who will not follow you in when you forget to come out.

If you feel tendrils settling on your back and you start to find it *comfortable* - you have already stayed too long.
