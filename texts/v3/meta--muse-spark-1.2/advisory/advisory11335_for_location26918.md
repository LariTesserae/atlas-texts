You should not prepare for this place like an expedition. Prepare like you are going to be a guest in someone's house, where the house is also a body, and you will be inside it.

This is not a ruin to explore. It is not a cave to survive. It is a living room that happens to be forty strides wide and a hundred strides long and sunk just below the surface. It is warm, wet, crowded, loud, and completely full. Nothing there is hostile to you - but everything there is *close* to you, and it does not stop being close.

If you go down the brick stair as an ordinary human, this is how you get ready.

### 1. Understand What It Will Do To Your Body First

Your body will notice this place before your mind does.

**It is warm and wet and it will not dry you.** The air is held at that point where brick sweats. Not steaming like a bathhouse, not cold like a cellar - a steady, mammalian warmth, about body temperature, at 85-95% humidity. The walls bead constantly. Water falls on you from nowhere - *tik* on your scalp, on your shoulders, on the back of your neck. Your clothes will be damp within ten minutes and stay damp. Your sweat will not evaporate well. You will feel slightly breathless at first, not from bad air but from close, smoke-sweet, guano-and-cooking air that has been breathed many times.

Prepare for that:
* Wear almost nothing that needs to stay dry. Cotton will cling and stay cold. Linen, thin wool, or modern synthetics that wick and still insulate when damp are better. But best is what they wear: simple, loose, soft, already lived-in. No waterproof shell - you will just sweat inside it.
* Wear soft, thin-soled shoes or go barefoot if your feet can handle it. The floor is herringbone brick worn into shallow bowls, patched with tile, slick with condensation and moss near the pillars. Hard boots will slip, clack loudly, and chew the soft brick. You want grip and quiet. Bare feet are fluent there; everyone knows where to step.
* Tie your hair back and keep it covered if you can. Dust, smoke, falling grit from the vault, pigeon down, and constant drips will land on you.
* If you have asthma, mold allergies, bird-droppings sensitivity, or are very sensitive to fungal spores - take this seriously. The air is full of warm particulates: woodsmoke from a hundred tiny fires that never go out, pigeon feather dust, dry moss spores, the ammonia of guano high in the vault. It is not toxic for a healthy visitor for a day, but it will irritate your nose, throat and eyes. Bring a simple cloth to pull over your nose if you need a break, but don't walk in wearing a respirator - you will alienate everyone and you will miss the point. If you know your lungs are reactive, make this a short visit and plan to breathe through your nose, slowly.
* Bring water, but do not plan to drink constantly. You will not dehydrate as fast as you think because you aren't losing water efficiently. Sip. And do not drink from the central well without being explicitly offered - it is six feet across, fenced in low brick, and it is the only water that matters. It is not yours to take.

**It is small and you will be touched.** The stairwell is exactly shoulders-wide, walls burnished glossy by generations of palms. Inside, you cannot extend your arms without hitting a ladder, a hammock, a hanging gourd net, a sleeping dog, a jar stack, a child. Ladders lean at every angle. Hammocks sag across your path. Laundry lines cross your face. This is not rudeness - this is how space is used when no space is wasted.

If you are claustrophobic, if you need a bubble of personal space to feel safe, rehearse this before you go: stand in a small, warm bathroom with a friend and let your shoulders brush. Practice moving sideways. Practice saying "excuse me" with your body instead of your words - a slight turn, a hand lightly on a pillar to steady yourself, waiting for someone else to climb before you do. You will need to crouch, stoop, and climb. The platforms are two and three stories up a single pillar, lashed with rope and rawhide. They creak. They hold.

Travel light. A big pack makes you twice as wide and you will knock things over. One small soft bag you can hold against your chest on the stairs. Everything else - keep your hands free.

### 2. Understand What It Will Do To Your Senses

**Light:** You will be able to see, but not as you expect. Four or five oculi throw hard, solid columns of daylight down through smoke and insects. Where the light lands, brick glows amber and dust turns like snow. Everywhere else is a soft, even gloom lit by small oil lamps that never go out. Your eyes will adjust after 5-10 minutes. Do not bring a headlamp or bright flashlight - you will blind people whose homes are on platforms in the gloom, and you will wash out the way the whole hamlet tracks time by following the light as it crawls across the floor. If you need light, bring a very dim, warm, shielded lamp and keep it pointed down. Better to just let your eyes adapt.

**Sound:** This will be the strangest part. The vault is a perfect echo chamber made of damp brick. It catches everything and holds it - loom clack, baby crying, pot scraping, pigeon cooing, rope creaking, frogs in the well, feet on ladders - and layers it into a single, loud, market-harbor murmur. And because it is so reverberant and so damp, sound arrives *wrong*. A drip you see fall makes its *ping* half a breath late. A voice from the far end seems to come from right behind your shoulder. The warm brick seems to exhale.

Your brain will try to locate sounds and fail. That is normal there. Don't chase every noise. Don't startle. Trust your eyes more than your ears for the first hour. If you feel dizzy or doubled, plant a hand on a pillar - the brick is warm, solid, real - and watch the light column for a minute. It will pass. Do not wear earplugs. You need to be part of the sound, not sealed off from it. If you are autistic or highly sensitive to auditory overwhelm, plan for a very short stay and give yourself permission to sit at the edge near the stairs where it is slightly quieter.

**Smell and Taste:** Damp lime plaster, warm brick, woodsmoke, frying oil, human bodies living close, onions, pigeon droppings high up, the green sharpness of ferns and moss, and underneath it all, water on stone. You will taste it on your lips. Don't wear perfume, strong deodorant, or scented lotion. You will be smelled before you are seen.

**Touch:** Everything is burnished, rounded, soft-cornered. The plaster is glossy from hands. The bricks at the base are worn into bowls. The mortar has receded and each brick stands out like a rib. You will want to touch it. Do. But touch lightly, with respect. This is a place where every brick has been touched on purpose for a hundred years.

### 3. Understand What It Will Do To Your Social Self

There is no empty space and no wasted thing. There is no privacy as you know it. People sleep, cook, work, argue, and pray within arm's reach of each other, in niches, on platforms, in hammocks. If you go looking for a corner to be alone, you will not find one. If you stand at the well and stare, you will be stared at right back, not unkindly - you are just another body in the room.

Prepare to be a good guest in a dense house:

**Move like they move.** When the light moves, work moves. Follow it. Don't stand in a sunbeam if someone is working there. Don't block a ladder - someone is always about to use it. Step where feet have already worn the brick. Walk slowly. The boy with kindling on his back knows his rungs blindfolded; you do not.

**Do not waste.** Water is waited for, never forced. It comes as seep and sweat and rain through the oculi. Don't pour it out. Don't let a tap run. If a child holds out cupped hands to catch a drip, don't knock her arm. If you are offered food - and you may be offered a bowl of glossy black beetles, or a handful of pigeon eggs, or a stew with lichen in it - accept graciously or decline graciously, but do not show disgust. That food is tended and shared. What flies in is allowed to stay because it eats what would otherwise overrun them. That is the rule.

**Bring the right gifts, or bring nothing.** They have almost no metal. A few green copper nails, stone and bone knives. Everything is cord and wood and fired clay and hide. Do not bring plastic trinkets, or steel tools as a show of superiority. If you want to bring something useful: good cordage, rawhide lacing, a small jar of good lamp oil, hardwood that is already well-seasoned, or plain, undyed cloth. Offer it to an elder near the well, not by tossing it on a platform. And understand you may be politely refused - they have what they need.

**Ask with your presence before your words.** You don't need to speak their exact language to be understood, but learn a few gestures before you go: hand to chest for thank you, palms up for "may I?", a slight bow of head for "I see you." Do not photograph, record, or take without asking. Do not touch a shrine in a niche, a pot of chives on a ledge, or a vine trained along an arch. Those are tended, precious, deliberate - a garden in a place with no soil.

**Expect to be offered help and to need it.** You will be steadied on a ladder. A woman with a stick will move her beetle bowl so you don't step on it. Someone will point where to put your feet. Let them. Say thank you by being easy to help.

### 4. A Practical Kit For An Ordinary Human

Keep it to what fits in a small cloth bag against your chest:

* Soft, light layers you don't mind getting damp and slightly smoky. Long sleeves and long pants in breathable fabric - they protect from scrapes on brick and from insects.
* Soft slip-on shoes with good grip, or foot wraps. Clean socks if you wear shoes - you will take them off on platforms.
* A small cloth for your nose/mouth, and a bandana for hair.
* A small, warm, dim lantern or candle in a jar - not a bright LED.
* A shallow bowl or cup of your own, clay or wood if you have it. To accept water or food without making someone find a vessel for you.
* A little cord, a little oil, as a potential gift - not required.
* No large pack, no trekking poles, no metal that clanks, no speakers, no strong chemicals.

Before you go, leave behind: expectations of quiet, expectations of being an observer, any idea that you are there to "discover" them. You are not.

### 5. How To Be While You Are There

Go down slowly. Shoulders will brush. Palms will find the glossy plaster on their own. At the bottom, stop at the edge, don't stride to the middle. Let your eyes adjust. Let the sound settle around you.

Find a low, out-of-the-way place to sit - on the edge of a runnel, against a pillar base where moss grows - and just sit for a while. Watch the light move. Watch the dust in it. Watch the geckoes. Listen to the drips come late. Feel the brick sweat under your hand.

When you move, move with the light. Speak quietly - the vault will do the amplifying for you. If you feel overwhelmed by the closeness or the doubled sound, return to the stairwell for a minute where it is narrower and simpler. Breathe.

When you leave, leave the same way: slowly, single file, hands lightly touching the walls that have been touched for generations. Don't take a brick, a feather, a frog. The place has found the shape it wants to keep. Your job is to leave it exactly that way.

You will come back up damp, smelling of smoke and wet lime, ears ringing a little from the beautiful, impossible loudness, with the feeling that you have been held very close for a little while just below the skin of the world. That is what it is like. Prepare to be held, and to be gracious about it.
