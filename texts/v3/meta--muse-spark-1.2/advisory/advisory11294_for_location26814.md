This is not an ocean you visit. It's an ocean you *enter* — like you enter a building, or a body. Prepare for it like a cave dive inside a cathedral that someone filled with winter.

An ordinary human is not built for this place. You are warm, air-breathing, pressure-sensitive, and psychologically calibrated for a horizon with a sky. None of that works here. Your multiverse ability gets you to the shaft door, nothing more. Everything after that is physics, cold, and scale.

Here is how you prepare:

### 1. Understand What It Actually Is

Do not think "sea." Think: **flooded industrial architecture at geographic scale.**

* There is no surface. There is a *ceiling.* A steel sky kilometers across, holding up rock. There is no beach, no shallows, no bottom you can stand on. There are only walls, tiers, and drop-offs measured in counties.
* There is no sun. There is work-light. Endless sodium-white flood banks that burn on a slow day/night cycle, humming, never blinking. It is bright enough to see for half a kilometer in water that has been filtered to drinking-water clarity. That clarity will betray you — distance collapses, scale collapses, vertigo is constant.
* There is no weather as you know it. There is *circulation.* The pumps making that water turn over create true currents the width of rivers, thermoclines that slide past you like cold fronts, upwellings under the lamps. The whole volume breathes every ~12 seconds with a subsonic throb you feel in your sternum before you hear it.
* It is young. The concrete is still sharp. Formwork grain, bright cut rebar, proud bolt heads, stenciled sector numbers 40 feet high. It has not been softened by time. It will cut open a drysuit, snag a line, and scrape you if you touch it.
* It is alive despite itself. Not empty. Schools of thousands of pale char and filter-feeders that turn as one wall of silver, clouds of amphipods around every light like moths, anemones carpeting grates, blind white crabs and arm-long isopods on the lower tiers. And something large that lives in the void and does not come close. This is a real food web now. You are not at the top of it and you are not part of it.

If you go expecting beauty, you will get it, but not the kind that comforts. This is the industrial sublime — awe mixed with the correct feeling that the machine does not know you are there and will not stop for you.

### 2. Your Body Will Fail First. Prepare It.

You are a 37C mammal. This water is 8-9C, constantly moving, and infinitely heat-sinking.

**Cold is the primary killer, not drowning.**

* You need a **drysuit, not a wetsuit.** A good trilaminate or crushed neoprene drysuit with heavy undergarments - think 400g thinsulate or two layers of wool. With bare skin, you lose dexterity in 5 minutes and rational thought in 15. With a 7mm wetsuit you still get cold in 30 minutes. You will want to stay for hours. The water will not let you.
* Triple seal: hood, dry gloves with liners, dry socks. Your face will be the only exposed skin and it will ache constantly. Bring a full-face mask if you can — it keeps your face warm and lets you talk and breathe through your nose.
* Acclimatize before you go: Take cold showers, do pool sessions in cold water. Learn what cold-shock gasp reflex feels like in a safe place so it doesn't kill you when you slip off the access gantry.

**Pressure is the second killer, and it is absolute.**

Every 10 meters = 1 atmosphere. The basin is *kilometers* deep. At 100m you are at 11 atmospheres. At 1km you are at 101 atmospheres — nothing made of flesh and air survives that without a pressure hull.

You have two options, and only two:

**Option A: Stay Shallow - The Human Way.** Treat this as an *overhead, cold-water cave dive.* Stay in the top 0-40 meters below the ceiling. This is where the light is, the life is, the entry points are. For this you need normal technical diving training: cold-water, overhead, drysuit certification. Even at 30m, nitrogen narcosis and decompression obligation are real.

**Option B: Go Deep - The Machine Way.** If you want to see the terraces and towers falling away below, do not swim. Bring a vehicle. A small hard-shell submersible, an atmospheric diving suit, or at minimum a 1-atm ROV you operate from the shallows. An ordinary human in scuba who decides to "just drop down a little further to see the bottom" will die of pressure, cold, or decompression sickness before anyone notices. The water is so clear you will think the bottom is 50m away when it's 800m.

**Breathing:**

* Do NOT plan on open-circuit air tanks. Your gas consumption will be enormous from cold and stress, and you need hours. A **closed-circuit rebreather with trimix/helium diluent** is the correct tool. It keeps your gas warm, silent so you can hear the place, and extends time. If you must use open-circuit, bring doubles plus at least two sling bottles, and calculate for 2x your normal SAC rate.
* Redundancy. Redundancy. Redundancy. Two independent regulators, two computers, two cutting tools, two lights even though the place is lit.
* You must plan decompression even for shallow dives because you will be cold and working. Bring a reel with a deco bar or plan to hang under the ceiling catwalk. The cold will make decompression longer and more dangerous — afterdrop will hit you hard when you get out.

Train as if you are doing a Great Lakes wreck dive inside a mine. If you have never done an overhead dive, a night dive, or dived under ice, do those first. This place is all three at once.

### 3. How To Move, See, and Not Get Lost

This is a navigational nightmare.

* **There is no natural reference.** No sun angle, no wave direction, no bottom. Up is toward the lights/ceiling, down is toward darkness. But with endless repeating towers and featureless water between them, you will lose direction in 60 seconds without a line. The schools of fish will also disorient you — they become the only moving reference.
* **Bring a continuous guideline.** Like a cave diver. Reel from your entry shaft and tie off as you go. Do not trust memory. Do not trust "I'll just follow the lights back" — they all look identical.
* **Bring a DPV / scooter.** The void between structures is the dominant feature. Swimming it wastes gas, gets you cold, and exposes you to the gyre currents. A small diver propulsion vehicle lets you cross the empty kilometers and hold position in current.
* **Your own lights matter.** Yes, the basin is lit. But the work lights are flat, shadowless, and sodium-yellow. They wash out contrast and hide current, monofilament line, and rebar stubs. Bring a narrow-beam primary and a wide video light — they will cut through particulate snow and show you texture and true color. The water is full of silver bubble sheets from aeration manifolds; they will blind you if you shine light into them.
* **Weighting:** This is almost certainly fresh water, not salt. You need less lead than in the ocean. Test your buoyancy in the shallows. With a drysuit and heavy undergarments you will need a lot of weight to get down, and that weight will try to kill you if your suit floods. Have ditchable weight.
* **Stay away from intakes.** Those cathedral-sized pumps are still breathing. Intake grates are carpeted in mussels but the suction underneath is real. You will feel the pull from meters away as a smooth, silent acceleration. If your guideline goes taut toward a grate, cut and leave it. Do not try to be stronger than a machine that moves a sea.

### 4. How To Listen and Protect Your Head

This place is loud in a way land humans don't understand.

* Low-frequency pump throb every 12 seconds will travel through your bones and interfere with your breathing rhythm. Electrolysis fizz, gantry trolleys squealing on rails kilometers above you, concrete popping, corrugated siding thrumming in the current. It is never silent.
* **Ear protection is useless underwater, but psychological protection is not.** Bring music or silence practice. Many first-time visitors panic because they can't isolate a single sound — it's all around, inside your head.
* Use through-water comms if you have a buddy, which you must. Hand signals are hard with dry gloves. A hard-wired comms system in a full-face mask, or at minimum a slate and pre-agreed line tugs.
* Never dive alone here. If you must solo, you are doing it wrong. The scale is too large for self-rescue to mean anything. A buddy can see your bubbles, your light, and pull you out of a current.

### 5. How To Prepare Your Mind

This is the part most travelers skip, and it's where the place breaks people.

* **It will make you feel impossibly small and impossibly exposed.** You will hover mid-water and see two "mountains" that are actually drowned factories 400m away, and between them nothing but lit void. No bottom, no surface, just haze and columns of light full of snow. Your primate brain will scream that you are falling even though you are neutrally buoyant. This is normal. Practice hovering in blue water where you can't see the bottom before you go.
* **It is both agoraphobic and claustrophobic at once.** Agoraphobic because the void is so huge you cannot see an end; claustrophobic because you know there is a lid — a roof on the world — and you cannot get to air without a long vertical swim and decompression.
* **The loneliness is industrial, not natural.** A natural ocean feels ancient and indifferent. This feels *abandoned.* Stenciled sector numbers, empty gantries still running loops with nothing on them, corridors where workers were supposed to walk and now only fish do. It triggers a specific grief. Bring an anchor object — something warm, human-scaled, textured. Don't lose time staring.
* **Limit your first visit to 45-60 minutes in-water, max.** You will want to stay. The cold and narcosis will make you feel euphoric and clear-headed when you are not. Set a hard time, a hard gas pressure, a hard turn pressure and obey them.
* **After you leave, you will still hear the throb for hours.** You may experience afterdrop — severe shivering 10-20 minutes after exiting. Have hot sugary drinks, dry clothes, and a warm quiet place. Don't plan to multiverse jump again immediately while cold and narc'd.

### 6. Practical Kit List For An Ordinary Human

If you entered from an access shaft with only what you can carry:

**Worn:**
- Drysuit + heavy undergarments, hood, dry gloves, dry socks
- Full-face mask with comms or high-quality low-volume mask + backup
- Twinset or rebreather with bailout, two computers, compass
- Primary canister light, two backup lights, strobe
- Two cutting devices, trauma shears
- Reel with 500m+ guideline, spool, cookies/arrows
- DPV with backup battery
- Surface marker buoy you can deploy underwater as a vertical reference

**Carried:**
- Thermal jug of sweet tea/coffee, high-calorie bars, spare dry clothes in a waterproof bag left at the shaft
- Emergency shelter/blanket, whistle, PLB that works through water or at least at the surface shaft
- Slate, pencil, laminated map if you have one of the sector numbers
- Extra batteries, extra gloves

**Vehicle/Exit:**
- A strong line to rappel/climb the shaft — those access shafts are vertical kilometers. You need ascenders, harness, not just fins.
- A return beacon you can activate with numb hands. Do not assume your multiverse ability will work perfectly when you are hypothermic and narced. Have a physical, button-press backup.

**Rules:**

1. Never enter without a fixed line to the exit you can follow blind.
2. Never go below your planned depth.
3. Never approach a moving intake or a bubble curtain you can't see through.
4. Never touch with bare hands — biofilm is slick and concrete is abrasive, and the cold will weld your skin to metal.
5. Take nothing, leave only bubbles. The biofilm and kelp fringe is the only thing softening that hard world; don't scrape it.

### What It Feels Like When You're Doing It Right

You drop from the shaft on your line, the roar of surface replaced by sudden, enveloping silence and then the throb — *wum... wum... wum* — in your chest. The water hits your face like needles even through the hood. You vent your suit, find neutral, and let the gyre push you gently sideways while you hang, scooter in hand, light off, just listening.

Columns of work-light cut the cold clear water and you can see particulate snow turning slowly in them. Far below, terraces step down into blue-dark until they vanish, and the faint grey outlines of drowned towers stand like a ruined skyline. A school of char comes from nowhere, a single organism of a thousand fish, parts around you with a rush you feel more than hear, and is gone.

You are cold, you are small, you are suspended in a machine that made its own sea and then forgot why. It is beautiful in a way that does not want you to be there.

If you prepared right, you can stay long enough to feel that without becoming part of it.

Go shallow. Stay warm. Stay tethered. Respect the pumps. Come back up with time and gas to spare, and let the place keep its strange, lit, humming self without you for a while longer.
