This place is not dangerous the way a desert or a predator is dangerous. It's dangerous the way an ocean is dangerous - not hostile, just completely indifferent to whether a human body can handle being wet, loud, crowded, and lost forever.

You are not visiting a city *in* a swamp. You are visiting a city that *is* a swamp that learned how to build itself. It will not give you dry ground, quiet, solitude, or stillness. If you fight it on any of those, you will lose. Preparation is about learning to be wet, loud, and moving.

Here is how an ordinary, baseline human stays alive and sane there:

### 1. Understand What You're Walking Into

**There is no Outside.** The basin is continent-sized and completely urbanized to past the horizon. Don't plan to hike out. Don't plan to find a forest, a hill, or a quiet edge to camp on. The only "nature" is the weather the city makes for itself and the animal mass it feeds.

**There is no Dry.** The water table is the surface. The air is at 95-100% humidity and warm. Water sweats out of walls, condenses on your skin, beads on metal before it rains. Then it *does* rain - short, hard, warm, often - and the plazas are designed to hold 3 inches of standing water forever. You will be wet within 10 minutes of arrival and you will stay wet until you leave. Stop trying to stay dry. Learn to *manage* being wet.

**There is no Quiet and There is no Dark.** Sound is a physical pressure - water in culverts the size of subway tunnels, millions of birds and insects, voices, and the low bone-thrumming of the city growing itself. At the same time, light is piped everywhere. Even a service trench 50 feet down has the flat white light of overcast noon. Your circadian rhythm will break in 48 hours if you don't intervene.

**There is no Stable Map.** This city churns as its steady state. Walls flow like wax to re-grade a plaza overnight. A market walks to another floor on temporary legs. A street becomes impassable for 20 minutes because a migratory tide of chitin is moving through, then it is clean again. Maps are fiction within hours. Navigation is real-time only.

**There is a Little Wrongness.** That puddle that's ankle-deep but your stick goes down two feet. The shadow with no object. The water that runs uphill for a few feet before it remembers gravity. It's low-level spatial unreliability. Not enough to kill you if you respect it. Enough to kill you if you trust your eyes over your feet.

### 2. Your Body Is The Weak Point

You are a mammal that expects to sweat to cool off. Here, sweat doesn't evaporate.

*   **Heat & Humidity:** It's warm, not scorching - think 82-90F / 28-32C - but the pressing, heavy air means you cannot dump heat. Heat exhaustion comes fast. Pre-acclimate for a week in a sauna/steam room if you can. Bring oral rehydration salts and electrolytes and use them religiously, even if you don't feel thirsty. You will lose liters without noticing.
*   **Skin & Feet:** The killer is maceration and fungus. Constant wet skin softens, cracks, and infects. This is trench foot country.
    *   No waterproof-breathable jackets - they don't breathe at 100% humidity, you just stew in your own sweat. Wear quick-drain, quick-dry synthetics or a thin neoprene-like layer. Embrace getting wet.
    *   Feet: Closed-toe, high-drainage shoes like canyoneering shoes. NOT waterproof boots - they will fill and never drain. Bring 3+ pairs of merino/synthetic socks, antifungal powder, zinc oxide cream, and let your feet air whenever you can. Check them twice a day.
    *   Bring a small bottle of povidone-iodine and broad-spectrum antibiotic ointment. Every scrape here will be exposed to warm biofilm and gutter water.
*   **Lungs & Nose:** The smell is warm stone, brine, ozone, and bodies. The air is full of aerosolized proteins, insect chitin, yeast spores from the condensation pipes, and biofilm. If you have allergies or asthma, you will feel it. Bring N95 or better half-masks for sleeping and for heavy insect smoke. Bring antihistamines and an inhaler even if you don't think you need one.
*   **Ears:** You need *double* hearing protection and you need it before you arrive. Foam earplugs + active noise-cancelling muffs or industrial earmuffs. The roar inverts into a pressure you feel in your ribs and molars. Without protection you will get tinnitus in hours, and you will be unable to think or sleep. Keep them on until your brain learns to filter - then keep them on anyway when you need to rest.

### 3. The Kit - Pack to Stay Wet, Not Dry

Assume everything not in a dry bag will be damp forever. Electronics die fast here.

*   **Sleep System:** You will not sleep on the ground - it's wet, it floods, and things run over it. Bring a lightweight hammock with a rain fly and mosquito mesh, or a bivy with a raised cot. And bring a GOOD sleep mask - blackout, padded - and your ear protection. Without them you will not get real sleep.
*   **Dry System:** Roll-top dry bags inside dry bags. One for electronics, one for one set of truly dry clothes to sleep in, one for food/meds. Silica gel is pointless. Use vacuum-sealed bags.
*   **Water:** DO NOT drink the standing water. It looks clear where it weeps from walls, but it's full of biofilm, fauna waste, and the city's own detritus fats. Bring a Sawyer squeeze or Grayl + purification tablets and only drink rain you catch directly or water you have filtered. Bring a collapsible bottle.
*   **Food:** You cannot forage. There are no edible plants and the local food chain is detritus -> biofilm -> cleaners -> lizards -> birds -> insects. The locals eat cultured fats and yeasts grown on the pipes. You *can* trade for it in the bazaar, but your gut will not thank you on day one. Bring calorie-dense, salt-heavy, non-perishable food for your whole stay + 50%. Energy bars, cured meats, nut butters in squeeze packs.
*   **Light & Power:** Everything sealed. IPX7 headlamp, not a phone light. Power bank in a dry bag with desiccant. The ambient light means you don't need a light to see, but you need one to signal.
*   **Clothing:** Cover your skin. Insect smoke is constant and the cleaners will investigate exposed skin. Lightweight, long-sleeve, treated with permethrin. Wide-brim hat with a chin strap - for rain and insects. Eye protection - clear glasses or sunglasses - the air is full of wings.
*   **Tools:** A trekking pole / wading staff is essential. You will be probing puddles that are deeper than they look and testing slick stone. Non-slip gloves for handrails that are slick with biofilm. Small amount of trade goods - the bazaar never closes and compact, universal things like stainless blades, antibiotics, sealed spices, or charged batteries trade well.

### 4. How to Move and Live There

**Walking:** The stone is old, rounded, and impossibly slick when the rust/olive-black biofilm is wet. Which is always. Walk flat-footed, slow, staff down. Never jump onto a plaza pool - assume it's deeper and the bottom is not where you think it is. If water is running uphill, walk around it. That's a thin spot, and your depth perception is compromised there.

**Navigation:** Don't navigate by landmarks. Landmarks move. Navigate by flow. Water always knows where low is, and the city's growth follows water. Follow the gutters and canals downstream to larger channels. Mark your own path with chalk that will wash away - you want temporary breadcrumbs. Better: drop a GPS pin every 5 minutes and carry a paper compass. When the light throws a shadow with no source, don't stare at it. Keep your feet moving.

**Fauna:** You are not prey, you are an obstacle. The clouds of silver fish/shrimp, the hand-sized birds moving as one fabric, the insect smoke, the lizard-runners on walls - they will part around you if you stand still. Don't swat, don't grab. If a migration tide comes - black chitin filling the slot-canyon street - do not fight it. Step up onto a ledge, a handrail, a doorway and wait. It's 20 minutes. The cleaners will come after and eat the dead. Let them.

Never touch the biofilm mats deliberately. Never eat anything that was touching them unless a local shows you it's safe. That dark patina is the base of the whole ecosystem and your skin biome is not invited.

**The City Itself:** Don't lean on a wall that's healing. Walls thicken, handrails swell, plazas re-grade. If you see scaffolds of light with dust organizing inside - that's a matter-loom - give it a wide berth. It will not stop for you. Don't camp in an empty-looking service trench. It may not be empty tonight.

### 5. Mental Preparation - This Is The Hardest Part

An ordinary human is not built for no horizon, no silence, no stillness, and no solitude. The crowd is animal and human and otherwise, shoulder-to-shoulder, but it's not a friendly crowd. It's just *density*.

*   You will be overstimulated within an hour and under-stimulated within a day - too much noise to think, not enough change to orient.
*   Bring something with a tactile, non-electronic ritual: prayer beads, a notebook with waterproof paper, a harmonica you play *with* earplugs in. You need a way to make a small, controllable quiet inside the pressure.
*   Plan forced rest. Every 4-6 hours, get up high - one of those broad, low terraces with the sheet water - put your back to a solid wall, put your ear protection and mask on, close your eyes, and just breathe for 20 minutes. You need to break the vibration.
*   The uncanny bits will get to you if you focus on them. Note them and let them go. "Puddle is deeper, noted. Not my puddle." Don't investigate an uphill rivulet or a shadow with no source for longer than it takes to step around it.

### 6. What NOT To Do

1.  Don't try to stay dry. You will get clammy, exhausted, and then dangerously cold when it rains.
2.  Don't try to find the edge of the city. There isn't one for you.
3.  Don't take off your shoes to wade "just for a second." That's when you cut your foot on basalt hidden under biofilm.
4.  Don't drink the beautiful clear water weeping from a retaining wall.
5.  Don't use a tent with a floor. It will fill.

If you do it right, this place is overwhelmingly alive in a way few places in the multiverse are. Once you stop fighting the weeping and the roar, there's a strange peace to it - sitting on a warm, stepped terrace while the rain drums on ten million roofs, watching the silver spirals turn in the shallows and the white birds snap like a sheet in the wind, feeling the whole warm basin breathe steam into clouds that will rain on you again in an hour.

Go to be saturated, not to be comfortable. Bring the gear to stay wet, protected, and oriented, and let the city flow around you.

And decide your exit *before* you enter. Set a hard time - 72 hours max for a first visit - and a recall anchor that doesn't need you to see or hear to activate. Because the most dangerous thing about a place that never drains, never quiets, and never stops rebuilding is how easy it is to forget you ever planned to leave.
