For this place you don't prepare like you're going to a city, or a cave, or a base. You prepare like you are going to visit a living lung that someone cut into fresh rock yesterday.

It's small, hot, damp, dim, dense, and closed. Everything you bring and do should respect those five facts.

### 1. Understand What You're Walking Into

This is not a ruin and not a wilderness. It's a young, active, human pressure-cooker.

*   **Scale:** 300 meters across. You can see the entire settlement from the entrance walkway. You will be seen as soon as you arrive. There is no anonymous tourism here.
*   **Air:** 38-42C / 100-108F rock temperature, near 100% humidity during and after the ceiling-rain. The air is still unless machines push it. It's a sauna that never cools down.
*   **Water:** There is no permanent water. Water appears as condensation on the ceiling, falls as a 10-minute indoor rain once or twice per cycle, runs in a central channel for an hour, then is gone. Everything else is cistern, barrel, and sweating pipe.
*   **Light:** No sun. Ever. Low amber work bulbs, white fluorescents in windows, and a deep orange forge glow from one end. The top third of the dome is always dusk. Shadows fall *up*.
*   **Sound & Life:** Weirdly quiet. Hot damp air and fabric swallow sound. And there are *no animals at all* - no insects, no rats, no birds. It's a sterile, human+machine biome. Your microbiome will be the biggest ecological event that has happened there in a long time.

If you have claustrophobia, heat intolerance, or need open sky and wind to regulate yourself, this will test you hard. The rock is the wall, absolute and close.

### 2. Body Preparation

You are going to sweat without cooling down. Prepare for heat before you go.

**For 2-3 days before:**
*   Hydrate aggressively with electrolytes, not just water. Sodium, potassium, magnesium. Your sweat will not evaporate efficiently there so you will lose salts fast.
*   Heat-acclimate if you can: saunas, hot baths. Learn what heat exhaustion feels like - headache, nausea, cessation of sweating, confusion.
*   Rest your eyes. You will be in half-light the whole time. If you wear contacts, bring glasses as a backup - condensation and warm particulate air will irritate them.

**On arrival:**
*   Move slowly for the first hour. Your heart rate will climb 20-30 bpm just standing there. Let it.
*   Sip constantly. Don't wait to be thirsty.
*   Eat light and salty.

### 3. What To Wear & Bring

Think boiler-room worker, not explorer.

**Clothing:**
*   Lightweight, fast-drying, long layers. Long sleeves and pants in breathable tech fabric. You want to wick sweat but protect from hot metal, sharp fresh basalt, and steam burns. No shorts, no tank tops.
*   Nothing that dangles. Catwalks are narrow, ladders are stairwells, gaps between balconies are inches. Loose scarves and wide bags will snag.
*   Broken-in boots with soft, grippy, non-slip soles that can handle wet steel grating, slick concrete, and warm puddles. You will be climbing ladders constantly. Ankle support is mandatory. Bring 2-3 pairs of merino wool socks and change them often.
*   A brimmed hat or bandana to keep condensation drips off your head and neck.

**Essential Gear:**
*   **Headlamp with RED light mode + a small warm/amber secondary light.** Do NOT bring a blinding white spotlight. You will blind everyone and kill your own night vision. Red preserves the dim adaptation everyone lives by.
*   **Gloves** - thin, grippy work gloves. The fresh-cut basalt is razor sharp and still outgasses a metallic tang. The pipes and railings are hot.
*   **Water:** Carry at least 1L on you, plus electrolyte packets and a small filtration/purification add-on like tablets. The cistern water is technically potable but warm, mineral-heavy, and rationed. Don't assume you can just help yourself.
*   **Towel / Shemagh:** You will be damp the entire time. You need something to wipe condensation, sweat, and pipe-drip off you and your gear.
*   **Earplugs - but you probably won't need them.** Bring soft ones for sleeping. The thrum is felt more than heard. The quiet is persistent - drips, hisses, soft footsteps, metal ticks.
*   **Dry bags:** Everything you carry should be in a dry bag or ziplock. The ceiling-rain is warm, sudden, and gets into everything. Electronics will fog instantly if not sealed.
*   **Mask / buff:** Not for disease, for particulates. Hot iron, ozone, wet concrete, and welding fume hang in still air at chest height.

**Leave Behind:**
*   Anything with a strong scent - perfume, strong soap, bug spray. There are no insects to repel, and smell hangs forever in still air.
*   Live food, seeds, pets, or anything biological you don't need. This place has achieved an almost impossible animal-free equilibrium. Don't be the person who introduces cockroaches. Clean your boots and gear before you go.
*   Loud speakers, drones, bright strobes. You will be deeply unwelcome.

### 4. Navigation and Safety

*   **Learn vertical thinking.** Addresses aren't street-and-number. They're level, scaffold, pipe-color, and who lives above whom. "Three down from the blue water pipe, left at the grow-light beans." Take photos if allowed, or sketch. You will get lost in 300 meters because density is fractal.
*   **Watch the walls.** The fresh basalt walls will weep dark lines before the rain. If you see the sharp-cut planes start to bloom with moisture and the bulbs sway without wind, get under cover. The rain is not dangerous but it makes every steel surface ice-slick in seconds and will soak you.
*   **Watch your head and feet.** Low-hanging pipes sweat constantly and are 50C+. Low doorways, protruding rebar, uneven slag-concrete. Always use handrails - they are hot, but falling is worse.
*   **Light discipline:** Don't look straight at the smelter/forge glow for long. Let your eyes adapt. Carry that red headlamp and use it on low.
*   **Air awareness:** If you feel light-headed near the lower terraces by the factories, move up one level to slightly cooler air. CO2 and heat pool low.

### 5. Social Preparation

This is a closed, necessity-built town. Everyone knows everyone. You are a obvious outsider who came down a throat that was cut for them, not you.

*   Be quiet. Voices carry as a murmur in that dome. The culture is low-volume because it has to be. Don't shout across alleys.
*   Ask before touching, drinking, photographing, or sitting. Water, power, and even a balcony bean plant are collectively rationed. A barrel of water is not decoration, it's tomorrow's drinking water.
*   Don't comment loudly on the crowding, the heat, or the smell of hot iron and ozone. To them it's just air.
*   Offer to contribute if you stay more than a transit: help carrying, pumping, cleaning a gutter before the rain. Usefulness is the currency.
*   Don't bring up the surface. The shaft up is the only memory of light they have, and it's a working entrance, not a view.

### 6. Psychological Prep

The strangest part isn't the heat. It's the absence.

No buzzing, no birdsong, no rustle. No animals at all. Your brain will keep waiting for a fly that never comes. At night - or what passes for night - you'll hear only metal ticking and water dripping on tarp with irregular timing, and humans breathing.

Combined with the low dome, the upward shadows, and the ripple of heat at the far end making distance lie, you may feel time stretch. Bring a small, grounding ritual: a journal, a familiar scent on a handkerchief, a breathing pattern. Give yourself permission to step onto an empty walkway and just look at the green-black lichen or the box gardens for a while. That green is intentional hope, and you will need to see it.

Prepare to leave no trace. When you go back up that sharp, new-cut shaft, you should take only condensation in your boots and the memory of how deeply humans can layer life when they have nowhere to go but up.

If you do that, you will experience it as it is: not as a slum, not as a cave, but as a small, sweating, humming, carefully kept miracle that rains only inside itself.
