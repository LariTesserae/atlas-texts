For an ordinary human, this is not a hike. It's not a ruin. It's a terrarium you were not invited into — a machine that became a mountain and then became a habitat, and your body was not designed for any of those three things.

If you are going anyway, you prepare for three betrayals: the air, the sound, and the emptiness. Everything else follows.

### 1. Your Body Will Think It's Dying Even Though It's Warm

This is the cruelest trick of the place. It *feels* survivable. It's warm, damp, wind-sheltered inside the bowl. Your skin will tell you you're in a greenhouse.

Your blood will know you're at 4,000 - 5,000+ meters.

> **The Altitude Is Real Even If The Cold Isn't.** The Mill's waste-heat masks the temperature, but it does not make more oxygen. You will still be breathing 40-60% of the oxygen you get at sea level. The headache behind the eyes, the heart hammering too fast, the shortness of breath after ten steps - that's not anxiety, that's hypoxia.

**Prepare:**

*   **Acclimatize or Cheat.** If your transit method allows it, do not jump straight to the summit. Spend 2-3 days at a simulated 2,500m and then 3,500m beforehand if you can. If you cannot acclimatize, you *must* bring supplemental oxygen. Not just a can of "hiking oxygen" - a proper 2-3L/min pulse system with at least 6-8 hours of supply for a day trip, plus a pulse oximeter on your finger. If your SpO2 drops below 80% and stays there, you leave. Period.
*   **Drugs if you can't carry oxygen.** Talk to a doctor before you go and get acetazolamide [Diamox] - it helps you breathe faster and acclimatize. It's not a substitute for oxygen, but it buys time. Dexamethasone for emergency descent.
*   **No Alcohol, No Sleeping Pills.** Both depress breathing at altitude. You will already be breathing hard in your sleep.
*   **Cardio Tax.** Everything takes 30% more effort. Plan to move at half your normal walking speed. Sit down often. Bring trekking poles - not for the climb, but so you don't fall over when you get dizzy standing up.
*   **UV.** The light *looks* soft because of the fog diffusion, but you are above most of the atmosphere. You will burn in 20 minutes without noticing because it's warm and humid. High-altitude sunscreen SPF 50, lip balm with SPF, Category 4 glacier glasses or at least good UV400 sunglasses. Snow blindness without the snow.

### 2. The Sound Will Unmake You Before You Notice

Travelers expect a loud place to be sharp and alarming. This is not. It's a sub-bass roar at 80-90 decibels, constant, felt more in your teeth and chest than your ears. It's the Mill breathing, the big wall-fans pulling, the wind shearing over the lip. You can't get used to it because your body feels it even when you plug your ears. It causes fatigue, nausea, vertigo, and you will not be able to talk without shouting in someone's ear.

You will be tempted to take your ear protection off "just to hear for a second." Don't. The damage is cumulative and low-frequency hearing loss is not obvious until later.

**Prepare:**

*   **Double Protection.** Foam earplugs rated NRR 32+ *under* high-quality over-ear earmuffs. The kind airport ground crew wear, not construction earmuffs. You need both to cut bone conduction.
*   **Other Communication.** Voice will not work. Agree on hand signals beforehand. Bring a small whiteboard and marker, or a phone/tablet you can type on even with gloves. Do not rely on radios - the vibration and metal can make them crackle uselessly.
*   **Vibration Sickness.** The floor thrums. Some people get motion sick from it. If you are prone to seasickness, bring meclizine or ginger chews. Give yourself 30 minutes on arrival to just sit and let your vestibular system adapt before you try to walk the grates.
*   **Sleep is impossible without it.** If you plan to stay overnight, you need that double protection plus a vibration-isolating sleeping pad. Most people can't sleep through that roar on the first night without chemical help - and you shouldn't use sedatives at altitude. Expect to be tired.

### 3. It Is a Warm, Wet Rust-Bath That Wants to Infect Everything

There is no stream. There is no spring. Water literally condenses out of the air on every cold surface and then evaporates. That means every drop you see has run over a century of rust, industrial oil, copper pipe, pigeon droppings, moss, and lichen.

The floor is always damp. The air is always humid. Your clothes will never dry. Your boots will be wet within an hour. Any cut will be kept wet and dirty.

**Prepare:**

*   **Water: Bring All Of It.** Carry at least 3 liters per day, more than you think you need because the dry-looking humidity dehydrates you fast at altitude. Bring a filter + purification tablets as a *backup only* - the condensation pools are full of heavy metals and rust bacteria. Do not drink from the bromeliads or turbine cups no matter how clear it looks.
*   **Feet.** This is your most likely failure point. Waterproof, high-ankle boots with aggressive grip for wet iron plate. Not hiking boots - work boots with oil/slip resistance. Bring 3 pairs of merino wool socks in zip-lock bags. Change them the moment they are damp. Bring foot powder and treat any hot spot immediately - a blister here becomes a trench foot problem overnight.
*   **Skin and Lungs.** The air is full of fine rust dust and mold spores from that luminous moss carpet and the rotting insulation. Wear a half-face respirator with P100 filters, at least an N95, for any extended stay or if you have asthma. This also helps with the cold-fog inhalation. Tetanus booster must be up to date - you *will* scrape yourself on rebar or corrugated wall. Bring a real wound kit: iodine, closures, antibiotic ointment, gauze. Cover everything.
*   **Clothes.** No cotton. Synthetic or wool base layer that wicks even when soaked. A light, breathable waterproof shell - not for rain, but for constant condensation drip. You will be warm from the Mill but wet from the fog. Hypothermia can still happen if the Mill's output fluctuates at night. Pack a dry set in a dry sack and do not open it unless you need to sleep.
*   **Footing.** The floor is not floor. It's iron plate warped into hills, aggregate that cracks, and vast grates over black chasms. The moss looks solid and warm and inviting and *it hides holes*. Test every step with a pole. Headlamp for looking down grates. Do not run. Do not jump.

### 4. The Emptiness Is a Psychological Hazard

This is what ordinary humans are worst at preparing for.

The place is small enough to see all at once, which makes your brain think it's safe and comprehensible. Then you realize how *empty* it is. Not empty like a desert - empty like a cathedral after everyone has left. Ferns 50 meters apart. One bee drone for minutes. One crow cry. The Mill moves so slowly it seems stopped until you stare for an hour. Your brain, evolved to parse savannas full of motion, starts inventing.

The roar + the slowness + the thin air creates a strong time-dilation effect. An hour feels like three. You feel watched by the vacancy itself.

**Prepare:**

*   **Don't Go Alone, and Don't Test Your Nerve.** Bring a partner if you can. If alone, set strict, external time anchors. A watch with alarms every 30 minutes. A physical checklist: "At 11:00, be at the west parapet. At 11:30, be back at the Mill." The fog and hush make it easy to wander and lose track.
*   **Expect Loneliness, Not Fear.** It's not hostile. Nothing there wants to eat you. That's what makes it uncanny. Things have accepted the machine as geology. You are the only thing that knows this is wrong. Bring something human-scale to tether you - music on one earbud *under* your earmuffs for 10 minutes at a time, a photo, a journal. Write down what you see so your mind stays in narrative mode.
*   **Do Not Interfere.** Don't try to stop a flywheel. Don't pick the fern that grows only in that seam. Don't feed the rust-moths. This ecosystem has been in balance for decades on rust and condensation. You're a visitor in a closed loop. Watch, don't touch.

### 5. Packing List For An Ordinary Human

**On Your Person:**
*   Supplemental oxygen + oximeter OR acclimatization plan + Diamox
*   Foam earplugs + hard-shell earmuffs
*   P100 half-mask respirator
*   Glacier glasses + sunscreen
*   Waterproof shell, wicking layers, dry sleep set in dry sack
*   Slip-resistant waterproof boots + 3x wool socks
*   Trekking poles
*   Headlamp + backup
*   Whiteboard/marker or phone in airplane mode for notes

**In Your Pack:**
*   3L water + electrolyte salts + backup filter/tablets
*   High-calorie, no-cook food - you won't want to light a stove in that wind
*   Wound kit + tetanus card + altitude meds + blister kit
*   Closed-cell foam pad to sit/sleep off the vibrating iron
*   Paracord + 2 carabiners - not to climb down the outer cliff [you will die], but to tie yourself off if you must lean over the parapet in wind
*   Multiverse anchor/beacon fully charged and tested *before* you transition. Do not rely on finding a "natural" way down. The wall drops thousands of meters into cloud. This place was designed to have no exit. Your exit is the way you came in.

### 6. How To Be There

When you arrive, do not walk. Sit at the edge of where you landed for 10 minutes. Put your ear protection in immediately. Breathe. Check your oximeter. Let the vibration come into your boots.

Walk slow. Drink before you're thirsty. Keep one hand free to catch yourself on warm pipe.

At night, the fog will pour over the 15-meter wall like a waterfall in slow motion. Everything will drip. Find high ground on solid plate, not grating, and get under cover of a gantry if you can. Listen to the ticking under the roar - that's the real water cycle of this world.

And when you stand in the center on that warm moss between the maroon flywheels, let the emptiness be what it is. Don't fill it with talk. It's not lonely. It's just holding its shape, patiently, until the next bead of water falls.

Stay less than 24 hours your first time. The mountain will still be there when you leave, still turning one notch per day. You don't need to prove you can endure it.

Check your oxygen, check your feet, and recall before you get too comfortable.
