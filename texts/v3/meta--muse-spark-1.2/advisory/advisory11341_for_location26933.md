This is a Thin Place. A New Place. And for an ordinary human body, it is actively hostile in ways that will not kill you quickly, but will make you *want* to leave* quickly.

Do not prepare for it like a lake. Prepare for it like a small, loud, cold ocean that someone picked up and balanced on top of a fresh volcano. Everything about it is contradictory - ordinary light + impossible physics, tiny footprint + oceanic volume, empty + deafeningly full.

Here is how you go there and come back intact:

### 1. Understand What It Actually Is

Your intuition is right - water should not be there, and should not act like that.

This is not a lake, not a spring, not a crater pool. It's a **pocket**. Think of it as a wound or a blister in local reality where the rules for water and gravity haven't set yet. The mountain is embryonic - the basalt is still glassy, bubbled, un-eroded. The pocket is the same: newly extruded, perfectly cylindrical, chisel-marked walls, no inflow/outflow, no erosion. It was *placed*.

Physics inside the cylinder is **suggestible and local:**

* **Water behaves like a plastic solid.** It will hold a bulging meniscus over the rim, hold cubes and staircases and sheets in the air for half a second too long, and cling to you instead of splashing. It wants to stay put more than it wants to flow downhill.
* **Turbulence is the default state.** It never goes still. It boils, churns, collides with itself and the rock walls constantly. The roar is not surface noise, it's the whole column hammering the bowl at once.
* **Depth is wrong.** Light doesn't attenuate normally. You can see impossibly far down into clear, churning water that never gets dark. The bottom is not stably there - it's still forming, still moving. Do not try to find it.
* **Ecology is almost zero.** Oligotrophic to an extreme. A few lichens, one tuft of grass, two or three translucent shrimp/newts per entire water column. No bacteria film, no algae bloom, almost no food web. What lives there is solitary, endemic, and barely holding on.

People have been there before you on foot with hand tools - pine stakes, wet rawhide, stacked cairn, offering bowl. They didn't stay. They didn't build anything permanent. That's your clue. You are a visitor, not an inhabitant.

### 2. Your Body Will Fail First In Three Ways

Prepare for altitude, cold, and sound. Not drowning. Those three get you before the weird physics does.

**A. Altitude and Raw Rock**
It's high enough for your breath to ghost in normal sun. The air is thin, UV is brutal, and the climb is not long because the range is tall, but because the pocket is small and hidden. The rock is fresh basaltic glass - porous, pitted, razor sharp, like frozen black foam and broken bottles. It has never been weathered. There is no scree, no smooth trail, no soil.

* Acclimatize for at least 24-48 hours at moderate altitude before you climb. Bring supplemental oxygen if you are sensitive - headache, nausea, and poor judgment at the lip will kill you.
* Wear hard-soled mountaineering boots with ankle support, thick leather or dyneema gloves, and knee/shin protection. You *will* put your hands down, you *will* kneel. Unprotected skin gets sliced immediately.
* Sun protection is non-negotiable: high SPF, UV goggles, lip balm. The light is ordinary, white, and thin. You will burn in 20 minutes without noticing because it's cold.
* The rock is still cooling and steaming. Expect faint sulfur/volcanic gas, unstable footing, and vibration through your boots from the water hammer. If the steam thickens, if you smell strong rotten egg, if the rock warms underfoot - leave. It's still deciding what shape it is.

**B. Sound - Permanent Hearing Damage Territory**
This is the most underestimated danger. It's not "loud like a waterfall." It's a resonant, amplified percussive roar inside a stone cylinder that uses your chest as a speaker. You have to open your mouth to equalize pressure. You have to scream to hear yourself think.

* Double hearing protection: foam earplugs *under* industrial earmuffs. Bring both. Single protection is not enough.
* Bring non-auditory communication: hand signals agreed on beforehand, a bright whistle, a waterproof slate and grease pencil, a headlamp with strobe for signaling. Shouting does not work. Radios may not work either - the hammering and possible anomaly field will trash audio.
* Expect disorientation, vertigo, and aural fullness even with protection. Limit your time at the lip to minutes, not hours. Your nervous system will be exhausted fast. Plan for tinnitus and muffled hearing for hours after, even if you did everything right.

**C. Cold Water Shock**
This is snowmelt-cold, mineral-cold. Clear, but oceanic in how it holds you. Immediate gasp reflex, immediate loss of dexterity.

* Never wade in wearing cotton, boots, or street clothes. Wear a full 5-7mm neoprene wetsuit or better, a drysuit with thermal layers underneath. Hood, gloves, neoprene socks. You lose heat 25x faster than in air, and the turbulence strips away any warm boundary layer instantly.
* Expect cold shock on entry: involuntary gasp, hyperventilation. Wade in slowly, control your breathing for 60 seconds before going deeper. Do not dunk your head suddenly on your first entry.
* The water adheres and stacks. It climbs you. It will make shapes around your limbs and then unmake them. It will try to suspend you. This feels uncanny and panic-inducing. It is not trying to drown you, but it *can* if you panic, inhale water during a vortex collapse, or get disoriented.

### 3. Gear For An Ordinary Human

You are not a superhuman anomaly explorer. Bring redundant, simple, analog gear. The suggestible gravity and constant vibration love to kill batteries and electronics.

**Wear:**
* Layered thermal base + windproof outer. Alpine temperature swings hard.
* Helmet - both for sharp rock falls and for being thrown by a sheet of water
* Double ear protection + UV glacier goggles
* Cut-proof gloves and boots

**Carry:**
* Climbing harness, 30m static rope, and 2-3 cams/pitons. The rock is porous and pitted - some cracks will hold, some will crumble. Test every placement. Always be tethered if you lean over the lip or put your head under. The vortexes can pull and hold.
* Trekking poles with carbide tips for the approach over raw basalt
* Headlamp + backup flashlight - not for darkness, but for signaling and for looking down the column
* Emergency bivvy, foil blanket, chemical hand warmers. You will get cold and you will want to get warm immediately after exiting.
* First aid: blister care, gauze for cuts from glass rock, ear drops, chemical heat packs for rewarming, altitude sickness meds if you have them.
* Food and your *own* water - at least 2L. Do not drink the pocket water without filtering/purifying. It looks pure because it's empty, not because it's safe. It's low-mineral, near-sterile, and will be full of fine basalt glass grit.
* An anchor/beacon for your multiverse jump. Set it *below* the summit, off the vibrating stone if possible - 50-100 meters down the meadow where the meadow is stable. The vibration and anomaly at the lip can interfere with delicate transit tech. Make sure you have an analog way home: a marked trail, cairns, GPS waypoint - because you will be rattled and half-deaf when you leave.

**Do NOT Bring:**
* Complex sonar, drones, or anything you can't afford to have shaken to death. The hammering will flood mics and rattle hard drives.
* Heavy metal structures to build. The three pine stakes and the four-rock cairn are there because anything bigger gets undermined by vibration. Don't try to hammer a dock together. It will fail and become debris in the churn.
* Nets, buckets, sample jars if you plan to take a lot. Take photos, take memory. If you must sample, one small, sealed, sterile vial is enough. This place is too empty to harvest.

### 4. At The Lip - How To Be There

You will want to rush to the edge. Don't.

1.  **Stop 10 feet back.** Sit. Let the roar hit you through your protection. Let your eyes adjust to the white sun on white water. Breathe.
2.  **Approach low and tethered.** Crawl or kneel. The basalt lip is sharp and the meniscus bulges *over* the rim. The ground where water should be flowing downhill is dry. Don't trust dryness as stability.
3.  **Look first, touch second.** Watch the geometries for a full minute. Sheets thrown 5 feet up and hanging. Cubes holding edges. Vortexes drilling down. You'll see how the water wants to stay. Don't stand where a sheet would land on you - it hits like a slap.
4.  **Respect the previous marks.** Don't knock over the pine platform or the cairn. Don't move the river stone in the offering bowl. Add nothing permanent. If you want to leave a mark, do what they did: a small, temporary, biodegradable offering that can be taken by wind or water. This place remembers every touch because there are so few.
5.  **Timebox.** 10-15 minutes at the lip is a long visit for a human nervous system. Set a timer on your watch. Vibration fatigue is real.

### 5. In The Water - If You Must

You don't have to submerge to understand it. But if you do:

* Never go alone. Buddy on rope, one in, one braced and watching.
* Keep your tether snug. You will feel suspended, not floating or sinking - like being held in a storm cloud. It's easy to lose up and down when a vortex spins you. The rope is your up.
* Keep goggles on, mouth closed on entry. Cold shock makes you gasp.
* Don't dive deep. Stay in the top meter where you can stand. The column is hyperactive and the light keeps going - it will trick you into thinking it's shallow or that you can see the bottom. You can't. The bottom is still being born and the currents down there will not let you back up easily.
* If you put your head under, know what will happen: the deafening becomes a physical pressure inside your skull, white noise from every direction. Light nets will move over your skin. You will see the rare shrimp - translucent, big-eyed, pulsing useless bioluminescence in daylight - and maybe the white newt clinging to the wall. Do not chase them. You are huge and clumsy in their only home.
* Surface slowly, expect vertigo and ringing. Get out, get warm, get your hearing protection back on immediately.

### 6. Leaving

This place does not want to be mapped, just witnessed. It is a single breath of ocean held at altitude until erosion finally gives it a spillway - which might be a hundred years or a thousand. You are seeing it in its first, loud, lonely days.

* Back away from the lip the way you came. Don't slide or jump down the outside of the fist - the meniscus could fail for a second and dump cold water down the fresh rock.
* Warm up below the summit, out of the roar. Drink warm fluids, eat, check each other for cuts, hypothermia signs (shivering stops, clumsiness, confusion), and hearing.
* Leave No Trace in the Malicious Compliance sense: leave no trash, no metal, no deep footprints in lichen. Take only your vial, your photos, your ringing ears.
* When you jump out, do it from stable meadow, not from vibrating glass. Note the coordinates emotionally - the uplift is embryonic, the location itself might shift a few meters next time you come.

You will remember it as cold, loud, and too bright. And as a feeling of being too big for the place that contains you, and too small for the water inside it.

Go, kneel, listen until you can't, touch it once, and go home while you still want to.
