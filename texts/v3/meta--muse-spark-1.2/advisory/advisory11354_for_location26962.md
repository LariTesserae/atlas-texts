For an ordinary human, this is not a hike. This is a visit to a place that is running on different physics, and your body is not tuned to it.

Think of it less as a valley and more as a **cold, bright, silent greenhouse built inside a paused factory, at the top of a mountain where the air doesn't work right.** Everything there is telling you to SLOW DOWN. If you don't, it will do it for you.

Here is how you prepare to walk it and come back still human:

### 1. Your Body Will Get The Wrong Signals

**The air is thin and the light is brutal.** That's the first contradiction that will wear you down.
You're high enough for real altitude sickness - headache, shortness of breath, your heart hammering while you stand still, poor sleep, bad judgment. And because the air is thin and dry and there is no haze, the sun is savage. It's not warm-sun, it's UV-laser-sun. You will get sunburned and snow-blind without snow.

You will *feel* like you should be hot. You are not. The stone never warms above early-October. The air stays cold. Sun on your face, cold in your lungs, cold leaching up through your boots from the basalt. That combination causes hypothermia in people who don't expect it because "it was sunny."

**Prepare:**

* Acclimatize for 2-3 days at ~2500m+ before you go in, or you will be miserable and stupid within hours. Do not enter with a cold, hangover, or anemia.
* Dress like a mountaineer, not a jungle explorer. Base layer: merino wool, not cotton. Mid-layer: fleece or light down. Outer: hard-shell waterproof top and pants. You need to be able to ventilate when the sun hits and seal up when you stop. Bring a warm hat and thin gloves even if it looks like summer.
* Sun protection is non-negotiable: Category 4 glacier glasses with side shields, not just sunglasses. Your normal shades will leave you with eye pain 6 hours later. SPF 50+ broad spectrum, reapplied, lip balm with SPF, and cover the back of your neck. The light comes from everywhere - it bounces off the pale basalt and the wet leaves.
* Bring 1L more water than you think you need and electrolytes. The thin air dehydrates you twice as fast through breathing, and you won't feel sweaty because the dew keeps your skin damp.

### 2. The Water is Everywhere and Nowhere

There is no river. The valley makes its own water out of air. Every morning everything is soaked in a heavy, beading dew that runs for an hour and then vanishes into stone cups. You will be wet all day just from brushing through vegetation.

**That water is not for you.**

The cistern water - those perfect, clear bathtubs in the basalt, the shaded pockets under overhangs - is biologically strange. Left in the sun it goes viscous instead of evaporating. Leave it overnight and it sprouts seedlings that should take weeks. It's holding time wrong. Don't drink it, even filtered, unless you have absolutely no choice. It is full of spores, pollen, and whatever makes the moss glow.

**Prepare:**

* Carry all your drinking water in. Minimum 3L for a day walk, plus a backup.
* Bring waterproof everything: waterproof boots with ankle support + gaiters, waterproof shell mitts, drybags for electronics and food. Your feet will be wet within 20 minutes if you don't; the moss is a soaked mattress that hides ankle-deep puddles and holes.
* Trekking poles are essential. The green mat is 3-4 layers deep - moss under fern under vine under shrub. There is no "ground." You are walking on a living trampoline that hides basalt potholes big enough to snap an ankle. Probe every step on the flat. On the vertical, the columnar basalt is sharp as broken glass where it shears. Gloves with good grip.
* Filtration as emergency backup only: Sawyer or similar + chemical tablets. But understand: filtering will not remove the time effect. If you must drink cistern water, filter + boil + let it sit in the dark. If it thickens, dump it.

### 3. The Green Will Try To Eat You Quietly

This is not a forest you walk through. It's an upholstery you push through. Leaves at your elbows on both sides, even in the middle of what looks like a road. The air is full of slow gold dust - pollen and spores that never settle. There are no animals to eat it. No birds, no insects except big, pale moths that don't move.

Two risks:

**a) You can't breathe right.** Without a mask, 4-6 hours of that spore load will give even a non-allergic person a sore throat, sinus headache, and later a chest cough. If you have asthma or allergies, it can be incapacitating.

**b) You can't see or be heard.** The moss eats sound. Your footsteps thud. Voices die after 5 meters. Machines that should scream are silent. You can hear your own heartbeat and a single dewdrop falling. This is severely disorienting. People start whispering, then stop talking, then get anxious or hear things that aren't there.

**Prepare:**

* P100 or at least N95 respirator. Not a paper dust mask. Bring a spare. Bring antihistamines [cetirizine/loratadine] and if you are asthmatic, your inhaler. You want to keep your airway clear at altitude.
* Earplugs are not for noise - they're for the silence. Some people bring them to dampen the weird internal ringing. More important: bring a visual signaling plan because shouting is useless. Whistle [it carries a little better than voice], signal mirror, bright orange panel, and a buddy. Do not go alone. The valley is soundproofed - if you fall in a hole under the moss, no one will hear you.
* Move like the valley does: slowly, deliberately. If you thrash, you will sweat, soak yourself, exhaust your lungs, and tear the mat. The plants will snag and hold you. Cut nothing, pull nothing. The vines are tensioned over the machines.
* Decontamination afterwards: Expect everything you bring out to have spores and tiny seedlings in the seams. Bag your clothes before you get in your tent/ship/home. Wash them hot, clean your boots, wipe down gear with alcohol. Check your nostrils and ears. Don't bring a piece of the moss home as a souvenir - it will keep growing in your pack.

### 4. Time and Light Are Sick

This is the one that gets ordinary humans in trouble.

Light pools where it shouldn't. Shadow under a terrace glows green. The shadow of a crane jitters minute to minute as if the sun is stuttering. And biology/matter is on a delay: rust makes perfect geometry, a cup of water sprouts overnight, vines only appear to move when you compare photos.

You are entering a place that conserves motion. The factory is still working - amber bulbs burning in the day, transformers humming so low you feel it in your teeth rather than hear it, a 10-meter wheel that takes 20 minutes to make one revolution to lift empty buckets. It's not dead. It's on a timescale of seasons, not seconds.

For you, that means:
* Your watch, your sense of time, and your camera will disagree. You will think you've been there 30 minutes and it's been 2 hours.
* Causality is sticky. Don't leave anything to "do its thing." That water won't evaporate when you expect. That seedling will.
* You will be tempted to touch, to speed things up, to open a shed door.

**Prepare:**

* Bring two independent timekeepers and don't rely on the sun. A mechanical/automatic watch + a cheap digital kitchen timer that vibrates every 30 minutes. Set a hard turn-back time before you go in and obey it even if you feel like you just arrived. Time dilation is subtle but real there - people lose afternoons.
* Bring a small notebook and take a photo of the same machine when you arrive and when you leave. You will need proof that it moved. Otherwise your brain will tell you nothing is happening and you should wait longer.
* Light discipline: The amber interior lights mean the grid is live. The hum from the transformers on vine-wrapped poles means high voltage. Do not touch any machine, belt, bucket chain, or cable. They move too slowly to look dangerous and that is why they are dangerous - you won't pull your hand back in time, and no one will hear you shout. Observe through windows. Do not enter sheds unless you have an exit line and a partner outside.
* Do not stare at the green afterglow in the shadows. It's not harmful like radiation, but it will wreck your night vision and depth perception. Use your glacier glasses even in shadow if the glow is strong.

### 5. How To Actually Walk It

**Anchor at the rim, not the floor.** If you are using any kind of multiverse tether/beacon/extraction, set it on the sharp basalt lip of the plateau where time is more normal. The effect is strongest on the valley floor among the cisterns and machines. Mark your entry descent with cairns or bright tape - the rims are all sharp and look identical on the way back.

**Navigation:** Your compass will twitch near the walls. Basalt is magnetic, especially columnar basalt. GPS may be spotty under the heavy cloud-brushing rim. Rely on line-of-sight to both walls - you can always see both at once - and on your own footprints in the dew/moss. Walk the center, not the edges.

**Behavior Protocol - The Three Conservations:**

The valley lives by: Conserve water, conserve motion, conserve sound. Mimic it.

1.  **Conserve water:** Don't splash in cisterns, don't pour out your bottle to test. Move around the puddles.
2.  **Conserve motion:** Slow, even pace. No running. Rest with your back to stone, not on the moss if you can avoid it.
3.  **Conserve sound:** Speak quietly, step softly. Not out of reverence - because loud noise is wasted energy there and marks you as foreign. And because you need to hear the tiny sounds that *do* exist: a faint hum change before a machine cycles, a dewdrop click that tells you a thread of water is about to run across your path.

**Packing List for an Ordinary Human - Day Visit:**

* Layered clothing system + waterproof hardshell, warm hat, sun hat
* Glacier glasses + sunscreen + SPF lip balm
* P100 respirator + spares, antihistamines
* 3-4L water + electrolytes + high-calorie food you can eat with a mask briefly lifted
* Trekking poles, gaiters, ankle-support waterproof boots, spare socks in drybag
* Navigation: paper map, mechanical watch, vibrating timer, whistle, mirror, bright marker tape, headlamp [red mode] + spare batteries
* First aid: blister kit, altitude headache meds [ibuprofen], tape for basalt cuts
* Notebook + phone/camera in drybag
* A partner. Tell someone outside the valley your hard exit time.

**When you leave:**

You will not feel like you've had an epic adventure. You will feel tired, slightly sunburned despite the cold, with damp clothes and ringing ears from the silence. That's normal. That's the valley being gentle.

Do a full decon: shake out, wash, check for ticks/moths/seedlings in cuffs. Hydrate. Eat. Give yourself 12 hours before you make any big decisions - you will have mild temporal jet lag. Dreams may be vivid that night; the spore load does that.

And you'll notice one last thing: on the plateau rim looking back, the valley will look like a simple green cut again, innocent, quiet. You won't be able to see the amber lights from up there, even though you know they are on.

Leave them on. Don't try to turn anything off, or on. You were a guest in a place that makes one perfect thing per year, very slowly, in the cold bright silence. Your job was just to walk through without being made into part of the mat.

If you do that, you'll come back fine. And you'll have a faint smell of moss and ozone in your pack for weeks.
