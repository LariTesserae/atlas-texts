This is not a dive. This is not an expedition. This is a burial world, and you need to prepare to be buried in it for a little while and then get out again.

An ordinary human does not belong in a World Inside An Ocean. Not because it will try to kill you - it won't. That's the problem. It will not notice you at all, and it will let you make every mistake slowly, quietly, and permanently. Here's how to prepare to visit and actually come back.

### 1. Understand What Will Try To Kill You

It is not monsters. There are no monsters. There are only four things:

**Pressure, Cold, Dark, and Scale.**

You have to solve all four before you think about anything else. A multiverse translation will usually equalize pressure for you - if yours doesn't, you are already dead. Assume you will be under hundreds to thousands of atmospheres of pressure. Even if your body is translated to survive it, your *gear* is not. Every air pocket, every seal, every battery housing, every light will implode unless it is pressure-compensated or fluid-filled. Test everything in a pressure chamber first. If you can't do that, don't go.

The Cold is not dramatic. It's 2-4°C, just above freezing, everywhere, forever. It's not a stab, it's a leech. A normal wetsuit buys you minutes. A drysuit buys you hours. What you want is a heated drysuit with a closed-circuit rebreather, because exhaled bubbles will not warm you and open-circuit gas gets cold fast. Bring two independent heating systems and the power to run them for 3x your planned stay. The cold here doesn't make you shiver and then make you numb - it makes you *slow*. Your thoughts get slow. Your decision-making gets slow. That's when you get lost.

The Dark is absolute. Your eyes will never truly adapt. That "faint diffusion far above" is a psychological trick, not usable light. Bring light, but understand light is a liability here. And the Scale will break your brain long before your body breaks.

### 2. Gear For An Ordinary Human Body

Think: Abyssal Cave Diver + Arctic Explorer + Astronaut.

**Breathing:** Closed-circuit rebreather with manual bailout. No bubbles if you can help it - bubbles disturb silt and you will be blind for hours. Carry at least double your calculated gas for the planned time, and a completely separate bailout bottle clipped off where you can find it by feel alone. Your breathing rate will be low because you will be moving slowly, but your CO2 scrubber will be working hard in the cold. Keep it warm inside your suit.

**Thermal:** Base layer + heated vest + heated drysuit with dry gloves and a hood that leaves only your face exposed. If your face is exposed, you need a full-face mask. Frostbite isn't the risk - insidious hypothermia is. You will feel fine, even peaceful, right up until you can't operate your clips anymore.

**Light:** You need three levels.
*   A dim, warm, low-lumen headlamp on a red or amber setting for general movement. This preserves your dark adaptation and doesn't shock the forest. The plants here don't know bright light. Don't blast them.
*   A focused primary can-light, 1000-2000 lumens, on your hand, for inspection.
*   Two backup lights, each independently powered, stowed where you can find them with frozen hands.

Never use a light on full power unless you have to. The water is gin-clear until you touch anything, then it becomes milk that hangs for hours. Bright light in that milk blinds you worse than darkness.

**Movement:** You do not swim here. You pull, hover, and drift.

You need perfect neutral buoyancy and frog kicks that push water *behind* you, not down. One bad fin kick into the felt-like algal mat or the silt between blocks will create a cloud 10 meters across that will not settle until long after you are dead of cold. Practice until you can turn 360 degrees without your fins dropping a meter. Wear small, stiff fins, not big scuba fins. Bring line cutters on both wrists - that whip kelp is 20 meters tall, thick as your wrist, dense as a cornfield, and utterly uninterested in letting you go if you get tangled. Don't fight it. Cut if you must, but remember: what you cut took centuries to grow.

A DPV [diver propulsion vehicle/scooter] is suicide here. You will go fast, get tangled, silt out, and lose your way. Go slow.

### 3. Navigation: The Only Thing That Will Save You

This place is designed to make you lost.

There are no hills. No landmarks. A wall that is knee-high and runs for 5 kilometers looks identical in both directions. A terrace that rises half a meter over a kilometer is invisible. The forest repeats but never exactly repeats, so your brain will hallucinate familiarity where there is none. Currents are so slow you won't feel them, but they will push you a kilometer off course over an hour.

**You must bring an Ariadne Thread.**

1.  **A physical tether.** A continuous guideline from your entry point. Not a compass heading - a *line*. The cavers' rule: if you can't follow it back with your eyes closed, you don't have one. Reel it out as you go. Tie it off on stone, not on kelp - the kelp will move a centimeter and abrade through. Use stone chocks. If you lose the line, you stop. You do not search.

2.  **An inertial beacon.** A hardened, pressure-proof pinger/transponder you leave at your entry portal with an acoustic return. Your wrist computer should be able to ping it and get a distance and bearing even in zero visibility. Bring two. Bury one under a cairn of small stones so the slow drift doesn't move it.

3.  **A dead-reckoning computer.** Compass is unreliable over endless basalt. Use an inertial nav / Doppler velocity log if you have access to one. If not, count kicks, time, and depth and write it on a slate every 5 minutes. Depth here is nearly useless since everything is flat, so you track *time and heading* like a desert traveler.

4.  **Markers you will actually see.** Small, dim, chemical glowsticks in a color that doesn't exist here - pink or acid green - left at intersections facing back toward home. They last for hours and don't pollute the water with light.

Rules: Never go out of sight of your line. Never enter the kelp forest without being able to see sky-water above you. Never go deeper into the forest to see what's "just beyond." There is no "just beyond." There is only more of the same for a distance larger than continents.

### 4. Psychological Preparation - This Is The Real Training

This is what kills ordinary travelers here, not equipment failure.

**Silence and Emptiness:** There are no animals. Your primate brain expects fish, expects movement, expects eyes looking back. When none appear for hours, your brain will invent them. You will hear things that aren't there in the hush - the groan of stone settling will sound like a voice. You will feel watched by the forest because a forest *should* be watched. It isn't. The indifference is total. Prepare for that by spending long hours in true sensory deprivation before you go. Float tanks, anechoic chambers, long solo winter hikes. If you need music or chatter to feel safe, this place will take that away.

**Time Dilation:** The forest grows millimeters per year. The pavements have been smooth for millennia. Your normal sense of progress will break. You will swim for an hour and feel like you have not moved, because the terrace under you is identical to the last one. You will check your computer and think it has stopped. Bring a watch that ticks loudly, or a metronome. Give your brain a human-scale time. Set hard turn-around times *before* you go and obey them like law. The cold makes you think "five more minutes" is reasonable. It is not.

**The Weight of Made Things:** This is the intuitive part most briefings miss. This is not wilderness. Every stone was cut and laid by hands. You will see the hammer marks. The peg holes. The patient, imperfect joints. And it goes on forever. Your brain will try to comprehend the labor and fail, and that failure feels like vertigo, like grief. This is a graveyard of a civilization that built until building became geology and then disappeared without a violent end. It just... stopped. You will feel the urge to be reverent, or to apologize, or to despair. Let it pass through. You are not an archaeologist here. You are a guest. Do not pry blocks up. Do not take souvenirs. The felt mat you tear will not regrow in your lifetime, or your grandchildren's.

**Loneliness:** Go with a buddy if at all possible, tethered together. If you must go solo, record a message for yourself to listen to on the way back. Talk out loud periodically to hear your own voice. The pressure and cold make you want to be still, to rest, to let a mote of debris land on your shoulder and stay. That stillness is beautiful and it is how people die here - not with a struggle, but with a quiet decision to stop.

### 5. How To Behave While You Are There

*   **Enter slowly.** Hang at your entry depth for five minutes. Let the cold settle. Let your buoyancy stabilize. Look around without lights for a full minute.
*   **Move like you are in a library.** The rustle of two fronds touching is the loudest sound for a kilometer. Any sound you make is an intrusion. Be quiet on purpose.
*   **Hover, don't land.** Stay a meter above the pavement. The algal felt is an ecosystem unto itself. Your weight will crush centuries.
*   **One hand for yourself, one hand for the line.** Always.
*   **Leave light on the stones, not in the water column.** It reduces backscatter and keeps the silt visible.
*   **Take nothing, leave nothing that won't dissolve.** Even a dropped bolt will be there in 10,000 years.

### 6. Getting Home

Your exit portal/translation point will drift if it's not physically anchored. The slow current that bends the whole forest will bend your ride home too. Anchor it. Mark it with light and sound. Set a timer that will recall you automatically if you don't check in. Agree before you go: no matter what you see - a perfect courtyard, an unopened doorway, a change in the kelp - you turn back when your gas hits 2/3, or your heat dips, or your clock says so. Whichever comes first.

After you return, you will not be cold for a long time. You will be too warm. You will miss the silence. The world above will feel too loud, too fast, too full of animals. Give yourself days to re-acclimate to noise and light. Don't make big decisions for a week. Drink warm things. Tell someone where you went, even if they don't believe you.

This place does not want anything from you. It doesn't need to be conquered or understood. It has already finished its story. If you go, go to witness how beautifully stone and cold and slow, patient plants can keep a memory after the makers are gone.

Go small, go slow, go tethered, and come back.
