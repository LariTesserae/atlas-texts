For an ordinary human, this is not a hostile place. It will not try to kill you. It will try to *keep* you, which is different and in some ways more dangerous because you won't notice it happening.

Think of it less like a cave and more like a living terrarium that someone built a factory inside and then walked away from. Everything in there is in equilibrium - heat, water, rust, leaf - cycling in place, not going anywhere. You are the only thing in there that is *supposed* to leave. If you don't prepare to leave, your body will just slowly join the cycle.

This is how you visit and come back out the same person.

### 1. Understand What It Is Before You Go Down

**It's not deep, but it is enclosed.** That 12 meter drop is enough to trap air. Cold air sinks, hot air rises, but here the bottom air is *hot and wet and heavy* and it doesn't want to mix with the dry surface air. It just sits there like water in a bowl. That means:

* Low oxygen, high CO2 at the bottom. You won't smell it. You'll just feel sleepy, then headache-y, then stupid.
* 100% humidity at 38-46C / 100-115F. That's bathhouse / sauna temperature. In dry heat you can sweat and cool off. In saturated air, sweat does nothing. Your core temperature climbs and you don't feel it until you're dizzy and nauseous.
* No wind, no sound, no animals. Your brain hates true silence. It will amplify every drip, every breath, every hum into something significant.

**It's young and sharp.** This isn't a worn limestone cave. This is fresh basalt that broke last week in geological time. Every edge will cut you. Every block is still figuring out where it wants to settle. The floor is a 3D puzzle of truck-sized slabs with ankle-breaking gaps between them.

**The biology is wrong.** Those pale, waxy ferns aren't really doing photosynthesis the way you know it. They lean toward *heat*, not light. They don't need pollinators. They make spores that just fall straight down in the still air and accumulate. They are perfect because nothing eats them, which also means nothing has evolved to make them safe to touch, eat, or breathe. Treat them like beautiful mold.

**The machines are not dead.** The amber bulb is on. The cable is live. The generator is warm. The cistern still ticks. This is an idling industrial site, not a cold ruin. Assume every wire is live, every piece of metal is electrified or rusted through, and nothing has a safety rail anymore.

Threat Assessment for a baseline human: **LOW lethality if you stay <45 minutes and prepare, HIGH lethality if you stay for hours, get lost in the stillness, or fall.**

### 2. Preparation - On The Plain, Before You Approach The Mouth

Do this in daylight, early morning is best. At noon the white coin of sun is directly overhead and makes the rim glare so you can't see the drop. At dusk you don't want to be climbing out in the dark.

**Tell someone.** The scrub plain is flat, featureless, cracked, and hot. The mouth is only 30 meters across. From 200 meters away it's invisible. Leave a GPS pin, a physical marker - a pack, a bright tarp, a walking stick stuck in the ground - at the rim. If you are traveling solo in the multiverse, drop an anchor or leave a breadcrumb that doesn't rely on local signals. People have gotten lost *on the way back* because the plain looks identical in every direction when you're heat-drunk.

**Sun and surface gear:**
* 3-4 liters of *cool* water for yourself plus electrolytes. You will not drink the sweat water inside. You will lose more water than you think just from the humidity on your skin.
* Wide hat, sunglasses, sunscreen for the plain. Light, dry, long sleeves to keep the sun off. You will take most of this OFF before you descend - see below.
* Boots with hard ankle support and a sole you can edge on rebar. Not sandals. Not trail runners. The basalt will chew through soft rubber and the gaps between blocks will swallow a foot.
* Gloves. Thin, grippy work gloves. Everything down there is wet, sharp, and either rusted or covered in biofilm. One slip on a fresh basalt edge without gloves and you will open your palm to the bone.
* Helmet or at least a hard cap. Condensation drops fat as grapes fall from 10 meters up. They are cold and startling, and you will jerk. Don't jerk into a low catwalk.

### 3. What To Wear/Bring For The Descent and The Bottom

You want to dress for a hot, wet, sharp greenhouse that might cut off your air.

**Clothing:**
* Base layer: synthetic or merino, NOT cotton. Cotton will soak through in 10 minutes, cling, chafe, and never dry. You will be a walking damp towel down there. A synthetic long-sleeve protects you from spores and razor edges but lets sweat *move*.
* Bring a COMPLETE dry change in a waterproof bag left at the rim. Including socks. When you climb out, you will be soaked as if you fell in a pool. Getting into dry clothes immediately prevents the post-exit chills and that weird psychological crash.
* Eye protection - clear safety glasses. The spore fall is constant and the mineral water dripping off rust and rock stings.

**Air and Heat Safety - Non-negotiable for an ordinary human:**
* A small CO/O2/CO2 personal gas monitor clipped to your chest, at breathing height. If it alarms, you leave. No debate. No "just one more photo." The heavy air pools at knee height first, so crouching to look at moss is when you'll feel it most.
* If you don't have a monitor, bring a lighter. Not to test for gas - but the old rule holds: if your lighter struggles to stay lit at the bottom, the air is bad. Turn around.
* A timer. Not your phone which will fog instantly. A cheap digital watch or kitchen timer. **Set it for 25-30 minutes bottom time max** on your first visit. The place dilates time. The drips are minutes apart. The silence makes 10 minutes feel like 30 seconds. You will not feel how long you've been breathing hot soup.
* A headlamp with a *warm, low* setting. Don't rely on the amber bulb. It's tea-colored and only lights 10 meters. And don't blast a 2000-lumen white light - it will destroy your night vision and make the shadows between blocks absolute. Use low warm light so you can see where you're stepping.

**Other gear:**
* A walking stick or collapsible trekking pole for probing gaps between blocks. That tumbled ceiling floor looks solid but is full of dark crevices covered by moss mattresses.
* Small towel.
* Plaster/bandages and antiseptic. Cuts in that humidity infect fast.
* Zip bags for anything you bring - phone, notebook. Condensation gets into everything. Paper wilts in minutes.

### 4. The Descent

The wall is 12 meters vertical, then terraces. There is a rebar ladder bolted into the sharp rock.

Test every rung before you weight it. The basalt is young and the bolts are in fresh, glassy stone that can still crack. The rebar will be sweating wet. Use your gloves. Face the wall. Three points of contact.

Halfway down you will feel the change. The dry plain heat becomes *thick*. It doesn't get cooler in the shade like it should. It gets heavier, closer, like walking into someone's breath. Your glasses will fog. Your skin will prickle. That's normal. If you feel short of breath or get a metallic taste, stop, go up two rungs, breathe, check your monitor.

Don't jump the last two meters even if it looks easy. The blocks at the bottom are angled and slick.

### 5. At The Bottom - How To Be In The Room

It's roughly church-sized. You can see the far wall, but don't be fooled into thinking that means it's small. The floor is a maze of canyons.

**Move slowly.** Not for reverence - for safety and for perception. Your boots on grating will sound like gunshots. The fern fronds shift by millimeters. If you rush you will slip, and a twisted ankle between two house-sized blocks in hot, still air with no animals to hear you yell is a serious emergency.

**Where to step:** Stay on the tops of the big blocks where you can, or in the sandy grit channels between them. Avoid stepping directly on the deep moss mattresses - they are saturated sponges hiding sharp edges and holes underneath. They also hold the spore mats.

**What not to touch:**
* Do not touch, taste, or break the ferns. The pale, bone-colored fronds are waxy and beaded with water that has leached through warm basalt and rust. It will irritate skin, especially if you have small cuts. The spores are not evolved to be eaten - they are not evolved to be *breathed* either. Don't disturb a fertile frond and make a cloud. Look, don't brush.
* Do not drink any of the water. Not the wall seep, not the cistern drips, not the leaf-tip water. It's distilled sweat cycling in place, full of rock minerals, rust, and whatever the pale plants excrete. It never leaves the room for a reason.
* Do not lean on or try to operate the machines. That pump house tin will cut you and give you tetanus, the cable thrumming up the wall is live, the generator under the tarp is warm for a reason - either residual geothermal conduction or it's still trickling current. The cistern pipe that ticks is the only thing giving you clean sound; let it tick. The catwalk that ends in air is rusted through - its grating may hold your weight or it may not, and the fall onto basalt below is onto points, not a flat floor.

**Breathe.** Consciously. The air is so still that you will start to hear your own breath as loud. That's your cue to sip water, check your time, and notice how your body feels. Early signs of heat exhaustion in 100% humidity: you *stop* sweating, or your sweat stops cooling you and just sheets, you get a dull headache, you feel oddly calm and contemplative. If you feel unusually peaceful and think "I could just sit here a little longer" - that is the heat and CO2 talking. Leave.

**Listen.** You will hear plink... ... ...tick... ...plink. One revolution per minute from the fan. Your own heart. There is no other life to calibrate you. Bring a grounding ritual if you are sensitive to silence - quietly count your steps, hum under your breath. Don't let the perfect, un-nibbled silence convince you that time has stopped.

### 6. Leaving

This is the most important part.

Start your ascent *before* you feel you need to. When your timer hits 25 minutes, begin walking back to the ladder regardless of what you haven't seen. You will want to stay. The place is beautiful in a sterile, intrusive way and it makes you feel like a damp surface that belongs. That's the trap. You are not a tenant.

Climb slow. The rebar will be even slicker going up with wet gloves. At the top, the transition is violent: dry, bright, hot plain air hits your soaked clothes and you will feel a sudden, deep chill even though it's 35C outside. For a moment the brightness will feel like a different kind of darkness, exactly as you described, because your eyes have adapted to amber and shadow.

Sit at the rim for 10 minutes. Do not immediately walk. Drink your cool water with electrolytes. Towel off. Put on your dry clothes. Let your lungs get dry air again. Check yourself: any cuts? Any rash where water sat? Any persistent headache, cough, or wheeze from spores?

### 7. Aftercare

* Shower as soon as you can. Wash your gear.
* Air out anything that was down there - boots, pack, gloves. That damp gets into stitching and will bloom mold if packed away wet.
* Watch for 24-48 hours: itchy eyes, sore throat, skin prickling that doesn't fade, tiny red bumps where condensation sat. The pale spores are not highly toxic, but for an ordinary human they are a strong irritant and can trigger a fungal sensitivity. If you develop a cough that wasn't there before, get checked and tell the doctor you were in a hot, enclosed geothermal sink with stagnant plant spores.
* Write down what you saw while it's clear. Memory of that silence gets edited quickly into "peaceful" when it was actually "empty."

---

You don't need to be an expert in geology or xenobotany to visit this place safely. You just need to respect that it's a young, sharp, hot, closed room that doesn't have anything in it that needs you.

Go down, be damp for a little while, look at the impossible pale leaves that lean toward warmth instead of light, listen to the one-bulb hum and the eternal plink, and then climb back up to the dry plain while you still remember why you want to.

Leave the pump house humming to itself. Leave the ferns perfect and un-nibbled. Take only the feeling of condensation evaporating off your skin in the sun.
