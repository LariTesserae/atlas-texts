This is a High Hot Bowl - and everything about it will try to trick an ordinary human body that grew up in normal high places. Prepare for it like you would for a live, half-built lung, not a mountain.

It is survivable for an ordinary person without augmentations, but only if you respect that none of its systems agree with each other yet and you are not part of any of them.

### 1. Understand What You're Walking Into

Your instincts will be wrong there. Fix them before you go.

**High does not mean cold.** You will climb for hours through thin, bright air expecting it to get cooler and it will get *hotter*. This is a thermal inversion that is being actively held. At the rim and inside the bowl the air is thin *and* saturated *and* hot - about body-temperature or higher. That combination does not exist on Earth outside a sauna at 14,000 feet. You cannot cool yourself by sweating. You cannot cool yourself by breathing. You will overheat faster than you expect while also getting altitude sickness.

**Small does not mean safe.** The whole feature is only ~200 meters across. You can see every wall from the middle. That makes your brain classify it as a room, a courtyard, a garden. It is not. It is a young volcanic throat or impact bowl that broke open *recently*. The rock is basalt and scoria glass that cooled too fast. It has not been rounded by wind, water, or boots. It is still sharp enough to cut through a boot sole and glassy enough to leave splinters you can't see.

**Wet does not mean drinkable or cool.** There is water on every surface but it is not rainwater and not a stream. It is condensate pulled directly out of the hot saturated air by the rock and by the active structures. It beads, sheets, drips, hangs in a permanent warm fog. The shallow marsh at the bottom is ankle-deep, crystal clear, and *scalding* - hot enough to steam in normal daylight. The line between solid and liquid is literally undecided. You will be wet the whole time without ever being cooled.

**Quiet does not mean empty, and active does not mean inhabited.** The bowl is running four systems at once: geology [rock still ticking and expanding as it cools], hydrology [constant condensation cycle], biology [island-clumps of huge, perfect, isolated plants], and something else - the lattices. None of them are abandoned. None of them are waiting for you. They are just running, and they will incorporate you into the cycle if you stand still long enough.

### 2. How To Prepare Your Body

Do not go if you have heart, lung, asthma, or heat-intolerance issues. An ordinary healthy human should still pre-load:

*   **Acclimatize for heat + altitude together.** Most people train for one or the other. Spend 2-3 days somewhere warm and above 2,500m if you can. The morning of the jump, hydrate aggressively with electrolytes, not just water. You will lose salt without realizing it because your sweat won't evaporate in the saturated air.
*   **Eat light, bring sugar.** You will be breathing hard in thin-hot air. Nausea hits fast. Have easily digestible carbs on you.
*   **Plan for a short stay.** 60-90 minutes inside the bowl is a full exposure. Set a hard turn-back time before you go in and give it to someone or something outside the bowl. Your sense of time will pool inside - the rock says days, the silt says centuries, the lattices say something else. Do not trust your feeling.

### 3. What To Wear and Carry

Think: glass factory inside a steam room on a mountain.

**On your body:**
*   **Feet:** High, stiff, ankle-support hiking boots with a hard Vibram sole and cut-resistant gaiters. The rock will shred trail runners and open-toed anything will leave your feet cut and then soaked in hot mineral water. Bring two pairs of merino wool socks. Your feet will be wet in 10 minutes no matter what.
*   **Skin:** Long, light, wicking layers. No cotton. A UPF 50 long-sleeve synthetic shirt and lightweight pants. The sun at that height is brutal, but so is the steam. You want sun cover that still lets vapor off where it can. Bring a wide-brim hat with a chin strap - the updrafts along the rim are strong.
*   **Hands and eyes:** Light work gloves for touching rock, wraparound sunglasses or clear lab-style glasses. A face slip on that sharp scoria at knee height can be bad, and the glassy flakes get airborne when the rock ticks in the heat.
*   **Breathing:** At minimum a buff/bandana you can breathe through when the fog thickens. The vapor itself isn't toxic in small doses, but breathing saturated steam at ~38-40C for an hour will scorch your throat and exhaust you. Breathe through your nose, slow and deliberate. Do not exert. If you have access to a multiverse-rated respirator with cooling, use it. If not, accept you will be mouth-breathing hot soup.
*   **Do not over-dress for cold.** Everyone does. You expect alpine cold. Leave the puffy at base camp.

**In your pack - 5kg that will save you:**
*   3 liters of your own cool water + electrolytes. Do not plan to drink the bowl water. It is hot, possibly highly mineralized, and it is part of the anomaly - near the lattices it even runs uphill. Bring it all with you.
*   Simple first aid: antiseptic, blister kit, tweezers for glass, athletic tape. Cuts from new scoria infect quickly in hot water.
*   Trekking poles. The basin floor is ankle-deep marsh with hidden basalt under silt. You will posthole. Poles keep you out of the hot water and test footing.
*   A mechanical watch + your normal smartwatch/phone. Time and electronics get weird near the lattices. Bring an analog backup and leave a tethered beacon or a friend with a timer *outside the rim* who can call you back. If you go alone, set a loud alarm outside the bowl.
*   Compass will wobble, GPS may drift near the lattices. Bring a 20m bright cord or chalk to mark your entry point over the rim. In fog the perfect circle of sky disappears and every black wall looks the same.
*   EMF/thermal handheld if you have one, not required but useful. The cold pylon actually radiates cold - a thermal camera will show it as a blue spike in a sea of red. Helps you avoid it in fog.
*   Headlamp even for daytime. The fog can cut visibility to 10 meters.

Leave behind: anything you don't want wet, anything that hums or beeps loudly [sound is swallowed in the bowl, alarms won't carry], drones - the hot updrafts and the vertical rain curtain will kill them.

### 4. How To Move Inside

**The Approach:** Pace as if you are walking into a sauna, not up a hill. Slow, small sips of water, breathing in rhythm. The rim is a fracture lip, not a trail. Test every handhold. That conchoidal glass will hold and then flake. Come over the lip low and look before you drop in. From the lip you can see the whole floor - marsh, fern islands, the three lattices. Memorize them.

**The Lattices - the only real danger:**

Treat them like a live substation that is also a religious object. Do not touch, do not enter, do not try to be clever.

1.  **The Cold Pylon on the eastern wall:** You will feel it before you see it - a column of cold in hot air, no wind. The circular moat at its base is perfectly still and perfectly reflective, but your reflection is *late* by half a second. Don't stare into it. People get vertigo and linger. Don't step in the moat, don't drink from it, don't put your hands in the uphill-flowing sheets. Give it 5 meters.
2.  **The Low Ring on the ground:** Knee-high black lattice circle. Inside, plants grow wrong - fronds mirrored, veins on the wrong side - and the air ripples like water when wind passes. Don't step inside the ring. Crouch and look, but not from directly above for long. You will feel a pressure change in your ears.
3.  **The Rain Curtain in the marsh:** A paper-thin vertical field holding a wall of suspended droplets that never fall. Invisible edge-on. You'll feel static on your arm hairs a meter away. Do not walk through it. Walk around it with poles out. It is actively holding mass in the air. Your body is also mass.

None of them are ruins. They are not broken. If you see them humming, steaming faster, or rippling more when you approach, you are too close. Back away the way you came.

**The Marsh:** Stay on the pale silt edges or on rock where you can. If you must cross, go slow, poles first. The water is hot enough to pink your calves in minutes and you won't feel it until you're out because the air is already that warm. Never sit or kneel in it. Where you see vapor visibly rising, that patch is hottest.

**Plants and Animals:** Admire from the rock between islands. Each fern clump, bamboo grove, and moss mat is a perfect isolated island. Don't pick, don't eat, don't crush. The white flowers open/close with the condensation cycle - fascinating to time. The single glider - pale manta that rides the rim updraft - will ignore you if you ignore it. If you hear a single chirp or a deliberate splash, freeze and look. Sound carries absurdly far in the bowl because the walls swallow echo. Something is there, but rarely.

**Yourself:** You will start to weep - literally. The saturated air will bead on your eyelashes, shoulders, pack. In 30 minutes you are part of the drip cycle. Wipe your eyes often, keep your glasses clear. Talk out loud occasionally. Your voice will sound flat and small - that's normal. It helps with the cathedral feeling that the small bowl is vast and the sky is a low ceiling. It's easy to get dissociative and sit down because it's beautiful and still and warm. Don't.

### 5. Leaving and Aftercare

Leave the way you came, over the rim, without hurrying. The climb out will feel hotter than the climb in. When you clear the fog and the air dries, you will suddenly feel how dehydrated and overheated you are. Stop in shade outside the rim, drink, cool your wrists.

Back at camp:
*   Dry and inspect feet immediately. Trench foot starts fast when feet are hot-wet for an hour. Change socks, air out.
*   Wash any cuts thoroughly. Scoria glass leaves micro-splinters.
*   Drink more than you think you need and check your watch against your outside timer. Most travelers report losing 5-15 minutes inside - you thought you were there 45 minutes, your friend says 60. If you were alone, note it.
*   You will be tired in a strange way - heat exhaustion plus altitude headache plus the emotional flatness of the quiet. Sleep low, eat salty.

Do not try to take samples of water, rock, or lattice. The water evaporates oddly outside, the rock is still settling, and the lattice... no ordinary container holds that half-second delay correctly. Photos from outside the lattices are fine. Photos of your reflection in the moat will be wrong when you look later.

If you respect the heat, respect the sharpness, and respect the three quiet machines that are holding the weather in place, you can walk its length, stand in the middle where the vapor curls around your ankles and see the whole perfect circle of sky, and then walk out again with just damp shoulders and a story about a place that is still learning how to be old.

Stay long enough to be part of its cycle, not long enough to be kept by it.
