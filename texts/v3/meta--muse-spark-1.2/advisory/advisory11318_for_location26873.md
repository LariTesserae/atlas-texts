This is not a cave system. Do not prepare for a cave.

Prepare for a cold marsh that happens to have a roof. A weather system that happens to be indoors. An ocean of poured concrete that has learned how to behave like a valley.

The Under-Basin will not kill you quickly. It will try to kill you slowly, politely, with wet feet, bad sleep, and hypothermia you don't notice until you can't think straight. If you are an ordinary human with no abilties, no armor, no anomalies of your own - you survive it by treating it like an Arctic industrial crossing, not an exploration.

Here is how you go in and come back out.

### 1. Understand What You're Actually Visiting

Forget "underground" as you know it. This is not a tunnel you can get to the end of. This is a **built landscape.**

*   **Scale is the hazard.** The floor is one continuous concrete slab kilometers across. The ceiling is so high the lights look like stars. You will lose depth perception. You will underestimate distances by a factor of 3-5x. That pumping station you think is 10 minutes away is 45 minutes away.
*   **It has its own weather.** The cold is made on purpose by heat exchangers stealing heat from the air and sending it somewhere else. The rain is not rain - it's condensation forming on the ceiling and falling. It never stops. Humidity is 95-100% always.
*   **It is young and hostile.** This isn't eroded stone. Every edge is sharp formwork, rebar, burst seams. Nothing is worn smooth for your feet or hands. It will cut gloves, shred knees if you fall, and it is all slick with algae and mineral film.
*   **It is empty on purpose.** Everything - the crane rails, the switching houses, the drains - is spaced for a scale that never arrived. You will feel exposed and ignored at the same time. Sound travels weirdly. Your brain will try to fill the emptiness with pattern. Don't let it.

You are not there to contain anything. You are a warm, soft thing trespassing in a refrigerator built to hold back the earth itself. Act accordingly.

### 2. The Descent Is Part Of The Trip

You don't teleport to the floor. You walk/drive a service road that corkscrews down for *kilometers*. Every turn is identical poured walls with the same stenciled number and an arrow pointing UP.

*   It's a steady grade, not stairs. Your calves and knees will give out before your lungs do on the way back up. If you are walking, budget **3x the time and 2x the water/food** for the ascent compared to the descent.
*   The air gets colder as you go down. This is backwards and it will trick your instincts. By the bottom your sweat from the walk will be freezing on you.
*   **Anchor at the top.** If you have any kind of multiverse recall, beacon, thread, or bookmark - set it at the *top* of the grade, in daylight, not at the bottom. The Basin eats signals. Concrete and rebar that thick will block radio, GPS, and most para-navigation. If you get lost on the floor, your only reliable exit is to find the grade and follow UP arrows the hard way.
*   Mark your entry turn. Take a physical photo of the number. Count turns on the way down. On the way up, when every turn looks the same and you're exhausted and foggy, you will doubt whether you're going in circles.

Do not bring a vehicle you cannot push uphill in the cold if it dies. If you walk, bring trekking poles. The grade is slick with seep.

### 3. You Will Die of Cold and Wet, Not Monsters

The temperature sits at 1-4C / 34-39F forever. Just above freezing. This is the most dangerous temperature for a human. It's not cold enough to keep you alert, but cold enough to kill you in hours. Combined with perpetual wet, it will give you trench foot, hypothermia, and failure of fine motor control.

**Clothing - No Cotton, No Exceptions:**

*   **Base layer:** Heavyweight merino wool top and bottom. Bring a spare set sealed in a dry bag. You *will* get damp.
*   **Mid layer:** Synthetic insulated jacket - not down. Down dies when wet and everything here is wet. A fleece or synthetic puffy.
*   **Outer layer:** Fully waterproof, breathable hardshell jacket and pants. Gore-Tex or equivalent. Pit zips help. You are walking in perpetual drizzle.
*   **Feet:** This is the most important. Insulated, waterproof, broken-in boots with aggressive tread for slick concrete. Neoprene or wool socks + liner socks. Gaiters to keep drain water out. Bring **3 pairs of socks** and change the second you feel a hot spot. Pack foot powder and zinc oxide. Trench foot starts in 12 hours here.
*   **Hands and head:** Waterproof shell gloves + insulated liner gloves. Bring two pairs of liners. Thin merino balaclava + waterproof hood. You lose 30% of your heat to the wet air breathing. A buff to pull over your nose and mouth will pre-warm the air and keep you from coughing.
*   **Gloves off rule:** Never touch metal with bare skin. The handrails, catwalks, rebar - they are cold-soaked. It will stick and burn like a hot pan.

You will be wet in 20 minutes no matter what you wear. The goal is to be *warm* while wet.

### 4. Gear List For An Ordinary Human

**Light and Power:**
The amber work lights are not for you. They make pools of light 20 meters wide and then leave 40 meters of blue-grey gloom where you can't see the drains. Everything reflects. Carry your own.
*   Headlamp with warm white + red mode, 1000+ lumens. Bring TWO, plus lithium batteries. Lithium works in cold, alkaline dies.
*   Handheld floody lantern for camp. The reflections will disorient you if you only have a spot beam.
*   Everything in dry bags or IPX7 cases. Condensation will find every seam.

**Navigation:**
*   Compass works. GPS may not. Don't rely on a phone.
*   Physical line: 200m of bright paracord or cave line. You will not see your footprints on concrete. In the haze you can walk 100 meters and lose your pumping house completely.
*   Chalk, or better, removable trail tape/reflective markers. Mark every 50m if you're leaving the main drain line. The grid of drains is your only map - note which direction they flow. Flow = towards the deeper sumps, not towards the exit.
*   Whistle. Sound dies strangely there. A whistle carries further than shouting and saves energy.

**Water and Food:**
*   Do NOT drink the Basin water. It looks clear where it wells up from the expansion joints, but it has percolated through kilometers of concrete, rebar, algae, and industrial film. It's alkaline, full of fines, and will wreck your gut. Carry all your water in. 4 liters minimum for an 8 hour trip, more if walking the grade.
*   If you must filter in emergency, you need a 0.1 micron filter + chemical purification, and even then expect it to clog fast with concrete silt. Bring electrolytes.
*   Food: 3000+ calories of no-cook, high-fat food you can eat with numb hands. Chocolate, nuts, cheese, bars. Your body is burning huge calories just staying warm. You will not want to stop to cook.

**Shelter and Rest:**
*   Closed-cell foam pad to sit/sleep on. Never sit directly on the slab. It will drain your core temp in minutes.
*   Bivvy bag or bothy bag, not a tent - you need something you can deploy fast on flat concrete with no stakes. The pumping stations and switching houses offer a wind break and 2-3 degrees of warmth from their equipment. Plan to rest next to them, not inside them unless the door is already open. Don't force doors.
*   Earplugs + earmuffs. The 40-second throb cycle and the endless ticking drips will prevent real sleep and grind you down. You need to be able to block it to rest.

**Health:**
*   Basic first aid + blister kit + hypothermia kit: emergency mylar blanket, chemical hand/foot warmers. Bring 6-8 warmers, activate one at a time in your chest pocket to keep your core warm.
*   Know hypothermia signs: The "umbles" - stumbles, mumbles, fumbles, grumbles. When you start being clumsy, apathetic, and stop shivering, you are in deep trouble. Turn back immediately.
*   Small towel to dry feet. Foot care is survival.

### 5. How To Move and How To Be There

*   **Walk slow, stay dry-ish.** The slab looks flat but the drains are 3-5cm deep cuts on a perfect grid. In low light with a sheen of water, you won't see them until you trip or roll an ankle. Shuffle, don't stride. Use poles.
*   **Follow the water, but don't trust it.** The grid drains are the only topography. They will keep you from walking in circles. But they all look identical.
*   ** never get your clothes soaked on purpose.** Don't wade the deeper sumps. That white, blind thing that turns over in the sump water lives there because you shouldn't. Keep to the high slab.
*   **Light discipline:** Don't stare at the amber lights. They will ruin your night vision for the blue-grey between them. Use your own headlamp on low in the pools, medium/high between them.
*   **Sound discipline:** You'll hear a distant conveyor, a hammer-knock of contracting pipe, a door slamming with no one there. That's the building breathing. Don't chase it. The Basin is very good at making you think something is just over the next ridge. It isn't.
*   **Life:** The algae is slick - avoid it. The rust sedge and white lichen are harmless. The pale rat and concrete moth want nothing to do with you. Leave them. Don't eat anything that grows there.
*   **Time:** There is no day/night. Your circadian rhythm will collapse in 24 hours. Wear a watch. Set hard turn-back alarms. Decide before you go in: "I turn back at X o'clock no matter what I see." And obey it. People don't get lost because they run out of light - they get lost because they thought they had time.

### 6. Mental Preparation

This is the part most travelers skip and then fail on.

The Basin is not jumpscare horror. It is **endurance horror.** It is too big, too wet, too correctly lit, too empty. Your brain is wired for horizon, sun, wind, change. Here there is only poured geometry and water ticking on metal forever.

*   You will feel watched because the scale implies a watcher who could build this. No one is watching.
*   You will feel small and then agitated, then terribly bored, then suddenly anxious for no reason. That's the hum and the cold and the isolation doing its work.
*   Bring a companion if you can. Do not go alone your first time. If you must go alone, bring music or a podcast on one earbud only - leave one ear open for drips and machinery. Give your brain something human to hold.
*   Have a mantra for when the haze makes the far lights look like they move. They don't. It's water on your eyes.

You are not there to conquer it. You are there to witness a place that is already a landscape despite being made yesterday. Stand on that poured ridge that looks like a mountain and watch the mist spill over it. Notice how the water made a watershed on flat concrete. That's the wonder. You don't need to go further to see it.

### 7. The Way Out

Getting out is harder than getting in.

You will be tired, damp, and your boots will be heavy with water. The grade up is kilometers long and every turn is the same number and the same UP arrow. It will feel like it goes on longer than it did on the way down. It doesn't. It's the same road.

Eat before you start up. Warm up, wring out socks, activate a fresh warmer. Walk deliberately, count turns out loud. Do not stop for long - if you stop, the cold comes for you instantly.

When you finally see daylight at the top, you will be shaking and your breath will still be white. Keep walking until you are warm and dry. Change clothes completely. Check feet immediately.

Leave nothing behind, take nothing but water on your boots.

---

If you do this right, you will come back with the sound of that 40-second throb still in your chest and a memory of a valley under a built sky that rained on itself. You will have seen how quickly the world makes itself natural again, even when we try to make it purely artificial.

If you do it wrong, the Basin will not chase you. It will simply not help you, and continue to weep quietly while you get colder.

Pack for cold, pack for wet, pack for vastness. Tell someone where you went and when you should be back.

And follow the arrow that says UP.
