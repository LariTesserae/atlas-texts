This is a Pocket World. Not a biome, not a ruin, not a desert — a single closed room the size of a house that has learned to be a world.

If you go, understand what you're entering: a hot, dry, sealed listening bowl where two incompatible things — a town and a jungle — have reached an equilibrium without ever touching each other. It is not hostile. It is not safe for you. It is *complete* without you, and you are the contamination.

An ordinary human body is the wrong tool for this place. You are 60% water, noisy, wet-breathing, sweating, shedding skin and bacteria. This place is 0% water by design. Everything about your preparation is about managing that contradiction.

Here is how you prepare:

### 1. Understand What It Will Do To A Human Body

**You are going low and getting sealed in.** The entrance is a pressure seam, a crouch-door in buckled salt-crust. Outside is open pan — flat, reflective, wind-polished salt and basalt that holds heat like a stove top. Inside is 10-12 meters *down* from that pan, an oval blister about 30 x 15 meters. The walls curve over you. There is one mouth for air exchange. It breathes, but slowly.

That means:
*   **Heat is trapped, not radiant.** Outside is sun-hot. Inside is kiln-hot. The rock around you has been storing heat for a long time and re-radiates it from every surface. Air temperature inside will be 40-50C / 105-125F, still, with zero humidity. 0-2% RH. This is not "desert dry" where you can find shade and sip water. This is anhydrous. It will pull water out of every exposed membrane in seconds.
*   **Your mucosa will fail first.** In under a minute your lips will crack, your tongue will stick to your palate, your nostrils will crust shut, your eyes will feel like they are covered in dust even before there is dust. You will not be able to make spit. Contact lens wearers will be in agony in 5 minutes.
*   **You will not stop sweating, but your sweat will not help you.** It will evaporate instantly, leaving salt. You will dehydrate incredibly fast without ever looking wet.

**The air itself is the hazard.** The thicket on the west side — that dry, felted, green-grey forest of mineral fiber and salt-crystal fronds — is alive on heat and dust, not water. It is constantly moving, popping, splitting. It sheds a fine, scentless, ultra-light powder that hangs in the still air for minutes after every pop and collapse. The pocket is a dome. It focuses and amplifies every sound. A seed pod cracking sounds like a handclap next to your ear. Your own breathing will sound loud.

That powder is not pollen. It's silica/salt/organic fiber, hydrophobic and deeply irritating to wet human lungs, eyes, and sinuses. You will be breathing powdered glass and salt in a focused echo chamber.

And it is LOUD in a way that tricks you. You expect a sealed, empty cave to be silent. This one thrums, crackles, hisses, ticks and *whoomphs* as whole columns of the dry jungle collapse and are immediately re-covered. You will hear the rock expanding. You will hear plants growing. After 20 minutes in that focused bowl, without protection, you will have a headache from sound pressure alone.

### 2. What To Bring — And What Not To Bring

**On your body:**
*   **No exposed skin.** Long, loose, light-colored, breathable but woven layers — like caving coveralls or desert Bedouin layers. You want to trap a boundary layer of your own humidity near your skin while reflecting radiated heat. Shorts and tank tops will cook you.
*   **Head, mouth, nose, eyes sealed but not suffocated.** A P100 half-face respirator with elastomeric seal is non-negotiable. Not a surgical mask, not a bandana. You need filtration for fine mineral dust plus humidification of your inhaled air. If you don't have a respirator with exhalation valve, at minimum a well-fitted N95 with a damp gauze underneath. Bring sealed goggles — not glasses — that seal to your face. Ski or lab goggles work.
*   **Ear protection two ways.** Bring soft foam earplugs *and* low-profile earmuffs. You will want to take the muffs off to listen for a minute — because the place is meant to be listened to — but keep the plugs in. Without them, the amplified cracks inside the dome will leave your ears ringing for hours and you will not hear your travel partner if you have one.
*   **Knee pads, gloves, helmet, and grippy boots.** You crouch to enter. The floor is salt and basalt, polished then rough. The terraces are hand-stacked basalt blocks without mortar — beautiful, ancient, fragile. They are rounded at the edges from centuries of dry heat and will wobble if you lean on them. Thin, tactile gloves let you steady yourself without destroying stonework or getting sliced by vesicular basalt. A helmet with a headlamp saves your skull on the low dome.
*   **Pre-hydrate, don't re-hydrate inside.** Drink 500-750ml of water with electrolytes in the hour *before* you enter. Use lip balm, nasal saline gel, and artificial tears liberally before entry. Bring a small 250ml soft flask of water/electrolytes *for exit*, but do not sip continuously inside if you can avoid it. Every exhale you make is adding water vapor to an anhydrous system that has not had water in centuries. Every drop of sweat that falls on salt changes the chemistry of the floor. You are a leaky water bag in a place that has evolved to never have water. Minimize your stay to minimize your contamination.
*   For the same reason: **no water bladders sloshing, no wet clothes, no perfumes, no sunscreens that off-gas.** Go in dry. Deodorant, insect repellent, vapes — leave them outside. The thicket metabolizes airborne minerals; don't give it organics it has never met.

**Light and Tools:**
*   **Bring a dim, warm headlamp on the lowest setting, and a small red-light backup.** Do NOT bring a 1000-lumen white spotlight. The pocket has its own light — a sourceless amber-grey diffusion that seems to come from the heat and the salt itself. Your eyes will adjust after 5-7 minutes and you will see the dusty viridian of the thicket and the soot-blackened niches of the town clearly. A harsh white beam destroys that adaptation, blinds you to the diffuse glow, and is disrespectful to the darkness the town and thicket have shared. Use red to look at your footing, warm low amber to look at carvings.
*   **No trekking poles that punch the floor.** Bring a soft walking stick if you need balance, with a rubber tip.
*   **A tether or breadcrumb for the mouth.** The pan outside is featureless. The seam is invisible until you are on top of it. And inside, GPS, radio, and compass are useless under basalt. In a multiverse transition, anchor your return coordinate *at the mouth on the pan side*, not at the bottom of the pocket. If your anchor is inside, the dome's acoustic and thermal distortion will make reacquisition fuzzy. Leave a physical marker — cairn, unnatural color — at the mouth.

### 3. How To Move And How Long To Stay

*   **Timebox: 20-40 minutes maximum for a first visit.** That's enough to dehydrate an unprepared human to headache and nosebleed. Set an external timer that vibrates. The pocket is hypnotic — hot, humming, dim, empty — and you will lose time sense. The heat makes you slow to decide to leave.
*   **Move like you are in a library and a greenhouse at once.** The terraces on the east wall are not ruins, they are a vacant town at 1:1 scale. Streets are shoulder-width. Cisterns never held water — they held grain, ash, salt. Woven hangings are stiff as bark. Shutters are carved with sunbursts. Everything is still in place because there was no catastrophe, just departure. Do not sit on walls, do not rearrange, do not take a souvenir stone. The balance line is precise: the thicket stops exactly a foot short of the lowest terrace wall, spores fall daily and do not sprout there. Hundreds of years of chemistry maintain that line. Your wet bootprint can break it for decades.
*   **Do not touch the thicket unless you must.** From a distance it looks lush, soft. Up close it is dry, furry mineral felt that shatters into powder. Brushing it triggers a crackle of hairs breaking and a cloud that will hang at face height. If you must examine, hover a hand near without contact, let the heat coming off it touch you. Listen. You will hear filaments creeping a millimeter at a time. Let it do that without you.
*   **Sit once.** The intended experience is on the edge of the lowest terrace, feet toward the thicket, back against town-stone, in the hollow air between. You will feel the empty density immediately — each terrace house is marooned in too much air, the whole town feels like a throat holding its breath. You will sweat, your respirator will warm your face, you will hear the pocket breathe through its mouth behind you, and the jungle crackle in front of you. That is the visit. You are not there to explore every alley. You are there to be the temporary volume that proves how self-sufficient the place is without people.
*   **Sound discipline.** Speak in whispers or not at all. The dome will carry a normal voice into a slap-echo. The inhabitants left long ago; the place has settled into a stable strangeness of rock-tick and plant-pop. Your voice is the most alien animal sound it has heard in centuries. If you go with another traveler, agree on hand signals beforehand.

### 4. Coming Out And Aftercare

You will emerge dryer than you have ever been. Your urine will be dark even if you drank before. Your sinuses will be crusted, your eyes gritty, your throat sore from breathing hot dust even through a mask.

*   Do not chug ice water immediately — you will cramp. Sip warm electrolyte water slowly in the shade of the pan.
*   Rinse eyes with sterile saline, not tap water. Use nasal saline spray and let it sit. Re-apply lip balm and unscented moisturizer. Blow your nose gently — the dust is inside.
*   Stay out of wind on the pan until your temperature drops. The salt flat outside is polished and reflective; you are still radiating heat.
*   Check your ears. If ringing persists more than an hour, you stayed too loud too long. Next time double-plug.
*   Brush all salt/mineral powder off your clothes *outside*, downwind, before you transit. Don't bring that anhydrous dust into a humid world — it clumps and can irritate your next site. Shake out boots.

### 5. Psychological Preparation

This is the part ordinary travelers underestimate.

There are no animals. Not even mites. No hum, no guano, no burrows. But the place is not dead — it is *violently alive* with plants that need no water and no pollinator. The uncanny comes from watching lush, restless growth in a place your brain insists should be dead still. It will rewire your idea of what "lush" means.

And there are no people, but every stone was dressed by patient hands until the faces kissed. You will see soot in hearth-niches, ladders worn smooth at the rungs. The emptiness is not tragic like a disaster site. It is like a pot that is empty on purpose. It makes you acutely aware of your own single body in too much space. People report a sudden, vertiginous loneliness there — not fear, just the clear understanding that they are vacating too, in a few minutes.

Go ready to be quiet and temporary. Do not go to conquer, collect, or claim. Go to sit for a little while at the bottom of the world, let a dry forest crackle around you, and then leave the door exactly as you found it, crouching low again through the seam, back out into wind and sun — carrying no water in and no stone out.

If you have asthma, chronic sinusitis, claustrophobia, heat intolerance, or a tendency to heat fainting, do not make this your first pocket. Pick a wetter, larger, louder ruin first.

If you do go, go in the cool of that world's morning, when the basalt is at its coolest. And tell the pan you are coming — leave a marker so the flat knows where to return you.
