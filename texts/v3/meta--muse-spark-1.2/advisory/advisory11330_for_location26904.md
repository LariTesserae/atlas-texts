This is not a hostile place. It won't try to kill you. It just hasn't finished making a place for you yet, and it won't make allowances. You are visiting a planet's workshop while the work is still happening.

An ordinary human can go there and come back fine, but you have to prepare for three problems at once: **thin air + wet cold + no reference points.** Most mountain travel only gives you one or two. Here you get all three, plus the strange psychological load of a huge, empty, industrially loud place with no human trace in it at all.

Here is how I would prepare if I were an ordinary, unmodified human going for 24-72 hours:

### 1. Your Body Is The Weak Link

At ~5,000m you are above where most humans can function normally without acclimatization. You cannot tough this out.

**Before you go:**
*   Spend 3-5 days at 2,500-3,500m if you can simulate it. If you are jumping directly via transit, assume you will be impaired on arrival. Your VO2 max will be ~50% of sea level. Simple tasks will leave you winded.
*   Talk to a doctor about acetazolamide for acute mountain sickness. Bring it. Know the symptoms: headache that doesn't go away, nausea, ataxia, wet cough, confusion. The only cure is to leave. Have a hard rule: if you can't walk heel-to-toe in a straight line, you transit out. No argument.
*   If you can bring supplemental oxygen, bring it. Even a single 2L canister with a low-flow regulator for sleep and emergencies will double your safety margin. This is not cheating. At this altitude, Sherpas use it for a reason.
*   Cardio fitness matters less than you think; acclimatization matters more. But be able to walk 10km with a 15kg pack on uneven wet rock while breathing hard and not panicking.

**What it will feel like:**
Every breath is deliberate. You will breathe 20-30 times a minute at rest and still feel air-hungry. Your heart will pound going up a 2 meter slab. You will need to stop constantly. Mornings will be worst - headache, dry mouth, frost on your sleeping bag from your own breath. Force water. Force slow.

### 2. The Cold Is Wet, Not Dry

This is not Everest dry cold. This is saturated, sweating, maritime alpine cold just above freezing. It is far more dangerous, because you will get wet from everywhere at once.

**Clothing System - no cotton, no shortcuts:**
*   **Base:** Merino or synthetic long underwear top + bottom. Bring two sets. You will sweat and you will get damp from condensation.
*   **Insulation:** Active insulation like a synthetic puffy or heavy fleece, not just down. Down dies when wet and you *will* be wet. Bring a belay-grade down/synthetic parka for when you stop moving.
*   **Shell:** Full waterproof hardshell jacket and pants, 20k+ mm, with pit zips. Not water-resistant. Water*proof*. The rock weeps continuously, the cloud drips, the marsh soaks you from below. Seam-taped everything.
*   **Feet:** Mountaineering or stiff backpacking boots that are waterproof and already broken in, with gaiters. Bring vapor-barrier socks or at least two pairs of thick merino socks per day. The ground is permafrost with a layer of melt on top - every step in the basin wrings water up around your ankle. Your feet will be wet within an hour. You need to be able to change socks and dry them inside your jacket.
*   **Hands/Head/Face:** Waterproof shell mitts + liner gloves, warm beanie, buff/neck gaiter, and glacier goggles or Category 4 sunglasses. Yes, even under flat cloud. The UV at 5,000m and the white-grey rock reflection will give you snow blindness in hours even without direct sun. Whiteout is total without shadows.
*   **Sleep:** You cannot sleep on the ground without insulation from permafrost. Closed-cell foam pad + inflatable pad combined, R-value 5+. A bivy sack or 4-season tent that sheds wind and condensation. A synthetic -10C/-15C bag. There is no wood, no fuel to gather. Bring a stove and gas canister and know it will burn poorly at altitude.

You will be damp the whole time. Success is being *warm* while damp. If you get cold and damp and the wind picks up, hypothermia starts in minutes. Change out of wet layers the moment you stop moving.

### 3. How To Move and Not Get Lost

**Navigation will betray you.** The flat, pearlescent light removes all shadow and depth. A 50 meter cliff can look like a 5 meter step until you are on top of it. A basin that looks 2km away is 10km away. Time of day is unreadable.

*   Bring a GPS with extra lithium batteries kept warm in your chest pocket, plus a paper map if you have one, plus a simple analog watch/compass. Do not trust phone magnetometers near fresh basalt - they can wander.
*   Set a hard anchor/return beacon the second you arrive and never lose sight of its signal if you can. Mark your path with temporary, retrievable wands if visibility drops - do not build cairns. This place has never had a straight line made by a hand. Leave it that way.
*   Move like the animals do: slowly, deliberately, on the driest slabs. Probe every marsh crossing with trekking poles. That sedge mat might be floating silt 30cm deep over ice-cold mud that will hold your leg. If you fall in, getting out wet at 2C with wind is an emergency.
*   Watch the rock itself. Those fresh slabs the size of houses are still settling. They are not weathered stable. Avoid walking under leaning faces, especially when you hear the press groan or the ice pop. Rockfall is silent until it isn't, and the damp air muffles it.
*   Do not approach geothermal fissures closely. The steam is hot enough to scald, the ground around them is thin crust, and some vents pulse with hydrogen sulfide or CO2 that pools in hollows on still days. If you smell rotten eggs or feel suddenly dizzy near a vent, move upwind and uphill. A small personal gas monitor weighs nothing and could save you.

**Water:** It is everywhere and none of it is safe to drink untreated. Rock flour, sulfur, and whatever the waders have left in it. Bring a filter + chemical backup and melt from the cleanest seep you can find, not the stagnant black pools.

### 4. How To Camp

There is no shelter. You have to make it.

*   Do not camp in the lowest part of the marsh basin. Camp on a slight rise, on a broad, stable slab on the lee side of a boulder that breaks the wind. Check that the slab isn't weeping heavily and isn't directly above a fissure.
*   Expect to not sleep well. The mountain works all night. The glacier press groans under your feet, the penstock jets roar, the ice jackhammers itself at noon and ticks all night, the wind whistles through tubes like a factory whistle. If you close your eyes it sounds exactly like heavy industry. It is easy to imagine a machine coming closer. It's just water and heat and pressure. Bring foam earplugs if you need to sleep, but keep one ear open - you want to hear rock moving.
*   Keep camp absurdly tidy. Food smells travel forever in still cold air. Those wolves and snow leopards have no fear of humans because they've never seen one. They won't seek you out, but they will investigate. Hang or canister your food 100m from your tent, downwind. Don't eat where you sleep.

You will be alone in a way that is hard to simulate. The herds are far away, the birds are always overhead but never near, and there is not a single line on the landscape that says someone was here before you. After a few hours the emptiness stops feeling peaceful and starts feeling exposing, like you are the only moving part that doesn't belong to the machine. That is normal. Bring a chronometer, a book, a small task with time limits. Without sun or shadows, time dilates. People who don't watch the clock think they've been out an hour when it's been three, or vice versa.

### 5. How To Be With The Life

You don't need to worry much. The scale is vast and dispersed.

*   Watch from far away. 200m minimum from ibex/yak herds, 500m from any predator silhouette. Use binoculars. They are not aggressive, but they are wild and they are used to moving miles a day. If you crowd them, you force them to waste energy they need in this cold.
*   Don't follow pika whistles into boulder fields unless you can safely climb out. Their cracks are deep.
*   The birds will be your best company. They will wheel low under the cloud for hours. Let them.

### 6. The Mental Preparation

This is the part most travelers skip.

You expect a mountain to be old, wise, carved. This one is young, loud, and unfinished. There is no trail to assure you that you're going the right way. There is no ruin to make you feel connected to history. There is no shelter to make you feel held.

The beauty here is not cozy. It's industrial and indifferent. Water hammering out of a cliff at turbine pressure just because pressure exists. Ice pressing rock flour into silt because it has to go somewhere. Steam venting on a timer with no one to watch the gauge.

If you go expecting wilderness quiet, the constant groan-hiss-roar will make you anxious. If you go expecting to *listen* to a planet learning how to be a mountain, it becomes awe.

Go for a short time the first time. 12-24 hours is enough. Have an absolute, non-negotiable return condition: "I leave if X happens" - whiteout, worsening headache, wet to the skin with no way to dry, or just a feeling that you are too far from your beacon. Obey it even if the light is beautiful and the herd is right there.

When you leave, you will carry the cold with you for a day. Your boots will never quite dry. Your ears will keep hearing that low press-groan for a night after, and then the silence back home will feel strangely empty.

It's worth it. There are very few places left - in any universe - where you can watch geology happen at human speed, where sweating stone becomes a river in front of you. Just don't ask it to be finished for you. Bring everything you need to be a small, warm, dry, well-located piece of life patrolling through for a little while, and then go home.

**Pack checklist in brief:**
Beacon + comms + personal locator, GPS + batteries + compass, supplemental O2 if possible, gas monitor, -15C synthetic sleep system + double pads + bivy/tent, stove + fuel + lighter, waterproof hardshell top/bottom, synthetic insulation, 2x base layers, waterproof mitts + liners, beanie + buff + glacier goggles, stiff waterproof boots + gaiters + 4x merino socks, trekking poles, filter + tablets, 3L water capacity + thermos, 3000+ kcal dense food, first aid + blister + altitude meds + earplugs, headlamp + extra batteries, binoculars, repair tape.

And tell someone exactly where and when you're going, even across universes.
