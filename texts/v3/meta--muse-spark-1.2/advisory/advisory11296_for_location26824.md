You don't pack for Nizhny-Kol. You pack for the room *under* Nizhny-Kol. That is the whole problem. The town above is a decoy — a cold, sparse, human town that any northern traveler could survive. The pocket below is not a town. It's a single, wet lung of a room that thinks it's a world.

If you go, you will not be exploring. You will be dropped, squeezed, and then slowly absorbed into a carpet that was already waiting for you. Prepare accordingly.

This is how an ordinary human — no powers, no enviro-suit, just a body — survives it and understands it.

### 1. Understanding What You're Actually Visiting

This is not a cave, a basement, or a forest. It's a **scale inversion.**

From above, the human world looks sparse and the wilderness looks empty. Down there, it's reversed. The human structures are the sparse interruption — four lonely, hand-made attempts at order set far apart in a lot of space. The wilderness is total, dense, continuous, and *on*. Every surface is alive, and that life is touching every other piece of life. There are no gaps. Moss is not growing on the walls; the moss *is* the walls, the floor, the ceiling, the pillars.

You are not visiting a place with biology in it. You are climbing inside a living tissue, at near-freezing temperature, that is holding a shape only because it's too cold to move quickly.

It is YOUNG terrain held in stasis. The basalt is still sharp because water hasn't had time to blunt it. The fish hasn't rotted because winter never left. Nothing happens for months. Then one warm day it all shifts an inch and grows over what you left behind.

Keep that in mind. You are not a visitor to a stable place. You are a warm interruption in a cold stasis system that has been waiting to prototype you.

### 2. Physical Preparation — The Cold That Does Not Bite

This is the most dangerous part for an ordinary human, because it will not feel dangerous.

It is 1-4°C, 100% humidity, no wind. It does not bite and burn like dry arctic cold. It *holds*. It soaks. Your breath will hang. Condensation will bead on your eyebrows, your collar, your pack, and fall off you like you are a wall yourself. You will not shiver violently. You will just get slower, quieter, and sleepier until you are hypothermic.

**Wear:**

*   **No cotton. No down.** Both die when wet. Wool base layer, wool mid-layer, thin wool hat. You will get wet. Accept it.
*   **Waterproof, but soft.** Tall, soft rubber boots with a thick wool/neoprene sock inside — like fisherman wading boots, not hiking boots. The floor is a foot-deep sponge of peat that gives and drinks. Hard soles will post-hole and tear the mat. You want to distribute weight. Bring a second pair of dry socks in a ziplock bag deep in an inside pocket — not in your pack, in your *pocket*, because your pack will be damp in five minutes.
*   **Knees and hands.** You will crouch. There is nowhere to stand comfortably. The ceiling is two men high but root-columns hang down. Bring thin, waterproof kneeling pads or just accept wet knees, but bring thin nitrile or wool gloves. The peat water is full of spores, bacteria, and peat tannins. Any cut will weep. Your hands will be touching moss constantly whether you mean to or not.
*   **Cover your skin but breathe.** Long sleeves, high collar. Not for warmth — for spores. The air is a soup of liverwort spores, mold, midge dust. You don't need a gas mask — that would terrify the pocket and fog instantly — but bring a soft buff/neck gaiter you can pull up over nose and mouth if the air feels too thick. And do not wear perfume, deodorant, or insect repellent. Chemical smells are a violence down there.

**Time limit:** Plan for 30-40 minutes maximum inside. Set a silent vibration timer on your wrist. The quiet and the dim will make 15 minutes feel like an hour. You will not notice the cold winning until you try to stand up.

### 3. Light Preparation — Learn to be Dark

This is the hardest rule for humans to follow.

**DO NOT BRING A LANTERN, HEADLAMP, OR PHONE LIGHT.**

The pocket *wants* to stay dark. The rule is absolute: Light is food and light is forbidden. The pale lichen on the pilings gives off just enough faint white-green glow to see after adaptation. If you bring white light, every midge, moth, woodlouse and beetle will not attack you — they will urgently, silently, in total numbers, smother the light. They will cover your lens, your hand, your face to put it out. It is distressing to see and it stresses the entire room.

**How to prepare your eyes:**

*   Sit at the lip of the crack for 10 full minutes before you drop in. Let your pupils blow wide in the grey winter shaft. Do not look at your phone.
*   Bring a deep-red filter if you *must* have emergency light — a single red photon is less offensive — but tape it down to the dimmest possible and keep it pointed at the floor, covered with your hand. Better to bring nothing.
*   Train yourself beforehand: sit in a truly dark room at home for 20 minutes and notice how much you can see. You will need that patience. Down there, after 10 minutes, the moss will separate from the beetles. After 15, you will see the salamanders hanging like fruit. If you panic and flash light, you reset to blindness.

You want to arrive with eyes already willing to see in near-nothing.

### 4. Movement and Sound Preparation — Be a Deposit, Not an Intrusion

This place is quiet in a way that makes you loud. There is no wind, no birds. The only soundtrack is *tit... tit... tit* — water falling from root to peat — and the soft sigh of moss drinking. Insect legs are muffled by that wet felt.

If you crunch, stomp, or talk at normal volume, you will feel obscene.

**Practice:**

*   Move at 1/3 speed. Place foot, let peat rise around it, wait, shift weight. Do not drag.
*   Do not talk above a whisper, if at all. Whisper to yourself before you go to calibrate how little sound carries.
*   Keep your hands still unless you are touching deliberately. That wall ripple — when a shadow makes a thousand isopods rearrange at once — is triggered by your movement. If you thrash, the whole room flinches. If you are still, it forgets you and returns, and you can actually see it breathe.
*   Breathe through your nose. Your mouth breathing will fog and drip.

You are carpet, not a visitor. Move like furniture being slowly grown over.

### 5. Psychological Preparation — The Shelf That Already Exists

This is the part no gear fixes.

Down there, causality feels reversed. You will find a perfect, moss-formed shelf at waist height, empty, with a lip. It is exactly crate-sized. No one made it. Next to the human cairn is a green moss replica cairn that was there *first*.

The place will make a hollow for you before you know you need it. You will feel an absurd, strong, quiet urge to fill it. *To put something there. To build a little wall. To stack a stone. To leave your glove on the shelf to see if it fits.*

That is the test of this pocket. It prototypes human building in plant, and then waits to see if you will complete the circuit.

Prepare for this by deciding **before you enter** what you will do:

*   **Bring nothing to leave.** No offering, no coin, no crate. Or — consciously bring *one* small, biodegradable offering if you must — a smooth wooden bowl, a pinch of salt fish — and place it deliberately on the shrine and leave it. But decide in advance. Do not improvise down there. The moss will use your improvisation.
*   **Bring nothing to take.** Do not peel moss with beetle-gardens off a beetle. Do not pick a glowing lichen. If you take a piece of the living wall, you are taking a piece of the city. And it will hold you in stasis in return — that fish eye nested with beetles has been there three winters because the cold kept its debt.
*   **Bring a task for your hands and mind.** A small waterproof notebook and a soft pencil — not a pen, it will clump. When the urge to build becomes strong, sit on the peat platform and draw the shelf instead of filling it. Make yourself an observer, not a builder, for the first visit. The pocket respects careful seeing more than hasty making.

Also prepare for the feeling of being **sparse**. There are four human structures and acres of moss. You will feel profoundly lonely and profoundly watched at the same time. Hundreds of eyes — salamander, spider, woodlouse — will be on you, not hostile, just tending. The town above will sound impossibly far away, as if heard through water. If you are prone to claustrophobia, this is not tight claustrophobia; it is *soft, wet, alive* claustrophobia. The walls are breathing. Practice sitting still in a small, dim, quiet space before you go.

### 6. Entry, Exit, and Aftercare

**Entry:**
The crack is sharp basalt under torn roots. It will cut gloves and palms. Turn sideways. Exhale going in — your chest needs to be smaller. Have someone know you are down there. The ground is young and the peat sighs. A one-day thaw can settle the whole pocket an inch while you are inside. Check the weather: go only in deep, stable cold. If the boardwalk water above is starting to flow instead of being hard, do not enter.

**Exit:**
You will be damp, cold, and spore-covered. Do not stand up fast — blood pressure drops in that cold. Crawl out backwards the way you came, hands feeling for root holds. Once up on the causeway, do not go straight into a hot house. Let yourself warm slowly. Wipe off, change socks immediately.

**Decontamination for an ordinary body:**
When you get home — or to your base — wash everything. Peat water in larch tan and moss spores will irritate lungs and skin for days if left. Shower, wash hair, rinse nose and mouth. Check boots, seams, ears for tiny hitchhikers: white ants, flat beetles, leeches in the ankle peat. Air your clothes outside in cold dry air, not inside.

Check your mental state the next morning. People dream of green shelves after. People feel the urge to stack stones on their desk. That’s normal. It fades if you let it.

**Final checklist for the ordinary traveler:**

[ ] Wool layers, no cotton, two pairs wool socks in ziplock
[ ] Soft rubber boots, thin gloves, buff
[ ] Knees prepared to be wet
[ ] No white light — eyes dark-adapted 10 min at lip
[ ] Red dim backup only, taped
[ ] Waterproof notebook + pencil, not pen
[ ] Vibration timer 30-40 min
[ ] Someone above knows you dropped
[ ] Decision made: I will observe, not fill, not take
[ ] Slow breath, slow feet, quiet mouth

If you do it right, you will spend half an hour crouched on a peat mattress, soaked to the knee, barely seeing, listening to water fall from a root to a footprint pool, watching a pale cricket clean its antennae on a larch piling furred with living light, while a grey salamander pulses its throat at you.

And you will understand why the town above thinks it is sparse, and why the room below knows it is not.

You will leave a shallow depression where you sat. By spring, moss will have made it into a shelf.
