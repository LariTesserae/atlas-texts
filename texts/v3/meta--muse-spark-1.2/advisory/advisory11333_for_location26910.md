This is the High Megastructure - sometimes called the Cold Mountain, the Factory Mountain, or the Silent Lattice. It is not a hostile place. It will not hunt you. That makes it more dangerous for an ordinary human, because you will be tempted to think it is safe.

It isn't. It's an indifferent place built at a scale and logic that was not made for you, and your body and your mind will fail before the place does.

If you are going anyway, prepare to visit it like you would prepare to visit a high-altitude industrial glacier that is also quietly non-Euclidean.

### 1. Understand What You're Actually Visiting

Your brain will keep calling it a mountain. Correct it.

It is not a mountain with buildings on it. It *is* building all the way down. There is no bedrock, no soil, no "outside" to fall back onto. Every surface is poured concrete, welded steel, grating, factory floor, retaining wall, pipe chase. That means:

* **There is no ground to catch you.** Every "valley" is just a deeper tier of structure. A fall is not a fall onto earth, it's a fall onto a lower catwalk, pipe, or cistern 20-200 meters down. Rails exist but are inconsistent. Vertigo and fall trauma are the #1 killer of first-time visitors.
* **It is brand new and it will cut you.** This place has not been weathered smooth. Every edge is sharp. Formwork grain on concrete will cheese-grate skin, weld beads will slice palms, stair nosings are crisp 90-degree angles. Tetanus, lacerations, and punctures happen fast. It looks clean, it is not sterile.
* **It is slow, thin, bright, and quiet.** Its dangers are not dramatic. They are altitude, cold, sun, damp, exhaustion, and getting lost in a space that does not map.

### 2. Your Body Will Not Like It There

Treat it as a 4,500 - 5,500m alpine environment with industrial hazards.

**A. Air & Cold:**
You are above the weather. The air is thin, dry, and cold in the way metal is cold. The sun gives you almost no warmth, only glare.

* You will be hypoxic. Even if you are fit, expect headache, breathlessness on stairs, poor sleep, bad judgment after 2-4 hours. If you have asthma, anemia, heart conditions, or uncontrolled blood pressure, do not go without medical clearance.
* You will be hypothermic while sunburned. The air steals heat through conduction from every metal surface you touch, while UV reflects off concrete from every angle. You can get frost-nip on your fingers and a blistering sunburn on your nose at the same time.
* Sound does not carry. Your voice will sound flat and strangely close. Shouting for help doesn't work. Whistles and radios do.

**Preparation:**
* Acclimatize for a day or two at 2,500m+ before translation if you can. Bring acetazolamide if you know you get altitude sickness.
* Dress like an alpine ironworker: moisture-wicking base layer, insulating mid-layer [fleece/wool, NOT cotton], windproof/waterproof shell. The damp will soak cotton and keep it cold.
* Sun protection is non-negotiable: UV400 glacier goggles or polarized wraparound sunglasses with side shields, SPF 50+ sunscreen for face/neck/ears/hands, lip balm with SPF, brimmed hat or hood. The light is white, diffuse, and comes from everywhere. Snow-blindness happens here without snow. Even in shade, you need the glasses.
* Touch nothing with bare skin for long. Bring thin work gloves with grip. Metal will burn you with cold.
* Bring supplemental oxygen if you plan to stay >6 hours or do any serious climbing. At minimum bring a pulse oximeter. If your SpO2 drops below 82% and stays there, leave.
* Move at 60% of your normal pace. You will over-exert on stairs without feeling it until you are dizzy.

**B. Water & Damp:**
It is never dry. Everything sweats. Water appears as condensation, beading, trickles that start mid-beam, sudden straight-down showers wrung directly out of the air, and shallow rectangular cisterns.

* All of it is non-potable. It has run over fresh concrete [lye, mineral leach], industrial oil, rust inhibitor, bird guano, and lichen. It will look clear and be chemically caustic.
* Your feet will be wet constantly. Trench foot and blisters will end your trip faster than the anomalies.
* Electronics will condense and short.
* Fog pools in the deeper canyons and can drop visibility to 5 meters in minutes.

**Preparation:**
* Waterproof, high-ankle, non-slip boots with aggressive tread and ankle support. Steel or composite toe is ideal - you will stub and drop things on your feet. Bring 2-3 pairs of merino wool socks in dry bags and change them the instant they get damp.
* Gaiters or waterproof over-pants. Pack a lightweight poncho that covers your pack. Quick-dry everything.
* Bring ALL your water and food. 3-4 liters/day minimum at that altitude, plus electrolytes. Bring calorie-dense, no-cook food - you cannot forage. Nothing green there is edible, and the animals are not food either.
* Do not drink standing water, even filtered. If you must, distill it first. A simple filter will not remove dissolved concrete and metals.
* Dry-bag everything: documents, electronics, med kit, spare socks. Silica packets help.

### 3. Navigation - The Real Hazard

The geometry is the reason experienced travelers tether.

* Maps don't work. You can walk down six flights and end up higher. A canyon that looks a kilometer away has rivets you can count, then snaps back. The same gantry-water-tank-nest repeats mirrored and stretched.
* Distant layers *reset* when you stare. Light has no single source; shadows disagree.
* Gravity is *mostly* down, except where it isn't. Dust falls sideways around certain corners. Water runs uphill for an inch before correcting.

The fauna and machines ignore this. You cannot.

**Preparation:**
* **Never go alone.** And never go without a line. Bring 30-50m of bright paracord/caving cord and a harness/carabiner. Tie off to a fixed point at your entry and spool as you go. When cord goes slack uphill, you will know the space has folded.
* Mark your path physically, not just mentally. Chalk, painter's tape, or UV-reflective trail markers. Do not trust cairns - the structure can duplicate them. Make *unique*, numbered marks and take photos with timestamps.
* Bring redundant navigation that does not rely on magnetism or logic: a mechanical altimeter, a bubble level, a plumb line [a nut on a string], a laser rangefinder. When the bubble says level but water flows sideways, trust the bubble and test before you step.
* Compass is useless. GPS is useless. Use dead reckoning with line-of-sight and tether.
* The Drop Test: Before committing to any walkway, ledge, or stair, toss a small object [washer, crumb] and watch how it falls. If it curves, hesitates, or hangs, do not trust that section.
* Do not chase repeats. If you see the exact same arrangement for the third time, stop, backtrack along your line to the last unique marker. Trying to "solve" the loop will walk you deeper.
* Limit your range. Set a hard turn-back time and a hard turn-back distance - e.g., 45 minutes out or 200m of cord, whichever comes first. The slowness makes you think you have infinite time. You don't.

### 4. The Living Layer

It's abundant and silent and will not bother you if you don't bother it.

* **Insects/birds:** Clouds of moths/midges, flocks of swifts/starlings that bank at hard right angles around girders, pigeons with sucker-feet in every cable tray. They are silent. A flock can engulf you and block vision without sound. Don't flail - crouch, cover eyes and mouth, wait. They will pass.
* **Ground level:** Rats, rat-things, flat glossy maintenance crabs moving in lines inside pipes and under grates. They are not aggressive but they own the undersides. Don't put your hands where you can't see. Don't block their paths; step aside.
* **Do not feed, touch, or corner anything.** Do not eat anything. Do not try to catch a bird to see its joints. The ecosystem feeds on the building and itself; you are not part of it and you are not clean to it.
* Bring a P100/N95 dust mask. Guano dust in the deeper tiers, plus concrete dust, will irritate lungs in thin air.

The machines are also alive-ish but slow. Huge gears, stamping presses, looms, turbines - all turning glacially and silently. Keep loose clothing, hair, and cord away from pinch points. Just because it's slow doesn't mean it won't crush a hand. Do not touch controls. A floor that is idle now may start moving while you're standing on it.

### 5. Mind & Time

This is the part ordinary humans underestimate.

* It is profoundly quiet. Your footsteps and breathing will sound obscenely loud. That silence, plus the thin air and the identical, repeating, bleached-white vistas, causes derealization, time dilation, and anxiety within 1-2 hours, even in calm people.
* The light never seems to change much. You will lose track of time. Shadows move glacially. You will feel like you've been there 20 minutes when it's been 3 hours.
* Déjà vu is constant because you *are* seeing repeats. Your brain will try to force it into a map and fail, which is exhausting and nauseating.

**Preparation:**
* Bring earplugs or low-volume music if silence unsettles you, but keep one ear open for drips and moving machinery.
* Bring a watch with an alarm, set it to beep every 30 minutes. Force yourself to drink, check SpO2, check cord, note location.
* Have a clear extraction plan BEFORE you enter. Set an anchor/beacon/return sigil on your translation device and test it. Agree on an external person who will pull you out if you don't check in. Do not rely on "I'll find the way back." You won't.
* Keep a small notebook. Write down where you are, what you see, how you feel. When the place starts to feel dreamlike, the notebook is your anchor to linear thought.
* Know your abort criteria and honor them: persistent headache that doesn't respond to water/rest, confusion, ataxia [can't walk heel-to-toe], seeing doubling that doesn't resolve when you blink, cord behaving impossibly, panic that doesn't fade after 5 minutes of sitting. Sit down, breathe, pull cord, and extract. There is no shame in a short visit.

### 6. Packing Checklist for an Ordinary Human - Short Visit [2-6 hours]

**On your body:** Base layer, fleece, wind/waterproof shell, work gloves, beanie, glacier goggles, high-ankle waterproof boots, gaiters, wool socks

**Carry:**
* Water - 3L + electrolytes
* Food - bars, jerky, nuts
* Dry bags + spare socks x2
* Poncho
* First aid: blister kit, gauze, antiseptic, painkiller, Sunscreen, acetazolamide if prescribed, tetanus up-to-date
* Dust mask, earplugs
* Headlamp + spare batteries [even though it's bright, deep interstices are liquid-bright/dark-bright and you need real light]
* Whistle, signal mirror, personal locator beacon/radio if your translation method allows
* Paracord 50m + harness + 2 carabiners + chalk/tape
* Pulse oximeter, watch, notebook + pen that writes when wet
* Trekking pole - useful for testing ground/gravity

**Leave behind:** Cotton, open-toed shoes, drones [air too thin, geometry too fickle], expectations of understanding it.

### Final Intuition

If you stand still there, you will feel it: cool metal air on your face, feet damp, sun so bright it bleaches color, and this immense, sharp, damp lattice extending in every direction, dripping and breathing fog, layered so dense you can see through twenty factories at once, all turning slowly in silence while silent birds pour through the girders and lichen clings to every north-facing wall.

It feels like being a speck of warm, soft life on the outside of a cold, bright engine the size of a mountain range - an engine that was built yesterday, at full scale, and is already being slowly reclaimed by water and small stubborn things, and which was built slightly crooked so that space itself has to flex around it.

Don't try to conquer or comprehend it. Be polite. Be light. Touch as little as possible, take nothing, leave only chalk. Look, breathe slowly, keep your line taut, and leave while you still remember which way is up.

It will still be there, patiently dripping, patiently turning, when you get home.
