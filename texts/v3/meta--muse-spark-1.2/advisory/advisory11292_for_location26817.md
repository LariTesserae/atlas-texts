For this place, you do not prepare like an explorer. You prepare like a newborn. You are not visiting a city *in* a landscape — you are visiting a place that is still being decided. Nothing there is finished, including the rules.

An ordinary human body and mind are built for old, eroded, settled physics. This place is pre-erosion in every sense. It has not learned to be consistent yet. If you go, you have to compensate for that. Here is how:

### 1. Your Body Will Be Wrong There First

Before reality gets you, the mountain will.

**Air and Cold:** You are very high up. The air is thin, cold, damp, and full of fine basalt dust, coal smoke, and spores. It is not breathable for long-term exertion without help.
*   Bring supplemental oxygen, not just for emergencies. A lightweight concentrator or bottled O2 with a demand valve. You will think you are acclimated because you can walk — then you will try to climb a gantry stair and black out.
*   Bring a real respirator, P100 with spare filters, and wear it when the forges are firing or when you are near the moss. That moss sporulates constantly in the condensation hour.
*   Dress for permanent late-autumn dusk at 3,000+ meters. Heavy wool base layers, windproof canvas outer, waterproof shell. Everything will get damp every morning during the condensation hour whether it rains or not — water drips off *everything*. You need gloves that still let you grip wet iron, and boots with soft, gummy soles for glassy, moss-slick basalt. Hard hiking soles will skate right off.
*   No shadows and diffuse light destroys depth perception. Bring your own warm, directional light — a headlamp with amber and white modes. Amber cuts the industrial haze better. Do not rely on the city's sodium pools. They are designed to be islands.

**Light and Time:** Your circadian system will collapse. There is no noon, no real night, only blue -> violet -> black and back. You will not feel sleepy or awake at the right times and you will make bad decisions.
*   Bring a strict timekeeper. A mechanical watch, not one that syncs to local EM fields. Set a 24-hour alarm schedule and obey it regardless of what the sky looks like. Eat and sleep by the watch or you will wander for hours thinking it's been minutes.
*   Bring Vitamin D and a small SAD lamp. The sun is a rumor above the cloud deck. Locals have adapted. You have not.
*   Expect condensation to soak your sleeping bag. Bring a synthetic bag, a bivvy, and a way to hang everything. Nothing ever truly dries there. Bring anti-fungal powder and treat trench foot as a certainty, not a risk.

### 2. Navigation is Not Geography. It is Reality Hygiene.

This is the thing that will actually strand or kill an ordinary traveler. Distance, gravity, sound, and even the number of objects are not stable.

**The Rules Locals Live By — Learn Them Before You Go:**

**a) Never trust what you can see.**
A block that looks 1km away can be 4 hours one direction and 10 minutes the other. Chasms show a different time of day at the bottom. This is not an illusion. Space is being stretched as the mountain is born. 
*   Preparation: Do not navigate by sight. Navigate by *thread*. Bring 200+ meters of high-vis cord, pitons, and carabiners. When you cross an open bridge or empty green between knots, fix a line. The locals do. Their bridges are all on chains and cables for a reason.
*   Bring trail markers that are not visual — RFID tags, chalk that glows, or a spool of biodegradable tape you can leave. Visual memory will betray you. The water tower that is one when stared at and three in peripheral vision is not a trick. The duplication is real when you are not collapsing it with direct observation.
*   Rule: NEVER stare directly at a load-bearing joint, chasm edge, or duplicate object to try to "fix" it. Glance, note, move on. Staring forces a consistency the local physics can't hold and creates stress. That's why workers look *away* from welds. Practice soft-focus peripheral awareness meditation before you go.

**b) Gravity has potholes.**
Dust falling sideways, water dripping up for a few seconds — these are not curiosities. These are patches where down is now 30 degrees into the cliff. The grazers use them. You will not, and you will fall.
*   Bring trekking poles and keep your center low. Test every new surface by tossing dust or water first. Watch the animals — if the six-legged grazers are walking up a vertical wall, that wall's gravity is anomalous. Don't follow them.
*   Wear a climbing harness at all times when above ground level. Every balcony, gantry, and bridge is hung on chains and flex joints because the rock shears a centimeter at a time. You should be clipped in too. The groan you hear is not background noise. It is the mountain moving while you stand on it.

**c) Sound is precognitive.**
You will hear the hammer *before* it falls. Your own breath will echo before you exhale. This causes profound nausea, dissociation, and panic in humans because causality feels broken.
*   Bring good earplugs and active noise-canceling earmuffs. You don't want to block the industrial thrum entirely — you need to hear rockfall — but damping the pre-echoes keeps your brain from trying to reorder time.
*   Don't trust audio cues for danger. Look for vibration through your boots and hands first. The big pistons shake your ribs before you hear them. Trust haptics over acoustics.

**d) Move at 0.25x speed.**
The whole city runs slow for a reason. Fast, rigid movement tears.
*   Practice moving slowly and deliberately before you go. Do not run, even if disoriented. Sudden load on a flex-jointed walkway or a fresh basalt plate will snap it or you. The cranes move degrees per hour. Match them. If you feel impatient, you are moving too fast for the mountain.
*   Build slack into everything. Don't pitch a taut tent. Don't tie a tight knot. Leave loops. The ground *will* shift a few millimeters under you overnight.

### 3. The Biology Is Not Hostile. It Is Indifferent and Abundant.

You are the sparse species there. Everything else is dense.

*   Do not touch, feed, or corner anything. The translucent hawk-swifts never collide with cranes because they see airflow you can't — they will collide with *you* if you flail. The raccoon-things with compound eyes are everywhere in girders and they bite when startled. The dog-sized beetles eat coal dust and are not aggressive, but their carapaces will slice you if you brush them. Leave scraps alone — the foundry workers have an arrangement you don't understand.
*   The moss will colonize you. It regrows a foot overnight on warm tracks. It will grow on your pack, boots, and respirator if left still. Bring a stiff brush and scrape down every morning like the locals do. Check your lungs — if you cough green, leave.
*   Water: That beautiful still black pool reflecting the lamps is temporary and drains through a new crack without warning. Do not camp in a chasm floor or beside one. Collect condensation from metal surfaces with a clean cloth during the drip hour, then filter and chemically treat it. Do not drink pooled chasm water without filtering — it is full of mineral fines and spores.

### 4. Psychological Preparation — This Is the Hard Part

An ordinary mind wants consistency to feel safe. This place will not give it to you.

*   You will feel derealization. The diffuse light with no shadows flattens everything. The unreliable distance makes you feel like your legs don't work. The pre-echo makes you feel like you are remembering the future. This is normal for there. Do not fight it.
*   Before you go, train for ambiguity tolerance. Spend time in fog, in anechoic or echo-heavy places, with peripheral-vision exercises. Practice the mantra locals live by: *Note, don't fix.* See the three water towers? Note: "there are three when not stared at." Don't count again. Keep walking.
*   Bring a human anchor. Do not go alone. A second ordinary human to reality-check with — "Are you hearing the clang before the hammer too?" — is more valuable than any gear. Decide in advance on return triggers: if either of you can't remember how long you've been walking, if breathing echoes more than 2 seconds early, if you haven't seen another person in 40 minutes — you both turn around and follow your line back.
*   Bring a journal and write down *felt time* vs *watch time* every hour. It externalizes the temporal wobble and gives you something consistent to stare at. Locals maintain normalcy through repetitive industrial ritual — scraping moss, tending slow machines. Adopt a ritual. Tend to your gear at the same watch-time every day.

### 5. Your Exit Is Part of Your Preparation

Because the city is sparse and the mountain is still arriving, rescue is not coming.

*   You need a multiverse tether that does not rely on local space. Set your beacon on a stable frequency *before* you descend below the cloud deck, in the dun blue where physics still remembers itself. Test the recall twice a day. Distance distortion can detune it inside a chasm. Never trigger a jump from inside a chasm, on a flex-bridge, or while staring at a duplicate.
*   Mark your ingress point with something that cannot duplicate — a messy, entropic marker like a scattered pile of distinct colored stones and a recorded voice note. Clean, ordered structures duplicate more easily. Mess does not.
*   Plan to stay no more than 72 watched hours your first time. That is enough to experience that the world is still arriving without letting the mountain decide you are part of what should stay. The city leaves room for a reason. Do not try to fill it.

---

If you go well-prepared, this is what it will actually be like: Cold drips down your neck at "dawn" while every surface ticks. Your headlamp makes a small warm pool in the blue gloom that only goes ten meters before it dissolves. Somewhere a stamping press hits with a *thud* you felt in your boots a second before you heard it. A herd grazes vertically above you, defying a down you cannot feel. The moss is impossibly soft under your hand on rock that should cut you. You are alone on a street built for thousands, but you are watched by a thousand eyes that do not care that you exist.

And standing on that half-finished gantry, feeling the whole slow, loud, patient thrum of a city breathing in sync with a mountain being born inch by inch beneath it — you will understand why no one there asks why they built it. They didn't. It is *building itself*, and they are the part of it that learned to use tools.

Don't try to understand it fully. An ordinary human can't, and doesn't need to. Move slow, stay tethered, keep warm, don't stare, and leave while you still remember which direction is down.

Take a brush for the moss. You'll need it every morning.
