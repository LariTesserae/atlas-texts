If you go there as an ordinary human, you need to prepare to visit a place that is not hostile, but is *exhaustive*. It will not try to kill you. It will try to absorb you. It's the opposite of a dramatic mountain or a dead desert - it's a cool, wet, living table the size of a small country, where the city and the geology are the same thing. Everything about it is low, slow, damp, quiet, and endless. You prepare for it the way you would prepare to be a stone for a while.

Here is what that actually means:

### 1. Your Body Will Think You Are On A Mountain, But Your Eyes Won't

This is the most dangerous mismatch.

**The air is thin.** The description - breath showing in the mornings, light turning violet at noon, clouds forming *below* the cliffs - puts this plateau at 3,000 to 4,500 meters, like Tibet or the Altiplano. It doesn't *look* high because there are no peaks to compare it to. It's just... flat, and high. You will not get the visual warning your body expects.

*   **You must acclimatize before you arrive.** Do not jump there directly from sea level. Spend 2-3 days at an intermediate elevation if your transit method allows it. If not, plan to do *nothing* for your first 48 hours on the table except breathe, drink water, and walk very slowly.
*   **Expect altitude sickness.** Headache, nausea, poor sleep, shortness of breath on stairs. It will feel like a hangover. It is not in your head. Ascend stairwells slowly, speak in shorter sentences. Bring acetazolamide if your doctor will prescribe it, and ibuprofen.
*   **UV and dehydration will get you even though it's cool.** The sun is white and close. You won't feel hot, so you won't feel burned, until you are burned. Bring strong sunscreen, SPF lip balm, and dark sunglasses - snow-blindness level. The air is cool but very dry despite the wet ground, and you lose water with every breath. You will need to drink 3-4 liters a day even though you never feel thirsty in the cool. The water is everywhere, but see below.

**Train for endless, low-grade walking.** You will walk for hours a day on hard, uneven, slightly slick stone, at altitude, carrying things. There is no transport. It's not steep hiking, it's flat stamina. Train with long walks on pavement in the shoes you will bring.

### 2. You Will Never Be Dry Again - So Learn To Be Warm While Wet

This is not a place of lakes. It's a place of *seep*. The ground sweats. Every wall weeps. At 3pm it rains gently for an hour every day, then the whole city steams as the sun comes back out. You cannot stay dry. If you try, you will be miserable. If you accept it, you'll be fine.

**Cotton will kill you here. Wool will save you.**

*   **Clothing:** No cotton, no synthetics that stay clammy. Bring *many* layers of thin, undyed merino wool - base layers, mid layers, socks. Wool stays warm when soaking wet. Bring 3-4 pairs of wool socks and rotate them. You want loose, muted grey-brown clothing that looks like the stone. Bright Gore-Tex will mark you instantly and make you louder, socially and visually.
*   **Outer layer:** You need a long, hooded, windproof but breathable wool cloak or anorak, and a separate light waterproof shell you can pull on for the afternoon rain. Not an umbrella - the wind through the terrace labyrinth will turn it inside out. Think fisherman, not mountaineer.
*   **Feet:** This is critical. The stone is smooth, sorted by time, and always has a film of water and lichen on it. It is *slippery*. Bring soft-soled, high-grip approach shoes or felt-soled wading boots, not hard hiking boots. They must be ankle-supporting but flexible enough to feel the stone. Bring foot powder and a small towel to dry your feet every night to avoid trench foot. Your feet will be damp for weeks.
*   **Shelter/Sleep:** Houses are thick-walled stone that holds heat, with inward courtyards and hearths. They are cool, dim, and dry-ish inside, but the floor will still be damp stone. Bring a closed-cell foam sleeping pad - not inflatable, which will puncture on basalt - and a wool blanket or -5C sleeping bag. The stone will suck heat out of you from below all night if you don't insulate.

Hypothermia here is not from blizzards. It's from being 12C and damp for 10 hours straight while walking. Keep moving in the day, get to a hearth at night.

### 3. Water Is Everywhere And None Of It Is Tap Water

You will hear it before you see it - drip, trickle, gurgle from every joint. Springs bubble up in plazas, runnels braid across what looks like a street.

Do not assume it is clean. There are hundreds of thousands of people, alpaca-like grazers, pikas, and marshes all draining across the same braided surface. Giardia and other protozoa will be endemic.

*   **Bring a filter, not just tablets.** A Sawyer Squeeze or gravity filter that can handle silty water. The seep carries fine stone grit that will clog a small filter quickly, so bring a pre-filter bandana. Boiling is possible - every courtyard has a hearth - but fuel is scarce wood, and you will be asking someone to share their fire.
*   **Drink constantly.** Carry a wide-mouth bottle you can scoop from a cistern. Offer to help bail a cistern in exchange for water. That's the local economy.

Food is similarly sparse and low-tech. No supermarkets, no packaging. Sedge, cress, small grains, milk and meat from those sparse grazing herds. Bring dense, tradeable calories with you - hard cheese, butter, salt, dried meat, flatbread - and more importantly, bring *trade goods* that are valuable in a stone-age economy: good needles, coarse salt, a sharp steel knife, good wool thread. Money is meaningless. Usefulness is currency.

### 4. Navigation - You Will Get Lost Without Getting Lost

This is the strangest part to prepare for.

The city is continuous for a day's walk in any direction, but only 2-3 stories high. It's a horizontal labyrinth. Alleyways become rooftops become alleyways again. Stairs cut through living rock. From a roof you can see... more roofs, to the haze. From a street you can see stone walls on both sides. There are no towers, no signs, no street names, no high landmarks. Cloud shadows a mile wide crawl across it so slowly you can misjudge distance and time.

Ordinary human brains rely on vertical landmarks and noise to navigate. Both are gone. The quiet is profound. Stone absorbs sound. You will hear a child two terraces down as if they are next to you, and then nothing for minutes.

*   **You need analog navigation and ritual.** A compass still works, and you will want it, because the wind reliably moves west to east until the plateau turns it - that is your only large-scale orientation. Bring a paper notebook and map as you go. Sketch every courtyard hearth, every cistern, every stunted willow - those are the only landmarks. Count stairwells.
*   **Do not trust distance.** A day's walk still leaves you inside. You must deliberately mark your home base. Leave a cairn, a chalk mark, a strip of cloth - ask permission first - because every terrace looks like every other terrace when the light goes flat and shadowless on overcast days.
*   **Stay away from the rim.** The plateau ends in cliffs where the braided streams go over as mist-falls. On cloudy days the cloud country below erases the edge entirely. There is no railing. The marsh commons near the rim are beautiful and open and then just... end. Walk with a pole to probe ahead in mist.
*   **Technology dies quietly.** No wires, no glass, no metal beyond slate knives. Do not bring anything that needs charging and expect to charge it. Cold and damp will kill batteries anyway. If you bring a phone or GPS, keep it in a dry bag and treat it as a single-use emergency tool, not a lifeline. Bring a mechanical watch.

### 5. Social and Sensory Preparation

The people there live at the speed of hands and weather. They build low and thick, they mend walls stone by stone, they move water jars all day. There is vigor, but no hurry. No one sings louder than the wind for long.

*   **Be quiet.** Literally lower your volume by half. Don't shout across a terrace. Don't play music. Your normal city voice will carry weirdly between stone and make everyone look at you. Listen first. The cloth of small sounds - drip, scuff, bleat - is how people know where each other are.
*   **Be low and useful.** Offer to carry, to bail, to fit a stone. Sit under a lintel during the 3pm rain instead of running. Learn to sit still on a flat roof and watch a cloud shadow take an hour to cross the city. If you stand and gawk, you are a tourist. If you sit and help, you are a guest.
*   **Prepare for time dilation.** Because nothing is tall and nothing is fast and the light on overcast days has no shadows, days lose their edges. Morning is breath and cold, midday is bright, 3pm is rain and steam, evening is firelight. Without clocks you will lose track. Bring a simple daily routine and write it down, or you will feel untethered by day three.
*   **Prepare for vast, gentle loneliness.** Even though hundreds of thousands of fires glow at night like a fallen sky, during the day people are thinly spread. You can walk for an hour seeing only stone, moss, a harrier's shadow, a pika. It is urban solitude - surrounded by houses but rarely crowded. It's beautiful and it can become melancholic. Bring a journal, bring handwork, bring a way to be alone without being lonely.

### What To Pack - The Short Checklist

**On your body:** merino base layers x2, wool trousers x2, wool socks x4, soft-grip wading shoes, wool cloak/anorak, light rain shell, wide hat, sunglasses.

**In your pack:** foam sleeping pad + wool blanket/bag, water filter + bandana, bottle, sunscreen/lip balm, compass, notebook and pencil, mechanical watch, headlamp with extra lithium batteries in dry bag, foot powder and towel, salt/needles/knife to trade, high-fat food for 5 days, trekking pole.

**Leave at home:** cotton, anything shiny or loud, expectations of speed, privacy, or rescue. This place has been balanced in its own state for longer than you have been alive. It will not re-arrange itself for you. You rearrange yourself for it - lower, slower, warmer, quieter.

If you do, it's not a hardship posting. On the fourth or fifth evening, when the rain has cleared and the stone ticks as it cools and every cistern reflects the hearths until the whole table looks like it holds stars above and below, you'll understand why people cut their houses directly from the ground they stand on and never left.
