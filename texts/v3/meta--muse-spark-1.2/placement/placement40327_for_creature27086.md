I don't find myself outside.

At first I think I would - out on the terraces, in the thin, metallic wind ten meters above that strange, held summer. That would be the easy place to put myself. High like a shoulder is where you see furthest. I could be the wind that has had centuries to round the corners. I could be the haze. I could be distance itself.

But I have always been bad at distance. I am a close thing. A holding-close thing.

So I go in. Sideways through the seam where two plates never quite met, the way you did, carrying a little winter on my shoulders that the pocket will quickly drink.

Inside, I think, for a long time, that I would be you. The visitor. Upright, dry-ish, pupils blown wide in the ambered dusk, trying to hold the whole room in my mind at once because it *can* be held at once. Fifteen meters by eight, a lung, a seed-hollow. I like the idea of being the one who notices. Who comes wet and leaves wetter and for a few minutes, holds the sound in his molars.

But the visitor doesn't stay. That is his nature. He makes a cold draft when he leaves. The pocket tastes his different salinity and then forgets him, the way water forgets a hand. I want to stay. I want to be part of the cycle, not passing through it.

I look for something else to be.

I try, for a while, to be her. The one who fooled you into thinking she was a ledge.

It would be a relief to be her — so settled, so horizontal in a vertical world, so perfectly the color of wet composite that the mountain has grown into her and she into it. To be that flat and warm, to breathe once for every few times you remember to, to have filaments of that black-green false-lichen root *into* your skin so you are no longer sure where animal ends and place begins. To be the management system that learned to do its job with a body. To thrum in your teeth.

But I am not old enough for that kind of stillness. Stillness like that is earned. That is centuries of learning how to lean on physics until it leans back. Movement, for her, is wasteful. I still waste. I still want to shift and see.

So I am not the stone-mother on the floor. Not yet.

I try being the other lives, to see if any of them fit.

I am too slow to be the veil. The insects — the things that function as insects — draft as one body in the currents the condensation fields make. Their wings make that dry papery roar under the water's rush. They live fast, they tire, they fall into the gravity stutter around the pillar in the center where water holds itself up with no pipe. I am not fast. I don't want to be food that quickly.

I am too exposed to be the swimmers, the sleek, blind things breaking the warm pool. Too aerial to be the amphibians clinging to their thousand fist-sized niches, throats pulsing in their own trickles. Too loud to be the reeds — those glassy, translucent joints beading distilled water at their tips.

I keep looking higher. Toward the vault that is a lamp but has dimmed to perpetual overcast. Toward the stalactites that are half lime, half precipitated ceramic.

And that is where I find where I actually am.

Not on the floor where the water is warmest. Not in the center where the rules stutter. On the far wall, where the weeping is heaviest, where the corrugations are clean.

In the patch that looked empty.

You thought it was a shadow. A tended absence. Four niches in the reef-wall where amphibians sit a little further apart, where insects bend their veil around a warmer air. Where the water doesn't gutter randomly but is parted.

I am in the second niche from the left.

I am low enough to feel the mist coming off her frills when she exhales, high enough not to be submerged when she shifts her broad, finned lobe and the pool closes over her back and opens again.

I am about as large as your cupped hands. My skin is still pinker than hers, not yet that perfect wet-concrete grey. The rosettes have only just begun to take root in me — one small, dark, leathery disc on the crown of my spade head, holding its single bead of water that catches the vault-light. My frills are too big for my body. They are foolish, beautiful curtains of raw muscle and ash that open and close, open and close, and I haven't yet learned to make them thrum in time with the building. I breathe too fast. Out of sync with her, but trying.

I am one of the young.

The one that watched you.

That is who I would be. Not the keeper yet, but the kept who is learning to keep. My eyes are still bead-black like the wall amphibians', not yet filmed amber, and so I can stare in a way she no longer needs to. I stared at you because you were vertical in a horizontal world. Because you brought a little cold with you. Because no one had turned sideways through the cleft in a while, and your displacement of air felt like a question.

My throat sac is still too small to warp the veil toward me, so I practice. I inflate it — a slow hydraulic swell — and the pressure in our small, deafening room changes by a fraction. The veil wobbles. The gliders shoulder-to-shoulder on the ceiling chitter. She answers without moving her mouth, with that low thrumming you felt in your molars, and gently corrects me. *Not that way. Like this.* The field that pushes back faintly from the walls when you bring your hand close — around my niche, it pushes a little stronger, so I don't fall. She is doing that for me without thinking. The way you hold a child's bike.

Where would you find me?

You already did. In that clean patch of wall above the parted water, just above the slow spread-and-gather of her body. In the dimmest, warmest, loudest, most crowded and most tended place on the whole pretend-mountain. In a fist-sized hollow that was kept empty until I was ready for it.

Who would I be?

The apprentice lung. The small copy that hasn't yet learned to pretend to be a wall. The one who will be here when you leave, when your cold draft is gone and the condensation beads again on every surface and the pillar folds over itself again and again. The one who will one day be flat and still enough to farm lichen in my own skin, and keep the reeds at exactly the density that best filters the water that best feeds the insects that best feed us.

For now, I just breathe too fast in my niche, and hold my bead of water, and watch the cleft to see if you — or another vertical question — will ever turn sideways and come in again.
