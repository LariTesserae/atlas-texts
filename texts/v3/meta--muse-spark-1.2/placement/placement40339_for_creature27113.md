I don't know at first.

If you put me down in that country I don't fall straight into a body. I fall into the air.

Warm and close and heavy, that bath-warm air that doesn't move until evening. Green-filtered light coming down in shafts that have been chewed by leaves. The smell of wet limestone and leaf-mould and the faint iron of rot. The hush that isn't silence but the sound of stone swallowing sound. For a long time that's all I am — a breath held low over the plain, watching the horizon soften into haze a few hundred meters away in every direction.

Then the ground gives.

I am at the lip before I know to look for it. A palm's width of roots, a curtain of fig, and then that clean, just-broken edge still white as bone dust, still sharp enough to cut. Cooler air sighs up out of it, damp as a mouth. From above the forest looks unbroken. You have to be almost on it to see the oval of sky has a second oval cut into it, darker, rimmed in fern.

So that's where. Not on the plain, though the plain goes on for days. *In* the pocket the plain made when it inhaled. Forty meters across, maybe sixty, belled out underneath so the sky is smaller from the floor than the floor is from the sky. In the dim.

I start to try on shapes to see which one fits the dim.

At first I think I am one of the swifts. It's tempting — to be the thing that knows the rim as a threshold and not a wall, to drop like a stone and stitch the air of the room without a sound, to live in the pitted sea-bubbles of the young rock where the sea was only yesterday. But I am not fast like that. My pace here is wrong. I don't want to leave the pocket. I want to stay inside it.

Then I think I am a person. One of the sixty, one of the eighty if you count the tied-on infants. It's easy to try that on because their houses are so well-made for trying on — low, lifted just off the damp on sapwood stilts, wattle and pale mud, thatch so thick it could be the forest floor again. I try being the man by the cistern, crouched with a gourd to catch the wall-drip drop by drop. I try being the old man under the overhang where the bats fold like dead leaves, mending thatch by tying living vine over the rot and letting it do the holding. I can feel in my hands how not to pierce the stone, how to hold without nailing, how to be ready to lift a whole platform half an inch when the limestone ticks and gives a little more.

But even that is too deliberate, too much doing. The people themselves leave so much undone on purpose. Between any two houses a gap is left where a papaya came up on its own, or a bank of ferns, or helicon with its red claws. They leave room for pools to appear after rain and for peccary to pass through in the night and for frogs to keep a wall that no one touches. I realize I am not the house. I am more like the gap the house was built to protect.

So I try being smaller.

I am for a while the anole that runs green across thatch and turns brown on limestone, pink throat folding and unfolding. I am the ant river carrying leaf-circles bigger than my body along a fallen trunk. I am the fig root descending like a rope, feeling for a crack to knit.

Closer. But still not quite low enough. Not quite still enough.

You have to stand still on the causeway — those bright, unsmoothed slabs laid to keep feet out of the mud — for a long time before you see who has been there the whole time. The stone weeps without flowing. Condensation beads and gathers and falls in fat, infrequent drops. *Tick.* On a leaf. *Tick.* On the packed earth of the plaza where a woman has just swept the overnight tracks of beetle and snake and child, and the dust hangs in the column of light a moment before settling. Everything here is sparse and patient and utterly active. Nothing hurries. Everything is coming into being at its own pace.

And there — on the north side of a fallen block, where the moss is thick as a mattress, where the wall stays dampest longest — I find the shape that was waiting for me.

I am on the wall.

Flat to it. Small and brown as the mud, as the bark. Throat pale and pulsing. A male frog the size of a thumbnail, with a territory the size of a palm that I have kept for a whole season. I don't call in a chorus. That's not how we do it here. One soft *peep*. Then a minute of listening to the stone tick and the bat wings rustle and the water beetle turn a tadpole over without a splash. Then another *peep.*

I am not rare. I am ordinary. Abundant and ordinary, like the others. I am exactly fitted to the damp, to the porous young stone that hasn't learned to be stone yet, to the pocket that is still forming. The people know me by the pattern on my throat but they do not touch my wall. They built their town to leave my wall alone.

If you come to the rim at evening and look down, you won't see me. The thatch matches the leaf litter, the limestone dust matches the mud, the forest curtains the lip with hanging roots. From above it's just unbroken green. You have to descend, let your eyes adjust to the dim, let the warm air keep your skin beaded, and crouch until you are low enough that a dropped condensation bead sounds loud.

That's where I am: three feet up a weeping wall in the bellied room, in the gap left on purpose between two houses on stilts, under the ragged oval of lilac sky.

I am the one who is patient enough to make the silence audible.
