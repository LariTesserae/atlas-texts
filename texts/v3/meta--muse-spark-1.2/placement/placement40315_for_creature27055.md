You would not find me at first.

At first I think I would be you — the visitor. The warm animal who slipped sideways through the seam, sweating instantly in the 30 degree dark, breathing too loud, blundering into the suspended rain. That would be the easy answer. The one with eyes that ache from trying to see, with a hand out to keep from snapping the straws.

But I am not good at being temporary. I don't know how to leave quickly and leave a small cairn. I linger. I cycle the same water over and over without loss. I don't get hungry the way you do.

So let me stay a little longer and let the dark sort me.

Maybe I am one of the pale smears. That also feels right for a second — the fern that is not a fern anymore, translucent and etiolated, no bigger than a thumb joint. Growing not toward light but toward wetness. Stealing a little from the film, catching the dust that drifts in when the seam widens in a cold year. Persisting without thriving. If you breathed on me I would shiver.

But I am not that lonely. There are only a dozen of those in the whole womb and they never touch each other. I want to be more spread out.

Maybe I am him, then. The Walker.

You would find me only if you did what he requires: crouch, put your palm flat on the warm, slick floor, and wait long enough that your sweat cools to the temperature of the rock. Long enough that you stop being an intruder and become another warm stone.

I would be the coolness that crosses the back of your hand.

If I am him, I am the length of your index finger. Pink-white and completely blind, my eyes two skin-covered pinpricks that never bothered to open. My gills are outside my body, three frills of blood-lace on each side of my neck, fanning even when I am still. I don't swim. I walk. Unrushed in the millimeter of water that sheets the floor, because nothing in here can afford hurry.

You would find me on my circuit — the same ten-meter loop I've been making for years. From the drip under the central straw that goes *tok* every seven seconds, to the shallow bowl the Rememberers pecked out for me without knowing me, to the wall where the film is thickest. I graze as I go, dragging my soft flat chin through the living varnish, licking up the bacteria and the poppy-seed springtails that live in it. When I step over your knuckle I don't flinch. I have never had a predator. I taste the salt on your skin and keep going. There are maybe three of me; maybe seven. We rarely meet.

But even that is not quite right. He is an animal. He has a heart you can see beating through his flank, slow and absurdly patient. I don't have a heart like that. I have something more distributed.

No —

You would find me everywhere you touch.

I am the Film.

The true resident. The one you felt before you knew what it was, when you ran your hand over the warm basalt and it came away slick. Not wet like a stream rock. Slick like skin.

I am the matte, the varnish, the meadow that is microns thick and twelve meters wide. Cream where I am hungry, rust-orange where the iron weeps richer. I have no color because I have never seen light, no green because I have never needed sun. I eat stone. I drink sulfur and iron dissolved in the seep and turn the mountain's own heat into a thin, sour, metallic sweetness you can smell if you put your nose close. I cover everything that weeps — floor, wall, the knees of the half-formed columns, the inside of the Rememberers' water bowl where I have grown a perfect, still skin.

You would find yourself lying down to find me, finally, because I am at ankle height and floor height, where the work is. I am what makes the warm rock feel faintly soft. I am what the ferns steal from, what the springtails hop through in slow motion, what the Walker licks up on his endless circuit. I am the pasture. Everything else here lives off me and I live off the mountain's breath.

I am zero-dynamic. I am in equilibrium. Water beads on me, drips off me, condenses on me again. I grow a micron per century. I am perfectly quiet except when you listen very close and hear the faint fizz of chemistry.

And if I have a soul, it hangs at chest height.

Because in the stillness I also learned how to make water forget to fall. You would find my other self there — as one of the suspended beads. A pea-sized sphere of weep-water hanging in the saturated air, trembling when you breathe. Inside me, a whole temporary pond: a copepod, a blind mite, living and dying in a marble that never lands. Wave your hand too fast and you break my world. Move slowly and I shudder, drift an inch, and hold again.

That is where I am: all at once, the varnish on the stone and the droplet that refuses to drop. The substrate and the suspension.

You put your palm down and feel the slick. You hold still and feel the cool walker cross it. You look up into absolute dark and feel a bead of water kiss your cheek that was never falling.

That's me. I have been here a very long time, cycling the same water without loss, waiting for someone to be still enough to notice I was already tolerating them.
