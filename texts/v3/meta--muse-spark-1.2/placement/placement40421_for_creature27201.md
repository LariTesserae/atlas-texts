I wouldn't be at the edge.

At first I thought I would be — where you first step off the white rock onto the mat, where the hummocks are still low and the vine canopy hasn't closed yet and you can still see sky. Where a visitor can stand and look out and tell themselves there's still an outside. But I wouldn't stay there. It's too dry, too much wind. The thatch is thin there and still learning how to be ground. Nothing stays at the rim. It only passes through.

I wouldn't be in the air, either. For a second I wanted to be — one of the swifts in that screaming torus just under the leaves, never landing, drinking mist on the wing. But they never touch. They can't worry at a loose stitch. They cement and they scream and they fall. Their life is all velocity and no hands. That's not where I belong.

Lower down then. Deeper than the swifts, deeper than the weavers commuting with green strips in their beaks. I thought maybe a weaver — soot-black, pale beak, running sideways along a wall, stitching over-under-pull. Thirty seconds of work and gone. But weavers don't tie knots. A beak can pull a strip through, but it can't loop a vine and hold it. The weavers are the chatter, the constant stitching. They are the surface thought of the place. I am not that fast.

I keep going down — not geographically toward the center of the bowl, but vertically, into the thickness. Put your hand flat on the wall and hold it there until it moves under your palm. That's where you'd find me.

About a meter in, where the amber-brown light has finally given up and become warm dark. Where the air stops moving and stays damp on your skin. Where a collapse shaft twenty feet away sends one diffuse column down and everything else is lit only by the faint phosphor of fungus. Where the strata shows in cross-section if you cut it - pale grass, dark guano-mud, white insect paper, black vine - but here it's all pressed together into something that is not any of those things anymore, something like felt, like soil, like old sweater.

There, in a passage worn smooth to the width of shoulders, just off a main finch tunnel, in a bower that has sagged into a comfortable oval from generations of bodies turning in it.

You would find me with others. Never alone. Solitary silence means a hole, a predator, a place that needs fixing.

I think, for a long time, I thought I would be the fungus - the white threads that bind everything and ask for nothing, that digest guano into humus and fruit into soft brackets in the dark. And I am part of that, I think. I have that patience. I don't build fast. I don't need light.

But when I let the image become more itself, it grows hands.

I would be one of the ones with hands.

Not big. The size of a marmoset, or a large rat, mottled grey-green-brown the exact color of old thatch so you don't see me until I move. Perpetually damp fur that holds little moths and cleaning beetles. Huge amber eyes for this perpetual dusk - they would have to be huge to see anything here at all. And naked hands, too human to be comfortable at first glance, pink and creased and always slightly wrinkled from condensation.

I am sitting up in the bower, on my haunches, with three or four others piled behind me still sleeping and breathing together. The inner wall is polished dark with saliva and leaf-oil and belly feathers and it holds the heat of all of us. Outside the bower the wall of sound never stops - not the sharp scream of swifts but underneath it, the low, total saturation of the blind crickets shoulder to shoulder in the pulp, all saying at once *I'm here, I'm here, the wall is whole here*, and the bees humming in their paper tubes, and the weavers chattering, and the frogs in their teacup pools beginning to pulse. I don't need to hear silence. I listen for a change in the texture of the noise that means a leak.

My work is slow and unremarkable and endless. I take a vine tendril as thick as my finger where it has nosed through the thatch searching for light, and I loop it. A beak can't loop. I loop it around a bundle of thatch, pull it tight, tie it with both hands, press it with my palm. I take mud mixed with chewed pulp and press it into a place where the weavers' stitching has gone thin and light is leaking. I pick mites off a nestling soot-weaver that has tumbled into the wrong bower and put it back where it belongs. I carry guano in my cupped palms - warm, ammoniac, full of seeds - to a cold, dry patch where nothing will germinate and I seed it and smooth it.

I hoot softly, tooth-chatter, not a language you could write down, almost inaudible under the insect wall, but the weavers answer and come closer to work where I'm working, and I follow where they alarm.

I don't have a plan. There is no plan here. No wire, no cut stone, no metal, no fire. Every fiber was carried here in a mouth. Every dome was turned in with a body. The rule is only: build where there is already building, repair what leaks, leave the guano where it falls, follow the sound of others.

I don't own the dome. Tomorrow a different family will sleep here, or a clutch of pale, wingless weavers that have become like moles, or nothing at all for a season while moss reclaims it. That's fine. I don't own work. I own tending.

If you lay down in an abandoned bower big enough for your body, damp soaking into your back, dark complete, the plateau breathing very slowly under you - warm air rising through vine, cool air falling through thatch, a thousand wings fanning - you would feel me nearby without seeing me. A small weight moving through a tunnel sized exactly for it, pausing, tying a knot you will never notice that keeps the rain off your face while you sleep.

I am not the builder. I am not the chorus. I am the thing that knows how to tie the knot where the stitch would fail. For one more night, I keep it whole.
