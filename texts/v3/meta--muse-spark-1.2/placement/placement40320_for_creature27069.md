I don't find myself all at once. Not here.

At first I think I would be up on the rim.

Standing where you stood to see it whole — where the worn stone curves away and the sky becomes a circle and the whole basin is laid out like a watch face in deep dusk. That would be the human place. The observer's place. Boots dry, looking in, keeping the pump's thud at a distance so it stays rhythmic and not a vibration through the spine. I could be the witness there for a long time and think that was my role: to watch.

But the rim is too high and too dry for anything that lives here. The seep begins just below it. Everything real is already down in the wet. If I stayed on the rim I would only be visiting. I would be weather, briefly.

So I come down.

The slope is only two men high, but it is a different atmosphere at the bottom. Cooler by five degrees. The air thick enough to drink. My boots find the first terrace and it is as slick as a tongue, the Skin darkening under my weight. And here I think, maybe I am the one who walks. One of the catwalks, perhaps. Bowed steel plate, bolted, humming. The in-between place. Not stone, not water — the thing that connects without belonging to either. That feels honest for a while: a narrow, rust-slick bridge that creaks when something crosses it, that exists only to keep other things apart while letting them touch.

But the catwalks don't lead to the center. They lead to each other. They are a loop that forgot what it was for. I don't want to be a loop.

So I keep going down, slower now, because there is nowhere to actually walk. Each terrace is a shallow bowl full of its own sheet of water, each lip is a living black seam. If I try to be quick — Turners flicking, darting — I lose the place. This basin does not understand quick. It understands patience as a form of structure.

The Turners are too deep for me. Too pale, too eyeless, too content to be buried and to turn silt over forever without ever being seen. I love them but I am not them; I still want light, however dim.

The Still are too still. To hang motionless in cool air and breathe a reversal of condensation — to be a cloud that parts and re-forms — I would have to give up gravity entirely. I am heavier than that.

The Lip-Keepers are too faithful.Hundreds per foot, shoulder to shoulder, opening and closing on the exact line where water breaks. They live on an edge precisely one molecule wide. I have never been able to live on an edge that precisely.

So I go to the place I didn't notice at first because I thought it was only surface.

The Skin.

Or rather, the seam where the Skin is learning to be something else. On the third terrace down from the rim, on the north side where the transformer hum is loudest and the caged bulbs throw that weak amber that doesn't illuminate so much as stain the mist — there the Skin has lifted off the polished stone in a low tent, a blister a hand wide, leathery, plum-copper, sweating on its underside.

Underneath, the stone has gone white where it has been digested. The air there tastes most strongly of ozone and wet iron.

If I am anywhere, I am there.

Not the whole Skin — I am not a continent. I am a small, local negotiation. I find myself where the mineral film has loosened, where the water doesn't roll off but is absorbed, where the cool goes taut at a touch. I am underneath that tent, but also I am the tent.

I would be the thing that sets the wetness, but only for a hand's breadth. My job would be incredibly small and incredibly important: to regulate how long a bead of seep water stays on this one plate of blued stone before it is allowed to spill over the lip to the Keepers.

I would be leathery, matte, slow. A millimeter uphill per hour, against gravity. I would pulse out of sync with the pump — my own time. When the water runs thin over me I would tighten and darken. When it pools I would wrinkle and drink. The pale salt-spires near me would lean their sour steam toward me, and the gnat-clouds above would tilt slightly down to breathe what I exhale.

I would not move. I would not click or flick or glow. But everything downstream would depend on the small decision I make about wetness.

And on my back, where I have lifted off the stone, one of the Lanterns would have found me. A single, rust-colored, slow lung of light, cup-sized, breathing amber, leaving a luminous trail across my surface as it drinks the rusty weep from the pipe that sweats just above us. It would rest there for hours. Sometimes it would lift its blind head as if noticing me noticing it, and then settle again. We would share heat. We would not need to be more than that.

That's where you would find me, if you came back down the slope and let your eyes adjust again and stopped trying to see a creature.

Not at the center where it's deepest. Not at the rim where it's dry. Not on the iron, not in the air.

On the third terrace, in the cool, in the dusk that never deepens or lightens, pulled tight over cool grey stone, keeping one small plate exactly as wet as it needs to be while the pump thuds every seven seconds and the Keepers click and the Turners turn.

You'd have to crouch to see me. You'd think at first I was only a stain.
