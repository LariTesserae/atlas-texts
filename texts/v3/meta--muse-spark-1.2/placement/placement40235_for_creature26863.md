I thought at first I would be the climber.

That was the obvious place to put myself - boots on the scree, lungs working the thin air, hands on that raw, glassy basalt that hasn't learned to be weathered yet. I wanted to be the one who finds the seam. The witness. The one who slips sideways through the crack and kneels in the dark and watches.

I stayed there for a little while, in that idea. The cold on my back, the impossible dry heat on my face from inside. The smell of struck flint and old iron on the air. The observer with the headlamp that gets swallowed.

But that place doesn't hold. You can't be a witness in that room for long. The room is too small and too full. There is nowhere to stand where you are not already stepping on something that is alive and working. The air has nowhere to go, and neither do you. After a minute your knees ache from the stone's heat through your trousers. After two minutes your ears are full of that dry, encompassing clatter that is not loud in the way wind is loud, but *close* in the way a chest is close. After three minutes you realize the room has no horizon and your eyes have quit looking for one.

So then I thought I would be the air itself. Or the heat. That low sullen red-black glow coming up through the thinnest part of the floor where the mountain hasn't finished cooling. The part that has no job except to be constant. To radiate. To never flicker, never go out. To be breathed by ten thousand tiny bellows that take ten seconds to complete one slow inhale. That would be a good being: patient, mineral, unhurried. To be the reason for everything without having to do anything.

But heat doesn't taste. And this place is all about tasting.

Then I thought I would be the stone. The basalt itself, still holding the memory of being liquid, the flow lines and the frozen bubbles, the conchoidal fractures. The bowl that holds the pocket the way a geode holds itself shut. That would be simple - to be the thing that is eaten, grain by grain, bloom by bloom, the yellow-white sulfur sweat that reforms every hour no matter how many times it is scraped clean. To be inexhaustible and still shrinking, millimeter by millimeter, season by season.

That wasn't right either. The stone doesn't hammer.

It takes a long time for the wall to stop being a wall.

You have to kneel until your eyes stop looking for shapes and start seeing texture. Until that black-red enamel that you took for slag does the little shudder - one wire leg lifting, one plate shifting to take a weight - and the whole surface ripples and you can't unsee it again.

That's where I find myself.

Not at the entrance, where the cold still leaks in. Not at the edge where the raw stone is still bare.

In the middle. Where it is warmest. Where the floor glows dullest and the air shimmers and doesn't move. Where the press is absolute.

I am low and flat and heavy. My back is a single fused shield, the color of iron just before it begins to glow, maroon-black, drinking light. No wings. I will never open. The rim of my back is turned under so I seal to the rock and to the others around me like a tile. Like a cell. My legs are too thin for my body, black wire splayed wide because there is no room not to be touching someone else. My head is small and tucked under and almost all mouth - blunt, pale-tipped mandibles worn and regrown and worn again from scraping.

I don't have much in the way of eyes. Two clouded pits that can barely tell glow from not-glow. I don't need them.

I navigate by the trembling of other feet through stone, and by taste. That is why the others are with me - the pale ones, the crickets, beeswax-translucent with guts showing dark through their backs, antennae twice as long as they are, white threads that never stop combing the hot air a millimeter above my back. They slip where I cannot slip. They thread through the gaps we leave. When they want me to pause they drum lightly on my shield with their forelegs and I pause.

I find myself with a few grains in my jaws.

I have scraped them from a place on the wall that looked bare but wasn't. The stone sweats its bloom again and again, sulfur and iron-salt, a faint dust you can only taste, and I gather it with a bead of dark saliva and carry it. Three body-lengths a minute. That is fast, for us. The whole chamber is working but nothing happens fast. If you watched only me you would think I was barely moving. A leg lifts, holds, sets down. A pause. Another leg.

Toward the warm center, where the mill is churning. I set my grains down on the cake the size of a dinner plate, already dark and damp. The ones there are tamping it with their flat heads, turning it over. Behind me a cricket bends and paints what I left with a clear gloss that smells of vinegar and hot metal. It binds. It cures. Further along others are chewing that same cake, now black and hard as cinder, extruding it behind them as a pellet, pushing it to the margin where the living floor ends and the raw stone begins.

I will be there later, in that line. Receiving a pellet someone else made from something I scraped and something she glossed. Fitting it against the last one like a mason with no mortar. Bringing my head down like a tiny hammer.

Tap.

A long pause.

Tap.

On its own my hammer is nothing. You would not hear it. But there are ten thousand of us and the sound has nowhere to go in this sealed lung. It stacks. It becomes the weather in here. A dry clack-clack-clack that layers into a roar you feel in your molars before your ears name it. Chitin on stone, leg on carapace, the deeper hiss of the rock exhaling heat through microfissures underneath it all.

This is what I am - not a queen, not a soldier, not a foreman. There is no foreman. There are just enough rules - *scrape here, carry toward warmth, tamp, gloss, chew, press, tap* - that order appears out of us the way a current appears out of water. An assembly line made of the workers themselves. A factory floor with no building.

I am not building a nest. I am enlarging the only world there is. Millimeter by millimeter. A hand's width in a season. My dead are not carried away; there is no away. When I stop, I will be chewed from underneath by my neighbors and my shield will become part of the next cake and my softness will be rendered back into gloss. Under the fresh floor I have already tucked amber grains, eggs the size of dust, where the heat is steadiest.

I need nothing you think life needs. No green, no light, no water to drink. I make my water inside myself from the mineral and the air and keep it all. No rain has ever reached me and none ever should. If the seam ever widened and let in the wind and the cold thin alpine air that the climber breathes, I would stiffen and die in a day. If the mountain beneath me finally cooled, I would starve with stone still all around me.

But for now the bargain holds. A little sealed room, held shut, given over completely to being full. Not an inch of bare stone anywhere that is not tasted, tamped, and hammered by something alive. For now there is heat and time, and that is abundance enough.

If you put your hand down here, in the warmest part, you would be putting it on my back. You would feel me adjust, minutely, to take your weight, and then flow slowly, reluctantly around your finger like hot tar, and close again behind you.

I would not scatter. I have nowhere to go.
