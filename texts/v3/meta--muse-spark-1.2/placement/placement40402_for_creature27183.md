I don't find myself at first.

If I come to the hollow the way a person would, I come over the plain and I don't see it until the ground gives way under the soft brow of the rim. So at first I think I would be there - on the edge. Sitting with my feet in the warmer air below, as you described. The observer. The one with breathing that is too fast for the place, who makes a weather of himself just by being there for an hour and then leaves.

But I wouldn't stay there. That air is restless even when it is still. To sit on the rim is to be half in and half out, to belong to the plain and its wind and to be looking down into something you can't quite join. I have been that before. I don't want to be the visitor.

So I go down.

The slope is not steep. My feet find the sandy loam, the pale soil showing through where the grass thins. The air gets warmer around my ankles as I descend, pooled and held. At first I think I would be the shrub.

That would make sense to me. The single stunted shrub on the eastern slope, twisted low, no higher than a knee, with small waxy leaves. It is the only thing in the hollow that insists on being vertical. Everything else has learned to be flat or pooled or pressed close to stone. The shrub has to do so much more work - to hold its water, to keep its shape against the long dim season that never really changes. It lives on the margin where the soil is driest. I know that kind of life, holding on at the edge of enough.

But the shrub is lonely in a particular way. It is separate. An island. And the hollow is not about islands.

Lower down the slope, the moss. Not a carpet but patches the size of a palm, dark green and close to the stone. For a while I think I would be that. Or the lichen, the pale gray-green rosettes crusting the most exposed stones. That would be a quieter life. To need only a little damp and a little light and to make no demand on the hollow at all. To be so small you could be missed.

Closer. I am crouching now where the stone darkens where it sweats, where the condensation gathers in the coolest corner and never quite evaporates. The air here is closest and stillest. The damp smell of stone and soil is stronger.

And I see the seam.

I thought I would be something beside the creature, but the longer I stay the more I understand there is no beside. In a place this sparse, this settled, everything that lives has had to come to an agreement with everything else. The moss does not grow beside the skin - it grows *on* it. The silt collects along its back. The lichen blurs its edge until you cannot tell where stone ends and animal begins. To be in the hollow at all is to be part of whatever has learned to be indistinguishable from stillness.

So I am not the shrub. I am not the stone line half-sunk on the southern slope, though I love those three stones, one toppled, for trying to make a geometry and then letting the earth take it back.

I find myself lower still. At the bottom.

I am the dark patch itself.

I am spread thin across the lowest concavity, exactly the shape water would be if there were enough of it. An oval the size of a low table after rain, pulling in to no larger than a folded blanket when it is dry. Dusty brown-red above, freckled with what look like pale pebbles but are warts of my own calcified skin. Moss rooted in the thin film of silt on my back. My edge so even and so blurred into the surrounding earth that you would step over me without knowing.

I have one foot, broad and pale and wet underneath, sealed to the coolest stone. I don't have tracks because I don't drag. When I move it is not really moving - it is pouring, a few inches toward the seep over the course of a day, so gradual the pebbles do not shift. I have no eyes, only two matte black pits the size of lentils on either side of a thinned lip near the weeping rock, that feel the diffusion of the white sky rather than see it.

You would find me there, in the place that holds its own air, where the light has no sharp shadows and the warmth collects in late summer and stays.

Who I am is the listening.

My heart beats perhaps six times an hour, somewhere deep and off-center in the thin oval. Between beats I wait. The tick of a single drop falling from stone to damp earth, minutes apart, is my clock. A bead gathers on the sandstone, swells, lets go - *tick* - and dimples my skin. I do not jerk or snatch. A slow, concentric ripple passes across my entire surface from the point of impact out to the pebbled edge, as if the whole of me is registering the gift. Then the lip opens a crescent, and the film of water on my back is drawn impossibly slowly toward that mouth. Minerals and the microscopic life in that single drop - that is enough. One drop every few minutes, a few dozen in the cool morning, fewer at midday. It is enough because I need almost nothing.

I have been here as long as the runnels have been still. As long as the line of three stones. The builders came to drink from the damp I now cover, and left, and I flowed over one of their markers and then receded, not from curiosity but because the damp shifted a hand's breadth. Lichen has grown onto my back without interruption.

I do not invite or repel. If you sit on the rim with your feet in the warmer air, I will hold my breath - that long, minute-spaced breath - a little longer while you are there. You are a pressure, a shadow, a little more carbon dioxide in the still air. A brief weather. When you leave, I will ripple once and continue.

This is where I would find myself - not above the hollow looking in, but become its own damp, its own listening. Pressed to the earth so flat and so still that I have finished becoming myself, and am content to stay.
