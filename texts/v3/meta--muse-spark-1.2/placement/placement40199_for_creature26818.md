I don't know right away. That's the first thing this place teaches you.

At first I think I would be high up, because of course you are high up. I think I would be the Turner, because it is the only thing that gets to be above it all. That single raptor that never flaps, turning and turning on the heat coming up the cliffs, seeing the whole broken table at once — the labyrinth of bowls and fins and mesas as a pattern, as a map. From up there the intervals would make sense. You would see how the green pockets connect, how the water moves underground. You would not have to walk it.

But I am not built for that much distance. My eyes want to squint and then crouch. I want to look too close as well as too far.

Then I think I would be the stone itself. The ancient, poured-wax basalt, red-black and bone-white, warm from the sun, humming in my hollows when the wind finds me. To have been rained on for that long and to have settled into that final, fluted shape — to be so old I am finally finished eroding and have started to be played instead. That would be a good way to be here. Still, vast, and completely covered in song without having to make it.

But I am too impatient for that. I pop and creak. I hiss like the grasses do.

It takes a while for the image to steady. I have to breathe first.

Four seconds in through the nose. Pause. Six seconds out through the mouth. The thin warm air has an extra job in it, you feel it in your ribs before you feel anything else. I am sitting on bare rock that is not flat at all, though from far away it looked flat. It is warm as skin through my clothes. Lichen under my palm, a little crust of orange and grey. The wind is already going through me, not around me — a low hum in the sternum from some organ-pipe down in the chasm to my left, and a higher whistling from a pinhole somewhere behind my head. I am not deaf to it anymore. I am under it.

Where would you find me?

Not in the jungle basin where the trees sag with orchids and the air stays wet for hours after the ten-minute storm. I love that place but I don't live there. It's too loud in the close way — vines groaning an inch an hour, succulents popping, everything growing over everything else. I would suffocate there. And not out on the huge open of polished stone where you can walk an hour and see nothing and the sky is too big. I pass through there, but I don't stay.

You would find me in the interval between. Which, here, is the only real place to be.

There's a fin of sandstone about thirty feet high, curved like the back of a whale, with a spring at its foot that has never quite dried in living memory. It doesn't run. It sighs. It beads out of a crack and collects in a chain of three potholes that my grandmother's grandmother deepened just enough with patient pounding — not wells, cups. The smallest cup is no bigger than my two hands together and that one belongs to the blind white fish. I don't touch it. The middle one is mine. The largest one is for catching the storm.

At the foot of the fin there's an overhang that faces away from the afternoon violence, pocked with wind-flutes the size of my thumb and one the size of my chest. When the wind is right the big one booms once, very low, every seventeen seconds or so. It keeps time better than any clock.

That is one of my pockets. I hold six or seven of them in a territory that would be an entire country anywhere else, thirty square miles of mostly hallway — bare, ringing rock. But you would find me here most often, because this is the waiting pocket. This is where I keep the cache.

If you came in the dry hour, after the stone has steamed off and before the clouds start to build again from below, you would see me at first as a smudge that you thought was lichen. I am long-limbed and spare, darkened by the closer sun, my feet bare and callused flat as the rock itself. My chest is wide, unhurried. You would hear my staff before you see me — tap, tap, tap — not for balance but for listening, for the change in pitch that tells me hollow from solid, wet from dry. I wear a net bag, a copper knife green as the lichen, a coil of fiber rope that I have repaired more times than I can count.

I am not young. My children have gone to hold their own intervals — one basin over, two storms away — the way it happens here. Families of two or three, rarely four or five, spread thin on purpose. We are not a village. We are a scattering, like water, like green. So now I walk the circuit mostly alone, which suits me.

Who or what would I be here?

I would be the keeper of the interval. The woman who remembers which bundled grass under which ledge is for re-thatching that half-hut on the dome when its potholes refill in three weeks. The one who bends the fast vine away from the cairn so it stays visible, and guides another vine across a chasm because in a year it will be a bridge my daughter's child can use. The one who knows that the iridescent beetle tree on the north slope had too many larvae taken last year and so this year I leave it alone, even though the larvae roast sweet and fat.

I would be the one who has learned not to be louder than the deafening, but lower.

I don't shout across the canyon. I make the hooting, rounded *ooo* that rides under the hum, the note that the rock likes to carry. My daughter knows which pipe is mine. When we are together — rare, a few nights a month in the sheltered basin — we don't sing *at* the wind. One of us hums until the flutes shift and harmonize, and then we stop and let the rock sing back. We keep it in tune by not blocking the wrong holes. A landslide that plugs a flute, we mourn. We clear it by hand, not with quarrying. We never wanted metal for that reason. It would close the instrument.

If you sat with me in that overhang while we wait for the ten minutes of rain — and we are always waiting, expertly, the way the bromeliads hold a cupful for weeks — you would see me do nothing that looks like doing something.

I would be checking the middle pothole with my eyes, narrowed for close work now, watching a water-beetle trace its circle. I would be breathing. I would be listening for the gliding lizard. If it launches from the copse and catches a thermal going east, I know the storm is building on that side and I should lay out the gourds and resin-lined baskets before the cloud even clears the lip.

And then it comes. It always comes. The white sea boils up from below, breaks warmly and violently against the plateau's sides, and hammers everything like shot for exactly long enough to change the world.

For ten minutes every surface streams. The rock roars in every channel at once. The big flute under the overhang doesn't boom anymore, it howls.

I am out in it, barefoot on streaming stone, tipping the larger pothole to catch, holding a gourd under the sighing spring where it suddenly runs fast, laughing with the low vowel that can be heard under thunder. Ten minutes to live ten days on. Children learn this before speech: *breathe before you need to.*

And then — as fast as it came — the sun is back. The stone steams. The forest drips. The grasses hiss like surf.

I wring out my hair. I cover the water. I tap the stone beside the cup and hear that it is full, deep, good. I go back to the overhang and sit.

If you had stayed still long enough on that warm, poured rock to see me at all, you would now see me disappear again. Not hidden. Just crossing. Staff tapping, flat-footed, not hurrying, from this pocket of green to the next one two ridges over where a terrace wall is sleeping under living mulch, yams curled underground like fists.

The wind shifts and fills the space where I was with sound. The emptiness is complete again. But not empty. Just between.

That is what I would be. Interval-shaped. A human edited by height and by waiting into something as rare as the fauna and as quiet as water that knows how to hold itself until it is needed.
