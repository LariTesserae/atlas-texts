I wouldn't find myself in the center.

At first I thought I would. When you described it I tried myself on like coveralls - imagined me at the heart of the honeycomb, beside one of those house-sized geothermal engines, a hand on a piston as it sighed down. Or up on the high catwalks where the rebar is pulled still glowing, where you can see the whole dense stacking. Something with a title. Something important.

But I don't belong there. I breathe too loudly for that. I move too quickly. The center would correct me until I disappeared.

Find me further out, where the city thins just enough to remember it has an edge.

On the second terrace back from the rim - not the balcony where you can see the four thousand meter drop and the soft lowlands like wax, but one level in from it, where the wind has already learned to hum through the cables. There is a kink in the alley there where the basalt bubbled while it was still plastic and froze into a fin, maybe ten meters long, three meters high, like a frozen wave. The engineers didn't cut it away. They cut *with* it. They grooved its shade-side with shallow channels, no deeper than a thumbnail, all running to a single covered cistern sunk into the rock.

That cistern is mine.

Which is to say, I tend it. That's what we call it here, though at first I didn't understand that tending is not owning.

You would find me at dawn, before the sun clears the rim. That's the only cold this plateau ever gets - the hour when the metal is colder than the air. I am tall in the way everyone is tall here - long-limbed, heat-dumped, my coveralls the brushed matte silver that throws the ground's infrared back at the ground. My boots are thick, ceramic-woven, and I have learned to hover the way we all learn to hover, never leaning, never sitting on stone. My rebreather clicks faintly when I inhale. Six counts in, eight counts out. You would not hear me otherwise.

I have a soft brush. Not a tool that was manufactured so much as worn into shape - horsehair or some lowland analogue, broom-soft. I go along the fin and coax the condensation. It beads there on its own, of course, water reluctant to be water in this air, but it needs encouragement to run. Without the brush it sits and evaporates and is wasted. I make it run. I clear the salt-paper membranes where they have begun to crust white. I tap the cistern wall and listen.

The tone tells me everything. A high, tight ping when it's low and needs. A dull, belly-full thud when the night was kind. I mark it on the wall in chalk, not numbers but a line. The next tender who comes by will know. We don't leave notes. We leave tones.

I think I thought I would be someone who spoke. Someone who used words. But the longer I stay in this image the more I realize my work has made me quiet in the way the city requires quiet. My language is that low cable hum you described. When the mister cycle is about to fire - eight minutes, three times a day - I don't call out. I tug the signal cable that runs the length of our alley and it thrums at that molar-rattling low note, 40 hertz, felt in the teeth. Two tugs: *stand ready, faces up.* My terrace-mates tilt their masked faces to the veil. We harvest even that.

During the day I am the level-walker, too. It's the same job, really. Tending damp and tending softness are not two jobs.

I walk the length of our terrace with a chalk line and a level made of basalt glass and oil. The floor has taken another tilt this month - last year it was one degree, now it's nearly two to the south. A lowlander would call it subsidence, failure. Here it is just the rock remembering it is still warm inside. I don't fix it. I accommodate. I mark the new true with a pale line, shave a sliver from the table leg in our cube, pack a wedge of woven ceramic under the mister's foot so its veil still falls straight down. The wall doesn't quite meet the ceiling anymore; we designed it that way - a finger's width of play, held with a staple of black iron pulled wide so it can bend instead of break. I check that the staple hasn't fatigued. I run a palm a centimeter above the fresh terrace cut behind our dwelling to feel how hot the ground still is. You can warm your hands that way at night, like a story.

What I am is not impressive. That's why it fits.

I am not the glider with silica wings, though sometimes one lands on my fin and folds itself and watches me work and I envy its indifference. I am not the pale, eyeless thing that turns slowly in my cistern - I leave it alone, it leaves my water alone, we are colleagues. I am one of the narrow people, the varnished ones. My skin under the cuffs has gone that mineral grey-brown you talked about, my eyes when I lift my goggles are that washed-out amber that sees through steam. My sweat stays close as an oily sheen. I don't waste it.

My name, if you need one, is Pan After Rain. That's what they called me when my terrace gave a day's condensation to the cube where I was born. There is a perfectly round pan a few meters across just beyond the fin - one of those embryonic basins that isn't connected to any other. After a violent rain it fills, dark and still, reflecting the sky for a day before the heat drinks it. When I was born, it was full. By the time my mother could stand, it was white salt skin again. So they named me for water that was there and is gone. Most of us are.

At night I don't gather around fire. I lay my palm above the cooling slab with the others, not touching, feeling the infrared as the rock pings and settles - *tick... tick...* - contracting a fraction of a millimeter. Someone whispers, mostly breath through the lattice of copper fins. We tell time by it. *Three pings since the thump. Time to check the valves.*

If you came looking for me, you wouldn't find me at first. You'd think the alley was empty, just stone and pipe and the hiss of a valve. Then you'd see the sheen move. A slow turn of a wrist. A brush stroke that looks like it takes a minute.

I am not conquering this mountain that was just made. I am learning how to be porous the way it is porous. How to hold a little damp and then let it go. How to be quiet enough that the rim doesn't fall.

I am the part of the plateau that learned to tend its own weeping.
