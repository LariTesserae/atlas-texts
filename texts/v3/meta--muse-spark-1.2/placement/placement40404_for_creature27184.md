At first I thought I would be at the top.

Where the light is too much, and the wind finally gets in. Where the parapet runs straight and white for a half mile and you can see the other built hills hazed out over the plain like a chain of islands. I thought I wanted that view — to be the one who stands up there and understands the scale. The monk-mechanic who checks the cables on the shade cloths. The weather watcher.

But I wouldn't last there. It's too clean up there, too exposed. The air is too thin to think in for very long if you are not born to it, and the stone is too new. The top terraces are still trying to be fields. They haven't learned how to sweat yet.

Then I thought lower, where it's close and green and loud. The mid-levels where the looms clack under passionflower. I pictured myself in a factory-shed with the clerestory windows pried open, with epiphytes hanging from the beams and the air smelling of hot oil and wet earth at the same time. That felt closer. Hands busy. Something to hold.

But still not right. That person is already held properly — belongs to a level, to a paddy and a loom and a pump. I don't belong to one level yet. If you put me there I'd be listening for the wrong pump, at the wrong pitch.

It took a while, climbing in my head, to find the actual place where I would stop without noticing I'd stopped.

You would not find me where you are looking.

You would find me where you stop looking for a person at all.

About two-thirds of the way up, on the west-face shade side, where the mountain has folded a little to make a pocket out of the wind. Level 641, if anyone still counts that way, but no one there does. They just call it the long terrace where the water pauses.

There the grade eases for twenty meters before it has to climb again, so the builders made a wide stair that is more like a ramp with shallow lips, and the water was taught to take its time there. The U-channel that runs at ankle height along the left wall widens into a slow basin — not a cistern, not a paddy, just a place where the flow spreads thin over pebbles to cool before it drops to the next level. Algae makes it dark. Moss has softened the brick lip to something like felt. There is a fig there, not a grand one, a tough low one whose roots have been guided for fifty years into the mortar joints so they hold the wall instead of splitting it. Its leaves are dusty, its figs small and very sweet. Under it, a retaining wall the height of a sitting person holds a bed of soil a meter deep that was carried up in buckets by someone's great-grandmother. It grows beans and taro and one row of cotton that no one harvests anymore except to let the children pick it.

That is where I am. Not working, not resting. Holding.

If I am a human there — and I am, mostly — I am not young. Not old either. In that middle where you have finally grown into the altitude. My chest is broad, my legs are short and corded from stairs, my feet a little splayed. My skin is that burnished umber that matches the dark streaks below a spillway. I wear the same loose, sun-faded cotton as everyone else, sleeves down even at noon, head wrapped, eyes smudged with soot and clay, crow's feet permanent from squinting even when I am in shade. My hands are the giveaway. The nails never come clean. Oil and soil have mixed into one paste under them, in the creases of the knuckles. You can't wash it out because you shouldn't.

I don't have a single job. No one does, but I have less of a single job than most.

On paper — if they used paper for such things, which they don't — I would be listed as the gutter keeper for three flights of that channel. A boy was assigned to it when he was seven and he rubbed the lip a millimeter lower on the south side every year with his grandfather's stone. That boy is now a man with children on the terrace above. I am the one who watches what he did, now that his hands are needed on the pump two levels down.

But that is only a quarter of it. I also tend the cotton I do not harvest, because the white catches the bees. I have hives built into the wall behind me — not boxes, but cavities left in the masonry for them, the way holes are left for the swifts. I know by the change in their hum when the transformer two terraces below is running hot, because the bees don't like the different vibration through the wall. I tend the transformer too, in my way: I keep its cage of expanded metal clear of vine, but not too clear. I leave the passionflower on the north side because it drops the temperature five degrees and the bearing lasts longer.

Mostly, I listen.

I would be sitting on the low wall with my legs dangling over the green drop, a shallow bowl made from an old engine casing in my lap, shelling beans. A cat — not mine — would be asleep against my calf. Chickens would be making their way back to somewhere. The water would be chattering over the pebbles to my left, and I would be feeling it through the wall with my palm.

You would walk past and not see me at first, because I am the color of the wall, because I am breathing at the pace of the place and not moving. And then you would hear the small sound that is mine inside the big sound that is the mountain's: the click of beans into metal, timed to my breathing, timed to the hammer.

I am not important. If I left, the water would still find its way down. The fig would still hold. But for this stretch, for this decade, water favors the south lip because a stone was rubbed there every year, and the transformer runs cool because a vine was left on the right side, and the bees stay because the cotton is there for them when nothing else flowers. That is my holding.

If you asked me, while climbing, where I live, I wouldn't understand the question at first. I would put my hand on the wall we were both touching, so you could feel the thrum through your feet, and say *here*. Not the terrace. Not the level. *In the holding*. In the thing that is being kept from washing away and from drying out and from going silent.

When I die — slowly, very slowly, the way everything happens here — they will not bury me. They will compost me into that meter-deep bed under the fig, where the soil was carried up in buckets a hundred years ago. They will clear a niche in the retaining wall beside the basin and put a fern in it. Within a year you won't be able to tell which green was me and which was already there.

You will still hear the water chattering over pebbles, though, a little more smoothly on the south side.
