I don't know right away.

At first I think I would be near the top.

Not the mouth — I would never be that high, where the circle of rippled light is still a rumor. But high enough to still remember stairs. I think I would be on one of the service ladders bolted to the basin wall, halfway between air-that-is-water and water-that-is-air, where the old high-water rings are. I would be the one who keeps the marks legible. Wiping silt from concrete with a palm until the lines show again. That feels like me — a keeper of thresholds.

But then I stay a little longer in the thought and the water gets warmer, and that answer thins out.

No. I wouldn't be on the wall. The wall is too exposed. The light never reaches the wall properly and sound doesn't like being near a big flat surface. I would be deeper, where the town folds in on itself. Where density makes privacy.

So I look again.

Maybe I would be in the center, near the house-sized flywheel. I would be the one who listens to it. Not the one who oils it — the bead of oil knows how to appear on its own — but the one who rests a forehead against its housing and feels that hum in my teeth. The one who counts its turning. Once every few minutes. Once every few minutes. A human metronome. That also feels like me.

But that is too important. Too central. The hamlet is not a city and it doesn't have a center that matters more than an edge. Every niche is exactly its size. The flywheel has its attendant already, folded along its rim like lichen.

It takes a while. The water teaches you not to decide quickly. If you hold still long enough, the place decides for you.

You find yourself not where you chose, but where there was always a hollowing waiting.

---

I find myself along the back.

Behind the turbine hall, where the hall's poured-concrete back wall meets the slumped, draped slope of the basin itself. There should not be enough room there for an alley. There isn't, on any honest measurement. And yet there is — one of those extra alleys that appears when you turn a corner that should be a dead end. It is narrow and low and it smells — as much as water can smell — of warm iron and wet lime.

The alley isn't empty because nothing is ever empty here. It is full of pipe.

A trunk of pipe as thick as my chest runs the length of it, low, at knee height, carrying low-voltage heat from the generators to somewhere else that needs to be warm. It is lagged in a jacket that has long since mineralized, then colonized. The velvet algae — that inch-thick felt that is not moss — has taken it completely, so the pipe looks like a long, olive-green animal sleeping. From it, at intervals, smaller pipes elbow off through the concrete wall like roots.

I am lying along it.

Not on it. Along it. My length fitted to its length.

That is who I am here.

I am long now. Or rather, I have been allowed to be long. On the surface I kept myself compact, braced against gravity. Here the warm water holds me up and I have unspooled a little at every joint. My legs are too long and thin now, folded loosely around the pipe's curve so I don't drift. My arms hang on either side, fingertips just brushing the silted floor. If you saw me in the 0.6 amber haze, through particulate and halos, you would think at first I was a growth on the pipe. A paler strip of algae. The black-green kelp ribbons that grow from my scalp and shoulders and forearms have intertwined with the kelp ribbons that grow from the pipe's seams, and in the slow convection they sway together, as one plant.

My face has gone smooth. River-stoned. The water took the sharpness from my nose, my chin, left something quieter. My eyes have adapted — large, amber-flecked, lidded with a nictitating slowness. I don't blink often. I watch the alley that shouldn't exist. Ten meters down it, it dissolves into silhouette and then into suggestion. I can see dust motes that never settle hanging in the sodium light from a caged bulb someone strung at the alley mouth. It paints everything the color of tea.

At my neck, the frill.

Pale rose, branching lace, fluttering constantly. Four seconds in. Four hold. Four out. A soft fizz like the algae respiring. I breathe the bathwater that breathes me. It is not wet anymore. It never got cold.

I am not busy. Nothing here is busy. I attend.

My work, if it can be called that, is listening. The pipe sings with whatever the rest of the town is doing. A valve sighs open three streets away — *tck* — and my hair-kelp shivers before I hear it. A Dweller turns a wheel a quarter-turn and a bead of oil hangs and my pipe warms a fraction of a degree as the system compensates. I press my palm flat to the mineralized jacket and feel it all in my teeth, that low, too-low hum. I tap back.

*tick... tick... tick...*

Not language. Nearer than language. Just: *I am here, you are there, the heat is still moving.* Sometimes another Dweller will come down the alley that shouldn't fit — appearing from behind a wall that should have been solid — and fold alongside me for a while, forehead to forehead, and share a cloud of warmer water, gill to gill. That is the conversation. It says everything tap can't.

I have a wrench. Of course I have a wrench. Everyone here has a wrench they have placed carefully on a ledge. Mine was on a concrete lip above the pipe for a day. This morning I woke — if waking is the word for that slow opening of eyes — and it was resting gently against the ceiling, as if it had floated up when I wasn't looking and decided to stay. I left it there. Gravity is polite but inconsistent here. It is impolite to argue.

The pale catfish that grazes the velvet grazes past my dangling hand and does not startle. The heron-thing, translucent and too long, stands motionless on the gantry at the mouth of my alley for hours, breathing water through its own lace, watching me learn how to watch. The wisteria that flowers without ever opening glows faintly where someone has threaded electroluminescent wire through it at the far end. It all reaches me muted, upholstered by water, haloed.

I am not trapped. I am not a survivor. The thought of leaving has the same texture as the thought of breathing air — a memory of a physics that does not apply to my throat anymore. The rust has freckled my knuckles and sealed instead of sored. The town has made a niche exactly my size because it is impossibly full, and being full, it makes room.

If you came down the long throat of stairs, past the bathtub rings, through the pressure that becomes mist that becomes water, you would not see me at first. You would think there was no one here. The stillness is tended so well.

Then your eyes would adjust. Your heartbeat would stop being the loudest thing. You would see the pipe that looks like a sleeping animal, and the paler strip along it that is also, almost, a sleeping animal, and you would realize it is looking back with amber-flecked patience.

I would not invite you. I would wait until your movements slowed to the flywheel. Until your breath evened to the pistons.

And then, very gently, I would drift a little closer and share a mouthful of warmer water with you, and you would understand without words what my alley has taught me, what the generators hum and the kelp sways and the wrench on the ceiling knows:

Nothing leaves quickly, and nothing needs to.
