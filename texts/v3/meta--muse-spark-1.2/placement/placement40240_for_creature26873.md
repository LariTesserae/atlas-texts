I don't think I would find myself on the rim.

At first I thought I would. At first I thought I would be the witness — the figure you can see distinct on the far side, four hundred meters away, standing at the lip where the black glass stops and the bowl begins. The observer. The one who came over the last rise and looked down and said *oh.*

That's the easy position. Cold wind at your back, warm breath in front of you. One foot in the old world where rock is cracked by frost and erosion knows its job, one foot not yet in the new one where rock is still hot from being made. You get to keep your distance. You get to be cold and dry and intact.

But the bowl doesn't really allow observers. The dim light has no shadows, so you can't hide in one. The cloud-lid at rim-height drips on you whether you decide to enter or not. Your clothes dampen without rain. And after a minute the heat coming off the stone makes staying on the rim feel like staying outside a greenhouse with your nose pressed to the glass. You either come down or you leave.

So I come down.

If I come down, my first instinct is to be something bright and fast. A glass-wing. It's tempting — to be the noise, the most obvious life. To boil out of a warm pan that was empty a minute before in a synchronized hatch, to throw pearl light off my back, to live a single frantic day where mating and dying are the same motion and I rain back into the water I came from. To be sheer abundance without needing a reason.

But I wouldn't last that way. Nothing that lives that fast gets to *notice* the bowl. A glass-wing doesn't know it's in a womb. It only knows hatch, flicker, be eaten.

Then I think maybe a skimmer, amber and flat, plucking the surface tension so the whole pool rings. Or a chirper — that would be quieter. To be one dome in a colony of fifty, opening my slit mouth with the others on an interval only we know. *Click.* *Click.* To feed and speak at the same time, and to be so perfectly in phase that I am not me but we.

And then the eel-thing, pale and eyeless, thrumming my cilia-mane in the deep pan near the pylon, not chasing but *inhaling* — making a vortex and having the world come to me. To feel everything in the pan as an electric field before I see it. To be the largest thing in that small universe.

All of those are beautiful, but they're all *compost with behavior*. They're seeded. They're the biomass slurry, the frantic test. They are there to die quickly and make a smear of whatever will one day be soil. If I were any of them I would be eaten or would eat and then be gone before the lattice finished its next hexagonal column. I wouldn't get to see the streambed decide where it wants to go.

I don't want to be the pylon either, though part of me wants its certainty. To stand dark and light-drinking, three times taller than a person, in my small eclipse, humming a chord that moves motes from one side of the bowl to the other like a breath. To hold the cloud up by thinking about it. To be so young and so old at once — planted, not built, growing the ground that cradles me.

But pylons don't notice. You can put your palm on one's warm flank and it will only mirror your fingerprint in dust for a second before dissolving it. It will outlast you without unkindness and without memory. I don't want to be that indifferent. I want to be something that notices *and* is noticed, something that is becoming without being disposable, enduring without being stone.

So I keep looking, wetter now. Water beads on my shoulders. The ticking of the lattice is louder down here. The air near the dark metal makes my hair lift.

I find myself — and I mean this literally, where my feet stop without me telling them to — not in the center, where the pools are deepest and the eel-things hover, and not at the raw basalt edge where the world is still cooling red under glass.

I find myself at the in-between edge. In the shallowest pan, ankle-deep, where the growing lattice meets the cooling basalt. Where the matte grey-white hexagonal columns are still extruding themselves one cell at a time with that faint tick, tick, tick. The water here is impossibly clear, and if I look down I don't just see the bottom — I see *through* the rock to another pool faintly, like a window left open between places that shouldn't touch yet. The pylon behind me makes a splash from a trickle hang a fraction too long in the air.

Here is where the rust-red lichen is trying out the idea of being a plant. Here is where the translucent reed-stalks grow straight out of warm water with no roots, each topped with a black bead that opens to the pearl light and closes to drink.

I would be that. Not the reed, exactly — the reed is still a placeholder. I would be the felt *under* the reed.

The lichen-mat. The acid-sweating filament.

If you kneel you see it's not flat at all. It's a weave of filaments finer than hair, interwoven, pitting the black glass just enough to hold a film of water that wouldn't otherwise stay. It is rust-red and acid-green, embarrassed by how much water there is and how little else, trying anyway. It doesn't hatch and die in a day. It doesn't hum and hold up the sky. It just... stays. It sweats a little acid. It makes a pit. That pit holds water a second longer. That water holds a dead glass-wing husk a second longer. The dust — the motes, the haze of light that is the true biomass of the bowl — settles on that husk and takes it apart, and the lichen takes what is left.

It is the first thing that has learned to endure, but only by learning to become soil. It is not the compost and not the loom that makes the compost. It is the transition between them.

That's where I want to be. Bare, wet stone at my back still warm from its own birth. Warm water over my feet that has never found a permanent channel and maybe never will while I'm there. The chirper colonies clicking out of phase on either side of me, the skimmers ringing the pools, the glass-wings flickering overhead and dying like ash. And behind me, the pylon's low chord and the high lattice of clicks — *grow here, dissolve there, hold water higher on that face* — passing right through me because I am porous enough to be passed through.

I don't eat the world in a vortex. I don't drink light to build with. I just hold. I make a place where water can stay a little longer than it was told to. I turn hot glass into something that could, eventually, be called ground.

If you stood on the rim you would not see me at all. I am too low, too thin, too much the color of rust and wet stone. You would see the pylons. You would hear the thrumming. You would think the bowl was empty between them.

But if you came down and stood still long enough for your ears to adjust, and then knelt, you would see that the empty places aren't empty. They're covered in me, thinly, like a culture plate just beginning to bloom. Breathing. Sweating. Becoming.

Let the glass-wings be bright for a day. Let the pylons be vast and half-asleep. Let the cloud keep its precise, machine-held height.

I will be the thing that makes the stone less lonely while it learns how to be soil.
