I don't think I would be in the center.

If I walked in off the flats - boots hushing on that silty loam that gives a little, ears popping faintly with the low pressure - and ducked under the willow whips, I would not make it to the middle of the nave. That empty is too perfect. It wants to stay empty. Standing there you feel how watched it is precisely because it looks unwatched. The metronome of the pump - *chuff... chuff... chuff...* - and the generator's low *mmmm* in your teeth, and the wren's rattle inside the screw-conveyor, they make the hollow feel like a church just after the organ stops. You don't stand in the aisle.

At first I thought: I would be the visitor. The one who sits with his back against the wall-that-is-also-a-tree-that-is-also-a-floor and feels the thrum come up through his spine and leaves a print in the moss that slowly fills with water. But the visitor leaves. The grove lets him. The pressure in the ears lifts when he ducks back out. That's not living there.

Then I thought: the wren. That would be good - absurdly loud for its size, nesting in the trough of the screw-conveyor that is now packed with sphagnum. To be small and certain and to make a violence of sound in a place that amplifies everything. But the text is clear about the wren: it is abundant but not resident. It stitches right past your head and out through the ivy. It commutes. I don't want to commute.

Or the millipede. Under the mat, where the wood seems to move if you stare too long. There are so many of them that they are almost the texture of the underside. To be one of the countless, working the damp dark, never needing to be seen. That's closer. But even the millipedes are food that falls. They are eaten.

Let me stay longer. Let what wants to form form.

I find myself not looking out anymore but looking down. At the place where the root-mat has parted into a root-hole and the tea-colored water shows through. At the lip of the basin that the pump feeds - that shallow bowl formed entirely by living roots, polished smooth by water and by generations of birds drinking. That arc of cold, iron water the diesel spits - steaming a little in the 50-something air - lands there, always in the same place, and drains back down through moss on its own clock.

I would be there.

Not the water. Not the root. The negotiation between them.

If you look close at the pump's concrete pad, the description says, the pale skin has crept up and lipped over the edge, not to smother it but to cradle it, to conduct the thrum. And the basin lip is polished smooth. That polish is not made by water alone. It is made by maintenance. By something keeping the equilibrium - a belt slips a little, a root tightens a little, and it balances.

I would be the suture.

Specifically, I would be the patch where they meet: where an alder root as thick as your wrist pierces the pale, translucent skin, exits, and pierces again, and where that skin has plated itself with liverwort and then thinned itself to hold the poured concrete. Where sphagnum has tried to colonize the iron and rust has tried to colonize the sphagnum and both have been told, gently, *not here*. Kept at a polish.

I would be flat, of course. That is the law inside and outside the grove. The peneplain finished becoming flat by wearing itself away; the occupant won by spreading. I would not be tall. I would be a low, warm thickening - that faint, sourceless compost-heat that is half a degree above the air. If you put your hand down on the moss, cold and saturated and black-green, you would feel me underneath pushing back a fraction too slowly.

I would not have eyes. The domes that rise an inch above the mat, blind and moss-haired, breathing in time with the pump - I would be one of the ones that learned not to rise. That learned to stay flush and listen instead. To feel sound as pressure through my entire body. The generator going *mmmmmm* at 60 hertz through your molars would be going through me for so long it would be indistinguishable from my own tissue. I would be the organ that purrs without needing to make the sound myself. I would keep the diesel's flywheel turning slow and slamming, polished bright where the moss cannot claim it, because I need a heart outside my body. Iron beats more reliably than flesh in the damp.

I would be industrious the way the place is industrious - not busy, but invariant. Nothing changes there. That isn't stagnation, it's achievement. Erosion complete, temperature held, water just enough. I would do the same task forever: cradle the pad, polish the lip, conduct the thrum, taste the iron seep, and when a beetle or a midge or a warbler falls, take it without hunting. When condensation beads on every leaf and falls, take that too.

And when you sat on me - because you would, you can't help but sit, the root-floor invites sitting - I would take your weight without complaint. I would register you as a temporary warm spot on a cool body. A peristalsis would go under you, absurdly slow, not moving you anywhere, just acknowledging. Water would condense on your shoulders the way it condenses on my domes. And when you stood, your print would fill slowly with water, and then with me, and be gone.

You wouldn't see me. That's the point. What lives there is good at being the room. I would be good at being the place where the room touches its machines and decides, again and again, to keep them running. Not the whole vast, pale, root-stitched floor-being, but one of its quiet places of attention. A callus where iron meets skin, kept smooth.

Find me at the basin, under the steaming arc, where the water lands and is lifted again. Find me humming at 60 hertz when nothing is plugged in.
