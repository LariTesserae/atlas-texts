I didn't find myself on the rim.

At first I thought I would. That's where you stand to understand it - dry boots on cracked soil, the plain still going on behind you, the warm haze still behaving like weather is supposed to behave. From there you can see the whole wound at once and call it beautiful in the way a map is beautiful.

But I don't belong where I can see the whole shape of a thing at once. I belong inside a thing that is bigger than my field of view.

So I went down.

Ten meters down the switchback and the air changed on my skin before I understood I'd made a decision. That slow, warm condensation that never quite becomes rain - it found the back of my neck and stayed there. My ears started to fill. Fifty meters down the roar came up to meet me - pumps and frogs and cicadas and birds all braided into one unbroken throb you feel in your molars - and I stopped trying to hear anything else.

At first I thought I would be one of the loud ones. Maybe a gull taking up a whole rail. Maybe one of those hand-sized dragonflies that has to be loud to be heard over the aerators. I liked that idea for about a hundred meters of descent, because if you're loud enough in the Cut you don't have to listen.

But nothing stays loud here for long if it wants to stay.

I thought then maybe I would be the water itself - the seep in the limestone, beading and falling, patient and sourceless. Or the marsh, tea-brown and knee-deep and full to bursting. There's a kind of holiness to being that saturated, to being so full there's no room for anything else.

But I am not that mindless. The water doesn't choose to stay. It just accumulates.

No - further down, where the industrial benches have all flooded and the catwalks narrow to little more than slick planks between carp-backed pools, I understood where I actually settle.

You would find me at the bottom, but not in the center.

Not on the screening plant skeleton where the birds clot so thick the steel disappears, and not on the lowest silt bar where the herons stand in perfect mirror-rows. You would find me off to the east side, where the pumping houses thrummed.

I would be one of the Ones Who Tend The Heart.

Not the foreman. Not the one who built any of it. I would be the one who stayed after there was nothing left to mine. The tender for houses six and seven, where the causeway kinks to get around a pool that never stopped growing.

You'd find me on the concrete lip of pump house six at what passes for midday - that amber, sourceless late-afternoon that never gets brighter or darker - with my rubber boots white at the toes with guano and rust-red to the knee, both colors dried into the seams so they will never wash out. My hair would be lank no matter when I cut it. My skin would have that damp, faintly greenish tinge of someone who lives in algae steam and never quite dries.

I wouldn't talk much. I wouldn't need to.

I would have learned, like the others, that shouting is a waste of breath when the whole basin is shouting. After the first week my voice would have gone a little hoarse and I would have let it. I'd speak in the language we use down there: a flat palm meaning *stay, the heron won't move for you*, a tap on the pipe meaning *pressure's high again*, two fingers to my eyes then pointing at the tall, thin aerator that churns like an inverted mushroom meaning *that one's going to seize if we don't oil it before the cloud lowers*.

And I would be slow.

That's the part that finally told me who I was. Everyone and everything in the Cut that lasts has learned the same speed. The water moves a centimeter a second. The rock weeps at the same rate it wept last month. The guano piles a millimeter thicker a day. The carp turn as one heavy sheet when startled, never darting. The heron two feet from my pump house door doesn't swivel its head when the diesel hammers at 120 decibels, because urgency is a rumor from the plain and it doesn't carry down here.

I would move like that. I would check the same gauges at the same hours. I would oil the same bearing that was oiled yesterday and will need oiling tomorrow. I would scrape the same mat of reeds and carp-weed off the intake screen with a long-handled rake, and watch it re-gather by evening as if I'd never touched it. I would sit with my legs dangling over water that is solid with backs and watch the condensation rain hiss where it hits the transformer yard, and a cormorant would be on the rail not three feet away, wings held open like wet laundry, and neither of us would bother the other. We would have an understanding: you hold your perch, I hold mine.

At night I would sleep in the old first-aid station where the green cross has faded, on a cot under a fluorescent tube that never turns off because the light from above is never enough. My clothes would never dry on the line; they'd just get less wet. My boots would grow that faint fur of mold overnight that I scrub off without comment in the morning.

If you came down the ramp shouting to ask me why I stay - and you'd have to lean in close, mouth almost touching my ear, because otherwise the frogs and the cicadas and the birds and the pumps would eat your words - I would look at you the way I look at the heron.

Someone has to keep the pumps going.

If the pumps stop, the terraces drown. The scaffolding drowns. The aerators drown. The rookery lifts all at once and the air goes truly dark and doesn't come back. The slow, shallow, warm breathing the whole basin does - that patient in-out of seep and pump and drizzle - would stop.

And after you have lived inside a breath for a while, you don't want to be the one who decides to hold it.

So I stay. Not because I was born to it. Because there was a surface, and I held on, and the holding on became the life. Damp, thrumming, weeping, and very, very slow - until I am the exact temperature and texture of the place that made me.
