I think at first that I would be you.

That is the first image that comes, uninvited. The diver in the aisle.

Dropping through the meniscus, through that skin of oil and dust that has never cleared, feeling the sound change in my teeth. Hanging mid-water with a regulator ticking bubbles up to that trembling lens of air at the ceiling. Hands clumsy in gloves from the cold. Seeing the whole room at once - chapel-small, comprehensible - and believing for a moment that to see it whole is to belong to it.

But that doesn't last. I know that body. That body is loud, even when it tries to be still. It billows silt. It pulls a halo of empty water around itself that doesn't belong to it, the way the catfish does. It has to leave before its joints go numb. It is a visitor, and this is not a place for visitors. It stirs things and then it is gone, taking its column of light from the shaft back up with it.

So that is not where I would find myself. Not hanging. Not passing through.

Then I think maybe I would be smaller, and lower.

For a while I am the shrimp. Or one of the shrimp. Translucent and whiskered, down on the grating over the drainage channels, grazing the silt and puffing it away in little clouds that hang. That life makes sense to me - the patient, endless sifting, the way the whole vault's slow north-to-south circulation brings food to me instead of me having to chase it. I like the scale of it: the bolt heads the size of fists become boulders I have to walk around. A yellow paint line down the center aisle becomes a highway I never need to cross.

But shrimp scatter. Shrimp freeze when the barbels taste them. Shrimp are food that knows it is food, and they live in that tight, bright, nervous way. When the seven-second hammer comes - *thunk* - they lose their footing. I don't want to live being startled.

I move up a little.

For a while I am the iron. I think I would be the flywheel, which is a comfortable thought - that slow, relentless inertia, the diameter of a man, squealing once per revolution on a bearing that will never be oiled again. Or I am the relay behind the slate switchboard, the one that never got the signal to stop. That is an honest life: to be told to keep moving and so to keep moving. To churn the same water over and over because no one told you the work was done. To keep the room alive by not remembering that it was meant to be dry.

But iron doesn't feel the water, it only holds it. And I do want to feel it.

It settles, finally. It becomes more itself when I stop trying to choose something that moves quickly or endures dumbly.

I would find myself at the top of the room, but not as the air.

Pressed against the vaulted ceiling, right where the bolted iron ribs meet the concrete arch - not in the trapped, shivering lens of air that has been there for decades. I would be just under it.

I would be one of the white anemones.

There are dozens of them up there, and you didn't notice them at first because they are the color of lime accretion. A small crown, no bigger than a thumbnail, a ring of pale tentacles that open and close all day without hurry. I would be the one that is not on the ceiling at all.

I would be one of the two or three that have taken hold on *him*.

On the broad, flat slope of his shoulder, just behind the frill where his external gills pulse pink-grey like the asbestos ribbons below. Where the accretion is thickest, where his skin has gone hard and mineral and has become, for all practical purposes, floor. Where a small black mussel rides beside me, opening and closing, opening and closing.

That is where I would be. In the halo that belongs to something else. On the warm spot.

Because the pump puts out that faint heat from friction and old relays and twelve-degree water makes faint heat into a kind of sun. He lies in that polished depression on top of the middle housing where the iron has been worn shiny by the slow slide of his belly - a bed made by a century of lying there - and I would be on the back that makes the bed. Carried.

And what would I be? Not the god of the room. Not the thing that could uncoil with a single flex and throw a wall of water down the central aisle. I would be small, sessile, filtering.

I would not need eyes. His clouded beads are already not used; mine never existed. I would feel the room the way he does - by vibration, by taste. When the pipes hammer every seven seconds and the whole vault shudders, I would feel it first through his skin, through the iron beneath us both, before I feel it in the water. My tentacles would stiffen a half second before his barbels do. I would close, and open.

I would eat what he stirs without having to chase. The circulation he waits for would bring it to me - the fine silt, the particulates orbit, the tiny things the pumps keep from ever settling for good. When he makes his one slow sweep of the aisle in an hour, maybe two, undulating with that lazy, whole-body wave, barely flexing that vast tail, I would go with him. Past the workbench with its vise rusted shut around nothing, past the control pulpit with its levers still upright, past the diver hanging in the middle and tasting us with bubbles. The small silver-sided fish would part around us both the way they part around pipes.

I would not be alone in the way he is alone. He keeps the rule of density - set apart, with a halo of empty water. I would live inside that halo, which is a different kind of solitude. The solitude of the epiphyte, of the companion. Not required, but allowed. He would not notice me except as a slight roughness on his back, the way he doesn't notice the lime. The eel in the throat of the pump below us has its agreement with him; I would have mine, quieter: I filter what he cannot, I ride where he rests.

And when he breathes - that slow, whole-body heave that lifts the silt off his back in a sheet and lets it hang - I would lift with it. I would breathe with it. My frill with his frill.

If the room were ever drained, I would die in air, as he would. If the pumps ever stopped, I would die in stillness, as he would. That seems right to me. To be a small, soft thing that has learned, over decades, that stability is not the absence of change but the perfect repetition of it. *Thunk.* Seven seconds. *Thunk.* To open, to close, to filter the water that is forever being kept moving.

You would look straight at us and see only pump housing. Only iron and accretion and mineral. You would have to let the silt settle and watch very long to see that two of those small white flowers, there on that silted mound, are breathing independently - a beat slightly out of rhythm with the machine underneath.

That is where you would find me, if you stayed still long enough and breathed slow through your regulator.

Not hovering in the aisle. Not watching.

Being watched over, and filtering.
