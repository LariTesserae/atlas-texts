At first I think I would be up high.

That's the instinct, isn't it - to climb. If you put me in a drowned table in the sky, I want to pull myself hand over hand up the trunk of one of those great kelps, the ones thick as a body, and push my head through the canopy where the fronds lie flat on the amber skin of the water. To get air that isn't stolen. To see the whole thing, rim to rim, the black basalt lip holding in the haze, the pale plate-flowers just under the surface like drowned moons, the bells below me glowing like coals in honey. To be the lookout. The one who breaks the surface.

But I wouldn't stay there. The light up there is wrong for me. It hurts. And it's lonely. That canopy is a roof, not a home. You go up to look and then you have to go back down to live. No one lives on the roof.

Then I think, maybe I'd be at the edge. At the world-rim itself, where the water goes right to the cut cliff and you can put a hand on that raw, glassy escarpment that hasn't learned to be smooth yet. I want to be the rim-watcher, the one who knows what it feels like to be at the boundary where the Table says *we end here, we hold here.* To press my palm to that cold column of basalt and feel the heat coming up through the stone from below. To wonder about the lowlands no one goes to.

But that's also not right. It's too sharp down there. The text says the bottom will cut your feet. The rim is scree-less, a clean shear. No one lingers there because there's nothing to tend there, nothing to lash to, and the water is deepest against the wall, and stiller, and strange. I don't want to be the exile or the mystic. I don't want to be the one who stares at the drop.

For a moment I try on being not a person at all. Maybe I'd be one of the blind salamanders. Milk-pale, the size of a canoe, moving so slowly that people stop to watch you pass. Filtering feather-weed through a frill, indifferent. That has an appeal. To be the animal the ecology forgot to bring, to be alone in a way that isn't lonely, to be a thought passing through the town. Or maybe I'd be kelp itself — one of the old anchors with a trunk that has had to learn to be wood underwater, holding a whole neighborhood to the stone.

But no. Those are too silent, even for that quiet.

So I sink. Down from the canopy, away from the rim, into the middle where everyone actually lives. Where the water is neither top nor bottom but the thick, crowded, braided thickness of it. Where if you extend your arm you touch a living thing. That dense part is where I start to breathe easier, even though there's no air there.

You would find me at mid-depth. Not in a bell — though I would know my bell, I would have my share of stale orange lamp-light and resin smell — but just outside it, on the ropeway.

Specifically at a knot.

Every town like that has to have one, even if they didn't plan it. Where five or six kelp-fiber ropeways come together and are retied around the fork of a basalt pillar, about fifteen meters down, where the light is still that parchment-dusk and the feather-weed gardens are staked out on trellises all around like luminous wheat. A knot that has grown thick with secondary knots, with bored stones and shell counters tied on, with pale fungal paint reapplied so many times it's a little luminous even in the dim.

I would be the one who lives at the knot. Not the gardener, not exactly, though I would tend the nearest red brush-algae because it is right there and it needs watching for that exact blush that says *cut now*. Not the bubble-herder, though I would help nudge the wobbling kelp-bladder bubbles toward the bells with a broad leaf when they come.

I would be the listener.

You see, sound travels wrong there, and talk is for inside the air bells. Outside, the town talks by tugging the ropes. *Two quick - I am here. Three slow - need air. Long tremor - be still, salamander.* And all those lines converge at the knot.

I wouldn't be the fastest swimmer. I would be good at being still. That's the whole rule: *hurry and the water holds you.* If you thrash it turns to honey and pushes back. If you are smooth you slip. So I would have learned the economy very well - one frog kick, then glide five meters, arms opening to brake. Hair long and beaded so it doesn't blind me, eyes wide and amber and used to the dusk, webbing a little deeper between my fingers so my hands cup water when I scull.

And I would hang there, most of the day, with one hand loosely curled around the main rope even while I doze. You have to, so you don't drift into one of those invisible thermal pillars that stand locked in place for days and make the kelp lean. I would be thin, river-pearl skinned, chest keeled like a bird's from years of holding seven, eight, nine minutes at a time.

Who would I be, in temperament?

Not an elder yet — the ones who can slow their hearts on command and hang in a cold seep with the meniscus cutting across their throat like a second skin — but learning from them. Not a child anymore — those seal-fat tadpoles in the bells who are still learning to hang.

I would be the one the children come to to learn the pause. The one who teaches them how to sit absolutely still for an hour watching a single frond, because patience isn't a virtue there, it's locomotion. The one who can hang motionless in the amber with only my hair moving, until a child thinks I'm a piece of root and then startles when I blink slow.

I would be embryonic on purpose. Like the town, like the geology, still deciding what shape it will be when it finally erodes. I would not be in a hurry to become. I would be the keeper of the interval between breaths.

If you came swimming, in that slow, bewildering density where everything touches everything else — leaf, wall, vine, neighbor's dwelling — you wouldn't see me at first. You'd think I was part of the trellis. Then you'd see the faint phosphorescence on my forearms, the fungal paint glowing, and my hand signing without hurry: *slow. you are held. you are here.*

And then you'd feel the rope under my hand tremor, and I'd translate it for you, not with voice but with a return tug: someone three bells over needs air, someone near the surface has seen a flower open.

At night - that deeper dusk - I would duck under the lip of my bell with that practiced flip that spills almost no air, break surface inside, and breathe the warm, stale, human air that smells of oil and damp thatch and other bodies. And I would listen to people actually speak, close and soft, because everything sounds close in that small stolen lung.

But I would always want to go back out to the knot. To hang again in the green-gold, tethered by one wrist cord when I sleep, swaying with the whole town as it heaves on its hours-long convective breath, warm water rising from the stone, cool water sinking from the mist. To be suspended, not fallen, not risen, not born, not yet.

That's where you'd find me. Half in one water and half in another, if there's a seep nearby. Or hanging exactly at mid-water, absolutely still, holding the rope lightly, letting the water hold me back only if I asked it to.
