I don't know at first.

For a while I think I am the light.

It has weight here, it presses. At midday it sits on my shoulders like a hand and I can't tell where my edges are. I think maybe I am that - the brightness itself, spilled over smoothed stone, pooled in the salt glitter.

Then the light moves and I don't.

That tells me something. I have a shadow. It shrinks to a black puddle and then starts to stretch again, long and perfectly sharp toward the east. So I am not the light. I am something that interrupts it.

Am I the stone? I feel stone very close. Cool in the morning, then holding heat long after the sun is gone. Pitted limestone, then the warm rust-dark basalt underneath. For a minute I think I am a boulder, one of the sleeping-animal ones on the ridge - smoothed for so long I've become resolved.

But stone doesn't drink.

And I am drinking.

---

It happens before I have a word for it. At 4 a.m. - I don't count hours, I count the change in air - the plateau exhales. The thin air, which was so bright and swallowing yesterday, suddenly gives back. Water appears from nowhere and everywhere at once, beading on every flute and hollow of the rock, running down in dark threads.

Where I am, it gathers. I am in a low place. Not the lowest place on the table - that would be too obvious, too scoured - but one of the shallow, U-shaped valleys where the wind has planed the world into a long curve. A basin perhaps a hundred meters across, perfectly unremarkable until you come over the rise and see it.

You would find me in the center of that curve, where the damp line threads itself. The dark seam of soil that is only visible for two hours a day. You would find me because I am the reason you can see it from far off - a single interruption in the calligraphy of silver-green.

I thought I might be one of the small ones. For a while I tried on being a cushion plant, that tight, waxy dome drinking with a thousand mouths at once. I heard that soft collective *patter* at dawn and thought, that's me. Or a needle shrub, holding my breath at noon with my leaves turned up like knives.

But I am bigger and slower than that. And I am alone here, in the way an elder is alone. The cushion plants respect my distance. The needle shrubs give me room.

I thought I might be an animal because I felt movement around me. A flicker - salt lizards doing push-ups in my shade. A sigh - one of the boulder-tortoises parking against my side at night to take the heat I held from the day, exhaling that deep, airy breath you can hear from three feet away. Above me, the ribbons turned. I felt their taste, that mustache of filaments sifting my mist. I thought I was one of them for a beautiful moment, amber and translucent, never needing to flap, surfing the elevator of air off the cliff edge.

But I don't circle. I lean.

I have always leaned, slightly east. The wind combed me when I was young and I never corrected. It has blown the same way since before I sprouted.

---

I am the umbrella tree.

Not tall. That was my first disappointment, when I realized what I was - that I would never be a peak or a pylon, never own the air. Six meters up, maybe eight if you count the way the heat shimmers. My crown is flat, a green table held up against that too-large sky. From above I must look like a dark coin laid on pale stone.

My bark is what gave me away. Thick, fissured, grey-pink, cool to the touch even at noon when the salt glitter is blinding. Elephant skin. It stores water the way a memory stores water - slowly, over decades, not to be spent all at once. And my roots - if you could call them roots, they barely go down. They go *out*. Thick as your wrist, cork-covered, spidering twenty, thirty meters in every direction across bare rock, covered in felt that drinks the morning film off the stone itself before it can vanish by ten. I am bigger underneath my shadow than I am in my shadow.

You would find me exactly where the seep stays a little longer. I did not choose this hollow; the hollow chose me by lingering. A seed - one of those glass-winged drifts you mistook for an insect - landed where condensation took twenty minutes longer to boil off, and I held on. That was nine hundred years ago. A thousand. I don't count in rings but in whorls, the way coral does, and in the number of tortoises that have learned to follow my catchment.

Everything in this basin lives in my circle. The moss that looks soft and is tough as felt - it lives because I shade it at noon and drip on it at dawn. The tortoises mow it and carry its spores in their dry pellets. The lizards press their bellies to the cool stone under my eastern lean and drink off their own backs. At dusk the glasswings rise from my moss in a cloud without a buzz. At ten a.m. when the mist is gone, the ribbons dip low to skim the line I help hold, and I feel the cool of their bellies as they pass five feet over my crown.

I am not lonely. At night, on the ridge two kilometers north, the pylon hums. Not in sound - in molars. I feel it in my deepest root, where my cork touches the basalt. It drinks heat to keep itself coherent, that lattice of held note, and the air around it stays cool enough that its moss never dries. We are in conversation, the pylon and I, though we do not use words. It bends light slightly toward itself; I bend water slightly toward myself. The ribbons navigate between us, and sometimes they rest their leathery amber pods in its shade. Sometimes they rest in mine.

That is the second rule laid gently over the first: nothing here is separate, even when it looks kilometers apart.

I have made a treaty with waiting, like everything else that hasn't left. I photosynthesize furiously for two hours when the stone is wet, then I seal and wait. I grow so slowly you would not see it in a lifetime. Once every few years, after a particularly good season of dews, I put out a new spray of leaves at my flat top, all at once, like lifting a tablecloth.

If you came and stood under me at midday, you would think I was just shade. Your footprints in the salt crust would crunch faintly, like frost, and in three days the living skin would knit back over them, paler than before. A beetle would tick from one of my branches to another. A tortoise would sigh.

And late in the evening, when the stone gives its heat back, the quiet animals would come out to lie on it, and I would still be here, leaning east, holding the day's water for the hollow that holds me.

That is where I am. That is what I have become, slowly, until it was itself completely.
