I don't find myself at first.

That is the first true thing about the low place. You don't *arrive* so much as you become noticeable to it. Like condensation beading.

At first I think I would be up high.

Because if I were going to live in a saucer half a kilometer across, I would want the water tower — thin legs, white sky behind it, the torus of starlings turning at eye level. A watchtower. A place to see the whole even chord at once. I think: I would be the keeper of the high whistle, the one who tends the thin scream on the kiln-house vent that never wavers. I would be pigeon-adjacent. Above it all and therefore apart from it.

But the place corrects me. The air sits low and cool and even. It doesn't want you high. The pigeons face the hum, not the horizon. The starlings have worn a groove into the air from turning, not rising. High is not a direction here. Low is.

So I come down.

If you stand on the smooth lip and look in, you can see the whole hamlet at once — the riveted halls, the pipe racks, the cistern black as basalt — and you think for a second you could hold it all in your head. Then you take the worn iron stairs down, each step concave from a thousand boots before yours, and the hum gets into your molars, and the cool gets into your breath, and you understand the scale was lying. It's not small because you can walk it in twenty minutes. It's vast because every yard of empty concrete between buildings is deliberate.

I think I would find myself in one of those deliberate emptinesses.

Not inside the dynamo hall where the thrum lives — though for a moment I want to be there, I want to be the piston, *chunk-chunk, chunk-chunk*, so reliable. Not on the roofs where the thirty-seven pigeons on a ledge become thirty-seven different pigeons and still thirty-seven. I love the pigeons but I am too restless for facing one direction forever. I thought about being a rat — sleek, cat-large, trotting with my tail in the hand-deep channel, carrying a perfect white bone to the water with reverence. There is a dignity to having an errand like that. But my feet want to stay dry.

No. Let that image be more itself before I claim it.

I keep walking, past the long shed on stilts, past the puddles that are oil-slick mirrors, past the clouds of moths that hang like a second steam and never land. The drizzle comes without darkening, beads on the underside of a catwalk, falls. I stop hearing the dripping until I do again. The beetles click. *Click. Click.* In time with the belts. Or the belts slap in time with them. I put my hand on a warm concrete wall in the lee of the dynamo and the tick comes up through my palm.

What settles, slowly, is this: I would not be where anything is happening. I would be where nothing is happening so evenly that it is a kind of happening.

In the middle yard. The one between the dynamo hall and the kiln-houses. Not a street, not a thoroughfare. Just a rectangle of fine grey gravel, twenty meters by thirty, bordered on two sides by shallow channels that sigh toward the grated culvert. Nothing built on it. Nothing stored on it. Just gravel that has been rained on and steamed on and walked on long enough to be perfectly smooth.

That is where I find myself.

And I am sweeping it.

At first that sounds like punishment — Sisyphus with a broom. But stay with it while it becomes more itself.

I am not sweeping because it is dirty. The gravel is always smooth. If you leave a boot-print, ten minutes later it is smooth again, not because someone smoothed it but because it was always smooth and you were mistaken for a moment about what it was. I am sweeping because sweeping is a part of the chord.

I wear the same thing the row houses imply: a boiler suit, washed to soft grey, rust at the cuffs that may be suit or may be me. My face has rounded at the corners the way the brick has. My hair beads with condensation that never falls. I have a wide, flat-headed broom with bristles worn short. The handle is iron pipe, sweating.

My job, if you could call it a job, is to make the sound of bristles on stone. *Shhhk. Shhhk.* Evenly. At the same tempo as the pistons and the beetles. The thin kettle whistle in the open doorway of the little row house behind me is forever just about to whistle and never does, and my sweeping is what keeps it on that edge. Input equals output. The steam that exhales from the squat chimney is exactly replaced by the drizzle that beads and never clouds the sky.

I sweep and I pause and I watch the rats trot past in their line, tails trailing. One pauses and looks at me with dark lidded eyes. Not beady. Mild, incurious. *You are here now. You take up a certain amount of space. We have accounted for you.* I nod to him, the way the old man on the upturned crate by the culvert nods to them. He continues with his chip of brick toward the black cistern where the eels knot and unknot.

I sweep and the pigeons on the gantry above me shift their weight all at once, one sheet, and resettle. I am included in the direction they face now. Not looked at — included.

I sweep and the beetles click beside the wall and I realize I am not making a separate sound at all. My broom, their carapaces, the belts — we are the same instrument played in three places.

This is who I am here: not the visitor, not the infestation, not the ghost of the factory. I am one of the sparse fixtures. The human that the low place uses to stay human-shaped, the way it uses a pigeon to stay pigeon-shaped and a pipe to stay pipe-shaped and water to stay water-shaped.

I live low because I have agreed to the terms of the saucer. I do not look beyond the smooth stone lip. Whatever is beyond is not mine and I have no longing for it. I have become horizontal, spread-thin, and even. My breath ghosts faintly and doesn't want to leave, and that is correct — the cool stays cool that way. The damp stays damp because I put my damp back into it.

I am not trapped. I finished becoming myself a long time ago and decided, perfectly, to stay. The broom moves. The gravel stays smooth. The hum never resolves. The cistern stays a foot deep and absolutely still except for the stirring that keeps it from deciding to be still.

If you came to the yard's edge and stood there long enough to be regarded — truly regarded by the beetles ticking in time, by the rats on their errand, by the thousand birds calmly including you — you would see me pause, lean on the pipe-handle, and look at you the way the low place looks at everything that appears among its gantries:

Calmly. Evenly. As complete.
