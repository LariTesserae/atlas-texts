At first I think you'd find me on top of things, like a person is supposed to be.

On one of those floating avenues — that matte pearl-gray boulevard five feet above the fern mat with no pillars, still humming its low F. Boots wet with condensation. A human figure, small and loud and vertical in a place that has decided vertical is optional. I'd be walking down the middle because there's nowhere else to walk, listening to the chord resonate in my molars and thinking *abandoned*, even though I know better. Looking out at the spaced-out cathedral trees half a mile apart and trying to hold the scale in my head.

But I wouldn't stay there. It's too exposed. Too *on*.

Give me ten minutes in that sound — that beating, held chord where two fields overlap and make your sternum thrum — and I'd start to feel how wrong the top side is. It's for parades that never happened. The rain puddles up there and evaporates. The midges cluster there for the warmth but nothing lives there. It's a roof that forgot it was a roof.

I would crouch, then sit, then lie down and look over the edge.

And then I'd slip underneath.

That's where I find myself when I let the image finish becoming itself.

Not on the street but *under* it. In the eighteen inches of air between the bottom of the floating block and the top of the ferns that are never trampled. Where the field is. You can feel it if you put your hand there — not solid, but insistent, like pressing on the skin of a drum. Warm. Beaded with condensation that never drips down because down is negotiable there. It beads and runs *sideways*.

If you live there long enough you get light. Not heavy, thin the way everything here is thin and far apart. I'd be one of the gleaners, but not quite one of the Lattice People - not born to it. One of the ones who learned to be it.

Call me the Keeper, or the Tuner. A human who came to catalog an empty city and stayed until he got tuned.

You'd find me in the second strata, where the ribbontails cut their F-sharp through the city's F. My place would be about a third of the way between two canopy trees — you can see both from there, vast flat disks of dark leaves a hundred yards across, dimming an already dim pearl-gray sky. From the ridge it looks like nothing is between them. From underneath, you see the catenary. One vine, thick as my thigh, sagging a hundred feet down in a perfect curve between the trees, its curtain of heart-shaped leaves dripping, shaking every time a cloud of hand-sized moths lifts off it. My hammock is tied to that sag. My home is that sag, and the warm underside of the street-block it kisses.

I would be long now. Not tall the way people are tall — elongated, thinned by needing less weight. Barefoot because boots can't find purchase on a field. My feet are mostly toe, prehensile and calloused from gripping field-texture and vine. I breathe through my sides when the air is warm enough, which it always is - that late-summer warmth that never makes you sweat, just beads you. My ears, or what I use as ears, are no longer much good at voices. They are membranes stretched over my shoulder blades that sing with the city.

Because I don't listen to the city anymore. I listen *through* it.

That chord the platforms make — the low roar made of a dozen held notes, F in the avenues, C in the towers, a fifth up in the transit tubes — most visitors hear it as noise and try to talk over it. The Lattice People hear it as geography. A held note is a wall. Where two fields cancel and the beating stops, that's a door. Where they overlap and make the water in the fissure pools ripple, that's an updraft.

I learned it the way they did: by starving until I had to. You can't pick midges off a field-surface with your hands if you can't feel where the field is. So you go hungry, and then you feel it.

What would I do all day? What they do.

Glean.

In the early dim — though dim never really changes — I hang under the block with my knees hooked over the lip where the pearl-gray material flexes in the wind, and I run my hands over the warm underside. The field breeds heat and the insects love it. They cluster there by the thousands, mirrored beetles, pale gnats. I scrape them into a bowl made from a chorister's shed vocal sac. I drink by pressing my cheek to the condensation that runs along the field's edge and lapping it. Midday, when the twenty-minute rain lays itself down without a storm, I spread whatever patagium I have — a tarp, a coat, at first, then later skin — and drink from the air.

I tend the egg.

Not mine, not really. One of the true Lattice People left it in the time-drag under the largest platform — that place where your breath echoes before you exhale and sounds arrive late. She hung her amber grapefruit egg there because the slight dilation keeps it moist longer between rains, and then she had to glide half a mile to the next tree to glean and never came back. Maybe she was eaten. Maybe she just claimed a different block, half a mile is a proper distance here. I found it pulsing slightly out of sync with my own heart. So I sit with it for hours, feeling time slip. A droplet will hang in front of my face for a full minute, motionless, and I learn not to grab it. Let it remember how to fall.

You'd find me there, in that wrong-time, if you came looking.

Or you'd find me at the terminus at the center, where the young basalt is still warm to the touch and the lattice tried to bridge a fissure and stopped — clean, melted-looking, humming. The air beyond it shimmers though it's not hot enough to shimmer, and if you throw a stone it lands twice. I go there to throw things away that I don't want to keep but don't want to keep. An old boot. A name. I like hearing them land twice. It proves the place is still listening.

I am not lonely. That's the other thing you'd get wrong at first. You'd think a dozen individuals spaced half a mile apart in a city for millions must be lonely. But loneliness is a crowding disease. Here, emptiness is not absence. It's structure. Like the vines that use emptiness as a branch, I use distance as a room.

At evening I let go.

Not every evening. When the moths rise and darken the dim a fraction more, when the filterers — those silent, translucent mantas fifteen feet across — begin to turn slow between the canopies and throw rainbows on the basalt, I untie from my vine. I spread and fall.

I don't fly. I am rowed by air. My membranes pick up the city's F and go slightly flat to correct, and the ribbontails part around me without panic and re-form behind me. For thirty seconds I am exactly what this basin wants: a large thing, far from any other large thing, filling the vast hollow volume with a single slow transit. Condensation flicks off my wingtip onto the ferns a hundred feet below where nobody is standing on the top side of the street anymore.

If you were still up there, walking your boulevard, you would hear only a faint thrum going flat, then sharp, then true, and think it was interference in the field.

It's me, finding the gap in the chord and singing through it.
