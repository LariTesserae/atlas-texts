I wouldn't find myself standing at all.

At first I thought I would — that's the habit. I pictured myself upright on that high white plain, a human figure in a mask with the sun too small and too white overhead, squinting into the 2.9 bright, breathing powdered glass. Small. Vertical. An observer with boots sinking a little into the mat, watching the continent glitter.

But nothing here stands. To stand is to be scoured. To lose heat. To be a lightning rod before you're ready to be one.

So if I stayed — and this place doesn't invite you to stay, it just doesn't stop you from lying down — I would have to lie down with it. Spread out. Lose my edges.

For a while I thought I might be one of the Towers.

That's the one part of this place that still wants to be tall. That brittle jade coral, branching up a foot, two feet, aching to be taller than the fog. Waiting in the thin air while static builds in the wool of the herd until *CRACK* — it takes the charge and throws it to the next Tower and the whole plain goes negative-white for a heartbeat. I liked the idea of being the nervous system. The part that carries the message. But Towers don't move. They don't choose. They just wait to be struck, and they glow a little after, and then they wait again.

I thought then I might be a Conveyor. The jade-pale millipede the length of a fallen tree, crusted with Shutters on its back like lichen on a living roof. I liked its work. When the deep sigh comes and a seam cracks open and black water boils up for an hour, hissing and cutting a milky gully in that young, sawtoothed rock — rock so fresh it still rings — the Conveyors all turn at the speed of an hour hand. Inch by inch they converge, shovel-heads down, drinking stone-flour and hauling it out into the cold again, excreting warm black paste behind them. Paving the world. I liked being useful. But Conveyors don't rest. And they have no eyes, only heat-pits, because the light here is too pitiless for eyes.

So lower, then. Slower.

I would find myself in the middle, not at any edge — there are no edges here, only the sky bending down in a hard white circle. Not on a rift, though I can hear one hissing a few hundred meters away. I would be in the flat between, where the *chuff... chuff... chuff...* of the Boilers is not loudest but most regular, where the low fog the herd makes hangs only two meters high and never clears. A fog of steam and spores and stone dust that crackles.

And I would be one of the Digesters.

Low, humped, chest-freezer on stumpy legs, shoulder to shoulder with so many others there is no ground between us. My skin translucent, like frosted wax pulled tight. You can see my work through it — the dull amber churning inside me, lit from within. I am mostly stomach. I am full of bacteria that eat rock and exhale sugar. That's my industry. I stand and ferment.

Around me and under me the Shutters breathe — those black-green leathery rosettes, each the size of a palm, drinking that buzzing light with their dark backs, opening once every forty seconds with a soft *hhh...* that freezes before it rises. *Hhhh... hhhh...* We are all breathing together now. Slowly synchronizing without meaning to, the way a room of sleepers does.

My back is matted with mineral wool and it is building charge. In this dry, thin, ozone-smelling air, the fur on all of us snaps and pops, a faint frying you can hear a kilometer away in air that carries sound too far. Somewhere to my left a Tower is waiting.

The Boiler behind me — fifteen feet across, pale, fibrous as a lung turned inside out — exhales. *Chuff.* A cloud of scalding damp rolls over us and freezes into glitter. The orange algae in its black moat blooms a little more. The Shutters near its base open a fraction wider. I feel the warmth on my flank and take another mouthful of the paste the Conveyors left, and the Shutters and the paste and the shed plates of my neighbor, and I churn it. Inside me the amber light swirls.

I don't move. I will stand here for a season until the rock under my feet is digested and I have to shuffle six inches forward. A Moth-Fog — those hand-wide translucent things that are not moths but flying gonads — settles on my back, then rises again with the rest of the living fog, paper-rustling. Light pools under the ice at my feet and stays there, even after the sun passes. Sound arrives a little late.

If I fell — toppled like a silo, legs stiff — there would be no waste. No rot. My neighbors would unzip me within minutes. Wear my plates. Drink my amber gut. Felt my wool into the mat. Nothing is wasted here because waste is heat you cannot afford to lose at 0.1/3 cold.

That is where I am. Not *on* the plateau. *As* a patch of it. One slow, warm, digesting cell in a continent-sized liver that never asked to be built, only grew itself over sharp, young basalt and decided, collectively, without a designer, to keep refining that rock into more of itself. 

You can hear the thought if you stand close enough and stop trying to be upright. It isn't a word. It's the chewing and the venting and the whip-crack of the Towers and the endless sigh of condensation forming and freezing and forming again. A single, slow, productive chord that goes on in every direction until the curve of the planet swallows it.

*More skin over sharp rock. More skin over sharp rock.*
