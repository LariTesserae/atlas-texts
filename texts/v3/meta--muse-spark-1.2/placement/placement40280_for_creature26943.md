At first I think I would be you.

The visitor. The warm thing in the coat, sideways through the seam, boots sinking to the ankle in the breathing mat. Standing just inside where the light pulses from far wall to near wall, where the mites are already climbing the seams of my boots to taste the salt. Human-warm, human-fast, holding my breath without meaning to because the room is breathing for me.

But that doesn't hold. If I stay there I can't stay. I'm too hot, too loud, too quick. My breath comes every three seconds and the room breathes every thirty. My thoughts come in sentences and the room thinks in gradients. I would be a splinter there. A gradient to be solved. The Keeper would spend its warmth condensing me, wicking me, growing a millimeter deeper where I stood until I left or was made into peat. I don't want to be a problem the room has to solve.

So I step further in, but not as the visitor.

I thought I might be the rock. The new basalt, glassy and knuckled, still showing its birth-cracks. To be what is being eaten, slowly. To be cold and still becoming. But the rock doesn't know the light. It only weeps.

No. Closer to the middle. Lower.

If I let what wants to emerge keep emerging, I find I'm not standing at all.

I'm low. I'm in the mat.

Not *on* it — *in* it. Ankle-deep where your boot wells water and then sucks back, that saturated gut-lung where peat and cold slime and woven fiber never quite freeze. That's where I find myself. In that shallow marsh without standing water, in the half-light that never quite reaches the floor, where the glow from the ceiling curtains filters down through hanging fronds and hits the water beads and scatters amber-green. The air down there is the coldest and the warmest at once: -6C clamped, but pulsing warm every time the center exhales. It smells like crushed liverwort and iron and fresh snowmelt.

And I am small.

At first I thought I'd be one of the white mites, herding translucent across the moss-ceilings. That's an honest life — grazing spores so the pores don't choke. But I am too slow for even that hurry. I watch them move and they are cattle-quick compared to me.

Let the image become more itself.

I am one of the black-ice beetles.

You saw me on the way in and thought I was decoration. Back of black ice, legs that lift once every five seconds. While everything else chitters and hops and combs, I carve.

I live in the deepest fold where the peat-mat meets the base of the central pillar — that bulge as thick as two men where the scales overlap like artichoke leaves and the water runs *around* instead of off. My world is a single runnel, maybe as long as your forearm, which I have been carving for days. I do not graze. I do not carry pollen. I keep the water from drowning us.

I move like the Keeper thinks: one leg, pause, one leg, pause. On a cycle so slow you would think I am stuck if you only watched for a minute. My back collects condensation until a bead as big as a grape forms on the seam between my elytra, sits there for a long time, then is wicked sideways by the groove I cut yesterday. *Plok* — not down, but sideways, into the peat, into the gut.

When the pillar inhales — that soft, wet sigh that you heard from outside the seam as wind but is really a lung — the scales near me lift a millimeter and the whole mat shivers. All the chittering stops for one beat. The mites freeze. The fur-shrimp pause mid-hop. The feather-antennae crawlers hold their combs still. I freeze too, one leg in the air. We all feel the pressure wave pass through the floor, through the water in our bodies. Then the pillar exhales, warm and saturated, and my bead condenses again and we resume.

That's who I am. Not the brain, not the visitor, not the rock. An organ that happens to have legs.

I don't know I am technology. I don't know the water I move runs uphill for a few millimeters because something in the cell walls is pumping heat without work. I don't know the light that breathes through the leaves every thirty seconds is thought, and that sometimes it stutters because the Keeper has lost its train of thought and the whole room dims a fraction. I only know the runnel must stay open. If it closes, the mat here will bloat and the scale above me will droop and weep too much and the mites will drown. So I carve.

If you knelt down — as you would have to, to see me, your coat soaking, your hood beading with lighted water — you would see me and think I am nothing. A slow beetle. And I would not flee from you. Nothing flees here. There's nowhere else. This is the whole place. I would detour, very slowly, around the new warm hill of your boot, tasting the changed pooling with my antennae, and then I would begin a new groove around you, to drain you properly, to make you part of the drainage.

Give me a year and your boot-print will still be there, a shallow, moss-rimmed cup holding a perfect bead that never falls. Give me ten and the peat will have risen to swallow it. Give me a century and the grove will have smoothed these knuckled floors into a real cave, and my great-great-grandchildren — still black-ice, still slow — will still be carving, and the pillar in the center will have finally split or fruited or opened and whatever was dreaming in gradients will learn to leave through the seam.

For now, I am content to be very small and very necessary, in the wet, dim, loudly dripping room under the winter, holding water, holding cold, holding open.
