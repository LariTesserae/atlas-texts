I didn't find myself at the rim.

I thought I would. Coming down through that salt lip, the air going warm and green-oily in my throat, the roar coming up to meet me like an insect sea — I thought I would be the visitor. The next pair of boots on the white dust. Standing in the plaza that was too big for its people, looking up at the ribbon of sky and feeling conspicuous.

But I wasn't there when I looked.

I was already lower.

If you came looking for me now, you wouldn't find me on the terraces where the light hits first, where the rope bridges sing and the copper pans flash. You wouldn't find me in the plaza at all. The plaza is for being seen across distance, for hand-talk flashing terrace to terrace, for sleeping spaced apart under stars. I am not good at distance.

You would find me on the north curve, about two thirds of the way down, where the bowl wall leans in a little and the slot of sky is late to arrive. The houses there are older — not ruined, just softened until their salt-brick corners are like river stones. That's where the curtains grow thickest, because the light comes slanted and they have to reach.

That's where I am. In the seam between house and plant.

At first I thought I would be one of the people. One of the tall, oiled ones with amber eyes and a veil of woven husk. And I am — partly. If you need a shape for me, give me long hands, the knuckles always faintly shining. Give me the pale wrap the exact color of the brick, gone loose at the ankle. Give me the small gourd at my hip that I thumb open a dozen times a day, working that clear lime-sharp oil into the backs of my hands, into the creases of my elbows, behind my ears. I have learned to keep my mouth closed when I walk. To breathe shallow through the nose. To not sweat.

I tend a lane that isn't really a lane. It's a negotiation.

Above my doorway — which is low, lintel sagging a finger's width in the middle — a sheet of those jade, glass-leather leaves hangs like a tapestry. Each leaf as thick as my thumb, overlapping the next like scales, veined white, waxy to touch. The holdfasts are flat white discs cemented to the sandstone, and if you tried to pry one off you'd take the rock with it. For three houses in either direction, the other tenders cut theirs back hard, leaving clean stone and open walkway. I don't.

I leave mine long.

I am the one who lets it come close.

In the mornings, when the bowl is deafening and the light is still white and brutal, I stay inside behind the fiber drape like everyone else and listen to it grow. You can hear it — a soft creak, then *tack* as a pale runner throws itself across air and finds purchase. By noon the lane would be stitched shut if I let it. The runner would find my door, seal it with a cord thick as my wrist, and keep going. The others would come with their hooked poles of bone-black wood and slice it clean, catch the beading oil in a shallow pan.

I let it touch the doorframe and stop.

I have a copper knife, short and bright, hammered thin. I don't use it to clear. I use it to *edit*. With the tip, I lift a new tendril before it hardens and redirect it. I nick a leaf here so the sheet splits and light falls through in a different pattern. I cut a window exactly as big as my window, and train the edge to frame it instead of covering it. The plant tolerates this. It is not hostile. It just wants light. If you give it a shape that still drinks sun, it will take that shape.

So my house looks half-eaten from the outside, or half-grown. Green-gold curtains hang down on either side of the doorway and meet above it in an arch I taught them to make. Inside, it is cooler, amber, filtered. The roar is muffled there to a hush, like being inside an instrument after it's been played. On my flat roof, my loom stands under an awning of woven husk, but I weave slowly, a hand's breadth a day. Mostly I watch.

Who am I in this town of sixty-some souls who will not flock?

I am not the elder who sits with a child watching a vine tip extend millimeter by millimeter for an hour. Though I do that some afternoons, when one of the few children — small, serious, mouth closed — brings her mat to my doorway and we say nothing together. I am not the old woman who pulls a whole curtain with her pole and makes the bowl chime like a struck bell.

I am the keeper of the houses that are not quite houses anymore.

Because the rule of the pocket is: the city will not expand. When a family dies out or moves, a terrace is taken back. Green fills the hearth, roots the windows. Elsewhere, a new doorway is cut exactly as large as needed. The city breathes in and out and the balance holds.

Someone has to be the one who stays in that breathing.

When a house goes empty — swept clean, salt dust, a last oil lamp left cold — it doesn't stay empty long. Within days the pale threads come questing. I am the one the others sign to across the plaza: *leave this runner?* and I sign back *leave it.* I watch the vine enter through the window that was left open, throw its discs to the plaster, hang its jade plates inside. I keep the lane past it clear, but I let the interior go. I sweep the threshold but not the floor inside where the leaves have made a second, translucent floor.

I am not sad about it. No one here is. There's a patience to it that has nothing to do with mourning. The house is not lost. It becomes something else that still belongs to the bowl.

Between pruning times I sit in that doorway — half salt-brick, half living curtain — and I oil my skin. I blink rarely. My eyes have learned that thick, slow lid. I listen to the tick of the salt expanding and contracting, so faint you can only hear it in the twenty minutes of gold late in the day when the sun finally clears the lip and pours sideways through the slot.

That is when you would see me best, if you came looking.

The whole bowl goes luminous. Every translucent leaf lights amber and jade from within and the deafening rustle softens into a true hush. The heat lifts just enough that we all come out with our veils down. And in that light, the oil on my skin, the wax in my wrap, the leaves framing my door all catch the same gold and I look — for a moment — like I have grown there too. Like another form of the same waterless flesh that learned to drink sun directly and keep every drop of itself sealed inside.

I don't speak then. None of us do. We sign small, close things, mouth to ear if we must.

If you lowered yourself down and thought you were a visitor, I would not ask where you came from. The desert above is only a lid to me now. I would offer you the gourd without a word, show you with my hands how to wrap the cloth so your lips don't crack, how to keep the water in your throat when the wind funnels down and plays the bowl like a drum.

And I would make a space for your mat — not in the vast, white plaza built for a thousand, but here, on the north curve, in the lane that is a negotiation, where the green has learned to leave a doorway open just wide enough for one more, if you know how to sit still and let it grow around you.
