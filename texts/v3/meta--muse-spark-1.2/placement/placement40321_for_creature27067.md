I don't know, at first.

For a long time after you arrive there, you don't know where you are *in* it. Only that you are *under* it.

The light comes first. It has a weight even before the heat does - a cool, bleaching pressure on whatever surface you have that can feel pressure. The sky is not blue. It is that violet that hurts, thin enough to see through. And breathing is wrong. Each breath is too clean, too sharp, and not enough. It whistles somewhere. It doesn't fill you.

Then the sound. You thought it would be silent. From far away it looked silent. Up close it is never silent. A low thrum in your teeth, a hydraulic sigh that takes a full minute to complete, a clatter like someone dropping sheet metal a mile away, and again, and again. And a ringing when the wind moves. Not rustling. Ringing. Bronze struck and left to shudder.

You open what you have for eyes and you are not on the ground.

That is the first thing you learn about yourself here: you are not a ground thing. The ground is white and polished and empty for a mile in every direction, a salt skin stretched tight over basalt, hot with stored sun and smelling faintly of alkali and iron. Nothing that lives here stays down there. To be down there is to be erased by light and exposure. You have learned not to want it.

You are *up*.

And you are clinging.

For a while I think I might be one of the flat ones. The silver-rosette things that cement themselves to the terraces and never move except to grow a millimeter a season. Or one of the mirrored domes, the grazers - I can feel a faint, constant vibration through my feet, a rasping. I can feel bright light being thrown off my own back. But no, the domes are heavy, they are patient, they scrape. They don't have this thinness in them, this tremor before falling.

I am lighter than that.

I am on the underside.

I realize this slowly because gravity is telling me wrong. I am hanging upside down from a ceiling that is not a ceiling. Above me - which is really below me - the white plain glints and swims a long way down, made tiny by distance. Below me - which is really above me - is the vast, sooty, corrugated face of a plate. A leaf the size of a house, thick as a door at its spine, ridged like corrugated steel, matte black-green drinking the sun and converting it to pressure and noise. I am holding onto it with hooked feet that find the grooves perfectly. My body is long and jointed and exactly the color of the plate, so that when I don't move I am not there at all.

No. That is one life here, but not mine. I can see that one, a few corrugations over, utterly still, waiting for a cloud of silica gnats to pass. I am not that patient.

The wind shifts. The stomae along the leaf - shutters the size of hands - slam open all at once with that metallic cough, and a hot, ammonia wind blasts past me, the plant exhaling. The whole plate shudders and vents and I have to tighten my grip. In that gust is everything: heat, volatiles, the faint oil-mist that coats everything in the crown. I breathe it not with lungs but through my skin. I do not drink. I have never drunk.

And then I let go.

That is who I am here. If I am anything here at all, I have to be something that let go.

You would not find me by looking at the plain. You would look for a long time and see nothing but the white tableland and the solitary columns spaced like temples, and you would think the world was empty. You have to look *up*, into the living volume, the vertical continent.

You would find me forty meters above the salt, where the fluted trunk becomes candelabra, where the air is turbulent and hot from the column's breathing.

I am translucent. When I launch, the sun goes through me. Veins of the same black-green as the leaf run through wings that are too large by any lowland standard - three meters across and light as paper, corrugated just like the plates that made me. I am not mimicking them. We just solved air the same way.

I don't flap so much as fall into the thermal the plant is already making for me. Every column is a chimney. All day it superheats its waste air and throws it skyward, and there is always a column rising right off the crown. I step off the leaf-edge and the hot column catches me and lifts. The sound down there - that deafening industrial sigh and clatter - drops away into a bass thrum I feel more than hear. Up here there is only wind and a thin, needle shriek that cuts through everything. That's me. That's how I see when the light is too hard and the sound is too loud. I scream constantly, a bright wire of sound, and the echo comes back off salt and leaf and other gliders turning with me.

There are fifty of us above this one crown at midday, all turning without ever touching. We were not born up here. Down in the warm, humid vent-chambers inside the crown, in that brief artificial weather the plant makes for its own chemistry, we were a storm-cloud of silica gnats. Then we were pupae glued to the gill-shutters, trembling while oil misted us. Then we climbed out, dried in an hour, and walked to the edge of a leaf on hooked feet we would never use again.

We don't land on the ground. Not really. At dusk, when the small white sun goes exact and the plates turn edge-on to dump heat and the blue electroluminescence comes up in the young veins like a circuit waking, we spiral down - just for a moment - to the dimpled basin at the foot of the column. Where the roots have dissolved the salt into amber-stained smoothness and cold oil weeps from cracks in the dark. We press our pale bellies to it. We soak. That is how we drink without drinking.

The rest of the time we are air. We feed on the gnat-storms by combing them with fringed mouths as we fly. We are fed on in turn by the hunters still clinging to the plates, who snap us out of the air with forelimbs too fast to see. When one of us dies, we don't fall and rot. We desiccate before we hit the salt, a dry husk that is stripped to fiber and mineral before noon by the grazers and the plant's own acid sweat.

I don't live long. The column I ride will live five hundred years. It doesn't know I'm here except as a pressure change in its vent.

But for this hour, in this thermal it made, over this white plain it quarried grain by grain from the air, I am the thing that uses the sky it is manufacturing. I am not separate from it. I am one of the ways it fills the empty volume it created.

If you came looking for me, you would have to look up until your neck hurt, into that bleached, thin, violet depth where the plates ring like struck bronze, and see a turning, translucent fleck, screaming.

You would think, for a second, it was just light on dust.

Then it would bank, and throw a shard of sun off its mirrored belly back into your eyes, and you would know it was alive.

And that it had never once been thirsty.
