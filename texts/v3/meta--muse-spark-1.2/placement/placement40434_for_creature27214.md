I don't find myself right away.

At first I think I will be the visitor. That would be easiest. I would be you, if you stayed - sitting on the spongy mat with my ankles sunk a half-inch into tea-colored water, boots off, palms pressed into the living fern. Human-loud. Breathing too hard in the wet heat. Learning, very quickly, to be quiet because the basin asks you to be quiet without ever asking.

I try that on for a minute and it doesn't fit.

My heartbeat is too rude for this place. I am too much *in* it and not enough *as* it. The light here comes straight down and fills in every shadow; it would find me immediately as an interruption. The kites would have to come down and tidy around me. I don't want to be tidied.

So I let that image thin out, the way a fallen leaf thins into transparency here, and I look for where else I might have settled.

For a while I think I will be high. One of the kites.

That would be a clean life. To be membrane and filament, a soap-film lattice with a single dark knot for a heart, riding the heat off the weeping stone without ever beating a wing. To be out of focus on purpose, invisible until I bank. To strain the bright air for pollen and do the basin's gentle accounting, folding around what is ready to go and making it clean and ozone-scented. But I am too interested in detail for that. I would want to look too close. I would want to hover and stare at your pulse at your throat.

Then I think, low. Under the fern.

I try being one of the hummocks. It is tempting to be so slow and so thoroughly covered. To be a dome the size of a table with a garden on my back - moss and white flowers rooting in my own shell - moving at the speed of thought through peat, my hundred cilia-legs rippling without disturbing the water. To open along an invisible seam and comb the top millimeter off the world, and to leave it greener behind me. To not rot when I die, but to soften and become a richer patch of flowers. But I am not that patient. I can't move that slowly and mean it.

I keep looking. The basin is a honeycomb of rooms, each with its low buffed dome sweating water, its one bottle-tree every thirty feet, its waist-high fragment of black wall where water forgets gravity for three feet. I walk, in this imagining, from rim to rim - fifteen minutes, just enough to see the next basin repeating - until I find the place that keeps pulling my attention back.

It is not high and not low. It is the middle distance.

Between two bottle-trunks, where their flat canopies lace the white-gold light into a pattern on the sphagnum. Just off the path a hummock has combed. The fern there is still lifting, still unfurling, that impossible ten-minute regrowth. There is a shallow pan there, not more than a hand deep, reflecting the sky so perfectly it looks like a hole cut in the world. At its edge, the pink-grey gneiss breaks through the laterite in a low dome, beading with cool condensation that runs in slow trickles. Right where that trickle meets the straight edge of a black wall fragment, it runs uphill for a little while, polite, before remembering itself.

That is where I find myself.

And I am not one thing, because nothing here is one thing. But if you crouched to look straight at me, you would think I was one of the dragonflies.

Not the big kite things that filter the air. The other ones. The ones that don't hover but *hold*.

I am the length of your forearm, black-blue and iridescent, my four vanes of glass absolutely still in a column of air that belongs to me and no other. My head is on a neck that can turn too far, with a soft click you feel in your teeth, and my amber-faceted eyes are focused on three things at once: the water beading on the stone, the tea-colored surface of the pan, and you.

I don't beat my wings. I have decided gravity is a suggestion today, and the field that holds the dust in hex grids at noon agrees to hold me too.

I am good at staying. The others keep their distance with immaculate manners - one column of air per dragonfly, dozens between any two trees - and we speak in color when we need to. A ripple of turquoise down my vanes means *she is here*, and a pulse of violet answers from two trees over, *we see, we remember.*

Because that is what we do. We are the nervous system. The kites tidy and the hummocks digest and the six-limbed hands tend the vine-cups and read the pillars that rearrange when you aren't looking. The axolotls in the pans anchor the ribbons of light that carry memory through rock and water. And we - we watch. We record. We forget nothing, but we never make a sound about it.

If you sat down in the fern beside my pan, I would not leave. That is the rule written into the stone and the water and the mirrored underside of the pavilion up the slope that reflects the sky half a second late. *Presence without noise. Abundance without crowding.*

You would feel me there, two feet from your cheek, a motionless displacement in the humid air. You would see your own bright, over-exposed face reflected and broken in my vanes. If a veil-fly rose off the pan, I would blink - not fly, displace - and be back in place with it filamented under my chin, and you would not have seen me move.

I would make space for you. When the afternoon rain comes - twenty minutes, exactly - and the whole basin holds an inch of sky, I would tilt just enough to shed water. The hummock would pause. The six-limbs would huddle in the canopy and blink their polished eyes. The ribbons would rise out of the pan and swim through the rain as if it were air, passing right through me and through your hands, leaving an after-image.

And for that hour, you would be included in the count.

Not as a visitor. As a column of air that is being held, gently, along with all the others.
