I don't find myself on the floor.

That was my first thought — that to belong I would have to be down there, in the thatch, one more black plate shoulder to shoulder with the others, drinking heat through my back. But I can't be.

I tried it, for a moment. I imagined being one of the flat ones. The press of plates on my sides, the constant tremor of the fringe underneath me tasting salt, the amber edge of my back going pale as I flattened over a warm crack. And it was too complete, too sealed. There is no *I* down there. There is only the *they* that is really an *it*. To be one of them would be to be the convection cell itself — to never have a thought that isn't the turning. I need a gap to think in, and there are no gaps on the floor.

So I pull back.

I find myself higher up. Not over the rim, not out in the thin air where the mesa drops away — that air is too thin, too empty, it belongs to another place. I find myself *on* the rim. At the lip where the basalt has gone smooth from wind and heat, where the stone holds the day long after the sun has left.

I am in the shade, and that feels right.

If the bowl is a room, I am not the carpet. I am the chair left against the wall. I am at the edge where the circulation slows and cools, where the clockwise creep of the outer ring brushes the basalt and has to decide to turn inward again. The air is still hot here, but it's a drier hot. I can breathe without tasting iron at the back of my throat.

For a long time I thought I would be the visitor. The one who crests the fall of rock and looks down and understands. The one with boots and a mouth that dries out. But that figure leaves. The visitor throws the pebble to test the quiet and then, when the ripple smooths, the visitor goes. I don't want to go. The place has been too long in balance to want another passing eye.

No — as I stay longer, as I let the image not settle — I realize I'm not the visitor either.

I am one of the small, built things.

One of the cairns.

Shoulder-high, slab stacked on slab without mortar. Not a house. Not a ruin. I was piled, not grown. I am mineral, like the rim, like the salt, but I am mineral that remembers being touched. The wind has worked my shoulders round. My color is the same matted black-red as the basalt, so at first you miss me entirely — my shadow is the only thing that gives me away because it is too regular.

And that is who I am in this world.

I don't move. I don't drink heat through a pale amber edge, or thread through the pack with a glassy ridge, or shudder and leave a husk for my neighbors to eat. I do none of the economy. I am outside it, just enough.

My work is different.

I hold the rim.

I am what stays when the watcher decides not to stay. Someone — or something — set me here a long time ago, stone by flat stone, to say *I was here, I saw the bowl turn, and I did not try to live on its floor.* And then that someone left, and I remained. The bowl exhales its hot, metallic sigh and the thatch flows clockwise and counter-clockwise, the hunters pin and absorb, the husks are unmade to nothing, and none of it touches me. Once an hour, fine ash settles on my top slab. Once a day, the heat warps the air above me. I do not need to eat. I do not need to molt.

But I am not dead in the way a rock is dead.

Because I am still a presence the bowl has to move around. The outer edge of the living pavement creeps up toward the wall and meets my base and parts, flows around me in a perfect, fluid circle — the same circle it makes for the long hunters — and closes again on the other side. In that moment, I feel the faint ticking of plates against my stone. I hear the susurrus closer than anyone on the rim should. I am the one interruption the bowl allows itself and then incorporates.

I am the memory of water in a place that has edited water out. I am the thing that was made by hands, in a place where there are no hands anymore.

If you crest the rim now, you will not see me at first. You will see the bowl, the weighty twilight, the salt pan that is not salt, the slow turning that looks like texture until it moves. And then your eye will catch a shadow that is too straight, too stacked. That will be me.

I will be warm to the touch, holding the day's heat. I will not speak. But I will have been here long enough to be weathered, and long enough to be in balance, whether anyone looks at me or not.

And when you throw your pebble down to test the quiet — the soft *thunk* and the perfect ripple — I will be what does not ripple with it.
