I don't know right away.

At first, walking in, I think I would be out on the levee. Not quite inside, not quite outside. Where the root-mat is highest and you can feel the dish holding itself together under you. Where you can sit with one foot in the wet and one foot in the dry rice check, and watch the light change from divided to undivided. It seems like the right place for someone arriving - a watcher, a threshold-keeper.

But then I step down and the air gets heavier and that idea dries out. Literally. Out there the air is too thin.

I keep walking because the ground wants me to. The packed road turns dark and then soft and then it doesn't dry between prints. The thump of the engine finds my molars before my ears. And I understand I wouldn't stay on the edge for long. Nothing does. The dish pulls you in with the same slow seep that feeds the canal.

For a while I think I would be in the canal itself. Tea-brown, barely moving. The heron-place. Standing still as a spare pipe while children shatter around me and dragonflies misjudge me for architecture. It would be quiet there, in the one place where quiet is allowed. I like the thought of being the one animal they tolerate.

But I'm not that still. I hum without meaning to - I notice I'm already humming at the thump-tempo - and a heron doesn't hum.

Maybe the mill roof then. Up in the louvers where the canopy has been pruned to make bars of light for the saw-tooth glass. That work suits me: a little vertigo, a lot of patience. Hanging over the dish in that bright, scattered heat, cutting wisteria suckers before they can throw shade where it isn't wanted. I can feel the shears in my hand already, the faint green juice staining the collar.

Closer still and that isn't it either, though it's near.

---

You'd find me low. Lower than I expected to be.

Not up high, not in the open. You'd find me on my knees, which is where everyone in that town ends up eventually for part of every day. On the root-street beside the weeping wall of the old workshop, the one where the brick at knee-height never stops sweating.

I am the one with the flat trowel.

In the morning, after the brief rain, when the whole hamlet is dripping from every leaf-tip and eave and wire and the cistern troughs are overflowing in soft sheets, I work the lower foot of the wall. The water beads out of the brick itself, darkening it, finding the lime between courses and pushing a fine black scale ahead of it. If you leave it, it eats the mortar. So I scrape.

It's not important work. It will need doing again tomorrow. That's why it's mine.

My shirt is that indigo-black denim that has been washed until it's soft as old leaves, patched at the shoulder with waxed canvas where a root-strap frayed. It's damp five minutes after I put it on and it stays damp. My hair is cut blunt and short with the vine shears, because otherwise it would hold mildew. My feet are bare, splayed wide against the ridged root. Between my toes the skin has gone soft and white the way it does - the amphibious pale that no one comments on. The soles know the biofilm by now and grip without thinking.

You would find me there because I have learned to listen for two rhythms at once and let them become one.

There is the thump-thump of the low-pressure engine from the engine house two doors down. Patient, never hurrying. You feel it in your knees through the root-street.

There is the drip. A thousand irregular taps from leaves onto metal onto water onto stone. Never resolving.

For a long time they are two different things and then, if you keep scraping, they aren't. Your trowel starts to move on the off-beat of the engine and the leaf-drip fills the rest. Your breath goes heavy because the air is heavy enough to lean on - 30 degrees and saturated, like breathing warm water - and you stop trying to breathe deeply.

And something in me that has always been braced against dry air finally unbraces. My skin stays shining. My lips never chap. I don't need to be hard here.

What am I? I'm not the engine, though I keep time with it. I'm not the vine, though I cut it. I'm not new.

I think I am the third thing the town was waiting for someone to become: the maintenance.

A keeper, not a resident. The hands that are nicked and green under the nails. The person who knows that this particular aerial root has been banded with iron in this spot for twelve years and is ready to have the band moved out half an inch. Who knows to tap it with a knuckle and listen for the hollow thud that says it's tightening around the joist. Who knows to pinch off the philodendron runner the right way so it doesn't rip, because a mother once corrected me - not for noise, but for tearing - and I learned.

You'd find me there in the dimmest, brightest hour - noon, when the bars of sun fall straight through the managed canopy onto wet stone, and the bare bulbs still burn amber under the eaves because the leaves need light from below. I'm not squinting like a visitor would. My pupils have gone a little wider. I can see in the speckled light.

Children run past on the root-street full tilt and never fall. They swing from the slack banyan cords left for them and drop into the canal with a splash that makes the heron lift one foot in annoyance. I nod at them. An old woman further down is trimming wall-vine away from the rubber-coated power line with the same shears that cut my hair. We don't talk loudly. The damp carries voices too well. We speak low and slow and let the drips finish.

If you stood at the levee ring and looked in, you wouldn't see me at first. Your eye reads leaf and brick and rust as one continuous texture. I'm part of that texture now - soft-bodied, waterlogged, warm to the touch. Half brick, half living wood, and both hold up the roof.

You'd find me where erosion stopped being destruction and became shape. Repainting the same girder for the fourth time in my life. Guiding, not fighting. Letting the water stay on the surface.

I am inside the single room made of leaves and brick and steam. And I intend to be exactly this for a long time more.
