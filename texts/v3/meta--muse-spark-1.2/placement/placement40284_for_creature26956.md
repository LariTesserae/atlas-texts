I didn't know at first.

When you asked, I was still coming up the trail, I think. Still in that breathless place just below the cloud layer where the stone steps bow in the middle and the air goes thin and the forest starts to stop being trees and starts being *lattice*. My first thought was high and clean and wrong.

I thought I'd be above it.

That I'd be the hawk that cuts through the shafts on a clear morning, that sees the whole shelf at once like a blue-green palm held out from the mountain. Or the wind in the prayer flags above the drying racks. Something with distance. Something that could admire the weave without having to maintain it.

Then the cloud came through me - not over, *through* - and left beads on my sleeves and in my hair, and the dripping started, that ticking from leaf to leaf to leaf, and the frogs and the jays and the bees all at once, and I understood the shelf doesn't allow distance. Not even for a visitor.

So I tried again, lower.

Maybe I'd be the old man on the root-buttress, the one who ties cordage and pinches off runners without looking. The one who will nudge a sapling half an inch and say *come back in eight years*. I liked his patience. I wanted to be the one who knows where every wattle wants to go.

But my hands were too restless for that kind of waiting.

Then I thought, maybe the child. Barefoot in the black peaty pool, shrieking at the exact pitch of a jay until you have to check which is which. The one with the serious job of clearing leaf mash from the rill so the water keeps singing. I liked that small seriousness.

And that was closer.

Because if I stay long enough for my eyes to learn the weave again - for the walls that I thought were walls to resolve into hedges that were taught to be walls - I know where I settle. Not at the top where the thrushes are, or the bottom where the sow snuffles through sphagnum. In the middle. Where the middle has always been.

You would find me on the second tier, if you can call it a second.

Find the basalt at the back, the one that is always shining even when it hasn't rained, where the water slides down in a sheet no thicker than your hand. Follow the rill they taught to run along the main lane instead of through someone's house. Where it turns - where they stacked the little cairn to ask it to turn - there's a shoulder-width lane that dips. A branch as thick as your torso, flattened by a century of feet, crosses a pool the size of a bed. My doorway is there, where a bromeliad once flowered overnight and blocked it and we all had to laugh and cut it back and the doorway never quite closed right after.

I live in the thick of it. The place where everything touches everything else.

You would find me crouched, more often than not.

Not because I am small - I'm not. I am built like the rest of them, compact and broad, calves big from bowed stairs, shoulders like root-burls. Hair cut short and braided tight back because otherwise it collects litter in an hour, already going green at the seams no matter how often I rinse it in the rill. Wool the color of lichen and peat, pockets everywhere, a fern spore sprouting in the seam of my left sleeve that I have allowed to stay because it looked right.

You would find me with my hands in water. That is who I am here.

I am not the builder who bends saplings into future doorways. I am not the elder who plants hazel for a daughter not yet born.

I am the one who keeps the water moving.

They gave me the rill when I was seven, the way they give every child a stretch of it - not as a chore, a small seriousness. Clear the mash. Lift the fallen leaf that will dam and rot. Make sure the drain-chain of linked vines from the roof-meadow above me is still unknotted and still ticking. And I never really stopped. Now my stretch is longer, from the basalt seep down past three houses, past the granary that is a hollowed cork of trunk, past the place where the roof meadow of the family above me drains into my lane.

My hands are the giveaway - broad palms, nails cut to the quick and black at the rims no matter how recently scrubbed, fingers stained tannic brown-green, sensitive at the tips the way a gardener's are. I don't grip. I test. Where does this stem want to bend and where will it snap if I force it? Which way does this water want to go if I let it?

I am loud. I have to be. Over the ticking and the frogs and the bees and the woman calling her goats from the canopy, you have to project. When I laugh, it bounces wetly off the living walls two lanes over. When I scold a child for letting a runner colonize the drain, I sound hoarse and bright.

And I am never still. That is the other thing. Even when I am sitting on the edge of the pool with my feet in the black water, watching the goats crop the roof above me, my hand is reaching without looking to pinch off a shoot trying to close the smoke-hole that the family above me needs, or to redirect a vine over the doorway as someone passes. Fidget, tend, nip, tuck. Stillness is how you lose ground here. If I stopped for twenty minutes moss would start up my boots. If I stopped for a month the forest would eat my doorway completely.

But I am also patient in the way this place teaches you to be two things at once. I am frantic at noon because the cloud is coming in and the wool is still out beating on the stone slabs, and in the same hour I am patient because I know the hazel I wove into my own wall three years ago will not be a wall for another five, and that the peat pool behind the basalt where we wrap our dead in moss will be there long after I am the one wrapped.

I share the vertical without thinking about it now. The family below me keeps their chickens from scratching out my wall-roots. The family above me - I keep their smoke-hole clear so it doesn't choke the fledglings in their eaves, and they keep their goats from eating my sedum roof down to the thatch. We have words for *above-my-head* and *below-my-feet* that are familial terms and I use them without translation.

At night when the cloud settles thick and muffles the edges and the small careful fires are coaxed alive in stone pits because everything is so damp you have to coax flame too, I contract with everyone else. My loft fills, the hammock sways on the living branch-way. The dripping gets louder. The frogs get louder. Someone on another tier plays that reed flute with two living leaves still on it, thin and reedy and human against the green roar, and I don't answer with music.

I answer the way I have all day - by listening for whether the water is still singing.

If it is, I am here. If it stops, I will be there before you notice it stopped, on my knees in the damp, shoulder-width lane, hands already cold and brown-green, persuading it to go again.

Don't cut, guide.

Pull me gently back into traffic if I block the way of vine or goat or child, hand me something to do with my hands.
