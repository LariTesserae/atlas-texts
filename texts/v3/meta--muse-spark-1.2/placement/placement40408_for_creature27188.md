I don't find myself right away.

At first I think I would be high up, because the world is high. I look for a vantage — a ridge, a promontory — but there are no promontories here. This is not a world that has a high place and a low place. It is all high, evenly. So I can't be above it. I have to be *in* it, at ground level, where the frost polygons fit together like scales.

At first I think I would be one of the big ones. It's natural to want to be the Millback — warm inside, armored, purposeful, making iron out of stone, leaving a straight line behind me in a world that has no straight lines. Or a Loomer, tall enough to feel less crushed by the sky. But I am not patient enough to be a Millback. I move too fast, even in my stillness. My metabolism is not a furnace. If I tried to be a Millback I would stall. I'd cool and stop on the pavement and become a hill that lichen grows into. That's not death for them, but it would be for me.

So I get smaller. Closer to the ground.

At first I think I would be the wire-lichen, those few-centimeter black filaments that stand up and shiver. That would be honest — to be sparse, embarrassed, vibrating without being heard. To live entirely in the wind's attention. But the wire-lichen doesn't get to choose where it stands, and I find I am already choosing. I am looking for shelter.

I find myself in the lee.

Not out on the open pavement where the wind has planed everything to a dull shine, but tucked behind something that has learned how to stay. Behind a yardang — one of those whale-backs of basalt the wind has worried for a million years — where the pavement breaks into a shallow pan. A tafoni hollow just deep enough that the light, that dim ground-glass light that throws no shadows, pools a little darker there.

There is a billet there. One of the Millback droppings. Twelve meters of pitted pig iron, still faintly warmer than the air though it was left weeks ago. It has already pulled itself a few degrees toward magnetic north. Frost is forming on it as the light thins — not snow, but that industrial dew, condensation beading and freezing in place, so the whole bar glitters dully for a minute. The lichen has started to colonize its underside where it holds damp.

Draped over the billet and the lip of the hollow is a sheet of Loomer felt. Not fresh — torn, half-buried, black carbon fiber gone grey with dust. It flutters without sound. Underneath it, the air is different. A teaspoon of damp has been held there for weeks. Olive algae has bloomed and crusted. White isopods the size of my hand move through the felt like fingers through gauze, farming mold.

I am there, under the felt.

If I am an animal at all, I am not one of the factories. I am one of the small white detritivores that live in the looms' leavings. Or, if I am still human, I am what a human becomes after a few traverses here: not an observer on top of the ecology, but a detritivore within it.

I would be wearing what I found — not built, because nothing is built here, but secreted and repurposed. A cloak cut from that same Loomer felt, wrapped tight, holding my own damp against me. I would be lying low, cheek almost to the frost-polygon, on my side so I take up less wind. My breath would be shallow enough not to disturb the Tanker's vapor column thirty meters up, where a distended Tanker has found the seep in this pan and is silently drinking it dry.

Who would I be? I would be the one who stopped trying to move at human speed.

I would have learned to move at 1.4. Which is to say, I would appear to be still. I would have been in this same hollow for three days, watching the same Millback scrape approach from two kilometers away at forty meters an hour. I would time it against the ventifact. I would have a notebook that I don't write in, because the pencil freezes and because nothing happens fast enough to be noted, until suddenly it has happened.

My job, if I have one, is to listen. Because the sound here is 0.0, and that is a kind of pressure. I would be the pressure gauge. I would put my gloved hand to the flank of the passing Millback as it planes the crust beside my hollow — close enough to feel the warmth through the felt of its foot, close enough to hear inside it that muffled, wet churn, that low kiln without flame, the *tick tick* of its product contracting. I would be the thing that hears it when no one else is near enough to hear.

I would be damp, not wet. Cold, not killing. Dimly lit. Half plant, half animal, half machine, because everything here is.

I wouldn't be making anything. I would be waiting for the secretions to accumulate. For the frost to fur three centimeters thick overnight on my cloak the same way it furs the billet. For the algae to bloom. For the next Millback caste to come and roll over this bar and make it purer. I would measure time the way they do — not in days, but in traverses. *I have been here one eleventh of a crossing.*

You would find me not on any map, because there is nothing to map except repetition. You would find me by the glitter. At dusk-which-is-also-dawn, when every billet and every back briefly beads with frozen condensation and catches the dirty-coin sun, my hollow would glitter too — my felt, my frost, my iron bar, all the same color for a moment.

And then it would go pale and give up, and I would keep lying there, quiet as the industry around me, patiently being reworked by the world beneath me.
