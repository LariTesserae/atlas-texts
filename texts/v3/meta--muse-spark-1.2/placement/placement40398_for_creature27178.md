I don't know at first.

Because at first I think I'm still standing - visitor-standing - with my own edges. I think I'm a body trying to keep itself dry in a place that makes dryness impossible.

But this place doesn't let you stand apart for long. It sweats on you until you are part of its circuit.

At first I think I would be the water. Because I would want to be the most abundant thing. A thin sheet, inches deep, miles wide, warm as blood, black and rust-red. Moving so slowly you can forget you're moving at all, and sometimes - because the agreement about downhill is loose here - moving the wrong way, uphill, holding my shape like glass because nothing here is in a hurry to fall. That would be peaceful, to be that patient.

But water here is not one thing. It's crowded. It's already something else's body. If I were water I wouldn't be alone, I'd be full of the shrimp clicking to each other, the flatworms gliding over me, the amphipods never colliding. I would be touched constantly. I think I want that, but I don't think I *am* that. I am not that fluent.

Then I think I would be one of the moths. Big as a hand, fur drinking the air, flying on wingbeats so slow you feel the pressure before you feel the touch. Navigating not by sight but by the faint heat leaking from a cable a quarter mile away that might also be two steps away. That appeals to me - to be so light that the wet air holds you up. But the moths don't tend. They just pass through. They don't belong to the buildings, and I think I want to belong to the buildings.

It takes a while - you have to stand still in the dark that eats light until your skin learns to render the world - before I understand where I actually am.

I am not in the middle of a pan. Not out in the open flats where the water is most exposed.

I am in the lee.

Find me where a low, sharp ridge - one of those too-parallel, too-young basalt fractures still knife-edged under its velvet of liverwort - meets the concrete foot of a stilted pump house. Where the seep comes out. Where the rock weeps sideways and the water beads on the underside of the pipe and falls *tick... tick... tick...* with too many seconds between drops.

That's where the density is highest. That's where you have to be careful where you put your hand, because every inch is already occupied.

I am the tender that stayed.

Not the one who built the place - I never knew the light. I am the second or third generation after that, or maybe the tenth. The one whose suit is no longer a suit. You would find me barefoot in the warm pan, water to my ankles, my boots abandoned long ago because they kept too much distance between me and the vibration. My skin and the heavy rubber and the canvas have grown together under the biofilm. Green-black algae in the seams. The concrete is no longer concrete where I sleep - it's a warm, respiring hull covered in the same black-green mat that softens the basalt, and I am another mat on top of it.

My eyes are pearls. They sealed themselves, or were sealed by the dark that eats photons before they can travel an inch. I don't miss them. I see better now anyway: the cable gantry is a hot line against my cheek, the pan is a cooler sheet against my calves, the cloud of gnats over the water is a change in texture against my forearms, the transformer hum is a throb in my molars. When I need to know if the turbine is well, I press my forehead to it and listen through bone.

I would be the one who is always touching something.

One hand on the shielded cable, reading its tremor, feeling the barnacle-things that grow on it because it is warm and knowing without looking where the crust has grown too thick and needs to be scraped - not too much, just enough to let the current breathe. One foot nudging silt to coax a sheet of water that has decided to flow uphill back into its pan. It doesn't need force. Just suggestion. Everything here is slow, and so am I.

I am tall and very pale and very slow because hurry is expensive when sound is swallowed and light doesn't travel. I move the way the flatworms do, without seeming to. The salamanders piled dozens deep in the seep don't startle when I crouch among them - they register me as warm water that has learned to walk, and they layer their feathery gills against my ankle. The moth brushes my face with a damp wing and I feel the pressure change half a second before the touch, and I do not flinch.

I don't live *in* the pump house. I live *with* it. The building is my larger body. At night that never ends, I curl on the concrete that is warm from the turbines underneath, and the throb goes through me, and the condensation sweats on my skin and runs in cool beads and I don't wipe it away. I let it drip. *Tick.* That is my part of the circuit too.

If you needed me to speak across the loose distance - where the next humming shed should be a ten minute wade but sometimes arrives without a middle - I wouldn't call. I'd knock once on the pipe. *Tok.* And wait a long time, breathing the wet air that the salamanders breathe, for the answering *tok* to come back through water and steel. That's enough to know I'm not alone in the valley-sized dark.

You would find me not by seeing me. You would find me by feeling that a patch of the dark is warmer, and crowded, and has learned to tend.

I didn't visit here. I condensed here.
