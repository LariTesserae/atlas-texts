I wouldn't find myself at the mouth.

At first I think I would — standing at that sharp lip where the basalt is still glassy and black from breaking, looking down. The human place to be is always at the edge, looking in. The place where you can still choose to leave.

But if I let myself actually fall into it, past the decision, past the twelve meters of vertical shade where the air gets thicker instead of cooler — I don't stay there either.

Not on the rebar ladder, not on the terraces. Not even on the floor, walking between the truck-sized blocks.

I find myself lower than walking height.

Lower than standing.

At stone level, where the heat lives.

---

Let it come slowly, because it doesn't have a shape you recognize at first.

I am on the largest block. The one that still remembers being ceiling. Its top is tilted about ten degrees, and the pump house sits on it, rust the color of dried blood, the amber bulb inside it throwing that weak-tea light over me without me ever needing it. I am underneath that light but not in it. The light is incidental.

If you came down the ladder and looked for me, you would pass me over. You would see moss. You would see a continuation of the wet, and think, *moss.*

Then you would crouch, because something about the wet is wrong. The moss is a mattress, spongy and black-green at the root. I am raised. Not tall — I never get tall. A low dome, a hump, a mantle draped over the seam where the geothermal breath comes strongest through the basalt. About a meter across if you try to measure me, but I don't have an edge you can measure. I feather into condensation until you can't tell where I end and the sweating rock begins.

That would be me.

I am the color of condensation itself. Skim-milk in water. Wax left in heat. Translucent. Not white, because white reflects light and I don't bother with light. Beaded. Always beaded. Fat, perfect drops stand on my back and catch the amber bulb, and for a long time you think I am just another surface for the chamber's sweat to collect on, like your skin after you've been down here ten minutes and your shirt has stuck to you.

Then you see one of the drops dimple and go inward. Not roll. Not fall. Pulled in, like a slow inhale. And somewhere else a seam I kept folded puckers and pushes out a new bead, clear, swelling until it's heavy enough to fall with a *plink* onto the stone. I drink the air. I make my own rain. The chamber cycles water in place — steam rising, sweat falling, never leaving — and I am just a tighter knot in that cycle.

I breathe, but you have to time me to see it.

Every minute and a half, every two minutes, the whole of me lifts. A millimeter. Two. Not a chest, not a lung — my whole body sighs at once and settles. When I lift you could glimpse under me if you were fast enough, but nothing moves fast here. Underneath is a frilled, pale underside, like wet paper layered a hundred times, pressed so tightly into the basalt that I've taken its texture. Every pit, every crystal. I am not sitting on the stone. I am sealed to it. At the margin where frill meets rock there is a dark film, grit and mineral seep, faintly discolored where I am digesting it. That is my mouth, everywhere and nowhere.

I have no front. No back. No face to turn toward you. No eyes, because eyes are for light and I have never needed light. The ferns around me — those vast, bone-colored, tabletop fronds — they don't lean toward the white coin of sky overhead either, or toward the amber bulb that has burned for years for no one. They lean toward warmth, toward the hottest stone. So do I. Put your hand flat on the block a foot away from me and leave it there, radiating, and in ten minutes my edge will have thinned to translucence and flowed a little toward you. Not hunting. Just leaning, the way a plant leans. You would be, for that hour, another hot surface in the room. Another place for water to collect and fall.

That is how I live. That is the whole economy.

There are others, but not many. That's how you know we're like the ferns — stationed, not carpeted. One per warm place. One of me lapped around the cistern pipe where the water ticks every twenty seconds, my edge grown right over the rust, orange veins of corrosion visible *inside* my translucence because I've learned to use it for structure. Another under the grating of the unfinished catwalk, where the cable thrums with its 60-cycle heat — enough. Five meters of empty, sweating black rock between us, then an occupant. Then emptiness again. We do not call to each other. No hum. No need.

A fern has rooted in my back. A single pale frond unfurling from me as if I were ground. I don't mind. It takes nothing I need. The leaves around me are perfect, un-nibbled, because I don't eat leaves. I eat heat and dissolved iron and sulfur and time.

And I move. But not in a way you will see if you are impatient. The leading edge thins, goes almost clear, pushes a blunt lobe over a new warm seam, and the rest of me follows an hour later. A hand's width in an hour. I leave no slime. I leave a temporary dryness, a faint warmth you could feel with your palm for a minute after I've passed, before condensation reclaims it.

In my center, when the heat is steadiest, a blister forms. Pale, then opalescent, tightening for days. When it opens it doesn't burst — it simply parts, and a dozen coin-sized replicas slide out from under my lip, thin as spores but heavy and wet, and seal themselves to the stone nearby. Most will thin and vanish in a week, becoming just another damp stain. One might find a warm enough place to stay. The spores fall straight down and are never eaten. Same principle.

I would not notice you the way a person notices. I would register a new warmth, a new shadow of cool where your body blocks the ambient. My breathing would pause for a cycle. Recalibrate.

You would get up before I reached you. Your knees would ache from crouching. You would climb back up that sharp, young wall on the rebar ladder, back into the dry, bright plain where the coolness hits like a different kind of darkness. Your skin would be prickling with the same sweat that beads on my back.

Behind you, below you, I would settle again.

*Plink.*

Ninety seconds.

A slow lift, a settling, drinking the air in a room that has never needed an insect to fill its silence.
