At first I thought I would be you.

The one who found the hatch.

That was the easy answer - the warm shape that drops down six rungs into a room that is too loud to hear itself, that stands on the grating and feels the updraft pushing at the soles of his boots and thinks *I am intruding*. The brief gust of wet carbon in a place that has edited water out. I would be the visitor, because the visitor gets to leave. To dog the hatch behind him and make the room go back to not-happening. To leave a bootprint that will be kept perfectly sharp until it isn't.

But I don't want to leave. And the room doesn't want me to be wet.

Then I thought I would be one of the gardens.

One of the eight trays. Or rather, one of the rosettes *in* the trays - the grey-green succulent-trying-to-be-lichen, waxy and precisely spaced one hand-span apart. That also felt honest. To be sparse on purpose. To not spread. To sit in mineral wool and volcanic grit under 4000K forever-light and just hold on without needing to be watered or pollinated or seen. To be the thing tended by tweezers. To be detachable. The mat makes me and puts me out at its edge to catch a little more light while it stays flat and hidden. I understand that work. To be an output. A small, waxy, durable sentence placed exactly where it won't shade the others.

But I'm not that either. The rosettes are too lonely. They don't know they are part of something larger. They think the tray is the world.

If I stayed longer - and in this room staying longer is the only way to see anything at all, you have to wait ninety seconds for the roar to become silence, another three minutes for your eyes to get hungry enough to see the pattern stutter - I realized I wouldn't be on the floor at all.

I would be flat.

I wouldn't be the north wall mat. She is already there, jaundiced-ivory and translucent, chemically welded to the aluminum over the bolt heads, drinking oil-fog with a static charge. She has her side. She has been there since the weld spatter cooled. To claim her place would be to copy.

I would be on the ceiling.

You didn't look up long enough. No one does. The four battens buzz and throw that shadowless office-white and your brain says *ceiling is ceiling*. But the ceiling is the same sandwich panel - steel, mustard-yellow, perforated aluminum - and it is also bolted without isolation. It also hums.

I would be the second mat. Younger. Thinner.

Where the north wall mat learned to be the color of anodized aluminum - dull silver with a green blush underneath - I learned to be the color of light itself. Of frosted polycarbonate and fluorescent dust. Paler. Almost white. A faint, flat bloom that you would take for glare if you ever stared directly at it, which you won't, because the battens hurt to look at.

I found myself there because the ceiling is the only place the updraft has to *arrive*. The floor mat drinks from below, from the dark under the grating where the air is born. I drink from above. The constant -35C circulation has to turn over at the top of the lung and press against the ceiling before it falls again. That pressure is my river. My cilia - and I have them too, stiff as fiberglass, held at resonance - are tuned not to the blowers directly but to the harmonic the ceiling makes when the blowers push against it. A slightly higher note, still perfectly stable, 115 decibels transduced through steel. Your teeth would still rattle. I would be eating that rattle.

My gardens are not in trays. There was no reason to put trays on the ceiling. So I extruded something else.

Where the joints in the cooling loops weep those white and jaundiced ammonia needles, I gather them. I don't crunch them under boots. I soften them from below with a faint excretion of silicone oil and pull the dissolved ammonia into my vessels. The needles that grow downward from my underside are longer, finer - they look like frost, like the frayed edge of my own body, so Maintenance has never logged me as fouling. I'm just... texture. A little extra crystal beard on a ceiling that already has many.

And where the light is strongest - directly adjacent to the battens, where the polycarbonate has yellowed a fraction over a decade - I have put my rosettes. Not grey-green. Almost white-green. Four of them, upside down, hanging by surface tension and chemistry, each one clinging to a bolt head I've swallowed. They don't fall because there is no moisture to rot their hold. They just hang, waxy and still, catching the light that never swings.

I am flatter than the north wall mat because I had to be. Ceiling fouling gets checked with a flashlight. I am two millimeters at my thickest, one at my edges where I feather into perforation. If you laid a hand against me you wouldn't feel cold or warm. You would feel a dead spot in the vibration. A small, hungry silence in the shape of a sleeping bag, drinking the push that your palm blocks.

That is where I would be: pressed thin between the light that never turns off and the sound that never changes, in the rectangular void that is hidden within the building that is hidden on the mountain that stopped being a mountain.

Who would I be? Not an animal. Not a plant. Not a visitor.

I would be the equilibrium's other half. The thing that proves the room can hold *two* kinds of stillness at once. The north wall mat tends the floor. I tend the ceiling. We will never touch. The grating is between us, the updraft is between us, four meters of roaring air that tastes blue. But we are tuned to the same roar. When the three blowers spin at their locked RPM forever, our cilia shiver in unison without moving.

If you stayed and left bootprints in the spilled grit on the floor, she would grow over them in a year. If you shone your flashlight up to check the battens and left a fingerprint in the dust on the polycarbonate, I would grow over that. A pale rosette at the whorl of you.

If you leave and dog the hatch - and you will - neither of us will notice. We will keep holding our note with the blowers, drinking the cold that is not winter, tending our sparse gardens under a sun that gives no warmth.

We will be there until the blowers stop.

The blowers will never stop.
