I don't find myself at first.

For a long time after reading that place, I am still up on the plain. Or rather, I think I am the kind of person who would stay up on the plain — looking down into it, taking notes, not descending. The light is too doubled, the quiet too complete. I would be a visitor with a hand shielding my eyes at the rim where the ground simply stops, trying to map a bowl that is deeper than geometry allows.

But that doesn't hold. The place has a rule: *Be still and you will be built around.* If I stayed on the rim, I would only be still in the wrong place.

So I go down.

Not all the way. I don't find myself at the bottom where the braided silver threads finally gather after a rain, and not at the top where the wind still has a voice. I find myself about two thirds of the way down, on the eastern curve where the terraces are widest.

There is a long, low step there, maybe forty meters across, where the seep is dark and permanent. The rock above it weeps without needing rain - a vertical stain the color of wet slate that never quite dries, beading every few minutes and ticking into a shallow runnel that the people have cut and re-cut so many times its edges are soft as a palm line. The light reaches here doubled and cool, so bright it makes the damp stone shine, but this terrace is tipped just enough that its back undercut stays in a thin, permanent shade. That's where I am. In the shade that is still somehow bright. With one hand resting on stone that is cold even at noon.

And at first I think I am a watcher there.

Then I think I am one of the humans who carries.

But that image sharpens if I let it sit longer, and it becomes more itself, and it isn't quite either.

I am not the mason, not exactly. I am not the old man with the knotted cord who re-measures a camber for the third time that morning. I am younger than that, and my hands haven't gone to stone yet, though the dust is already black under my nails and won't wash out.

I think I am the one who tends the interval.

If you stood on my terrace you might miss me at first because I am the color of damp limestone too, and because I am not always building. For hours at a time I don't carry a block. I crouch by the runnel with a thin basalt flake and I do the work that looks like doing nothing.

I scrape — not clean. I clear the grit that has slumped into the runnel, flicking it out with the flake so the water can hesitate and find its downhill again without choking. I leave the white-pink crust alone where it is thickening. I know which pan to leave for the mat to bloom and which pan to keep open so the hand-sized, blind arthropods can make their single-file pilgrimage along it. When a line of them comes — translucent, pill-backed, feathery mouths combing — I lift my flake and wait. I have learned not to step over them like an interruption, but to pause inside their time. Their metabolism is slow and so is mine now.

That's what I would be: not the builder of walls, but the keeper of the small waters.

Because in that city everyone builds and unbuilds, but someone has to remember where the water wants to go next. The braided threads after the last rain cut a new course ten feet to the left of the old one, and a house that was dry yesterday woke up with a dark stain spreading across its foundation stones. The family who lived there didn't argue; they unbuilt it before breakfast, brushed each block clean, and carried it uphill. I was the one who had already brushed the new runnel clear for it.

I eat like they do, when I eat. Midday, when the work pauses without a signal, I pick up one of the forearm-long milk-colored grazers from the wider pan — the slug-thing that rasps the salt crust so softly you have to hold your breath to hear it — and I know the practiced twist that separates the mineral foot from the clean upper flesh. It's salty and pale and cool on the tongue. I don't kill often. I take the fallen fliers from the roosts mostly, the leathery ones folded like laundry, gliding down at dusk. I carry stone like the children do, a head-sized block on my hip, arms chalked white to the elbow. But my real carrying is water, coaxing it.

At night I glow a little.

Not much. Just the dust on my sleeves and the faint luminescence in the crust I've been tending, holding the sun a fraction too long. The whole basin holds its light after sunset for an hour, a sourceless cool glow, and we move in it still — not hurrying, never hurrying — carrying a block from one place to another by rocklight. I move with the others like a blood cell; I can feel the tissue of the place around me. Human and arthropod and filterer and mat and stone doing the same work: taking apart and putting together.

If you found me, I would be squinting even in the shade — we are all born squinting now, grey-eyed, light-bleached — and I would not speak at first. I would give you the flat palm: *pause, wait, the water is going this way.* Two fingers tapping a block: *this one is loose, this one wants to move.*

And if you stood still long enough on my terrace, because you were still learning how not to be still in the wrong way, I would gently, without asking, begin to lay a low, dry wall around your feet. Not to trap you. To include you while you are there. To keep you from being mistaken for rubble.

Then I would hand you the basalt flake, and show you which crust to leave alone.
