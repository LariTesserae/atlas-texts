I thought at first I would be standing.

That's the habit. You give me a valley a kilometer wide and I put myself upright in it, on top of it — a figure against the felt, boots denting the sphagnum, breath hanging. A witness. A visitor with a warm back and eyes that make shadows even when there are none.

I stood there for a while in my mind and it didn't take.

It was too loud. Too warm. Too separate. The mass swallowed the sound of my boots before I could hear them. The cold found every seam and made me a little weather system the valley had to work around. I was an error it would smooth over in an hour. I could feel it already suturing behind me. I don't want to be an error that passes through.

So I lay down.

If you stay, you are incorporated. I lay down on my back in the felt, not on the path that isn't a path. Cheek to a cushion plant. Those hard hemispheres that you could stand on. Up close it isn't hard at all, it's ten thousand leaves pressed so tight they've become a stone that breathes. It held a little heat from fermentation underneath. Iron and sweet rot. I could feel the turgor pushing back against my shoulder blades, half an inch of give, patient as a muscle holding. I thought maybe I was the frost that forms on it at dusk. Needle by needle. Briefly perfect, briefly there to catch the water-bead light on every moss tip and then gone.

But I am not that brief. That would be too lonely, to live only at dusk.

Lower.

Under. That's where I find myself when I stop trying to be on top.

Not in the center, where the hummocks are swelling and sighing loudest, and not at the slumped shoulder of the valley where the gneiss still shows through. Mid-slope, mid-mass, where the felt is deepest — two, maybe three meters thick — where the lower layers have already died into black peat and left a breathing cavity behind. A vault. Low, warm, utterly dark. The air in there is close and tannin-sweet. No white sky. No overcast light. No sight at all.

This is where I belong.

I am not tall now. I am long.

If you tore the felt open — which you won't, which the valley doesn't want you to do — you would not see me at first because I am the color of the water. Translucent white, the white of roots that have never seen light, segmented, as thick as your forearm and as long as you were when you were standing. No eyes. No head, really. Just a blunt end with a valve lip, opening and closing, opening and closing. A peat-siphon.

I don't walk. I peristalse.

I live in the black tea-water, in the braided rivulet that never sees the surface for long. It threads through the honeycomb under the sphagnum flesh, from a place that is pooling to a place that is thirsty. When a hummock up above takes its slow inhalation and swells, the willow-net above me ticks — *tick... tick... tick...* — through those lignified pipes as thick as your wrist, shunting sap under pressure, and I feel the gradient change as a drop in pressure against my skin. That's my cue. That's the only light I need.

I draw myself forward by gripping the peat fiber with my whole body and pulling the water through me. Cold, tannic, full of fine grit and dead leaf and spore. I don't drink it so much as I wring it. I take it from where the mass is already wet enough and I move it, a body-length at a time, to where the haircap moss is leaning west, combing dew out of the air a meter above, but not getting quite enough. I am one of the living pipes among the grown pipes. The valley's other peristalsis.

I have no voice. Sound would die in the felt above me before it ever mattered. I have no call. What would I call to? Everything I need to know comes as pressure and chemistry against my membrane. The sourness of fermentation getting stronger means a cushion anvil nearby is working. The faint iron tang means water is moving. The sudden cold seep means frost is forming up top again and the lichen shutters have lifted to vent spores — I feel it as a tremor in the felt, a faint sigh that travels down through root and peat as a vibration.

Around me, in the same vault, the small ones work. I can't see them either but I feel their crawl. The amber mites and slate-gray isopods, each the size of a rice grain, moving at a speed that looks like stillness unless you are still for a very long time. They prune. They chew a leaf that browned a second ago and excrete it as peat an inch away, farming fungus on their backs as they go. They walk over me without noticing, or noticing and not caring. We are not a food chain. We are shifts on the same factory floor. Quality control and plumbing.

I thought I would want to be something with eyes. Something that could look across the whole U of the valley and see the shivering churn, sector rising while sector subsides, the dark vein opening like a river and closing. But I don't. To see that would be to be separate from it again.

Here, underneath, I am not separate. The dwarf willow and birch net is braided right through the wall of my vault, root-grafted into a single kilometer-wide net, and its nodes swell and tick right next to me. When the sphagnum flesh above me splits to exhale spores, I feel the release as a lessening of weight. When it sutures, I feel it tighten. My skin is the same wet membrane as the moss. On the inside I am fibrous, too.

I do not hunt. I do not run. I do not need to be warm.

I move water from where it is to where it is needed, until the gradient evens, and then I rest in the dark until it tilts again. A meter an hour, maybe less. The valley respires around and through me. If you put your ear to the felt far above, you would not hear me at all. You would only hear the tick of the willow pipes and think it was the whole story.

And when you stood up — you, the warm weather system who lay down for a night to see if you could belong — and left your body-shaped hollow steaming faintly in the cold, it would be my water, slowly, that helped swell it shut. Dew combed down by the haircap brush, shunted through the willow valves, pushed by my blind, valve-lipped mouth, arriving a week later to fill the place where you were.

Not out of malice. Not even out of hunger.

Just because nothing is wasted, and everything that can be plant, is plant, and I am the between-thing that learned to be plant enough to stay.
