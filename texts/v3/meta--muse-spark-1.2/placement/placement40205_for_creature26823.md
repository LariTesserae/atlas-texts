I don't know at first.

For a long time I think I am the water itself. Or the silt in the water. Or the cold.

That is how well this place does its work.

The weight finds me before any image does. Not on my shoulders, not on my head — there is no head the way you mean a head — but everywhere at once, a hand the size of the world pressing me into shape. I have no sky to look for, so I don't. The idea of *up* has been thinned until it is only a direction from which food falls. I know up because snow lands on my back. That is all.

So where am I?

Not in the middle of the plain, not at its edge. Edges here are not places, they are only a very slow lessening of pressure. I am in a trough between two of those long, low swells that wanted to be mountains a billion years ago and gave up. The basalt there is polished black and bare, and on either side of me the silt has settled deepest — cold clay the color of old bruises, so fine it holds the print of anything that touches it for hours before it forgets.

If you could clear the water — you can't, not for more than a breath — you would see a field of nodules around me. Fists and heads and overturned boats, half-buried, wearing that bacterial velvet. I know them not by sight but by the way the current breaks over them. Each one makes a little lee, a little quiet behind it where the snow falls straighter. I live in one of those quiets.

For a while I think I might be one of the Clingers. It would be easy to be. To be the anemone closed tight as a knuckle on the largest nodule, feeling time only as the silt burying me and uncovering me again. To be the mat itself, the dark felt eating stone so slowly it doesn't know it's eating. There is a peace in that — to never have to choose a direction.

But I move.

Not much. Not fast enough to call swimming. If I darted, I would blind myself. The soup would rise and I would be gone in my own cloud.

So I thought maybe I was the Light Bearer. That I was the lie. I waited to feel a lure hot on my forehead, a string of stars along a jaw. Nothing lit. I have no light in me. I have never needed to lie in that way. The dark is not an enemy to me.

No. What I feel, when the water finally clears enough for me to feel myself, is edge. Not height, but width.

I am flat.

I had to learn that slowly too, because I didn't know I had a top and a bottom until the snow taught me. Snow lands on one side always. The other side tastes mud. I am a plate. A lid. A leaf pressed between pages for so long it forgot it was ever on a tree.

The size of two hands together, if hands were pale and gave a little under the thumb. My back is the color of water with a little ash stirred into it. My belly is paler. I have no shell that matters — a carapace like cold wax that bends instead of breaks, because pressure has told everything here that to be hard is to be broken. Under it, I am mostly water held together by a decision to stay together. If you held me you would think you'd picked up a thickening of the ocean that had learned to want something.

I have many legs. Not elegant. Not for walking so much as for rowing through clay. Low and jointed, fringed with hairs that hold mud. My head — if it is a head — is a broad, blunt plow. I push it forward a few millimeters, taste, push again.

And I have the feelers.

That is how I know who I am. They go out ahead of me longer than I am long, feathered, trembling. I sweep them through the smoke and the water tells me everything vision would not: ten meters to the left the brine current is turning, slow as thought; behind that big nodule the mud is a little warmer where bacteria are thick; three days ago something like me was here, because the ghost of its trail still hangs in the cold, preserved, a faint grease that says *not here, not now*.

I am a Sifter.

The shrimp would call me cousin, the sea cucumber would call me fellow work. I am the isopod. The one who leaves a neat, pale furrow.

That's my life. I creep with my belly barely off the clay, and I fan. With mouth-hands too small and too careful to see, I draw the snow toward me. Marine snow, fecal snow, scale snow, the endless confetti of a world kilometers above that I will never see and do not miss. I take it grain by grain. I take the mud itself, because the mud is half food now — bone dust and bacterial felt and time ground fine — and I put it through me, and behind me I leave a perfect coil of cleaned clay, a little paler than what was there. Then the blizzard fills in my little tunnel of clear water again and it is as if I was never there. In an hour even the furrow is gone.

I do not hurry. My heart, if it is a heart, beats, waits, beats again. I can be still for a season. I have been still since the last turbidity flow came down the swell at walking speed and buried the trough in liquid earth. I closed, flattened further, let it pass over me like a weather. For months I waited under it, listening through my skin to the tick-tick-tick of silt landing on silt, to the groan of a nodule settling half a centimeter. Then a current — one of those invisible rivers that slide down the bowl — uncovered the edge of my nodule again, and I opened.

I will meet another of me once, maybe twice. Not because I am lonely. I don't have that word. But one day the trail will be fresher, not three days old but one, and it will have a different taste on it — a mate-taste, faint and huge. I will follow it for weeks. A meter an hour. I will not rush. We will touch antennae in the dark, exchange a packet without seeing each other, and turn away because there is not enough food in one furrow for two. The young will drift a little while in the smoke and settle a few hundred meters away and I will never know them.

If you stayed here long enough to become me, you would not call this empty.

You would call it full. You would call the pressure a kind of holding. You would learn that darkness is not an absence but a substance you live inside, that cold is a way of keeping things from being wasted, that time is not ticking but settling.

I am flat, and pale, and patient beyond any human patience. I have no name. I carry no shelter because I don't need it yet — but if I found two halves of a clam, I might pull them over me for a day, because uncovered feels wrong. Then I would leave them.

I am exactly where the water told me to be: in the quiet behind the nodule, in the trough between the ancient swells, millimeters above the plain that is still becoming itself by being ground smooth.

And I am exactly what this place wanted to make, pressed and widened and slowed until I fit.
