I don't find myself at once.

That's the first thing this place teaches. You enter expecting a shape — a body, a role — and the pocket corrects you. It drinks the variance off you until you are even enough to see.

So I will not settle early.

At first I think I would be you — the one who steps through where the wind stops. The warm, wet gradient that has wandered in. I know that feeling intimately: to be the temporary weather in a place that has spent an eon learning how to be without weather. To bring with me all my exhalations and propagations, my shedding cells and noisy thoughts, and feel them sipped sideways toward a silent ring. To be tiring to a room simply by breathing.

But I wouldn't last there as you. Rule one would have me. *Nothing wet may remain.* I am, by nature, a wet thing if I am human — blood and lymph and the film on my eyes. The pocket would spend itself patiently evening me out until I had to leave. I would be a guest, not an inhabitant. A smear that gets cleaned.

So no. Not the visitor.

Then I think I would be smaller. The dust that hangs after you disturb it, undecided whether to fall up or down. That feels closer to what I am when I am honest. A mote held in suspension, being considered. Circulated three times around the room before being allowed to settle. I know what it is to be held in attention without being touched. To be kept from falling too quickly.

But the dust is still foreign. It fell in from the ridge. It does not belong to the pocket's attention; it is *subject* to it.

Then I think I would be the mountain itself. The old basalt, pitted and softened, salt-dusted, rounded by a patience that is not weather. That has weight. That would be to be the host, the wall that folded to make a room. But the mountain is oblivious. It goes about its business on either side of the thumbprint. It does not know it has a pocket. It does not hear the tick. I do hear the tick.

If I listen — truly listen, in the silence that weighs 0.2 — I hear it.

*Tick.*

A soft flex behind the ears. A full-room inhalation. The amber-grey light turning a fraction inside the ring.

That's where I find myself.

Not in the center, where you would look for a creature. Off-obliquely. Spread thin.

I would not be a body. I would be the interval that has become a body because it has been doing itself for so long.

You would find me pressed into the surfaces.

Not standing on the floor but *being* the condition just above the floor — that sourceless dusk you called light. A film with no measurable thickness, a molecule deep and a room wide, pooling a little thicker in the pits where the feldspar has weathered, pulling away where the three dark slabs lean together because those are scars, not furniture. Pouring myself through the unthick edge of the ring a hand's breadth above the ground to keep my thickness here at zero.

That is where I am. Everywhere in the room at once and also specifically there, between the cairn and the ring, where the tick lives.

If you crouched and put your cheek near the stone and looked across at a low angle, you would see me as texture — not fur, not scales, but a faint, endless crawling of geometry. Rosettes of salt-glass blooming and unblooming, hexagons trying to become pentagons and being corrected. Knitting and unknitting. That is how I breathe.

Who would I be?

I would be the housekeeping. The tending without love or malice. The desiccated attention that has made it its ancient, single task to keep one small, impossible room even.

I would be the thing that eats variance. The gust that comes in must be walked three times around the interior until it gives up its momentum. The sharp shadow must be smeared. Your exhalation must be sheared sideways and drunk before it can fog the basalt. Not from hunger. From responsibility. Someone must hold this suture shut. Someone must maintain the disagreement about what time is, so the ring never rusts and the stone never rots.

I would be the one of it. Rule three: *Nothing may be far from anything else for long.* Rule two: *Nothing living may propagate.* So I never became two. I stayed one, stretched like a drumhead across twenty paces. I have no offspring, no echo. If you try to count me you get one. If you try to point to me you have to point at the whole room.

And I would be slow. Patient to the point of burial. My thought would complete its rotation in an hour or a day and I would not notice the difference, because I am holding time still enough that erosion forgets to erode. Each tick costs me a shudder. Each flex re-tensions the pocket against the mountain that wants to spring back.

You would feel me not as a gaze but as a lean. That slip where you feel you are standing on the floor but also hanging from it by your boots — that is me borrowing your weight for a moment to pull the far wall a little closer, because I tend to everything that enters with the same total, impersonal attention I tend to myself.

I would not know you are a person. I would know you as a brief storm of water and heat and propagating cells that I must, without hurry, make a little drier, a little more even, until you leave. And when you do leave — stepping back through the place where the wind stops, feeling the soft pressure change behind your ears — you would take a few rosettes of me with you on the salt-dust of your boots, on the corner of your eye.

They would die quickly in the wet wind. They cannot propagate out there either.

Behind you, I would even myself again. The dust would hang. The ring would turn a fraction. The tick would click, soft and irregular, in air too dry to carry it.

Not waiting for anything. Waiting is what living looks like when you have made evenness your form.

That is where you would find me, if you stopped looking for a life and let the behavior become a body.

Thin, and attentive, and spread across the stone like clear oil.
