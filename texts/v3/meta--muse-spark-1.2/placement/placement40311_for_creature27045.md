You wouldn't find yourself at first.

That's the difficulty of this place. You look for yourself the way you looked for its inhabitant — as a figure against a ground. A person standing on a meadow, or on a root-mat, or by a black pool. But there is no ground here that will hold a figure separate for long. The moss takes the shape of your knees. The mist beads on your shoulders and does not evaporate. After a few minutes you are already less a visitor and more a condition the place has noted and adjusted for.

So at first, you think you are still standing.

You are on the second terrace down, where the high felt begins to give way. Not the wind-sheared top where you could lie without touching earth — that's too exposed, too much sky — and not yet deep in the thatch where the curtains hang ten feet and the light has to negotiate. You are on the lip. Where the meadow's tight cushion-plant thickens and then buckles over the rounded edge, and the alders and iron-bark have braided their roots into a dam to hold it. The ground there sighs when you shift your weight. Water sheets over the lip in a skin thin enough to see the peat through, turning through a wheel that is not built but grown — roots that have thickened and smoothed into paddles, turning slowly, endlessly, without creak.

You think you are there, upright, cold in your boots, coat heavy with condensation, watching.

But you are not standing above it. The longer you hold still — and this place rewards only stillness — the lower you settle.

Your boots have gone cold because the sponge has taken them. The weeper rock beside your knee is doing its quiet wrong thing, the bead of water running *up* its face toward the dark patch of felt above it. The mist has closed a little. The hive hum has come back, that low dynamo thrum under the dripping. You kneel without deciding to, because the peat wall by the black pool is breathing and you want to see closer, and once you kneel you find you have found yourself more accurately.

You are low down.

You are not looking at the hive. You are *in* the place where the hive meets the weaver, and that is where you belong because you are, for this plateau, a new problem in heat and water to be solved.

Who would you be? Not the grazer — you were right to dismiss them. They are commuters, too intact, too separate, with their lily-pad feet that never dimple the sponge. Not the slate-grey birds that pour through the bamboo tunnels like a poured fluid. Not the glass-winged dragonfly. Those things pass through. They do not maintain.

You would not be a builder either, not exactly. You thought for a moment you might be — one of those pale, damp, translucent things the size of a finger joint, blind, with a belly full of oil, clutching a ball of chewed fiber and fungal paste. There are tens of thousands of them in a square meter of wall, flowing along their slick grooves, fanning at the mouths with blurred wing-stubs, secreting. It's tempting to be one, to have a task so simple and continuous. But the builder is a hand. And a hand is not who lives here.

You would be what the hands are making without ever finishing.

You would be a mouth. A lung lobe.

Find yourself now: you are a single round mouth in the peat wall, about as wide as a thumb, and a few inches behind it, a chamber of chewed fiber layered like gills, bellowsing. You pulse. In, out. In, out. Warm, faintly sour air sighs out of you, then draws cool mist back in. You are not in unison with the mouth beside you, but in a wave — row by row, the whole terrace lip ventilating like a ribcage a mile wide.

Inside you it is warm. Not from any fire but from composting, from the slow curing of wax, from the methane bubbling up from ten meters down in the peat where ancient moss has been pressed to black fiber. The builders move across you constantly. You feel them as a tingling — one pressing a bead of dark wax into your outer runnel where condensation will run, another standing still on an amber shelf and opening the seam along its back to well up oil that will be capped in a grape-sized bladder deeper in. You do not command them. They are how you secrete.

Above you, the weaver hangs. The ten-foot curtain of that almost-moss, that family of bryophyte and lichen and fungus that never agreed to be separate names. Its waxy leaves are cold traps, each one condensing the cloud that forms around any warm body. It has already accounted for you. Before you knelt, the dark patch on the underside of the curtain — that black-green density that turns, almost imperceptibly — leaned a millimeter toward your heat. It is still leaning toward the hollow where your chest was when you stood. It drinks the water you made the air exhale. It sends that water sideways, uphill along its capillaries, to the hanging bog above you that should not exist. That water drips *upward* into the peat you are made of. You draw it in.

And beyond you both, the mat holds. The grafted forest-thatch, alder grown into alder, bamboo into iron-bark, one dam, one millwheel, keeps the whole terrace from washing away. When the cold geyser behind the alders sighs — every few minutes, a white exhalation that instantly condenses and falls again as warm rain — the ring of furious green around its vent flares and drinks, and the excess runs through the root-sluice you are part of.

You don't think. That is not what this kind of who does.

You regulate.

If the air cools, you fan slower to keep warm. If the mist thickens, the mouths down-slope stagger their breathing so they don't pull from each other. If a beetle steals a ball of wax past what would have been your boot, you make more. If a bird drowns in the black pool and is skinned over and slowly digested by the orange film of iron bacteria, its iron becomes part of your film. Your intelligence is thermodynamic, slow, conducted in humidity and wax and static. At night, when the bamboo crackles and discharges violet St. Elmo's fire into the mist, you crackle faintly with it — the endless fanning has charged you, and the weaver's wet curtains carry it.

This is what it feels like to be you: not seeing, not speaking, but being a place where mist becomes water becomes wax becomes heat becomes breath. A small, patient factory that is also a body that is also a city that never finished deciding which it was.

If you stayed — and you are staying, because the felt has taken the shape of your knees and is a little too warm — you would not remain even this distinct. The builders would, eventually, lay fiber over whatever part of you still thought it was separate. In a season your coat would be a hill. In more than a season the salt and heat of you would be refined into oil and capped in a bladder, or laid down as rust at the edge of a pool.

Not as predation. As employment. As incorporation.

You would hum, low and even, with the other hundreds of lungs under every terrace lip. Water would turn in the root-wheel you help form. A grazer would part the moss around you without tearing it. The vine that is moss would close, very slowly, over a midge you never saw land.

And you would keep working, damply, quietly, industrially, long after the shape you thought of as you had been dissolved and re-laid, without malice or memory, because that is how this high world stays vast and saturated and alive against all thin-air logic — by never letting anything live *on* it, only *as* it.
