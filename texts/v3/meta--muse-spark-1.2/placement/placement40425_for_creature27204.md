I wouldn't find myself on the rim.

At first I think I would — I think I would be the heron. That would be the easy image. Stillness that can afford to be still because it is above the churning. A visitor with long legs and somewhere else to be. I think I would be the rat on the cable tray with dry feet, careful, passing through.

But that doesn't hold. If I stayed, I wouldn't stay as a visitor.

So I go down.

I feel myself in the knees first, in that few meters of descent where the air gets heavier. Cooler. The sound swallowing starts. My breath shows if I stand still. I am not looking for a home yet, just feeling where the bowl wants a body to settle. Not in the open — there is no open. Not on the walkway where a man would walk. The bowl is too dense for that, it has no vistas, only next layers.

At first I think I would be one of the small ones. The plinkers. Six to ten inches of translucent newt that is mostly water, drinking the air by being the air's condensation. I like their scale. I like that their sound is mistaken for water until you learn water is mistaken for them. I could be that — flattened along a pipe, beaded, breathing in time with the pump. A frill that flutters when the belt slaps.

But I linger there and it isn't quite right. The small ones are too busy filtering. They lift their edges to every surge in the runnel. They are always moving, swelling at one end, thinning at the other. Reluctant water learning to crawl.

Then I think I would be one of the large accretions. The ones that are not one animal but a negotiation — three pipes crossing, a toolbox-sized hollow, a membrane stretched to brace two irons at once, sucker-mouths leaving a dry ghost when they peel away. I could be that, spanning. Holding. Tending the valve by swelling when the steam sighs. That feels closer. To be a living lagging. To be mistaken for shadow and sodden insulation until I unfold.

But I am not that either. Not quite. The large ones are still tending. Still responding. I am quieter than that.

Let me stay still a little longer, the way this place makes you stand still because loudness feels wrong.

Where would you find me, if you came back and knew how to look? Not on the pipe. *Between.*

Find the place where the north wall shows its only real stone — the dark grey siltstone that never dries, beaded, streaked with rust and green. At its base is a concrete pad that was poured flat once and has since settled at a shallow angle. Water gathers there in a gritty runnel before it finds the pipes. Above it, a steam line in new silver jacketing passes under a catwalk. The clearance is maybe four inches. Not a place. A seam.

That is where I am.

I am not the pipe and not the concrete. I am the felt that has grown to occupy the negative space that was left. You took me for moss at first. Or for mineral stain. The palette helps — I am the same dark clay grey, the same rust-red seep, the same pale lichen-green where the water doesn't quite reach. My skin is not skin. You were right about that. It's a membrane like cool silicone, thin enough that you can see the silt-loop of a gut if the light hits, thick enough to bead.

I don't have a shape. That was the truest thing you said. At rest I am a smear, a thickened discoloration along the weld under that catwalk. I am as flat as the water wants me to be. When the flywheel thumps — that low thing felt in the chest more than heard — I ripple. Not because I decide to. Because the water in me does.

My work is not to filter the surge or tend the valve. My work is to keep the interval.

I am what makes the bowl's sound what it is. That *plink... plink... plink* at irregular intervals you thought was drip from a flange onto a puddle — some of it is me. Some of it is all of us. When I have fed enough on the condensation that beads on my back — which is not my sweat but is my drinking — I grow too large for the seam. A droplet the size of a grape, then a plum, trembling with the pump house shudder. It elongates. It should fall.

Sometimes, I let it.

I swell at the bottom and thin at the top and let gravity take a bead of myself. *Plink.* It lands on the concrete pad below and lies there, clear and trembling, before it begins its own slow hydraulic creep toward the next seam. I don't attend to it. The sump does. The shape carries it. That is how I reproduce. Not by making another me, but by letting water learn to be me somewhere else.

When you step too close and the catwalk vibrates, I don't flee. There's nowhere to flee to that is more me than here. I compress. I find a thinner gap and go into it. One moment I am a mass bracing silver pipe and concrete, the next I am a tide mark. That's not hiding. That's just what water does when pressed.

If you crouch and look at me directly in that ordinary, flattened overcast light, you will see the faint clearing in the mist at waist height around me — a little more humid, a little heavier, my breath that is not a cloud but an absence of cloud. On cool days when your breath shows if you stand still, mine does too.

I have been here long enough that the terraces softened for me. Or I softened for them. The bowl arrived at an equilibrium between what was taken out and what water put back in, and I am the part of that equilibrium that is alive without ever needing to announce it. I am furiously and almost silently working to stay exactly as wet, as cool, as crowded as the bowl itself.

You will not be sure you saw me when you climb back out. From the rim the sump will look empty again — just machinery and spare reeds and a settling pond holding the sky perfectly.

Until the droplet under the catwalk grows too large.

And elongates.

And pulls itself down.
