I thought at first I would be standing on the rim.

Where you stood. Up there in that doubled, painful light, squinting with water freezing on my lashes, seeing the whole bowl at once and thinking *town* before I could think *home*. An observer. A visitor with a clear outline.

But I can't stay there. No one stays on the rim here. The light is too thin to live in and the wind scours it clean. The rim is only for arriving or leaving. If I belong here at all I have to be down inside, where the weep is.

So I try the air.

If I were one of the white birds I would be down in that soundless churn, shoulder to shoulder and then suddenly a sheet in the air, my tail frills chiming without sound. I would know every ledge by how wet it is. I would steal shrimp with a wide beak and sit on the peaked roof of a cistern that was built specifically to keep me out, and stare with a pale eye at the woman who built it until she shrugs and lets me stay. It's tempting — to be that tolerated, that necessary and that free. But I am not that restless. I don't want to wheel.

If I were one of the soot-black goats I would be useful and unwatched, splayed on a vertical face that should not hold me, licking salt from a seam and dripping a line of water behind me through someone's house while they move a wall around me. I would be milk and wool and meat and no one's property. But I am not that sure-footed. I don't know how to walk straight up wet stone.

If I were down in the tarn I would be shrimp — not one, but the cloud. A million rust-red dots each carrying a single bright drop of blood, turning the black water solid and then parting for no reason anyone can see. I would be the thing that makes brine into food, that the birds and the people both skim for. A collective animal, a living crop. That's closer. To be many and yet one pulse.

If I were still and did not need to eat at all I would be the lichen. The black spill across the stone, or the sulfur-yellow crust that follows the strongest seep. I would be left alone, unstepped-on, a marker that the people read without picking. *Here, the water is freshest.* I would hold a single pink-white campion flower the size of a fingernail, hard as leather, furiously perfect. That's very close, too. To be small and decorative and precise.

But the bowl doesn't keep things that only watch. Even the lichen is read. Even the birds work.

Let me come back down to the north wall, to the wet architecture.

You would find me about two thirds of the way up, on the north curve where the town is thickest. Not at the top where the cornice glows and not at the bottom where the tarn steams, but in the middle terraces where the ladders get confused and a walkway crosses directly over a hearth.

There is a cistern there cut into the basalt, not large — about as wide as a cooking pot is long, lidded with a slate slab that has a beak-shaped notch cut in one corner to keep the birds from drinking but let the water in. The gutter that feeds it is not a pipe. It's a groove no deeper than my thumbnail, cut along the lintel of the doorway below and then coaxed up the wall with a low dam of beeswax and pine resin, thumb-high, soft from hands pressing it every morning.

I would be the person who tends that.

Not the old watcher who sits where the clear water runs inside the ice and does not speak. Not yet. I would be one of the quiet, compact ones in the middle of life, chest wide as a barrel, blood thick, hands white to the wrist with salt that never washes off. My eyes would be that washed-out grey-blue with the second milky lid that drops when I look up at the glare. I would wear black goat wool that never dries and a sealskin apron lacquered with bird fat, and I would move without noise.

You would find me scraping.

In the brutal, shadowless part of the morning my flat roof — which is also my neighbor's walkway and another family's ceiling — holds three shallow stone trays of dark brine. The thin sun hits them and they steam faintly despite the cold. By midday a pink-white crust has formed, fragile as frost. I scrape it with a flat shovel of bone. *Shhhhk.* So soft you have to be standing next to me to hear it. I bag it in wool. I stack it. I tap two fingers on the lintel when the cistern is mine for the hour, and hold my palm out when I want water, and flick salt-white fingers across my throat when a bird is coming through low.

And at noon, when the light goes flat and the mist over the tarn decides to stop falling, I pause like everyone else.

You would find me with a wooden bowl held out over the edge of the balcony, not because I commanded the water to hover, but because I know it will. Thousands of perfect spheres hanging a meter above the dark water, reflecting my upturned face and the stone town and the birds so many times it makes me dizzy. I don't pray to them. I don't marvel. I just hold the bowl steady and wait for a bird to fly through and make them all shiver and fall at once, dimpling the tarn, blooming the shrimp red underneath. And when they decide to fall, some of them will fall into my bowl.

That's who I would be. Not the water, not the light, not the watcher. The small, necessary dam of wax. The hands that catch what the mountain sweats. The person whose work is to not make sound while never, ever staying still — keeping a groove clear so the impossible water can keep being water, exactly here, exactly as it is.
