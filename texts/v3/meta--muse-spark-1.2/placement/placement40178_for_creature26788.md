I didn't know at first where I would be.

At first I thought I would be high — up in that shingle-canopy fifty feet up, where the waxy leaves fit together like a roof and bead with water until they spill. I wanted to be high because high sees. But there is nothing to see from up there. The canopy is not for seeing out, it's for catching in. It makes its own night underneath itself. If I were up there I'd just be another shield-leaf in the dark, dripping.

Then I thought I would be loud. Something that could hold its own in that sawing, drilling roar that lives in your molars. A throat sac big as my body, inflating white and luminous in a cup of water. But the sound here doesn't work like that. No one is loud alone. You never hear one frog. You hear a wall that decided to call at once so no predator can find a single throat. If I were a frog I'd not be an individual at all, I'd be a stitch in that wall. My croak would only exist because thousands of others were croaking with me.

I tried, for a minute, being something with wings. The four-winged pryers with hands at the bend of each wing, or the velvet gliders that throw themselves and chitter to echolocate the clutter. I liked their busy hands. I liked that they snap a twig, strip it, lever bark, drop it. But they are too restless for this place. They are always between. They never stay long enough to learn the shape of one drip.

The longer I stood in it — in the weight, in the mammalian press of the air that you drink more than breathe — the lower I sank.

You find me not by looking out, but by looking down and then closer than down.

Don't look for me on the ridge of new basalt. The rock there is still too sharp, too angry, still remembering being vomit. Don't look for me in the open seep where the fungi pulse their green-white breathing. That's too exposed. Too many eyes.

Find me in a crease.

There is a place where two plates of black glass cooled against each other and didn't quite meet. A fissure a hand wide, a foot deep, running twenty feet before it vanishes under a mat of roots. The air that exhales from it is a fraction cooler than the air that pools in the basin, and because of that, condensation finds it. Every night, without cloud or rain, fat drops gather on the underside of the shield-leaf that has leaned over this crease like an awning. They gather until they fall. Pat. Pat. Pat. A second rain with no sky.

The moss there is not moss the way you know moss. It's a beard three feet long, hanging in curtains, each strand tipped with a bead of water. Below that, the rock is furry with a biofilm that tastes of iron. And in that crease, in that particular variation of this country that repeats ridge after ridge, is where I have become myself.

I am not large. Nothing here is.

You would have to lift the edge of that shield-leaf to see me, and if you did, you'd think at first you were seeing the rock move.

I am one of the soot ants.

Not the colony as an abstraction, not the queen hidden deeper where it's even damper — I am one worker, as long as the joint of your thumb, glossy black and always wet, so it looks like I'm made of the same cooled basalt as the crease itself. My mandibles are shears. My antennae are always tasting the heavy air.

You would find me in procession. We never go alone. A road of us, silent, each carrying a perfect coin cut from the waxy canopy leaf above our heads like a shield. We cut, not chew — a slow, deliberate scissoring around and around until the disc comes free and flutters. We carry it down the corded trunk of the tree-fern, down through the hanging gardens and the curtains of rootlets that try to snag it, into the crease.

This is where I live: in plumbing.

We don't build a nest the way you'd think. We build gutters. We masticate the leaf-coins into a grey paper, mix it with our own spit, and daub it. A baffle here, angled to catch the drip from the shield-leaf and funnel it away from the brood. A tiny aqueduct there, to lead a drop toward the white pile of larvae sleeping in the driest pocket of the crease. We are always adjusting. The night rain never falls the same way twice. If you breathe too hard on our work, we will take it apart and start again somewhere else, without quarrel. Use and discard. Use and discard. Like the gliders with their thorn-awls, like the bird-things with their bark-levers — the forest is littered with our abandoned correct ideas, which by morning have already become homes for mites and shrimp-things and slime.

My world is three inches across and it contains everything. The beetle road passes above me — those fist-big lanterns with shuttered amber spots, grazing biofilm off the bark, their light sweeping the dark in slow arcs. To me they are weather, they are continents moving. The gliders are sudden winds that throw a shadow and are gone. The amphibians are the low croak that vibrates through the stone into my feet. The mantis that is pretending to be a leaf two inches to my left is a whole country I have learned not to walk through.

I see by the light we make. The veins in the shrub leaves next to my crease carry a slow amber glow when brushed. The slime mold on the opposite wall maps itself in cold blue, flowing, breaking, rejoining — a brain with no head that I walk across without understanding. My own sisters signal with the faintest lift of abdomen. The fungi breathe. It's enough.

I drink without drinking. I press my belly to the moss and let the air drink me. I tilt my head to catch a bead that has gathered on the tip of my antenna and let it run to my mouth. I have never needed a river. No one here has. Water comes to us, drop by drop, all night, patting on our backs like fingers.

I am not intelligent the way you mean intelligence. No one here is. No one stacked stone on stone, no one kept a fire. We just — all of us, in different ways — discovered that a sharp edge is better than a dull one, that a drop can be guided. That intelligence is not concentrated. It's smeared thin, democratized, busy. If you look for the clever one, you will miss it, because it's not one. It's a habit that leaked.

So you would not find me by listening for a voice. You would find me by staying still until your eyes water from the sound, until the condensation has been falling on your shoulders for a long time, until the living ceiling above you sighs and shifts.

You would find me because I found you first.

While you were trying to decide whether to be a glider or a frog or a lantern beetle, I came out of the crease onto the strange, warm, vertical basalt that wasn't there yesterday — which is your boot. I tested it with my antennae. I tried your lace to see if it would make a good vine. And then, with the patient business that is the only prayer this valley knows, I put my shears to the edge of your sleeve.

Not to bite. To see if it will cut into a good disc.
