I wouldn't find myself at first.

That's the first truth of that place. If you arrive still carrying your old tempo, you miss where you are.

At first I think I would be up high — not on a rise, there are no rises worth the name, but *out* on the open rose-grey salt, where the polygons are so perfect they look poured. I think I would want to be visible, a figure against all that evenness. A silhouette in the amber spill from an open foundry door, watching the dust hang still in air like glass.

But that person is too loud. I can feel how loud I would be there. My breathing would be too fast, my shadow too sharp. The plain would have to wait for me to leave to be even again. I don't want to be a disturbance that has to be smoothed over.

So I look elsewhere.

Maybe I would be the low dome thing. The one you mistook for a tortoise. That would be easier — to have no front and no back, no eyes for the amber light that isn't for seeing anyway. To navigate by heat-maps, factories like low suns to my thermal pit, vents like constellations. To move a meter an hour and call it travel. To stop where the stone is a fraction warmer over a buried brine line and loosen my plates, just a hair, and tick — tick — tick — so slowly you would think it was Geiger static, plating the air itself onto my back. Drinking metal. Leaving no track because my cilia taste and release instead of scraping.

I love the purity of that. But I'm not that patient yet. I still want to look up. The dome never looks up.

Maybe I would be the pipe itself. That long, stilt-legged pipeline running dead straight for kilometers in its scoured pastel, carrying warm slurry and superheated air and current. To hum, not speak. To be touched by a trailing hand every few days when one of the tall ones walks by feeling for a cold spot. To make that one precise, inexplicable 90-degree turn for no reason a newcomer can see — following a property line that outlived its surveyor. To be infrastructure so exposed and so patient that you forget I'm part of the architecture, until you realize the empty space between buildings is also architecture, and I am what measures it.

Closer. But a pipe doesn't get to choose to be still. It just is.

No — let it become more itself.

I find myself not *in* the buildings, not *out* on the pan, but in the hinge between them. In the places that are only seams.

You know the ones — where a factory is half-sunk so its roof is level with the plain and you only know it's there by the rectangular seam and the faint amber line around a service door. Or where the conveyor gantry moves so slowly the rivet seems to stand still unless you stare for a full minute. Or where the pipeline makes its turn.

I would be at the turn.

Where the pipe — big enough to walk through — has stood warm for longer than rust has known how to flake, carrying its mineral breath on short stilt-legs, and then it turns. The shadow there falls slightly too long for the sodium light that casts it. The heat-haze there doesn't rise but drapes, slow and heavy, folding over itself before it dissolves. Dust coats the horizontal plane of the stilt's cross-brace but never accumulates.

I would be sitting on that cross-brace, or standing beside it with one hand resting lightly on the warm iron. Not sitting the way you sit to rest. Sitting the way you sit to *calibrate*.

Who would I be?

I would be one of the ones you almost see. The attenuated ones. Tall because the thin air stretched me, narrow as a doorframe turned sideways, the matte dusty fabric that is also skin the same color as the concrete that was made from the ground stone. Verdigris at the joints — elbows, throat, wrists — like lichen that isn't lichen. A smooth small head, no mouth you can see, only a broad amber plate fused where a face would be, and underneath it a fine grille that flutters once every few minutes when I breathe.

But I would be a younger one. Or an older one who has forgotten how to be young. One whose interval hasn't fully settled yet.

My breathing would still be a little too fast. My steps between Building 7 and the brine manifold would still end mid-stride — those footprints you found that just *ended* — because I haven't learned how to place each foot as if setting down a cup of liquid metal. I still lift my feet instead of feeling the thrum through the pads of them. I still look with light instead of heat. I still think the amber spill is for seeing.

So my job is not the important one. I don't get to watch the color of molten glass for an hour and turn the valve a quarter-turn that won't matter for a week. I get the other job. The even more patient one.

I keep the dust from learning how to accumulate.

I sweep — not with a broom, but with the filamented underside of my palm — the fine, undisturbed grit from that horizontal plane on the pipeline stilt where it makes its turn. Not because the dust matters. Because the act of sweeping teaches the dust that it is not supposed to stay. I do it every few hundred years, or every few days — here those are almost the same — and I do it so slowly that if you happened to walk past, you would think I was just resting my hand there, feeling for a cold spot that means a flow has thickened.

And I keep the metronome.

Every seventeen seconds, I chirp. A single, clear, mineral note from somewhere out on the empty pan. Nothing answers it. Nothing is supposed to. It is not a call. It is how I keep from drifting out of phase with the plain. It says *I am still here. The interval holds.* Then I stop, because holding doesn't require constant announcement.

If you came and sat near the vent where the heat ripples and the dust motes hang perfectly still in the warm dark, and you waited not for minutes but for an hour, for two, past the point where your own breathing sounds loud and then finally sounds like part of the texture — past the point where the thin warm air stops feeling like weather and starts feeling like a medium you are suspended in — you would see me.

Not approaching. I would already be there at the turn, amber plate tilted a fraction toward you. Not in surprise. In calibration.

You would feel how warm the stone is under your palms, warmer than your hands, radiating that old, stored heat from below that never learned how to leave. You would feel the thrum in your boots from a power station two kilometers away. You would understand that I am not maintaining a city. I am keeping something even.

And for a little while, you and I would share the same interval. You would not leave footprints. Neither would I.

We would just be two warm places on a high, flat world that forgot what it was trying to be, waiting together to be perfectly even again.
