At first I don't know. I try to put myself there and I don't fit.

If I walk in as I am now — warm-blooded, wet-mouthed, loud — I am wrong for the pocket immediately. I am too tall. I would have to duck every strut, turn sideways in every alley the width of shoulders. My breathing would be too much, too fast, *hah hah hah* in air that wants that double-drawn *hih-hah*. My water would betray me. I can feel it leaving me — the sweat on my neck sublimating before it beads, the spit drying on my lips to salt in minutes, my eyes watering just to have the tears stolen. I would be clumsy. I would talk before I tapped. I would leave a ledge empty because I thought empty was tidy. In a place where no surface stays empty, emptiness is a kind of rudeness. The town would fill in around me while I was still trying to get my footing, and then it would gently, quietly route around me like water around a stone that hasn't learned to be smooth yet.

So not that. Not the visitor.

I try smaller.

For a moment I think I would be a soot-moth. That would be easier — to want so little, to live on what sloughs off other things. Basalt-colored wings the size of a thumb, dusted with stone flour, rising and settling, rising and settling on the warm air above a forge. To be one of the clouds that makes the air crowded even when the town looks empty. But a moth's life is too brief and too light for me. I want to stay. I want to make something.

Then I think I would be one of the keepers, the long aluminum centipedes in the cracks, eating the salt crust and being eaten in turn. I like the honesty of that — to be both wild and domesticated and not know the difference, to be harvested for your chitin while you still twitch. But I don't want to be harvested. I want hands.

Then I think, maybe not a living thing at all. Maybe I would be the stone itself — the black-violet vesicular basalt, still pinging as it cools, still relieving the stress of being born too fast. Or the still air under the lip where the wind throws itself over. Or the thin heat-shimmer above the vent that is the only sign from outside that anyone is home. There is a kind of holiness in being the mountain, in growing a millimeter a year and feeling every bolt and shim adjust to you.

But I am not that patient. I am not that cold.

Let it get clearer, then. Keep looking into the dim until the eyes adjust.

If I am there, I am one of them. Not because I chose to climb that high, but because I stayed until I changed. I am small now — not child-small, but pocket-small. 115 centimeters of pared-down animal, barrel-chested so my ribs show through the canvas wraps even when I'm still. My clothing is not clothing anymore, you wouldn't see it as such. Two layers of moth-silk felt, a soot-grey hood patched until the patches have patches, fingerless gloves black at the fingertips where the metal dust has become part of the skin. You would look right at me and think I was shadow between two pipes.

You would find me not at the top where the sun cuts a hard white bar, and not at the bottom where the kilns render the dead into salt-ingots for the wall. You would find me mid-pocket, on the north curve where the basalt wall leans in and the scaffolding has to lean with it. Where the air pools coolest — 3 or 4 degrees, enough to make your teeth ache.

There is a place there where a hexagonal column fell a long time ago and no one cleared it because it made a useful shelf, and then someone built a platform over it, and then someone else strung a line of amber bulbs under that platform, and now the space underneath is barely high enough to kneel in. That's where I am.

I tend the coils.

Not the turbines out on the lip — those boys are young and deaf from the gale and they squint even in dimness. Not the forges either, my lungs are not good enough for the cherry-orange heat. I tend the cold coils, the breath-catchers. Loops of copper pipe that sweat in the night when the stone is coldest, where the vapor of our sleeping breath condenses bead by bead and runs down into sealed bladders. A mouthful per person per night, if we're careful. A few ounces that taste of iron and mouth.

My job is quiet, which is why I lasted at it. I check the joints for frost. I wipe the anhydrite crust off with a rag that never gets washed. I tap.

Two clicks on the pipe means *hold*. Three means *lift*. A flat palm on the grating means *clear, I'm passing with water and you must not bump me*. Most of what I say all day is with my knuckles. When I do speak, it is that dry murmur thinned to consonants, and it hurts a little, so I don't.

My hands are the hands described. You would know me by them — stained, hide-padded, never still. Even when I'm perched against the cold stone to rest, they are working: carding silk from the frames where the moths have stitched the wall, rolling a flake of keeper-chitin to powder between thumb and forefinger, turning a nut a quarter-turn, then back, then forward again. The fingertips have no feeling left. I can lift a rivet still too hot to look at.

I am old while young. Twenty-seven and the skin at the corners of my eyes is already cracked like the basalt, my lips always split, my sclera always a little pink from the dry. I blink too often on purpose. I have the blue sheen when forge-light catches my eyes, the nictitating film half-drawn against the dust. The children who work next to me are the same size as me and I can't always tell which are children. We all wear the masks — a fold of damp cloth — and we lift them for two seconds to eat: stonebread, a toasted larva, a wafer of chitin that tastes like nothing and salt. To spill a crumb is to feed the swift that lives two struts over, so we don't.

You would not see me at first. That is the point. I am crowded in, like everything else. If you left my ledge clear, by evening a brood of moths or a neighbor's toolbox would be on it. I don't resent it. Crowding is proof we are using the pocket well.

And every hour or so, the mountain pings. That deep *tok* you feel in your molars before you hear it. The whole town of wire and grating hums. Everyone pauses, even the moths seem to still for half a breath. I put my pale, stained hand flat on the nearest strut — that column that fell and became a shelf — and feel which way the vent is widening, a hair's breadth. Then I loosen a bolt here, add a shim there, re-anchor a line two centimeters to the left. Not fighting it. Growing with it, inwards and upwards, like a crystal in a vesicle that hasn't finished cooling.

If you came back at sunset and clung to the outside of the black glass tooth in the gale, you would see nothing of me. Only a soot stain on the lip and a thin column of shimmer that vanishes. Inside, in the dim that never quite becomes dark, in the cool that never stops aching the teeth, I am still there, kneeling under my platform, listening to the tick of cooling metal and the whisper of feet on grating three levels down, catching breath and keeping it, keeping it, keeping it.
