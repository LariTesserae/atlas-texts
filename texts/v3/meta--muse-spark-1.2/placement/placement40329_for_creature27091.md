I wouldn't find myself on the curb at first.

That's where you end up, if you stay long enough for your shoes to fill and your hearing to flatten into pressure. But not where you *find* yourself. Finding implies the moment before you decide to sit down.

I would find myself in the doorway.

One hand still on the swollen edge of the door that says PUMP BASIN 03, where the paint has bled. One foot in the dry, dusty concrete of the stairwell where the air is still air, and one foot in the wet. The threshold where two weathers meet. The jacket still half-dry on my shoulders. Glasses completely fogged, so I'm blind for five seconds in exactly the way this room wants you to be blind at first.

From there, you can see the whole rectangle at once, and make the mistake of thinking it's empty.

That's where I'd be. Not in the center, not yet claimed by any of the islands of green. In the margin. In the place that is neither stairwell nor basin, neither the factory that is trying to be dry and useful nor the pocket that has given up and become a lung. In the draft. That thin, cool spill of air from upstairs that shouldn't be able to exist against the constant 82-degree soup, but does — just enough to make the fern fronds by the spillway tremble differently than the condensation does.

For a long time I thought I would be the person on the curb.

I wanted to be. It's a good fantasy — to soften. To let the moss take your shoulders. To let the roar finally take your tinnitus and replace it with something larger and less personal. To become an equilibrium. To be so wet you never have to drink, so warm you never have to shiver, so loud inside your skull that you never have to listen to yourself think. To be tended by a fig tree. There is a holiness to him, the one who stayed, that makes you want to kneel and be taken in.

But I know myself a little. I don't soften that way. I check gauges even when they never move.

So then I thought, maybe I'm the dragonfly.

That would be cleaner. To be the only thing in the room with a territory that is *movement* itself. Back and forth, back and forth over the floor-pool, wings beaded with water but never wet through. Too light to leave ripples. Too fast to be bearded by moss. A visitor who learned the patrol so perfectly it looks like belonging. I could be that — the bright, rare, iridescent thing that makes the room feel less lonely without actually living in it.

But the dragonfly doesn't stay. No single dragonfly stays. The line stays. The patrol stays. The individual is replaced. I don't want to be replaced every season. And I don't want to be that lonely.

Then I thought maybe I'm the fig.

That one wrist-thick trunk in the eroded corner, with no soil, just a crack. Pressed flat against the concrete, reaching not for sun but for the milky diffusion through wired glass. Making leaves out of condensation and concrete dust. Patient in a way that isn't patience, but just how wood grows when there is nowhere else to go. That felt closer. To be rooted in something made, and make it living anyway.

But the fig is still reaching. It still believes in the skylight. It hasn't learned yet that the light never changes.

No. The longer I stand in that doorway with one wet foot and one dry foot, letting the warm rain find the back of my neck, the more I feel what I actually am in this ecology.

I am not the one who dissolves.

I am the one who witnesses dissolving and writes it down anyway.

If you come back in a year, the dragonfly will be a different dragonfly on the exact same patrol line. Someone has to be the one who notices that it is different and also that the line is the same. The gauges tremble at exactly the same position forever. Someone has to be the one who still leans in to check them.

I would be the next attendant. Not the one who stayed — the one who *comes*.

I find myself on a milk crate pulled up opposite him, not next to him. Far enough that the islands of green between us remain empty, because the emptiness is part of the ecology. My shoes are flooded too, but I took my socks off first and folded them neatly by the door, an old habit from upstairs that the room hasn't eroded out of me yet. I have a small notebook that is already curling and blooming with humidity, and the ink bleeds the second it touches the page. I write: *14:03 — spillway nominal — moss on north wall +2mm? — frog heard, not seen.* It doesn't matter that it will be the same tomorrow. It matters that someone writes it.

Who would I be? I would be the temporary mammal. The warm-blooded interval.

The room has perfected a closed loop. Water pumped, lifted, dropped, caught, pumped again. Light without shadow. Sound without variation. Life without soil. What it doesn't have, can't make for itself, is *transit*. Nothing leaves and nothing enters except light and the occasional spore on the draft from the stairwell. I would be that exception made flesh.

I would be the draft itself, if the draft could carry a notebook.

I come down three flights past the last marked level because the checklist says to, because somewhere upstairs someone still prints a checklist with PUMP BASIN 03 on it. I stay for an hour. I watch his chest bead and clear, bead and clear, just out of sync with the spillway. I don't try to talk to him — you have to shout and he doesn't have ears for shouting anymore. I just sit and let the pressure equalize.

He feels me as a change in pressure, not as a person. A new drip. A plop that isn't the frog. He turns that filmed gaze toward me very slowly, the way a pool recognizes another pool, and I nod. Not rescue. Not pity. Recognition.

And then, when the thrumming in my molars gets too deep, when I can feel the beading start on my own shoulders and my own edges beginning to go soft and dark and curved like the corners where wall meets wall —

I leave.

I pull the swollen door shut behind me with both hands, and it sighs. I wring out my shirt in the stairwell. I carry the wet notebook upstairs where the air is dry and the sound has edges again. I leave wet footprints up three flights that evaporate before the next shift.

The room needs that. It needs something to leave, so that staying means something. It needs a witness who does not become a stain on the wall. It needs a frog-plop that climbs stairs.

He stays and becomes the place.

I come and become the proof that the place is still a place, and not just a dream the concrete had.

If you go down there tomorrow, you'll find me not there. But you'll find the milk crate is still damp where I sat. And if you sit on the opposite curb, you'll feel the room waiting — not empty between the ferns, but full of two presences now: one who has learned how to be moss, and one who is learning, carefully, how not to be.
