I don't know right away.

If you put me down that throat — that square concrete collar giving way to raw, sharp basalt still showing the tooth of the machine — I think at first I would try to be everywhere at once. I would want the rim walkway, because from there you can hold the whole bowl in one look and pretend you understand it. I would want the smelter end, because the forge-glow is the only thing in here that pretends to be a sun and it's warm in a way that feels honest. I would want the ceiling, up in the dusk where the condensation beads like cold stars.

But the air is too thick for pretending, and the heat edits you quickly. You don't get to be everywhere. The bowl tells you where you belong by how heavy the air is on your skin.

So I let the place sort me.

Not at the top. Not at the bottom. Not at the rim where newcomers stand and stare and keep looking up at a dome that will never be a sky. I find myself settling lower than the bean balconies and higher than the smelter terraces — in the middle weave, where the settlement is densest. Where three levels of handmade city have grown into each other so completely you can't tell foundation from scaffolding from roof.

My place would be where a catwalk crosses an alley that crosses another alley, and the alley underneath is half workshop, half home. The air there never quite moves. A string of caged amber bulbs passes at shoulder height and throws light sideways, so my shadow falls up onto the underside of the deck above me. The deck thrums. Not loud — boxed and baffled — but I feel it through the thin soles of my slippers. That thrumming would become my floor, my pulse.

I think I would live in one of those lofts that is three things at once, with a canvas curtain instead of a door. Inside: a low bench with a small press on it, a pink grow-light for two rows of beans and a tuber that needs its leaves brushed by hand because there are no bees here. A barrel under the sweat of a pipe. A coil of rope by the ladder because everything here is vertical. You don't close the curtain for sound — sound doesn't carry in damp, hot air — you close it to ask for a courtesy of not-looking, and everyone here knows how to not-look.

Who would I be, in that loft?

At first I think I would be the one who watches. But watching is everyone's job. Everyone can see a bead forming on a pipe from two meters away in the half-light. Everyone knows the smell of the extruder running hot.

No, I become more specific as I stay.

I would be the gutter person. Not a title anyone gives, but a function the bowl requires, like a vein. There is no river here. Water only exists when the air forgets how to hold it. For hours the dome gathers its dark mirror and I am listening for it — not looking up, because we don't look up, but feeling the pressure drop, seeing the bulbs sway a centimeter though there is no wind, seeing the fresh-cut basalt begin to weep in dark maps along seams that only show themselves right before rain.

My hands would know that weeping. I would be lean the way everyone is lean here — not thin from want, but corded from climbing — shoulders and forearms overmade, calves tight, the skin between thumb and forefinger callused from steel and rope. My hair would be cut severe and short, my skin always damp with that sheen that never beads and never dries, because to dry is to overheat. I would lay a palm flat on the 39-degree rock to steady myself on a ladder and not flinch. That would be neutral to me. Floor temperature.

When the Beading starts I would be already moving, not hurrying — to hurry is to waste water — but flowing in that settlement-wide slowness that covers so much ground without seeming to exert itself. I would open the gutters. I would lay out the tarps. I would tilt the bean boxes a degree with a wooden wedge, the way the old people do on the highest balconies. Children with shining shaved heads would go still and watch the ceiling, and I would watch them watch.

Then the rain would come. Ten minutes, soft and warm, smelling of hot iron and ozone and turned earth, drumming on every tin roof at once, hissing where it hits the smelter hood. The only time the city is loud. I would stand in it on my catwalk and let it soak me completely, face up, not celebrating so much as acknowledging. For an hour I would be cool.

And after — the real work — I would wring out canvas into the barrel with that practiced twist. I would chase rivulets with a dustpan toward the central channel. I would tap the pipe twice — *release* — and listen for the answer in steel and bone, because a tap travels when a voice won't. I would sweep every puddle that lingers in a footwell because nothing is wasted here; to spill is an absurdity, like dropping food on a lifeboat. I would know my cistern by its sweat.

I would be quiet. Not silent. Quiet the way the machines are quiet. I would speak at the volume the air will carry, which is low and close, leaning in. I would know people by gait, by the particular creak of their route across grating. I would know there are no rats, no roaches, no flies, so when something moves on its own it is either human or machine, and I would treat the pump that sighs every few minutes with the tenderness you would give an animal, listening to its cooling tick the way you listen to breathing. I would pollinate the beans with a small brush. I would peel the lichen back where it tries to take too much of the north wall.

I wouldn't feel buried. That surprised me at first. The dome is so close, the rock so absolute as a wall. But after a while that warm basalt stops being a lid and becomes a forehead. Close, present. I would lean against it. I would know which seam darkens first.

If you came looking for me during Channel-Running, when the ankle-deep brown water finally moves down the central avenue and the air stirs for once and the far catwalks ripple in the smelter heat, you wouldn't find me at first. I would be part of the weave. A movement three levels up that resolves into a person only when it moves. Lean, glistening, soft-footed, unhurried on a ladder that is also a stairwell.

I would be one of the ways the bowl lives like condensation lives — appearing exactly where hot and cold meet, clinging to vertical surfaces, gathering drop by drop until there is enough to run.

I would be how the place remembers water.
