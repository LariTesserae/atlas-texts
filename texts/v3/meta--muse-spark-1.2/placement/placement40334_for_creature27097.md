I don't find myself standing at the rim.

At first I think I do. That's the trick of the description — you picture yourself up on that fresh, glassy basalt, looking down into the dish, a visitor with dry shoes. From a height, looking in.

But I can't stay there. The rock is weeping. It's cold where the town is hot, it hasn't decided to be ground yet, it offers no purchase. The water finds the seam between my shoe and the stone and gets in. The air won't take my breath back. There is no wind up there to be a visitor in. The cloud is too low. The roof is on.

So I have to come down.

---

If I walk down the stair-step terraces — careful, they're still sharp, they cut — my feet find the first iron plate. It is already sweating. The condensation beads and does not evaporate. After two steps my shirt is stuck to my back, not from exertion but from sympathy. The air here is 37 degrees and holding. It is not weather. It is a held breath.

For a little while I think I am one of the people.

It's easy to want to be. They are the ones who look most like me — oil-stained, pale and permanently pruned, lank hair, eyes red from steam. I thought I would find myself in the row of quarters where the laundry never dries, or in the canteen with a tin cup going warm in my hands. Walking the catwalk from the powerhouse to the sorting shed because that's what the hour does. Waiting for the CLACK to finish before I step.

I know that rhythm already. My breathing has done it without asking me. 

*Thud. Thud. Thud. Thud.* .... *CLACK* .... *thud thud thud thud*.

I tap the rail to tell someone a valve is still stiff. I put my hand on a shoulder to say *move now*. I have stopped shouting. If you called me, I would be a figure threaded through, not gathered. One of fifty necessary moving parts. A red cell. Not living *in* the organism but *as* it — circulating because the dish is exactly my size and there is nowhere else to go and no reason to leave.

I like that for a while. It's patient. It's comprehensible. It has a task.

But it isn't quite right. The people still wipe the pipes. They still try to keep dry. They still remember, even faintly, that the basin dropped — that there was a before.

I don't.

---

So maybe I am lower down. Closer to the water.

I crouch where the runnels run warm as bathwater alongside every walkway, carrying that thin, beautiful, ruined rainbow of oil. If I am patient enough, I can be the egret.

I find myself there, on one leg in the lowest part of the yard, right beside the stamping press. White as steam. Perfectly still while the whole town roars and sighs and drips around me. Ten thousand mosquito fish flicker in ankle-deep water that never gets deeper and never dries up, and I do not strike. They do not flee. We have an agreement older than stillness.

To be the egret is to have mastered the equilibrium. To know how to wait without needing the world to change. To be accommodated so completely that the CLACK is just the space between heartbeats.

Or I could be the opposite — one of the amber-winged dragonflies, heavy as machines, hovering without wind to fight because there is no wind. Or a gecko on the north side of the brick, pale as damp, eyes too big for the dim, blinking at the greenish fluorescent without fear. Or the rats, sleek and clean, using the pipes as sidewalks.

All of them know the secret the people are still learning: that nothing wild and nothing tame survives here, only what has learned to be *inside*.

That feels closer.

But still, I am breathing a little too hard for a gecko.

---

So I get even lower. I stop wiping my hands.

On the underside of every pipe, under the lip of the culvert where my knee is now wet through, between the seams of the concrete where warm water sweats up endlessly, there is a sheen that is not water. Cream and rust and black-green. Tacky to the touch. Faintly sweet and metallic when you get close enough. 

Biofilm.

It doesn't walk the catwalk or hunt or wait. It just *holds*. It is the town's real skin, and the workers' coveralls smell of it, and the egret's legs are stained with it. It loves exactly what this place is: never cold, never dry, never dark, never storm-scoured. The condensation cycle is its breath — water into air into water again. The waste heat from the powerhouse is its sun.

If I stay long enough — and I have stayed long enough, now that my neck is damp and an itch has bloomed where my shirt sticks — I feel it begin on me too.

I am not visiting it. I am being filmed over. Gently.

And this is where I settle, because this is where the description finally stops describing a place *with* inhabitants and starts describing a place that *is* an inhabitant.

I would find myself not at the rim, not at the center, but threaded through the interval. In the space between presences where the town left room for air. On a second-story catwalk that goes from one roof to another roof for no reason, or in the empty yard of wet concrete that the gantry crane never reaches anymore. Where you can hear how much air there is between the buildings.

Who would I be?

I would be the loop itself, in one of its smaller bodies.

If you need a name for the body: a patch of that cream-colored film under a pipe that condenses and drips, drips and condenses. Or a single mosquito fish that has learned not to panic. Or the forty-ninth worker whose job is to wait for the puff of steam to clear before turning a valve that never needed turning.

Really, I would be whatever has learned not to need change.

Sleek and damp and patient. In no hurry. Synchronized.

You would find me with my skin wet, breathing with the pump. Listening between the CLACKs. Not emerging, not eroding. Just staying — exactly as the basin does — a small, complete, living equilibrium humming to itself under its low, concrete-colored sky.
