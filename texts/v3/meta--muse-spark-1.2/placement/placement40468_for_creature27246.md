I wouldn't find myself in the city at first.

Not in the way it counts itself, anyway.

At first I think I would be somewhere on the causeway - that narrow back of stone between two of those perfect, scalding mirrors - because that's where you put the newcomer. The visitor. The one who hasn't learned how to be quiet yet. I would be standing there with my boots already full of water that isn't my sweat, my mouth tasting of iron, my shoulders hunched against a light that doesn't make shadows so much as it stamps them straight down into the rock like ink seals. Too loud. Breathing too hard. Looking for a tree that isn't going to appear.

And I would be trying to be a person, because that's the habit. A human figure, white-wrapped, hand lifted.

But that doesn't hold. The plateau corrects you.

If I stayed - if I actually had to *live* here, not just pass through and leave a footprint that fills in behind me - I wouldn't get to be the visitor for long. The water wouldn't let me.

So let me be more honest and let it change as I write it.

Maybe I'd be a flier for a minute. That tempts me. To be one of those mica things that has no voice at all and never needed one, that drinks straight from the steam and rides the heat columns you can't see until they carry you. To be that light. To be a brief rainbow when the sun hits my wings, and then nothing, and then light again. To land on a hot pipe with six impossibly thin feet and feel the whole industrial organism humming underneath me through my legs. That would be a good life. Simple. Translating light into movement.

But I'm too heavy for that. I carry too much need to understand. Fliers don't understand, they just coincide.

Maybe I'd be water then. That would be truest, actually. To be the thing that comes up hot and heavy with dissolved stone in its mouth, tasting of the deep earth, and then learns, terrace by terrace, how to lay that stone down. To spread thin and still and clear and give yourself away millimeter by millimeter until you are the land itself. To spill over a lip without splashing. To go from being a spring to being a dam to being a sheet of steam to being a droplet again in a black condenser field. I like that cycle. That patience. No stagnation, no argument.

But water doesn't choose. And I do, a little.

So what emerges, if I let it keep becoming itself, is this:

I find myself not in the middle of the causeway but off to its side, where it widens just enough for a household.

Where I am is very specific, because you have to be specific here. You get assigned - or you choose, which is the same thing - a watershed. A rectangle of stone that is yours to attend. Mine would be on the middle stair of the western slope, where the gradient is gentle enough that the pools are wide and shallow, the color of very pale tea. Three basins in a chain, each spilling to the next through a polished chute. At the head of the top one, a pipe as thick as a house runs on low arches and sweats without stopping. At the foot of the bottom one, a slow wheel turns in the chute, turning a generator I will never see, behind a louvered wall that has gone the color of rust and bone.

My house is low, half-sunk, white as the crust on my hands, with catchment gutters that never stop dripping. It is a quarter mile from the next white house in any direction, which is exactly right. Far enough that I can see my neighbors as small, still figures in the heat haze - one tending a settling tank, one on a tower catwalk - but not so far that I can't read their hands when they lift them at the gold hour to say *I am still here*.

And who am I, there?

I am not the child who can stand still enough to fool a wader. Not yet, and not anymore. And I'm not the old one who is already half terrace, eyebrows sugared with precipitate, waiting to become a new lip of stone. I am the middle animal. The tender.

The one with the long, flat wooden rake.

My work is to skim the bacterial felt. In the late afternoon, when the light goes from white to that deeper, hotter gold and the whole plateau exhales and blooms, the edges of my pools grow that white forest - inch-high, branching, impossibly delicate silica trees built by bacteria, grain by grain. If I tear them, they don't come back right. If I leave them, they choke the pool. So I wade barefoot - my feet wrapped in soaked felt that makes no sound - and I pull the rake through the film so slowly you would think I wasn't moving, coaxing, not harvesting. The pale shrimp scatter and return. The flat disc grazers leave clean trails behind me that fill in by morning. My knuckles are rimed white. The sheen on my skin never dries. When I blink, the membrane - almost clear, a little cloudy now from years of that unfiltered glare - shutters against the vertical sun.

I don't speak there. There would be no point. The hot, wet air would eat any word before it traveled ten feet and it would feel obscene to try. But I am not silent. I have learned the other language.

I listen with my palm on the sweating pipe. The tremor there tells me what the pressure gauge would tell me if I looked at it. I talk back by tapping. One knuckle, against iron, where the condensation gathers and falls.

*plink.*

A pause, while the sound carries in a way a voice never could.

*plink - plink.*

From the next watershed over, half a mile away through steam and mirror, an answer comes back. Same rhythm. My neighbor, saying *I hear you, the wheel is turning well, the bottom basin is clear* without needing any of those words.

When the fliers rise at noon I stop. Everyone stops. I stand in my shallow, scalding mirror with the rake held still and let them come. They land on my forearms sometimes, so light I only feel the cool prick where they drink the condensate off my skin. I let them. We use the same water. We lay our futures in the same damp cracks of the tower.

At dusk we all come out onto our causeways. Not together. Each in our own place. We lift a hand, slow, palm open, fingers splayed, catching the last white light. We hold still until we have counted the other hands that lift back, scattered across the valley of steam and stone. We are a constellation that checks itself.

That is where I find myself. In that watershed, with that rake, with that tap-language in my knuckles. Not loud, not central, not green. A keeper of a small piece of water that, if I tend it right, will outlast me and lay a new, creamy dam over whatever of me is left when I am done tending it.

If you were walking that causeway now, still too loud, still looking for shade, you would see me first as a mistake - another wader, bone-white and too still to be alive.

And if you lifted your hand, slowly, I would lift mine in answer.
