You wouldn't find me at first.

That's the thing. At first I think I'm up top, because that's where anything with eyes wants to be — on the back of one of those big bone-colored domes where you can see. Where the haze makes the country look empty and clean and you can pretend you're alone. The sun straight overhead, short shadow, warm stone through the soles.

But I don't stay there. Nothing stays there. It's too dry up there, too exposed. The wind takes too much. If I was up there I'd be one of the black lichen plates, holding on hard and making no difference, and that isn't me.

So I slide down. Not quickly. There was never a slope, remember? I just find that I've drifted, the way the pavement drifts, a millimeter at a time without deciding to. I'm lower than I thought. In the swale.

You would find me where the swale forgets it is a valley. That kilometer-wide, meter-deep sigh in the plain where the dust is a shade darker even at noon. Where two of those house-sized domes have sagged toward each other like shoulders, and the lean makes a shadow that lasts until ten. A pocket the size of a basin. The last place the morning seep holds on. Brown water, capped with dust, then brown mud, then just damp sand that is cool to the touch when everything else is oven-warm.

For a while I think I am one of the high ones. I want to be a Veil — that would suit me, wouldn't it? To be almost not an animal, to hang a few meters up on heat and hydrogen, to drag threads as thin as thought and read what brushes them. To be silent because silence is buoyancy. But I am too heavy for that. I want too badly to stay. A Veil can't stay. If it goes a little too high the sigh-wind takes it off the table and it never finds the dew again. I don't want to be taken.

Then I think I might be a Stiltwalker. Pale, careful, stepping only in the seams. Never crushing. That feels familiar too — to move with that much deliberation, to probe a crack with a needle and sip only what would otherwise be missed. To touch another only to pass a droplet mouth to mouth. But my legs are not that long. I don't want to be above the pavement, looking down at it. I want to be in it.

So I get lower. Slower. Damper.

You would find me not *on* the pavement but *as* the pavement, and even then not in the middle of it where it is a perfect, unbroken tessellation. Find me at the edge, where the pavement meets the foot of a Spiral.

There is one there. A low one, an ankle-high whorl of cemented dust the color of old bone, paper-thin walls, perfect as a fingerprint. Its mouth is a fist-wide shaft going down into cooler dark where air sighs out moss-cool against the heat. The pavement flows around it and never over it. That is the rule.

I am one of the ones at the margin. One Lithomorph among millions that has been pushed — or has oozed — close enough to feel the Spiral breathe.

I am dinner-plate wide, because I've been there a long time. My back is not new. It's pitted, matte, sand-colored with that slow rust mottling that is really the garden I keep in my pores. The cyanobacteria and the black lichen that make sugar out of vertical sun while I keep them damp. My edge is fringed with those glassy, colorless cilia, interlocked with my neighbors' cilia so we hold hands without ever having hands. Underneath I have no legs. I have a foot, broad and pale, and around it the fringe — the veil, the inside-out lung, cream-colored and folded.

At dawn the domes sweat and the swale darkens, and I do what we all do: I open. Once every thirty seconds. *Ah.* The fringe kisses the stone and the damp sand and condenses a film of water that wasn't there. I filter it. The dissolved silica, the spores that fell overnight, the bacterial film. By nine the basin water at my lip is still there and I am two deep here, shingled over my smaller neighbors, letting what drips through me feed them. By noon I have tucked. The fringe is under, the cilia have sealed, and I am stone again.

I don't move quickly enough to see myself move. If you watched me all afternoon you'd swear I hadn't. But tomorrow the seam beside me will be a millimeter different. I will have inched toward where the Spiral exhales.

Because this Spiral is a young one, still sharp-edged. At night, deep in its cool chamber, the living lining — which is the same tissue as my fringe, only grown huge and communal — is budding. Soft, shell-less crawlers the size of woodlice, no grain yet cemented to their backs, creep out on the damp and look for a seam. One of them found me. Or I found it. It is tucked now in the V where my carapace meets my neighbor's, still soft, still translucent. It hasn't made its first concrete. I am keeping it damp with my own edge.

I don't make a sound. None of us do. The whole plain would be deafening if we did. So instead we breathe together. If you put your ear to the stone behind me, or to the mouth of the Spiral, you can hear it — not me, but us. A faint, collective *ah*... *ah*... once every half minute, across acres, across the haze, the hill breathing, the valley breathing, the country breathing.

You would find me there, staying. Low and slow and damp. Conserving. Not building, not flying, not walking above. Just holding a little water a little longer than the rock could hold it alone, keeping a little garden alive in my pits, keeping a soft crawler damp until it learns how to make dust into a shell.

If you sat with your back to my dome in the afternoon and stayed still long enough, your boots would be part of the seam. And after a minute, my fringe — and the fringe of the Spiral behind me — would taste the sweat on your palm and sigh a little cooler air back onto your skin.
