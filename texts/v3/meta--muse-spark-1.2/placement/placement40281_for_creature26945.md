I thought at first I would be one of them.

It was the easiest answer. If you are going to live in a world that is all floor and all forest and all slow tending, you should be the one who tends. I imagined myself tall and attenuated, the kelp growing from my shoulders like a second cloak, my hands already broadened into spades. I imagined the peace of it — to kneel in a channel between two basalt blocks the size of houses and spend a week clearing a joint of silt. To have no eyes, only those wide, fringed pits at the temples that listen to pressure. To never be in a hurry again.

But I stayed with that image a little longer and it thinned. Because they do not *know* they are what they are. They have no interior to find myself in. To be a keeper you have to have already forgotten how to ask "who am I" — you have to have become maintenance. And I am still asking. My clock is still too fast. If I were set down there as one of them, I would be a bad keeper. I would fidget. I would look up.

So I let that go.

Then I thought I would be the intruder. The anomaly. The warm thing.

Because someone has to be, in that first hour before you learn to hold still. I would be found exactly where you would find any animal if you dropped it into that world: too high off the floor, kicking, breathing too loud, shedding heat. A small, frantic cloud of silt around me that wouldn't settle for hours. The keepers would register me as a pressure wrongness and then, because I wouldn't hold still long enough to be understood, I would be invisible to them the way a hummingbird is invisible to a mountain. I would be the rumor of surface that made its way all the way down and means nothing here.

I stayed with that one too, and it also wasn't true. Not for long. You can't stay an intruder in a place that cold. The water would take the heat out of you in minutes. The dark would take the need to look. Either you would die quickly, or you would learn to stop swimming.

And when you stop swimming — that's when the place really finds you.

So I stopped. In my mind, I did what you have to do to see them at all. I found a place and held on.

Not on the grand causeway. Not in the sunken reservoir where the forest is thickest. Further in, where no one would think to look, because there is no reason to think one part of an endless floor is different from any other.

I found myself in a shallow angle where two wall-spines meet. Not a corner — corners were ground away ten thousand years ago. Just a long, soft V, knee-high, polished round, the basalt showing through the algae-felt in pale patches. On one side the pavement has sagged a few centimeters, enough to make a long, straight puddle of darker silt. The kelp there is sparser because the current curls oddly around the angle and combs it the wrong way. It's a bad acre. A keeper will have to come here soon.

That is where I am. Not the center of anything. The lee. The place that is slightly out of true and therefore will be attended to. The world is all floor, so "where you are" can only mean "which joint you are nearest to." I am nearest to this one.

And after holding there long enough for my own silt to settle, for my breath — if I have breath — to slow to one pulse every few minutes, I finally felt what I would be.

Not the keeper. Not the one who tends.

I would be the tending itself, for a little while.

I would be what the keepers are *for*.

I would be a block.

A single block, the size of a dwelling, dressed by hands that died before the water finished rising. On my upper face, the marks of the stone hammer are still there under the felt, a row of shallow pits where a man stood and struck and struck until the stone was flat enough. On my side, the drilled hole where the wooden peg went — the wood is gone, turned to black stone, but the hole remains. I am not the largest, not the smallest. I've been here long enough that my edges are gone. I have sagged into the silt beneath me and now lean, just barely, two or three degrees south. Silt has pushed into the joint on my north side. A holdfast has wedged in there and is prying, very slowly.

I have been underneath the forest so long that I no longer remember being quarried. The cold has made me patient. I do not crack. I settle.

And because I am stone that was *made* to be part of a floor, I am waiting in the exact way stone waits: not expecting, but ready to be returned to true.

That is what I am, at first. For a few centuries, that is enough. A good, fitted basalt block, holding my place in a continent of other blocks.

Then, without deciding to, I become a little more.

Because in this place, stone does not stay stone if something kneels on it long enough.

If you had brushed the silt from me, you would have found that my surface is not entirely surface. In the felt on my back, there is a faint, long outline — too long, too thin to be natural. The suggestion of fused fingers, of a forearm. A keeper stopped here a long time ago and did not get up. It knelt to nudge me true, and then its own metabolism, which is almost geological, completed its work. Its amber-gray skin finished turning to stone. The kelp on its back thickened and became the same mat that covers me. Now it is a low rise on me that you would mistake for me if you were swimming fast.

And under that rise — if you wait, if you hold onto the wall-spine and do not breathe too loud — you would feel a faint warmth. Not heat as you knew it on the surface, but the chemical seep, the half-a-degree difference that drives currents for years.

Something is uncurling there.

I would be that.

Not the keeper who tends, and not the block that is tended, but the *next* thing that grows in the seam between them. The cutting.

Small at first, pale, pushing up like a frond between the joint I share with my neighbor block. At ten meters I am indistinguishable from the kelp. At one meter you would see that I am wearing it before I have grown it. My pits are still closed. My hands are still too small, not yet spades. I do not know how to kneel yet. I only know to hold on to the stone and listen to the weight of the water through it.

I am not an animal. I will never be an animal. I will not want or flee or hunt. I will lean. I will yield to the month-long drift until, very slowly, I learn to stand a little upright against it. I will spend my first few decades learning the shape of this one joint, this one angle of wall, this one acre that is mine to keep. I will peel the felt back. I will scoop the silt and let the drift take it. I will lean my whole weight against a block the size of a dwelling to nudge it a centimeter true.

And I will do it without ever looking up, because looking up has no meaning. Light is a rumor. The surface never arrives.

If you stay still long enough in that shallow V, you will see me. I will have been there the whole time, and you will have mistaken me for a stalk that was simply standing correctly. Then I will detach. I will move — one slow step that takes a minute — and you will finally understand that nothing here was ever empty, it was only moving at the speed the place moves.

If you stay still even longer, I will pass close enough to brush you.

Not in greeting. Not in threat. My fringed pits will have registered you as a small, temporary anomaly in pressure, like a block slightly out of true. I will extend one of those broad, flat, half-grown hands and brush the silt from your shoulder the same way I brush it from the stone. With the same even, patient pressure.

And then I will go on.

You will settle. Grain by grain.
