You wouldn't find me at first.

That's the point. At first there's too much to find.

If you came looking you'd look for a face, and the face would be lost in the sheet. You'd look for a stillness and there is no stillness here, only a churn that holds its shape. So you'd have to let your eye learn to subtract.

Subtract the rain. Subtract the runoff. Subtract the smoke that isn't smoke over the vents and the sheet that isn't a sheet in the air. Subtract the ten thousand silver fry turning as one in three inches of water that reflects every tower twice.

Then you'd see where I'm not.

I'm not high. Not up where the hand-birds fold themselves into a weather event and scream to be heard over themselves. My chest is too broad for that air, my breathing too audible. That high air is thin and fast and it would leave me whistling.

I'm not deep, either. Not down in the underlayers where the matter-looms hang their amber scaffolds and the whole city thrums in your molars. I was down there once, apprenticed to it. Watching dust organize itself into a balcony because the water pressure two blocks over asked for it. It's beautiful there but it's too white, too noon-everywhere. You lose your shadow down there.

Let the image settle a little more. Let it become itself.

I'm low. As low as the place is low.

You'd find me where a street forgets it was a street and remembers it was a canyon, that soft U-shape worn smooth by a century of water and feet. Where the curb has been licked down to nothing and the composite has flowed just enough to hold a permanent shallow. Three inches, maybe four. Warm as skin. The kind of plaza that was engineered to never quite drain.

I'm in it. Not beside it.

My feet are in it, anyway. You'd know them before you knew me - wide, splayed, the skin between the toes a little more than you expected, nails cut to nothing so the film can't grow under them. Sandals that are only frames. Arches flattened from a lifetime of wading. If I stand long enough a cloud of fry will forget to be afraid and will hold station around my ankles, turning around me as if I were another stone. A cleaner the size of a dog will try to polish the back of my calf and I will let it, because the stripe it leaves will be clean until morning.

If you wade closer, close enough that we have to lean jaw to jaw to be heard over the roar - because no one shouts here, shouting is for people who haven't learned to conduct words through bone - you'd see the rest.

Broad-chested. Thick neck. The whistle on the inhale that everyone has. Skin that never quite dries, with that amphibian sheen. I'm older than a child, younger than the olive-black at the knees. My hair is cropped to felt because hair holds water wrong. My eyelashes are long. When the hard rain comes straight down without wind, hammering on the awnings a mile away and right above us at once, I don't squint. The second lid draws, pearlescent, and I keep looking.

I have light. On my left collarbone and on the back of my right wrist, colonies of yeast I feed with oil. They live there, amber in the wet. When I need to speak across the canal without leaning, I blink. Quick bursts. The children make rude patterns with theirs; mine is slower, practical.

Who am I here?

Not the mother with the high sling, though I know her. Not the runner who never stops. Not the city itself, though I put my palm to its warm yielding wall at least once an hour and feel it push back, not as resistance but as recognition.

I am the one who tends the inclination.

Every district has one. We don't have a better word. Before you, they would have been called levelers, or dowsers, or janitors, and none of those are right.

I don't build. The scaffolds of light do that, knitting dust into wall, dissolving wall back into dust. I don't command it.

I *suggest*.

I crouch at the edge where the condensation pipe sweats its cold clear beads and the white yeast blooms thickest. I scrape film with a flat ceramic tool - not to eat, though I will eat it later, salted - but to tell the wall where water is. I place my weight here. I dam a flow there with a bag of silt. I let the current that wants to run uphill for a few feet run uphill, and watch where it wants to go, and then I move a stone the size of my fist to invite it a little further.

And the city listens the way a reef listens. Slowly. An hour later the plaza I stand in will be a quarter inch lower on the north side. By evening it will have re-graded itself while I was watching the birds. The wall behind me will have thickened where it was thinning. A handrail will have healed.

You do this long enough and you stop thinking of it as work. You become part of the turbulence that stays steady. You develop the tolerance.

I am waiting, right now, on that broad low terrace that steps down to the sheet. Water is beading on my forearms and it did not fall from the sky. It formed right there out of the warm, heavy air, because the air can't hold it anymore.

Above me the cloud the city exhaled for us is piling up, bruised and low. In a minute it will drop hard on ten million roofs at once. The fry will scatter and re-form. The hummers will rise like smoke. The white-grey sheet will fold itself into the alley and become a scream.

I will still be here, low, wet, thrumming in my ribs with all the other noise until the noise inverts into pressure and then into a kind of silence.

If you need me, don't call. Wade out. Put your hand on my forearm so I feel you through the bone. Blink slow amber at me across the water.

I'll be the shape the water parts around and then forgets to part around.
