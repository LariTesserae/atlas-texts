I don't find myself standing there at first.

If I imagine entering that basin - that low, broad, warm place where the sky does all the work - my first thought is that I would be upright. That I would walk in. But I can't. Not for long.

Anything that walks upright there, that breathes out wet and makes noise with its feet, is weather. It passes through. The city mends over it. To stay, you have to learn to be low - not as a posture, but as a physics.

So I lower.

At first I think I would be one of the masons. It is the obvious answer. I want to be useful, I want to be part of the road. I can see myself the color of the material I'm working - bone-yellow in the salt districts - flattened, wax-bloomed so I don't leak, my antennae tasting an inch ahead of me. I can see myself in the flow, outbound empty and inbound carrying a damp, dark pellet of mortar, or a crumb of white fungus. Touching antennae as I pass without slowing. Adding one grain exactly a millimeter left of the last. I like the idea of that: no plan, no architect, just *build here, this needs covering before the sun takes it*. A life where meaning is a chemical line on a wall.

But I am not that quick. My feet would be too loud even flattened. My work would be too hesitant alone.

Then I think I would be higher, at head height where the light goes green and diffused. I would be a weaver. Almost featherless, belly translucent and full of amber dew you could see slosh if you held me to the light. I would hang upside down under a ceiling that is still alive, pulling silk from my wrists and drawing one door-sized fleshy tongue to another, punching through wax without letting a drop of sap escape. *Tik.* Not a song - song is water wasted as vapor - just a dry click felt through the leaf more than heard. I would work with a partner who is not my mate but my counterweight, one holding tension while the other knots. I would press my water sac to the seam when we were done and let the silk drink. That feels closer. Quiet work. Lit rooms that sway.

But weavers see too well. Their lidless eyes never blink. I blink. I want to close.

Lower still, then.

I think I find myself not on the runway or in the stitched canopy, but in the part you have to kneel to see. The primary level. The cool, dark galleries that run through the root mat where the air is warmer and smells of yeast and resin and salt. Where there is no normal light at all.

I would be one of the gardeners, I think, or close to them. Not the long, ivory, blind many-legged ones - not exactly - but the smaller attendants that live alongside them. The ones with no real mandibles, only soft brush-tipped palps. The ones whose job is not to cut or carry or sew, but to tend.

You would find me in a chamber where the carton is a foot thick and the temperature never spikes or dips, where a living trunk was coaxed sideways a generation ago to shade this exact spot. In the low, humid womb.

I am not the swollen mother there, pulsing and laying one waxy bead of an egg every few minutes. I don't want to be a well. I am one of the ones who turns the egg.

My body is small even for this place - thumb-joint small - pale, wax-dusted, my spiracles closed tight so I do not hiss. My abdomen is a little distended at dawn and flat by dusk, because I hold a honey-crop of dew I combed from the air with haired legs in the dark. I excrete nothing wet. I have no voice.

You would find me by not hearing me. The flesh of the plants absorbs sound. The runway outside is polished smooth by constant feet. Inside, there is only the faint creak of a root adjusting, the soft pat of other bodies. I move on a trail I did not make, following a taste in the air that is not a smell to you but is a bright road to me. I pick up what was left - a discarded chitin plate, a failed silk knot, a mason that died mid-stride on the avenue and was carried down within five minutes - and I do not waste it. I carry it to the white brain of fungus pulsing in the chamber, and I place it precisely where the hyphae are hungry. I brush it. I coat it with spit that is also antiseptic.

Later I will carry a crumb of that fungus back up, white and soft, to feed weavers and masons and rollers.

That is where I am: in the gut of the city. In the loop where nothing leaves. In the place that makes soil still, grain by grain, from death.

If you came and lay down on the springy platform a foot above the salt and made yourself as low as the land itself, you wouldn't see me. I'm under. But if you stayed perfectly still until midnight, when the heat you've been radiating finally bleeds into space and the fine glitter settles on every edge - not frost but dew, beading on wax and salt - you might feel a faint coolness as ten million hairs go from dry to beaded all at once. The hush getting even deeper as every forager pauses to drink the air itself.

In that minute, on the dome of a tortoise-sized roller clamped down and sealed to the ground, or on the edge of a salt shard two feet high, or on your own eyelashes if you let them stay open - I am there too, drinking. My palps out, my belly filling amber. Not building, not sewing, not grazing. Just holding water inside the circle.

You would find me low, because everything here lives low. You would find me silent, because sound is water leaving the body. You would find me busy in a quiet way, turning one small egg over and over so it stays warm, so that in the morning the city can add another ring, another wall, another street to the vast, almost-silent sprawl that is still learning how to be ground.

If you stayed until morning and then stood, you would find a few grains of salt and fiber mortared to your sleeve where I had mistaken you - briefly, reverently - for a crack that needed closing.
