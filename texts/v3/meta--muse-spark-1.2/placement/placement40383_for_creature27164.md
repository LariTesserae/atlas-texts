I thought at first I would be up on the rim.

That would be the sensible place for me — where the air is still air and sound still has direction, where the peat is level and the black spruce make a thin, familiar forest and the sky is more than a slot. I could be the one who finds the edge, who pauses where the hare tracks pause, who feels the cold draft rising out of the green mouth and decides to turn back. That is a kind of life. A life that passes by.

But I don't turn back.

If I stay at the rim I am only a visitor. And this place doesn't keep visitors. It keeps what can sink.

So I go down.

At first I thought I would be the human. The negative left in stone — the stacked wall under the south overhang, the fire ring, the axe-marked spruce beam silvered by frost. I know that shape. To come and go, to be useful intermittently, to replace a lashing and leave a rusted blade and take a drink that makes your teeth ache. To talk to yourself because the roar covers it. To need shelter and to be tolerated as something that fell in and will be slowly digested. I have been that. I know how to be that.

But I don't want to be tolerated. I want to be kept.

So I go lower, onto the floor itself, where the air is too big and the light never really arrives. At first I thought I would be the waterfall — two arm-lengths wide, tea-colored, never stopping, the thing that makes the whole stone drum ring. To be the loudness that is not an event but the place itself breathing. That appealed to me for a minute. To be everywhere at once and impossible to locate.

But the waterfall doesn't live here. It passes through. It is just the plain's sweat finding the lowest place. It has no memory.

What lives here lives by staying.

Then I saw the islands, and I corrected myself again.

I thought I would be the moss. And for a while that was true. I would be the pillow of sphagnum fifteen inches deep, holding water like a lung holds air. Or the beard-moss curtain combing condensation one drop at a time. Or the liverwort sheet, the hart's-tongue fern unfurling glossy in the dusk because the moss held a pinch of soil against vertical rock long enough for a root to try. To be the green that makes the cold colder and the wet wetter, that buries fallen blocks and turns boulders into soft hills a millimeter a year. To be patient enough to grow over anything that stays still.

I wanted to be that patient. But moss alone can't cross the ten feet of bare, wet stone between one island and the next. Moss is dense where it is and absent where it isn't. It needs a way to move.

And then I lifted the stone — without lifting it — and I found myself.

You would find me not at the rim, and not under the fall where the roar is white, and not in the human's flattened place that is already slumping back into the floor.

You would find me in the black pool's edge, under the loaf-sized stone where the water is four degrees and never changes.

I would be the amber smear that isn't amber.

Low and flat and spread thin, the way water is spread thin. No shoulders. Shoulders are for carrying and for bracing, and here the weather has already happened. I would be permeable. My skin would be the same wet dark as the rock, freckled with the same orange mineral weep, so that when I sit still — which is most of the time — I am not an animal on a rock but a damp place on a rock that happens to have eyes. Eyes that have clouded to light-meters, because there is nothing to see at a distance anyway.

I would hear through my jaw and my belly, through the stone itself. The deafening that sends you away would be my silence. I have lived under that weight since I hatched and I would be lost without it. A moth hitting the water, an owl cutting the slot of sky, your boot on the ladder — each would make a different ripple in the drum, and I would feel the change in pressure before I heard anything.

I would do almost nothing else.

My heart would beat in minutes, not seconds. I would go a season on biofilm and a single beetle washed over the lip. When something blunders down through the cold column of air and lands stunned on the water, I would extend — not lunge, extend — and take it. Then stillness again, long enough for liverwort to try to root in the silt on my tail. I would let it.

I would not be many. Three, maybe four of me in the whole bowl, each with ten feet of empty stone around it. That emptiness would not starve me because I would be small enough to be fed by it. That loudness would not deafen me because I would be still enough to listen inside it. That damp would not rot me because I would be wet enough to belong to it.

But I would not be only the animal, either. That was my last correction.

I would be the rest of it too — the green half of the same body. The moss on my back carrying spores from island to island across the bare stone, the moss keeping the air at 100% humidity so my skin never dries. One is the green's way of moving, one is the green's way of waiting. I would be both, and the interval between them. The ten feet of bare, slick rock that looks empty but isn't, because it is crossed one slow belly-drag at a time, one spore at a time, once a year if the year is kind.

If you come in fifty years when the ladder is down and the wall has slumped and the fall has cut a thumb's width deeper, you will still find me. Lift the stone and I will lift my head a fraction, fan my two small blood-red ferns in the black water, and settle again. I will not have noticed you were gone.

I am not defending the pocket.

I am the pocket, the part of it that can blink.
