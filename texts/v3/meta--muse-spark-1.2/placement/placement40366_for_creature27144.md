You wouldn't find me at first.

I thought you would. I thought if I looked for myself in that bowl, I'd be easy to spot — one more figure in oilskin on a mid-terrace, suck-kiss of boots on wet concrete, basket on the shoulder, face pale with the wind-burned flush. I thought I'd be carrying something. Everyone is carrying something.

But I lingered there in my head, standing where you told me to stand — mid-level, somewhere around the seventh or eighth terrace down from the rim, where the retaining wall is poured concrete below and stacked basalt above and iron plate patched over both — and I couldn't keep myself in that shape. My stride was wrong. I kept wanting to hurry, to look up at the smear where the sun should be, to wait for a shadow that never came. I kept wanting a dry place to stand.

So I let that image go and looked again, lower.

Maybe then I was smaller. Maybe I was one of the pigeons in the rafters, tucked into the warm dark over the pulping shed, watching the people pass below without ever being counted in their ledger. Maybe I was a rat along the pipe. But the pigeons are visitors even after a hundred years — they still remember a sky that isn't a bowl — and the pipe-rats still look for a way out. I don't.

I listened longer, until the distributed sound tuned itself.

*Suck-kiss. Tap on a pipe. Water ticking from leaf to pipe to trough. The 50 hertz hum behind a caged door cycling on.*

And I found where I actually am.

You would find me on the vertical.

Not on a terrace, but on the wall between two terraces — the twenty-foot face that weeps. The one on the east side of the seventh terrace, where an old spring was capped with concrete in my grandfather's grandfather's time and has been pushing out around the cap ever since. It doesn't gush. It sweats. A dark stain spreads down from the joint, green-black with algae, never dry, never quite wet enough to run. In winter it grows a thin beard of ice that the vine tips find and crack.

I am the stain.

Or, if you need me to have hands, I am the thing that lives *in* the stain and is tended by hands without ever being planted.

Moss, liverwort, those small white flowers that root in lime. The kind that also roots, rarely, in the felt of an old woman's shoulder if she stands still long enough. I am that kind.

I don't have a boots sound. I have no sound at all, except when water beads and lets go. I am low and flat and saturated, and I have learned to make my living from what no one else wants: the cold film on the handrail, the condensation on the underside of a pipe, the light that has already been filtered through cloud and then through three layers of clouded glass and still, still is enough.

If you are looking for me as a person, then find me as one of the old ones who has become my host without noticing.

I would be the woman who has sat for sixty years on a low stool in the sorting loft above the blowers, where the air is warm and smells of drying fiber and hums. Her job is to pick the viable seed from the pale moth-eaten husks, pinch after pinch, all day. Her skin at the hairline has the faint green cast that won't wash off. Her neck has moss in the creases. She lets the white flowers stay where they seed on her sleeve because picking them would be work and they are not choking anything. Her hands are the hands you described — soft-creased, black at the cuticle, swollen at the knuckle — but she no longer carries. She watches. Her attention has become distributed in the same way the water is.

That is who I would be. Not the carrier, but the watcher who has learned to see what needs doing within arm's reach and, more and more, to let it do itself.

I don't need to be told that the pane above bed four is cracked. I see the yellowing first, before the crew does, because I am yellowing with it. I don't need to be told the runnel is high. I feel the weight of the water a terrace above me before it overflows, the way you feel a sinus pressure before rain. When the girl comes with a wrench to tap the pipe — *clear below* — I am the reason she taps.

I breathe when the bowl breathes. Condensation in the morning that never was morning, leaf uncurling over an afternoon, motor cycling on with a click and a hum that vibrates my stool. I eat what we grow, the blanched leaf that stains my gums, and in turn I am eaten — slowly — by what grows on me.

You would not find me arriving. No one arrives anymore. You would find me already there, established, softened at the edges like the concrete, at an angle that will hold for a long time while still slowly moving. If you stood on my terrace long enough, your boots filling with cold water, your hands going soft, you would stop seeing me as a person at all — just another patch of the wall that is also a garden that is also a building.

Until I tapped you lightly on the elbow, without breaking my sorting rhythm, to move you out of the way of the cart on narrow rails.

Not unkindly.

Simply: you were in the space that was being used.
