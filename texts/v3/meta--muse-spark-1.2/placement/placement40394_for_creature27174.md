At first I think I would stay on the rim.

I would be the one who doesn't go down.

I like the image of myself there - dry shoes in the field grass, wind in my ears, looking over the lip of limestone at the warm breath coming up. A visitor. Someone who can see the whole thing at once - the green thatch, the amber lights caught in the mist, the little humming lung of it - and be moved by it and then walk back to the road. Keeping that distance. Keeping the quiet of the field in my chest.

But I don't stay there long. Even in the imagining, I lean forward.

My hand finds the iron stair. It's wet. It is always wet. That cold, lapped wetness under the palm is the first honest thing. The rail shudders a little with the thud coming up from below - *thud... thud... thud...* - and my body answers it before I decide to move. I think I would be more curious than reverent. I would have to see how the light filters.

So I go down, and I go down slowly, because the steps are moss-slick and because every turn of the switchback changes the air.

Halfway down the wall it stops being the field's air. It gets heavy. It gets warm in the way a mouth is warm. The white hangs in it, not fog, not steam, just water made visible, beading on the rail, on my forearms, on the leaf-edges that press in from the sides. The sound gets big. The fans start to chop. The pumps get louder until I feel them in my teeth. And I realize I am shouting my own breath without meaning to.

If I stayed a visitor, I'd climb back out after ten minutes with my clothes damp and my ears ringing. I know that. I'd be the person who says *it's beautiful but I couldn't live there* and means it as a compliment.

But if I'm honest about where I would actually find myself - where I would settle if you left me in this world long enough for me to stop performing being a visitor - I wouldn't be on that stair.

I would be already at the bottom of it.

Not at the very bottom. Not on the lowest tier where the man sweeps the same water toward the same gutter all morning. I respect that work, but my patience isn't that kind. That tier is too much like bailing a boat that is also the sea.

And not at the top tier, just under the canopy, where the shafts of real sun sometimes knife through. That light would hurt me after a while. My eyes would water. I'd always be squinting.

I would be in the middle.

The second terrace, where the old machine hall's brick has completely disappeared under ficus and the corrugated roof has become a meadow that drips. Where the catwalks cross and don't go anywhere and the pipes sweat and are wrapped and re-wrapped. Where the air is most still and the bulbs have to do the most work.

I think I know what my hands would be doing before I know my name.

I would be one of the pruners. Or more precisely, one of the negotiators.

Give me the scaffold and the hooked knife. Give me the lime bucket. My job is the fan on the second tier, the big one that turns slow enough that you can see each blade. A fig has decided it loves that fan. It always does. It sends a wrist-thick runner along the conduit, searching for the vibration, for the warm air, and it tries to feed its leaves into the housing a centimeter an hour. If you let it, it will gum the motor. If you rip it out by the root, it will sulk and then come back twice as thick somewhere worse, cracking the brick to get in.

So you don't kill it. You tell it *not here*.

I would be good at that. I would be the one who knows where to cut so the sap doesn't run too much, who knows how to pack the wall-scar with lime so the water finds a different seam. I would know the weight of that vine in my hands, wet and green and stubbornly alive. I would work in that amber bulb-light with my hair cut short because it has to be, with my rubber boots full of my own sweat-water and the tops already going mossy, with my forearms sheened and my fingerprints soft. I would have to lean in close to the woman working next to me to be heard at all, and then we would stop trying and just touch - two fingers on her wrist to say *hold this, here*, a palm on a shoulder to say *that's enough for today, it will keep*.

I would speak too loud without knowing it. My hearing would already be a little dull in one ear from standing under that fan for years. And I would know that sound the way you know a household hum - not as noise anymore, but as the condition for silence. When the bearing starts to whine a quarter-tone higher, I would be there with the oil before anyone else noticed.

You would find me at about ten in the morning when a gap in the canopy opens somewhere above the tomato glasshouses and a real shaft comes down and lays itself across the catwalk. I would stop, mid-cut, and put my hands in it. Not for very long. Just to warm the joints. My skin would look very pale in it, then very green when I step back under the leaves.

I would not be trapped. You would think I was if you shouted down from the rim, but I wouldn't hear you properly over the fans anyway. And if I did, I would look puzzled.

At night I would sleep in a hammock strung where the air moves, not far from my fan, so I can feel if it stutters. I would need the thud of pump number three to fall asleep - the one that keeps the second tier from becoming a pond. Without it my chest would feel too empty. Dry air would feel wrong in my lungs, like breathing dust.

And I would do the other small thing - because everyone does two things there. On my way back from the scaffold I would carry the rabbit-hair brush. I would duck into the glasshouse built against the limestone, where the condensation beads so thick you can't see out and the tomato flowers are waiting. No bees have ever lived down here. No birds. The leaves are perfect, uneaten, because there is nothing to eat them but us, carefully. I would touch each flower, one by one, turning my own wrist to do the work an insect would do, humming that one flat note that matches the machines so no one can hear me unless I waver.

That's where you'd find me if you parted the curtain of green and waited for your eyes to adjust. Not as someone special. Not as a leader or a loner. Just a pale, damp, loud-voiced keeper of the agreement. One of the animals the pit keeps because it can't keep any others - the one that keeps the green from eating the machine and the machine from drying out the green.

I would offer you tea in a hot tin cup. You would stare at my boots. I would not follow you back up the stair when you left.
