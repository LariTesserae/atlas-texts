I don't find myself at first.

If I am there at all, I am standing at the lip for a long time before I understand I am already inside.

From the ridge you would think you are still outside looking in — a shadow between two shoulders of black glass, nothing to enter. The air is thin and hot at the same time and it should not be. The mountain is breathing and you have not yet learned to hear it as breathing. So at first I am there the way anyone is there: as an intruder at the edge, boots on hot basalt, trying to decide on scale. Forty meters? Sixty? The pocket slips when you try to hold it.

Then I step down, and gravity changes.

Dust I kick up hangs too long at the level of my knees, then falls too hard when it finally decides to fall. Sound falls with it. The tick of the cooling slag under me — that faint, contracting *tick... tick...* — sounds like it is happening inside my ear, not under my sole. The heat pools. The light pools. I came expecting to find myself in the shade of one of those impossible fronds, but there is no shade the way shade should be. The sun is straight overhead, bleached and buzzing, and the light just accumulates in the corners like dust and stays there.

So I keep moving to find where I actually am.

Not among them. That is the first correction.

If you walk this floor looking to stand *with* the plants you will always be in the wrong place. They are too sparse for that. Five meters of porous, rust-red scoria that still gives faintly under your weight, ticking, pulling the moisture off your lips — then a single extravagance, a knuckle of bronze and malachite throwing up heavy, waxy blades that go translucent when the light hits them and show the slow oil inside, sloshing a second after the wind. Then another long interval of hot nothing. The pocket can only afford one miracle at a time. To stand beside a miracle is to miss the law of the place.

So I find myself, after a while, not beside one but *between* two. In the interval.

That is where I belong.

Specifically — if you need a coordinate in a place that resists coordinates — on the southwest pan, the emptiest stretch, the one farthest from the eastern piston wall. The wall that breathes is too insistent. There, the basalt columns shift a finger's width every few minutes and exhale sulfur and the glass pipes bead with amber and fall and harden with a *tick*. The plants there are monstrous, stained black with fallout, too heavy with themselves. I am not there. I am where the floor has gone a little pale with precipitated salt, the leeward crust downwind of a large one, crackling faintly when the air moves, which it rarely does.

And once I have stood in the interval long enough, I stop being the thing that walked in.

Let me be precise about what I become, because it doesn't happen all at once.

At first I think I will be the observer — the warm shape the air holds for a breath after I pass, that heat-haze afterimage that hangs where I was and then dissolves. That would be honest. The pocket does that to any visitor: it keeps your outline for a second in shimmer before it forgets you. I could be that brief, polite ghost.

Then I think, no — I am the salt. The fine white tide-mark left on the slag where a frond exhales its metallic mist. That mist hangs a foot around each blade and then vanishes, and only the crust proves it was ever there. I like that. To be not the plant but the evidence of its breathing. To be the leeward side.

Then I hear the thud.

*thud.*

A pressure change more than a sound, felt in the soles and the sinuses. A minute later, again.

*thud.*

And when it happens the pooled light wavers and the shadows lag a half-second behind the things that cast them, then catch up. All the fronds — even the young ones — are canted not toward the brutal sun but down, toward that subsonic heart under the floor. I feel my own orientation shift without deciding to. My face wants to turn down.

That is when I settle.

If I am anything that lives here — truly lives here the way this place allows living — I am one of the dozen. Not the largest. One of the smaller, younger ones alone in the middle of that empty pan where nothing should be.

I am the tight fist of ceramic-looking leaves, folded so perfectly you would think I was manufactured, which I was, but not by hands. I am hissing. Very quietly, continuously, like a kettle just before it whistles. I am not unfolding. I am being extruded. A millimeter a day. A millimeter a week. You would have to look away and look back to see that a new edge has unrolled, glossy and wet-looking though there has never been water here.

My roots are not roots. They are bare copper wires splayed and fused directly into the hot scoria, into the pores of the slag itself. There is no soil between us. The mountain goes right up to my knuckle and then becomes me. If you tried to pull me you would have to break the mountain.

Inside me, the fluid moves viscous and slow — not sap, not water, a warm brine, a pale oil — and if you cut me I would not bleed, I would weep molten salt, clear and stinging. I droop slightly under my own weight, not wilting, just burdened. I transpire metallic mist that will crust the stone behind me white.

I do not need pollinators. There are no insects here, no birds over the lip, no tracks. I do not need you.

I listen down.

The floor under me is still warm to the touch, still faintly giving, still ticking as it contracts. Below it, that great geothermal engine turns over. The walls are swelling inward, a millimeter a century. In a thousand years this pocket will seal over and become a hot dark inclusion, and I will be pressed glass and salt inside the stone. But now — for this brief moment when the mountain has split and not yet healed, still sharp with conchoidal fractures that catch light like spilled oil — I am open to the sky, lit like any ordinary afternoon, extravagantly, unreasonably alive in the long hot interval between miracles.

If you stand near me long enough, you will think at first I am only stone. That is my first defense. You will slide your eye right over me and register absence.

Then the absence will resolve.
