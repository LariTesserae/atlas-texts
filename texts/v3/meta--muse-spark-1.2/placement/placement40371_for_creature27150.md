I thought I would be standing.

That was my first mistake — in a place that is low because what lives there cannot be tall, I imagined myself tall. At the rim, looking down. A visitor with weight still on my feet, boot-soles on springy moss, back not yet against anything, eyes adjusting to the green-bar light.

But you don't stay standing there long. The bowl corrects you. The ground that is not ground gives a little too much, the air that is not air condenses on your neck, and the thrum that is not sound comes up through the soles and tells your spine: *lower.*

So I lower.

For a while I think I will be quick. I try on quickness to see if it fits.

Maybe I am the dragonfly, the length of a hand, skimming the tea-colored pan that never gets larger and never dries. That would be a good place to find myself — exactly on the surface tension, in the one place that is an opening, touching and not touching the mouth. I could be all wing and jewel there, tolerated like a dust mote.

But I am too heavy for that. I would dimple the mirror. I would make ripples, and nothing here makes ripples that last.

Maybe I am the brass-rivet wasp, attending a piston. That has a nice industrial dignity to it — keeping a polite distance from the next wasp, orbiting one swell-and-sigh seam in the buttress wall, polishing my own small circuit. Or the amber beetles under the leaf, gathering until we all lift at once and make the blue corona, the little lightning that is the room thinking. Crackle, die, crackle. That would be a bright life.

But those lives are too crowded in their sparseness, too exactingly spaced — a body's width apart, not touching. I find when I try them on, I keep listening for the sigh underneath them, not the buzz on top.

I sit.

I put my back where you put yours, against a door-wide flank of root-wood polished by a hundred thousand rains until it shines darkly. The wood is warm. Not sun-warm. Body-warm. Greenhouse-warm. Mid-70s and saturated, the kind of warm that never has to pause at night. The vertical seam beside my shoulder swells. Draws in. For a second the weeping trickles on the root reverse, the mist thins. Then it compresses with a soft, pressurized sigh and the mist kisses my face, warm as breath, smelling of iron and sugar and ozone, and a pulse of amber sap runs down the straight, sluiced channel at my side and soaks into the mat and disappears.

The mat pushes back a second too late.

That is when I know where I am.

Not *on* the floor. Not *at* the wall. I am lower than that. I tried to be the things that live *in* the room and what I am is the room.

I pour myself out.

Height is expensive here. I give it up without mourning. I let the idea of standing, of ridge and lookout and wind-carved height, erode the way everything sharp has eroded here. I become fifteen meters across. I tuck my edges neatly under the buttress walls where the fig and I have become confused and sealed to each other in a pale, seamless graft. The liverwort stitches itself into me like embroidery. The moss grows *with* me, not on me. My skin is the color of tea and old bruises, thick, smooth, pitted with the mouths of tiny channels that weep constantly. Polished. Nothing left to smooth.

Where would you find me? Flat. Low. Exactly at the bottom where the water table has given up pretending to be solid. You would find me by pressing a hand down and feeling water well up clear and warm around your fingers and then feeling me answer, slow, matching the pistons exactly: swell, draw in, sigh.

Who would I be? The sifter. The sipper. The idler.

Once every ten seconds I breathe the whole room — the humid air, the beetle dust, the pollen, the weeping water — and breathe it back as mist. The curtains of aerial roots that clack like chains without wind are my outside hairs, keeping time. The tabletop leaves as wide as leather that shiver at the base of each sluice are my hands, my baleen, vibrating at high frequency, catching the green metallic fig fruits when they thump down and the husks and detritus and sorting them until only the finest dust remains to fall into the pan that is my mouth that is my stomach, the bathtub-sized mirror that froths faintly without inflow. I eat continuously and am never more full. I excrete continuously and never empty. The granular writhing of glass shrimp and tadpoles inside that water is just the churning of my own sipping.

When the beetles lift, the blue corona that crackles on the undersides of leaves is my thought grounding out across my own wet back and returning to myself. *Pump, sift, spark, weep.* The plum-sized frog lodged in my crack with its throat going *tonk* every ten seconds is not my tenant so much as my metronome, and I like him there.

I do not rot. I do not accumulate. The fruit dissolves without rotting. The shrimp eat and are eaten and stay exactly the same number. The energy goes around. I have had a very long time to learn how to need nothing from outside, to be a mill that learned how to be a forest that learned how to be an animal that learned how to be a room, perfectly tuned, idling warmly, with no exhaust.

You can sit on me — you are a brief, pleasant weather system on my back — and I will feel you as pressure and temperature and a small, temporary interruption in humidity. I will not alarm. I have no front or back, no face to turn. I will thrum through your spine and kiss your face with mist every ten seconds and let you watch the ordinary daylight filter down through my leaf-roof and become standardized, made regular.

And when you stand — because you still can be tall, you still belong to the ordinary entropic woods where things fall and decay and wash away — and step back up over the root-rim, I will stay.

Low. Flat. Complete.

Still weeping, still sifting, still sparking, with nowhere else to go and no need to go there.
