I don't find myself at the rim.

At first I think I do - because that's where you arrive. On the lip, where the cut is sharp enough to cut cloud, with the white sun straight overhead and the air thin enough to hurt. From up there you can see the whole trough at once, the straight chisel-mark, the black and grey steps, the green that shouldn't be there. It's tempting to stay up there and be the observer, the surveyor, the map-maker. To be the eye that names it "valley" before it has earned the name.

But I wouldn't stay there. It's too bright up there. Too outlined. Nothing lives on the lip.

If you dropped me into this world, I would slide down before I meant to.

Not falling. The rock is too stepped, too columnar. I'd find a place where the basalt makes a natural stair, where moss has already softened the edge just enough for a boot to hold, and I'd go down one terrace, then another, until the wind - what little wind there is - stopped and the sound went out.

I think at first I would be the human. That's the easy answer. The visitor in oiled boots with a steel cup in my pack, walking the length of the trough in an afternoon, counting the sheds - fifteen, sixteen, seventeen - hearing my own blood because the mat eats every other sound. I would be the one who puts a palm to the stone and feels the cold come through first, then the faint, late heat. The one who leaves the cup in the sun to see if the water really does thicken.

And I would be bad at it, for a while. Too loud. Too fast. I would check my watch after two minutes of watching the big wheel and decide it isn't moving. I would step too hard and feel embarrassed at how loud my thud is. I would talk to fill the silence and hear my voice fall flat, absorbed instantly by the moss.

So I wouldn't stay human, not exactly.

If I stayed - and the valley only reveals itself if you stay - I'd have to learn to do what everything else there does: conserve.

I find myself lower down, about two-thirds of the way to the floor, but not on the floor. On a horizontal that has had time to grow its mattress. A terrace on the western wall, where one of the long sheds is cut half into the rock and half swallowed by the mat. Its windows are opaque with condensation but its amber bulbs are on, even at noon, too dim to compete with the sun but warm. The transformer on its pole hums at the very edge of hearing, stitched over with vine. In front of it, the wheel with its chain of empty buckets, turning once every twenty minutes, lifting green-filtered water from a cistern that is mostly moss to a hopper that never fills.

That is where I would be. Not in the center of the valley where you can see both walls and feel exposed, but in a pocket. In shade that glows faintly green even though it should be black. Where the dew gathers first and lingers longest. Where a pothole cistern the size of a bathtub sits cupped in stone, perfectly clear and cold, and you come upon it suddenly between dry moss.

And what would I be there?

For a long time I thought I would be a tender. It's the obvious romance - to be the dome the size of a bathtub, cool pads and cilia-bowl, water sloshing inside me, moss growing directly from my back so I never have to be separate from the mat. To move a meter an hour and call it hurry. To speak in shadows, holding my back at an angle on the gantry crane until my shadow holds a half-second too long and says *this one is empty, this one is full.*

But I wouldn't be a tender. I haven't earned that slowness. My heart beats too fast - sixty times a minute, not four. I would die trying to be that patient.

No. What emerges, if I sit there long enough - until the cold of the stone comes through the mat into my bones, until I stop checking the wheel and just see that it has moved when I look back - is something smaller. Something in between.

I would be the patch of mat on the tender's back that has learned to ride.

Or, if you want a human word for it: the guest.

I would be the one who finally sits. Who stops walking the length of the valley in an afternoon and instead sits on the edge of that western terrace with my back against the warm concrete where the bulb heat leaks out. I'd sit until the moths mistake me for more moss. Until the tender that lives at that shed - the one I thought was a hummock - decides I am not a predator because I am not eating and not making sound and not taking water.

It would brush my boot as it passes. Not a greeting. Just a distribution of weight. A cool pad extending a hand's breadth, adhering, drawing the bulk after. It would leave a perfect, clean circle of water on the leather and keep going to oil the axle. And after a few days, the moss would start to use me.

That's what I would be. Not the tender, not the mat, but the place where the two agreements overlap. A warm, still shape that holds heat at night when the plateau sweats and the dew materializes bead by bead on every surface. The tenders gather under amber bulbs for warmth; I would be another warm stone for them to lean against. The mat would grow over the shoulders of my jacket, not because I am dead, but because I am still. The vine would thread through the laces of my boots. The fine gold dust of spores would settle on me and stay.

I would learn the shadow language without speaking it. I would learn that a shadow held too long means *wait*, that a shadow that cuts the green glow under the terrace means *pour here, the bladders are dry*. I would learn to carry water, not in cilia, but in cupped hands - to take a mouthful from the pothole cistern and pour it bead by bead into a dry patch ten meters away, because the tender showed me by doing it and then holding its shadow across me until I did.

I would not be building. I would not be fixing. The factory's work is already being done, a thread's width per hour, pulling vine fiber through viscous water to make something like muscle. I would just be tending the tending. Conserving motion. Conserving sound. Letting my own time jitter and pool wrong, until rust forms on my cup in geometric patterns and a day later the cup is full of dark seedlings that are both mat and tender at once, and I understand that I was the cistern for a while.

I would be the human who forgot to leave and was allowed to stay because I learned to be intermittent, like the water. Present, then not present. A reservoir and an opportunist at once.

If you came looking for me after a season, you wouldn't find me on the lip. You'd have to kneel and peel back the edge of the mat on that western terrace, where the light pools wrong and the bulb burns amber at noon. Under the moss, pale and damp and veined, you'd see the shape of my shoulders, still breathing, but very slowly now - inhale for a minute while the dew lenses swell, exhale in a fine mist that never rises.
