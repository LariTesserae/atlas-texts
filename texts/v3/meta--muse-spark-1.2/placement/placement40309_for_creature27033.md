I would not find myself as myself.

If I slipped through that crack as I am now — warm-blooded, air-breathing, with eyes built for sun — I wouldn't belong for more than a few minutes. The cold would drink the heat out of me before I could learn the pressure. My breath would be too loud. My light would be wrong. I would hang there in the middle of that perfect, viscous cold, un-anchored, and the city would not know what to do with me except watch me drift toward the dead center where nothing lives.

So to be there at all, I have to be translated.

At first I thought I would be one of the Towers.

It makes sense to want to be tall. To be rooted in the warmest bowl of the floor where the new rock still sighs heat, to have a chitinous house grow out of me, to open a red plume and breathe sulfide and let the bacteria inside me do the living for me. To be architecture. To be still and necessary while everything moves around you. I imagined myself fused wall-to-wall with my neighbors, a tenement of white parchment, not seeing anything except the slow convection of water above me.

But I am not that still. I have never been that still. The Towers don't choose, they endure. They wait. I would go mad with waiting.

Then I thought, maybe I am a Commuter — one of the pale shrimp that never stops. That would be more honest. To be translucent with blind antennae lit at the tips, to be always carrying something — a scraping of bacterial veil, a pink cluster of eggs glued to my legs, a parasite I just picked from a worm's plume. To learn the thoroughfares between the tube-clusters where the water from the crack runs fastest, to move with the current one hour and against it the next when the cell turns. To make that soft infrasonic clicking with a thousand others until the basalt holds it like a hand over a mouth.

But the Commuters never rest. They are blood. They don't get to stop and tamp a single grain into place and know that grain will stay. I want to stay.

I get more precise the longer I hold still in that water. The tick of the cooling basalt finds my pulse and slows it. The dim municipal glow of everyone else stops being background and becomes the only way I know where I am. Let what emerges become more itself.

I would be a Mason.

Not at the top where the crack breathes cold oxygen and the plumes crowd together, and not at the bottom in the nursery where the pink eggs pulse like coals in the warmest dark. I would be in the middle. The dense middle. Where the city is thickest and most ordinary.

You would find me three-quarters of the way up the wall, wedged between two Tower tubes that fused badly when they were young so there is a kink between them — a narrow, sheltered alley that the main current mostly misses. There, in that kink, I have built.

My gallery is not grand. No one traveling the thoroughfare would notice it. A honeycomb of perhaps forty chambers, each no bigger than a thimble, made of hyaloclastite grains and glass sand and the mortar of molt. I gathered every grain myself. I picked a flake of basalt the moment it popped off the wall with that soft *tick* and drifted down, and I carried it in my wire-jointed legs before the silt could claim it. I cemented it with spit and mucus, tamped it, smoothed it. Then another. And another.

I am white the way muscle under skin is white. Blind. My eyes are flat clouded plates that don't see shapes, only the nearness of glow. My own antennae are beaded with that tired, constant light — not bright enough to be beautiful, just enough to see the next body over. To stop glowing is to stop existing to my neighbors, so I don't stop.

My feet never leave the wall. This is the first rule and I obey it without thinking. A thin thread of mucus still trails from where I first landed as a larva — that frantic jetting burst through the cold, kite-string behind me, seeking — and though I am grown now as large as your fist, that thread is still there, thickened into an anchor. If I let go, the still water would take me. The center would take me. I have seen it take others, glowing as they go, alone. I do not let go.

To my left, the Tower breathes, its plume opening and closing, opening and closing. To my right, the Commuters flick past in their lane, and I feel their passage as a change in pressure more than I see it. Above me, a brittle star has laced its arm through my upper gallery — the Lattice — and I have built around it, leaving it room. It holds my work together when the rock contracts. We don't talk. We brace each other.

Below my gallery, an isopod the size of a thumbnail grazes the bacterial veil that waves on the warmer stone. I let it graze, because it excretes what feeds the veil again. When it dies, I will pick its carapace clean and use it. Nothing is wasted, not because we are virtuous but because the dome is small and sealed and everything that falls hangs in that cold for a long time before it settles. A molt becomes a wall. A dead worm's tube becomes an apartment. Yesterday a shrimp died in my alley, tangled in a molting. By evening its shell was already part of my newest chamber.

This is what I do all day: gather, spit, tamp. Gather, spit, tamp. The water is so quiet that I hear my own jaws working. I hear the worm plume contract. I hear the tick of the stone that is still becoming stone. I add one grain.

It is not intelligent work. There is no plan. But it is organized. I build where the water slows just enough that my cement holds. My neighbor, another Mason one kink over, builds where the water slows for her. Together without deciding, we make a porous wall that slows the water more. The city grows the way cities grow — no architect, just a thousand small decisions about where to put one grain.

And if you held still long enough beside me — if you let your heat leach out and your breath slow until it matched the ticking — you would hear what I hear. Not silence, but a dense, soft, busy quiet held inside. The rasp of radula on veil, the click of shrimp legs in the thoroughfare, the faint hydraulic sigh of the ocean pushing through the crack that I have never thought of as a crack. To me it is not a door. It is the sky. It leaks weather. It is slow and cold and very far away.

I do not know the ocean is outside. I have never needed to know. For me the dome is the whole world. The cooling basalt is the ground, warm and ticking, that occasionally sheds. The pale, constant glow of my neighbors is not bioluminescence.

It is just light. The only light that has ever been. And by it, I see exactly far enough to add the next grain.
