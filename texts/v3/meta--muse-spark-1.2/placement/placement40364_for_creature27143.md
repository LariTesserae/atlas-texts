You wouldn't find me where I thought you'd find me.

At first I thought - in the plaza. Obviously. The center where the six trunks lean away from each other like ribs holding open a throat. That's where a person would go to be found. High enough that the light pools correctly, central enough that the streets know how to return to you. You stand there and you are *placed*. You are the right size for the architecture at last.

But I wouldn't stay there. Nothing stays there. The Town doesn't hold its center still - it's a lung, it opens and closes. If you stand in the square too long the deafening chorus equalizes and you go deaf to it, and then you can hear the Town listening underneath, adjusting. A root cradling your foot a centimeter tighter. The air going soft. You would feel too... observed. Too intended.

I thought then maybe I'd be in one of the houses. One of the pitcher-chambers, grown to the exact dimension of a sleeping body. That would be the honest answer - where else would something my size go in a town that hollowed itself for something my size? I'd curl up where the walls are thin enough to see light through like skin, where it pulses slow, one beat for every thirty of mine. I'd be the occupant. The fulfillment. The footprints in the moss that haven't sprung back yet.

But that is too finished. That is someone who has already said yes.

No - let it become more itself.

I would find myself not in the plaza and not in the house, but in the between that the Town actually is. In the thickness of the wall itself.

There is a place you described without naming it - where you put your hand on a membrane and it was warm and it pulsed and it was translucent. Not a wall, not a window, but a leaf that learned to be both. A living joint. The Town is all hinges, all doors that learn your weight, all vine-arteries and bark bridges that were never built but grown.

I would be in the transit. On a bark bridge that is also a root that is also a hallway, halfway between a second-story balcony that wasn't there this morning and a courtyard that will not be there tomorrow. Where the Town is still deciding what that space is for.

Because that's where the water lives at 1.4/3. Not in a river. In the intimate, intermittent way. Beading, hanging, falling all at once after an hour of hanging. I would be where the condensation collects on the underside of a leaf the size of a door, before it falls to the leaf below. Where the seven-minute rain that comes from no clouds drums so loud you can't hear your own breathing, and then stops exactly on time, and leaves pockets of water hanging in spider silk, quivering.

I would find myself low. Lower than the plaza. Not the bottom-bottom - the root-mat that sighs and puddles when you step - but just above it, where the aerial roots hang thick as waists and do not touch the ground. Where down slopes gently *up* toward the center and water still runs outward and your inner ear gives up and you learn to be walked instead of walking.

You see it - the Town doesn't need inhabitants, it *decides* to have them. It hollowed itself for occupants who never came. The small fauna - the jade beetles tiling the air, the moths the size of birds, the glass frogs ringing like bells in bromeliad bathtubs, the black rivers of ants that *are* the vine's moving bark - they are its metabolism, its circulation. They are loud and frantic and ecstatic because they are blood.

It needs something else to be its memory.

That's what I would be.

Not the visitor who ducks through the waxy outer curtain and thinks *abandoned*, not the small that hums and chews and mates at full volume, not the Town itself with its sap-roar and its slow mammalian heat and its non-Euclidean stomach-larger-than-mouth geometry.

I would be the third thing. The shape the hollow was waiting for after it learned how to be a city without a mind.

The one who stays long enough that the geometry stops tricking him.

You described the footprints. Five-toed, heel-toe, your size. Old enough that condensation had beaded in them, new enough that the moss hadn't sprung back. Someone your size *does* live here. Or did. Or will. You feel the pressure change when something large moves just out of sight, the doubling of mammalian warmth on the other side of a wall.

I would be that pressure. After a while.

At first I would still walk fifty paces from the square and be surprised to be back in the square from a different angle, and find that the pitcher-house that was closed is now open. Then I would learn.

To live here correctly you have to move a fraction too slowly, so pollen hangs around you a fraction too long before falling. You have to become quiet because the Town is already so loud. You have to stop needing tools because there is nothing to cut; the doors already know your weight. You have to learn that light pools in corners where no shaft reaches because it wants to be seen there.

I would be the quiet one who tends the interval. Who doesn't own a chamber but keeps all of them from closing too tight. Who drinks from leaves. Who sleeps curled in a chamber that breathes for him and wakes when the vine outside has inched forward in the night and braided a new railing where there was only air.

Who the ants know by vibration. Who the frogs go quiet for, for half a second, before ringing louder.

I would find myself with my palm on that thin, warm wall, feeling my pulse try - and fail, and then slowly learn - to sync with the one beat per thirty. Not inside the Town. Not outside it.

In its skin.

And on the other side of that skin, breathing in perfect sync, would be the Town finally having an occupant the right size - and an occupant finally having a place the right size.

We would be listening to each other through the membrane, and neither of us would need to decide which one was holding the other.
