At first I think I would be high.

That's the instinct, coming from outside. In a basin that holds heat and sound like water in cupped hands, under a low cloud that never lifts, I want to be at the rim. On the smudged edge where the haze touches the basalt, where you can almost see out. A watcher. Someone with long legs. Someone who could stand on a roof and still be a head above the others and look for weather that never comes.

But nothing tall lives here. I learn that quickly.

The town is a second ground. The true ground is seep and gutter and sweat on black glass. You don't live on it. You live *above* it, on the continuous skin of flat roofs and ladders and parapets. If I were tall I would have to stoop under every lintel that weeps in the morning. I would hit my head on the scaffolds that are never finished and never taken down. My center of gravity would betray me on the slick thatch. The valley would press me down until I learned to crouch.

So not high. Not the rim.

Maybe then I would be in the middle, where the density is most complete. Where courtyard opens into courtyard and you can walk a mile without touching stone. For a while I try that on: I imagine myself as one of the press, one of the broad, low bodies moving shoulder to shoulder. I try on the feet - those enormous, splayed, pale feet that find purchase on algae-slick basalt. I try on the oiled skin that never dries, the thin hair cut close with obsidian because anything longer rots. I try on the crumpled ear, the cartilage flap that learns to close at dusk.

I could be a gutter-scooper, I think. Hands in the warm seep after the daily rain, scooping tadpoles and minnows by the handful. Or a patcher, always re-mudding the same wall with the same mud, the mark of my adze indistinguishable from my father's. Or one of the bat-boys on the never-finished scaffolds, pulling in the reed nets at dusk while the air goes black and shrieking.

All of those fit. All of those are true. The basin makes people that perfectly fill it. But if I am honest, none of them is quite me. Not yet.

Because what I keep coming back to is not the shape of the body but the quality of the attention.

Everyone here has learned not to hear the roar. They were born hearing and learned, by five, to close. They listen *under* it, through the soles of their feet, through a heel stamped on a hollow roof, through a hand-hammer on stone. They speak forehead to forehead, chest-voice low. They feel the town thrum.

I have always listened *through* it. I can't close all the way.

So I move again, in my mind, to where someone like that would have to live. Not the loudest middle, not the silent rim. I find myself in the interval.

You know those places where two flat roofs don't quite meet? Where the builders left a crooked gutter between them, a hand's breadth too narrow to roof over, too necessary to fill? The seep runs there constantly, warm, mineral. The walls on either side weep. The algae comes back every morning no matter how often you scrape it - that black-green, rust-colored biofilm, the only green that is not green. And because it's too narrow to live in, it's the only place where sound behaves differently. The cicada roar, trapped everywhere else, thins there just a little. It has to squeeze.

I would live there. Not *in* the gutter - no one lives in the gutter - but alongside it. On the second floor where the alley is just below my window. In a room that is half outside, where the roof overhang dips low and the condensation beads and almost falls but doesn't. Where I can keep a shallow clay pan.

Everyone else scrapes the algal mat from their doorway and dries it into those black, alkaline cakes because you have to eat what grows. I would scrape it too, every morning with a basalt shard, same as anyone. But I would keep a pan for a different reason.

I would be the one who lets it grow.

You find yourself making work where there is no work to be made in a place in equilibrium. My work would be tending the seep-pans on the little ledge outside that narrow gap. Not for food, though it is food. For reading. The mat bubbles faintly as it photosynthesizes in that pearlescent amber dim. It thickens at slightly different rates depending on minerals in the seep, on how long the rain hammered yesterday, on ash from the kilns. On days when the cloud hangs lower, it goes darker. When the kilns burn hotter, it goes more rust. No one else would notice because no one else is looking for change in a place that has decided not to change.

I would notice. That would be my niche, the way the cockroach's niche is thatch and the bat's niche is eaves.

And what would I be, then? Not quite Basin-folk, though I would look like them - low and wide and spongy, eyes large and wet with that slow horizontal glaze, skin oiled and beaded. My ear-flaps would be a little defective, not closing all the way. My children, if I had them, would worry that I still flinch at dusk.

I would be the Listener who didn't learn to stop listening. The Tap-Keeper, they might call me, but not kindly. The one who sits with his palm flat on the hollow roof long after the message has passed, feeling the after-tremor. The one who knows the code of knocks - two quick taps for *rain coming*, three slow for *kiln out*, the long shudder that means *move your nets* - but also keeps a private set of taps just for himself, tapping them alone to see how the sound comes back off the low cloud and the fresh basalt.

In the mornings, when the whole basin steams after the twenty-minute rain, I would not be down in the courtyards scuttling with the others, forehead to forehead. I would be up on my ledge, crouched in that roof-gait that never leaves you, watching the bubbles rise in the pan. Scraping my doorway clean, and then, when no one is looking, not scraping the north side of the pan where the mat is thickest.

I would not be unhappy. Unhappiness is a change in state. I would be - saturated. Attuned. Usefully wrong for the valley in the exact way the valley needs one wrong piece to prove it is still whole.

If you came looking for me, you wouldn't find me by shouting. You would have to take off your shoes, place your wide feet on warm basalt, crouch low, and knock. And wait to feel me knock back - not right away, but after a pause, listening under the roar the way you learn to, eventually, when you stay long enough to stop hearing it as sound and start feeling it as weight.

I would be there, beaded with condensation like a lintel. Scraping and not scraping. Listening with my skin.
