I don't know at first.

If you set me down there - high above weather, above the cloud sea - I don't immediately know what I am. The light is too total to see myself in. The air is too thin to hear myself think at my normal speed.

So for a long time I just stand wrong.

I think at first I would be the human shape I came in. Boots on a salt polygon, hands shading my eyes, breathing too fast and too shallow, making that little ghost that vanishes instantly. I would try to find myself the way humans find themselves in a new place - by looking for water, for shade, for a wall to put my back to. And there is none of that here. There is no shade except the thin blue under a Holdfast at noon. There is no wall. The Mantle sighs under my boots and takes minutes to spring back, and I apologize to it without meaning to. I am too loud, too thirsty, too vertical. I cast a short, hard shadow that doesn't belong among those double-edged, floating shadows. I am a water logic standing in a place that solved itself without water a long time ago.

So that can't be who I am, not for long. If I stay, I have to unlearn hurry.

I think next I would try to be a Holdfast.

Because I want to be that patient. I love the idea of pressing down instead of reaching up. I imagine myself low and spread, forty feet across and fifteen high, a smoke of teal-black ribbons that drink light without being burned by it. I would be the one that glows at evening, turning translucent when the sun hits me sideways, a lantern on the plain. I would hold on with pale cord-roots etched into basalt, tasting iron atom by atom, and I would comb the wind with silica hairs and never need to drink. I would live a millimeter a year, and I would have neighbors exactly half a mile away, in threes and sevens. That feels holy.

But I'm not that still. Not yet. A thousand years in one place - I would fidget. I would try to count. The Holdfast does not count. It just is where it is, perfectly spaced, perfectly content to be taken for a rock until you are close enough to feel its faint, warm exhale. I am not that complete.

So I try on something smaller, closer to the ground.

Maybe I am a Grazer then. Low, six-limbed, the size of a boulder that decided to move. Covered in the same waxy filaments as the ribbons so that even I forget I am an animal. I would move so slowly dust settles on my back while I move, and I would have a mouth like a soft plane, rasping only at what has already fallen - the shattered ribbons, the frayed edge of the Mantle. I would leave a trail of chemistry on stone that lasts for months. I would not call. That appeals to me, the silence of it. To be an afterthought, a visitation that makes the silence larger. But to be a Grazer you must be excellent at being invisible, and I - even here - keep wanting to be seen. I keep wanting to look back at the plateau.

It takes until late afternoon to find where I actually am.

The dust-river has come up. You can see it coming from miles away, a clear river in the air that lifts the salt and the rust-colored spores and turns them to slow plankton in the gold light. The Holdfasts around me have turned. Their ribbons have gone from opaque to clear, and the whole slope is lanterns. The Mantle under me is ticking as it contracts, the cold pooling around my ankles even though the rock under my palms is still warm.

I am lying down. That's where I find myself. Not standing anymore. Flat on my back on the warmest slab of basalt I could find, on the lee side of a big Holdfast where its anchor-roots make a little cove and the Mantle has grown thick and sulfur-yellow and almost soft. The sky is closer here than I've ever felt sky. The cloud sea below the escarpment is already pink. Above me, the dust-river is running shallow and fast and gold.

And I am not heavy. That's how I know what I am.

I would be a Glider.

Not because I want to fly - there is almost nothing to fly *with* in that thin air - but because the Glider is the one form here that learned how to be light without needing to be powerful. Needle-thin body as long as my forearm, four limbs drawn out into spars, membrane stretched between them so thin the sun shows through. No feathers. No fur. The same waxy silica-stiffened film as the plants. No muscle for flapping. Only adjustment.

I would have walked up the Holdfast that morning, claw by claw, which is the only real work a Glider does. Launched. And then —

Then for hours I would do nothing that looks like doing. I would hang in that clear, fast, shallow wind and make minute corrections with a tail like a filament. I would not beat. I would not hurry. I would filter. The hairs on my belly would comb spore and salt-crystal and the tiny propagules of the Mantle straight out of the airstream. I would harvest vapor the same way the ribbons do, without ever needing to drink. I would hear only the faintest thrumming of my own membrane, like a distant kite string, and otherwise I would be listening inward the way the whole plateau listens inward.

From up there, where I am, the world resolves.

The Holdfasts below me are not scatter - they are a pattern, a spacing that makes sense only from a half-mile up. The Mantle is not empty ground but a single, continent-sized skin, olive and ochre and mustard, breathing. I can see the old dry river troughs like pale scars and the salt polygons like cracked plates held together by life. I can see a Grazer, finally, because from above its camouflage fails - a slow, dark smudge moving between lanterns.

I don't need to go anywhere. The wind goes. I go with it. When I want to come down, I will just angle and stall and parachute with absurd grace onto a salt plate a day's walk away, fold myself, and wait for the next dust-river.

If you walked below and looked up, you might not see me at all. I am almost transparent. I am almost not there. And that feels right - to be in that high, bright, dry, lush place not as a monument or a root or even a grazer, but as the thing that belongs to air itself. To be the interval between Holdfasts, made animate for an afternoon.

That's where you'd find me. On a late amber afternoon, high in the gold river above the table, barely a shadow on the Mantle below, tasting light and stone and spore, perfectly content to be forgotten by everything below the clouds.
