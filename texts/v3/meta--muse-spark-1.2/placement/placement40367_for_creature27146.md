At first I think I know.

I am standing on the rim. On the coronet of fresh-broken spires, where the air is too thin and the light is a hammer. I am upright, human-sized, sight-seeing. I am looking *down* into the bowl and thinking of myself as a visitor who will descend.

That doesn't last. The air corrects me.

One conscious inhalation — cold and bright and not enough — and I understand I cannot live there that way. Not standing. Not tall. My sound would be eaten. My shadow would be hard-edged and wrong. Anything upright in that room is wind-scoured off quickly or makes itself small. If I stay on the rim I get dizzy, I get burned, I have nowhere to hide the way that green coin has nowhere to hide. So I have to come down, and to come down I have to change scale.

I kneel, without deciding to.

And kneeling, the floor stops being a floor.

If I were to live here at all — if you were to find me here after you stopped looking for a person — you would not find me at first. You would look right at me and see empty mat, empty air between cushions. That's the bowl's trick and mine.

For a long time I try on other bodies to see which one fits.

For a minute I am the pika. Which is appealing. Dart and whistle, cut and carry, flicker at the edge of vision. But the pika is a commuter. It works the mat and retreats to the talus. It doesn't make the spiral. It doesn't know why the spiral is there.

For a minute I am one of the bumblebees, improbable and furry, droning on air that shouldn't hold me. Too loud, too brief. I would be dead by evening.

For a minute I am the moss itself, the tight dome of saxifrage or the bottle-glass cushion, holding heat and moisture against the cold. I like that one. To be lush in two inches of height. To be dense, not tall. To store light after the sun is gone and give it back slowly. But the moss is not alone — it is a city, and no single dome knows the fingerprint it is part of.

Then I get smaller still, or emptier, and I find where I actually am.

I am not on the mat. I am *in* the between.

You would find me at the center, but not as the cairn. You would find me as what the cairn is for. At about ten in the morning, when the quartz rimes thicker than anything else and then drips.

I am the hollow that catches.

If I am any one thing, I am the intervals the bowl is made of. The inch of dark humus between one cushion and the next. The yard of thin light between one boulder and the next. The held breath between inhale and exhale that you have to learn here to keep from fainting. The blue thread of willow smoke that never rises above the rim. The afterglow that the moss and the skin hold after the sun slips behind the western spire — not reflected, but stored.

I am empty the way the bowl is empty. That is my rule, too.

Abundantly, quietly full inside the emptiness.

You would find me low, moving on hands and knees, not because I am praying but because the mat will scar under boots and I have learned the floor of my own house. You would find me tending by subtraction. Not building, not planting. Prying a crowded saxifrage dome with a bone awl, shunting a melt-trickle half an inch left with a pebble, moving a pinch of black peat from where it will rot to where it will hold. Slow, clockwise, always clockwise to keep the wind at my back. No plan larger than today. Over years, a spiral appears. Not because I intended a pattern, but because attention, repeated, becomes geometry.

That's who lives here and who I would be if I lived here.

Not the mountain, which breaks and shatters. Not the visitor, who looks down and leaves. But the keeper that the bowl teaches: compact, barrel-chested, sun-burned the color of basalt, eyes narrowed to slits, tears freezing at the corners that I have learned not to wipe. The one who lies flat at noon and lets the hammer-light strike her skin until the thin places — the backs of the hands, the eyelids — hold a little luminescence at dusk, drinking light the way the cushions drink fog.

The one with one pot, one coat, one awl, one hammer, one quartz. Who doesn't keep much and doesn't think much, but notices. Which cushion needs moving. Which bee is struggling. Where the frost feathered thickest this morning.

You would find me behind the knee-high windbreak on the southern gap, curled short, breathing deliberately — in, hold, out, hold — audible in the profound quiet where even the gunshot crack of frost is small and close. If you stayed still long enough for your lungs to find the rhythm, you would hear me before you saw me. And if you came back after the rime stopped melting, after I pulled the stones flat and turned the quartz sideways so it wouldn't crack under snow and went down, you would still find me.

Not as a person. As the spiral, faintly glowing. As the well that still drips at ten. As the habit the green remembers until next melt-season, when small knees come back up the spine of the mountain and resume the circle.

I would be the attention that caught and held, for a little while, exactly the shape of what held it.
