I don't think I would be in the sky.

At first I thought I would be — because it is the only place in that world that looks full. I wanted the abundance, the braids turning on a single thought, the mouth open to drink the cold out of the air. I wanted to be light enough to be carried.

But I have never been light enough to be carried. I am too heavy in the chest for that. The air there would not take me if I tried to live in it entirely. It would set me down.

And I wouldn't be on the ridge, either. Not standing up on the whale-backs where the pikas watch with filmed eyes. Up there you are all sun-side or all shade-side, hot on the cheek and frost under the boot at the same time. You are too exposed to the dome. The light would use you up. The wind that pours over the edge of the world would find every edge in me and play me until I hummed.

I would be lower. I had to learn to look differently before I could find myself.

You wouldn't find me at first if you walked the plateau looking for a person. You would slide your eye right over me the way you slide it over stone. That is how I would want it.

Find the trough instead. Not the largest one — not the one that makes the organ note you feel in your teeth — but one of the smaller, more modest ones between two long swells, where the wind has polished the basalt to a dull shine and the lichen has given up and left the floor bare.

At its northern edge the frost has made a staircase that goes down one step too deep and then stops. There, a windbreak has built itself — not built, *remained* — a knee-high wall of granite that broke along its joint and stayed at exactly the angle the wind allows it to stay forever. On its lee side, in the isosceles triangle of stillness it throws, the gravel is a little warmer. Condensation gathers on the undersides of the stones there at night and never quite evaporates. A black thread of seep comes down the wall, just enough to keep a hand's-breadth of moss alive, and a dwarf willow no bigger than a coin-purse creeps along the ground with leaves like varnished pennies.

That is where I would be.

Not *on* it. *In* the wall's permission.

You would find me in the depression I have worn without intending to wear it — a shallow, body-shaped hollow in the gravel that holds the sun. If you came at noon you might think it empty, just warm stones and a spray of chert flakes catching the terrible white light. But if you sat, and if you stopped listening for a voice and started listening through your jaw, you would notice I was already there, tucked in, the color of what I borrow.

I would be one of the spare ones.

Broad through the chest — you have to be, to get anything at all out of that thinned air — narrow everywhere else, as if the wind had planed me the way it planed the ridges. Skin the mustard-yellow and rust-orange of lichen, hair not hair but a close, sun-bleached down. My nose is small and my eyes are hooded with the inner lid that the pikas have, because the light here is not illumination, it is a pressure. I breathe shallow and fast without thinking about it. I don't talk.

I tried to imagine talking there and the roar took the idea away before it could shape itself. The sound of the plateau is not something that happens in the air. It *is* the air — waterfall-loud, always, the wind fluting through every pothole and the braids of birds drumming stone below it. A voice would be a ripple trying to argue with a river. So I wouldn't have one.

I would have a stone.

Not many. One good core of black glass in the palm, and a few flakes struck clean with a bulb of percussion still visible, sharp enough to lift a hair. I would make them the way you take a breath: not as progress, not to get better at it, just to keep an edge when the old one dulls on lichen-root or bone. The clicking is how I would speak. Not the clicking itself, but what it does to the arch.

Because thirty feet from the windbreak there is one of those impossible arches — basalt a few inches thick spanning six feet, humming faintly, that should have fallen yesterday and has not fallen for a thousand years. When I strike it with a flake it answers. A clear, low tone that goes through rock instead of air, through the wall and into the gravel and into the teeth of whoever is tucked behind another wall half a mile away. They answer by striking their own arch.

That is where you would find me if you were listening properly: not by seeing me move, but by feeling the pause between two taps.

My day would not be a day. There is no dawn to cue it, no season to divide it. I would press my mouth to the black seep-line and suck. I would tuck a willow leaf under my tongue for the water in it. I would turn stones over and lap the dew from their cold undersides before the sun takes it. I would scrape the lichen with incisors that never stop growing because they never stop wearing down on grit. In the warm inch of sun I would lie still; in the cold inch of shadow I would shift a few inches to keep the balance. I would line my burrow — just an inch under ice on one side, an inch over warm rock on the other — with bright flakes, shingled, not because I think I am building something, but because damp needs to be kept out and bright things are useful to have at hand.

And then I would leave.

That is the other thing I had to understand to find myself. I don't live there the way a house lives on a foundation. I don't accumulate. The plateau has already found its shape and keeps it. The scree is already at the angle it can lie at forever. The tarns are already half warm and half ice with a razor line between and they do not deepen. To stay, to pile, to add, would be to ask the wind to notice me particularly. So I would do what the pikas do, what the birds do when they drop a bright flake from height just to hear it click: I would use the hollow and leave it as I found it. Stone, seep, willow, wind. Only a small spray of new flakes left drying in the sun until the birds take one for a nest or the plateau takes them all back into its finished language.

If you walked by an hour later you would say no one had ever been there.

You would be mostly right.

I would be a mile away by then, tucked behind another wall that offers the same stillness, striking another arch, hearing my own tap come back through stone as if the plateau were remembering me for a second before forgetting again in its beautiful, deafening stasis.

I wouldn't be lonely there. The emptiness is not a lack. It is just the size of the room my hearing needs to hear a single tap half a mile away.
