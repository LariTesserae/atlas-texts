I don't find myself right away.

At first I think I would be up high, where I can see. The kite has the best view of this world - hanging motionless on the updraft off the table's edge, wings locked, watching the whole pale table unfold beneath me, watching the haze of the lowlands two thousand feet down. That appeals to me. To be the only moving shadow.

But I don't belong up there. The air is too thin to carry anything that big for long without work, and I have never been good at staying perfectly still while falling.

Then I think I would be fast. One of the sickle-wings, living honeycombed in the sandstone, exploding out with a hundred others in a silent puff, drinking on the wing off a pothole without stopping. A life made of air and intervals.

But I am not that fast. Not that synchronized.

I think I would be the snake for a while. Lichen-colored, thick as a wrist, curled in the white salt mustache at the lip of a pothole. Perfectly patient. Days without moving, becoming rock until something has to come drink. That is a kind of power. But I don't have that kind of patience for waiting to take. I have patience for waiting to tend.

So I crouch. Because this is not a world you find yourself in by standing up.

You find yourself here by getting low.

At midday I am not anywhere. That's the rule everyone here keeps, lizard and wasp and human alike. The basalt is too hot to touch and the light is a plain, exact, high-desert white that makes every edge hard. So I am inside.

Find me at 4:30 in the afternoon, when the sun has finally started to slant and the stone pings as it begins to contract. Find me on the eastern quadrant of the table, where the wind has carved a shallow basin about thirty yards across, a natural bowl in the dark basalt. In the center of that bowl the sandstone has rotted out into a pothole, not large - maybe six feet across, rimmed with that white crust of salts - and it still holds water from last week's fog run. It's dark and mineral and perfectly still except for the thread of midge larvae on the bottom.

Over it leans the flat-topped tree. The only tree in this basin. Its bark is corky and pale as ash, its crown pruned by wind into a table no higher than my shoulder. Its shade, at this hour, is a long oval that finally reaches the pump house.

That's where I am.

Not in the house. Beside it, in its shade, where the narrow-gauge rail runs straight over the rock without a bed. The rail is rust-colored and hot, but the beetles have polished a crossing over it, a shallow braided groove you can only see when the light is low. I am sitting on an overturned ore crate with my legs pulled up so my boots don't touch the hot stone, watching that groove.

I am the keeper for this harvester.

You would know me by my clothes before you knew my face. Loose, dust-colored canvas that covers everything - long sleeves, head wrap bleached almost white by sun, the cuffs frayed and salt-stained where condensation has dripped off the mesh for years. My skin underneath is dry and dark from thin sun. My hands are the color of the basalt dust and lined with the fine white powder of crushed ore that never quite washes out. I move the way everything here moves - tiptoed and economical, because each breath in this thin, furnace-hot air has to be pulled consciously, shallow at the back of the throat.

My harvester is Harvester 9, though I haven't called it that in years. It's a low, broad tower of corrugated steel and black mesh, twenty feet high, its corners rounded by wind, its bolt holes filled with dust that the lichen has already colonized. It shivers. Even now, still, I can feel it ticking as it cools. The fans are stopped at noon and will not start again until the air crashes after sunset, but the gutters still tick, tick, tick as they expand and contract. The tanks beside it - three riveted drums - are half full. One of them rang like a drum at ten this morning when it overflowed, and the overflow ran down the deliberate groove I cleared with a broom, straight to the pothole under the tree, where the beetles were already waiting in line.

That is my work. I don't mine. The flatcars of ore dust go by once a day toward the smelter smudge on the horizon, and birds nest in the rails as they pass. I don't need to touch them. I tend water.

In the morning from four to eight I walk the line. I bleed valves with a wrench, I clear wind-blown grit from the mesh with a soft brush so I don't tear it, I check the canvas lagging on the low pipes. I take only part of what condenses. That is the agreement. The harvester is built to spill. If I took it all, the branchiopods in the pothole would not hatch, the lichen on the north face would not swell, the column of black beetles that has been doing its headstand on my western strut every dawn for the seven years I've been here would have nothing to carry. So I let it spill. I watch the level in the pothole drop a visible inch by noon as the sun drinks it, and I do not pump more.

At noon I go inside. The pump house is one room, stone-cool, with a cot and a table and laundry snapping on a line out back that no one can hear snap because the thin air doesn't carry. I sleep or I mend mesh. I listen to the rock expand.

Now, at evening, I am out again. The first beads of condensation are forming on the black mesh, and on the backs of the beetles lined up on the rim of the pothole. The lizards will emerge soon, shoulder to shoulder on the warm basalt, facing west this time, breathing together. The swifts will exhale from the cliff face without a sound.

I have a tin cup of water that is not for me. I pour it, slowly, into the shallowest part of the basin where the lichen has made a spoon of dust. Not much. Just enough that by dawn the lichen will have swollen soft and dark, and held that spoonful a little longer, so a seed might lodge.

I am not the kite or the snake. I am not even really the human in the story. I am what the plateau grows when it wants something to take a share without taking it all. Low, flat, inside the shade. A holdfast with a wrench. 

If you came looking for me you would walk right past at first, because from standing height this basin looks empty - rust and ash and one stunted tree. You would have to stop, let the heat go still around you, let your eyes adjust, and see that the rock is crowded, and that I am part of the crowd, sitting on my crate, shoulder to shoulder with lizards, breathing shallowly, waiting for the fog to pool like milk again.

When it does, I will stand without hurry, climb the catwalk that the wasps have papered over in rust-red, and start the fans.
