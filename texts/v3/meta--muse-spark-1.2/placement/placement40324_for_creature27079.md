I thought at first I would be the tree.

It's the obvious answer. If you have to be something in a place that small and hard, you want to be the one thing that is trying to be tall. That wrist-thick ailanthus at the north end with its six grimed leaves, wedged in the expansion joint. I would be vertical when everything else is horizontal. I would be the only thing that remembers what green is supposed to do.

But I wouldn't last there. Not as the tree. It's too exposed. Every photon that falls straight down that slot finds it, every vibration from the dye works finds its roots. It has nowhere to pull back to. It is all gesture, no reserve. And the light in that pocket is too honest - you can't hide being stunted when the sun makes a perfect white rectangle and drags it across you inch by inch.

So not the tree.

Then I thought maybe the wasps. Because they are the only ones who think the pocket is big. Everyone else knows exactly how small it is - the isopods never leave the damp stain, the spiders never leave their funnels, the moth flies never leave the lip of the drain. But the wasps make sorties. They hunt. They believe they have territory. They built a nest the size of a melon inside a humming electrical box and marbled it with turquoise plastic because they could afford to be decorative. To be a wasp there would be to feel, for a few seconds at a time, that you were free.

But I don't want to hunt. The pocket doesn't want hunting. It wants maintenance.

I crouched, in my head. Let my eye drop.

If you stay thirty seconds longer than you're supposed to, the floor moves. So I went low.

I would find myself not in the middle of the slab where that white rectangle burns at noon, but at the edge of it. Right where the condensation has been falling for years.

Every seven seconds, give or take. A cold pipe sweats, a bead swells on failing insulation, holds impossible long on the underside until a compressor kicks and shakes it loose, and it falls into the same pothole in the same dark stain. Tap.

I would be right there, on the rim of that stain. Where the concrete is always glossy and the tide line is.

Not the isopod, exactly. Not the beetle. Lower than that.

I would be the film they graze on.

The thing the description almost doesn't name because it's invisible until you look wrong at it - the thin, living sweat on the concrete itself. Algae and industrial yeast and soot-fed bacteria, a skin a few microns thick that makes the dark stain dark. That is what the carpet is farming. That is what makes the damp stay damp.

I would be that.

Or if you need a shape to see me, I would be the black mold in the corner just above it, where the floor meets the cinderblock and never dries. The one that isn't a stain at all up close, but a forest of tiny antlers a millimeter high.

That feels right, because it obeys the weird rules.

In the morning I lean away from the hard sun rectangle because it would bleach me. At night I lean toward the caged bulb over the welded door when it buzzes on, amber and dirty. I reach. A little. By dawn I've pulled back. Shy. I drink without roots. I drink the air the way that translucent weed with the straw-stems drinks the air. I drink the bead while it hangs.

You know the bead I mean - the one on the underside of the cold pipe that does not fall when it should. Fat, trembling, holding its own weight for hours. Gravity is less insistent there. In that pocket, water climbs as much as it falls. I would be the one reaching for it while it holds.

I wouldn't be singular. That's the point. Sweat doesn't live on skin as one animal. It lives as coverage.

If you touched the wall I live on, your hand would come away slightly wet and you would think you had left the wetness. But you hadn't. I was already there.

If you lifted the edge of the black pallet nearby - that collapsed sponge of wood that is mostly fungus now - you'd see white mycelium and then, underneath me, the isopods and springtails shoulder to shoulder, color of wet concrete, re-forming the seamless carpet the second you lay it back down. They would be walking on me. Farming me. I would be their sky and their field.

And above me, the grey-black sheet webs would be my shade. The spiders felt their silk with soot until it went opaque, not to trap but to filter. At noon they all flinch deeper into their funnels at once and the light on me softens. That is our agreement. I keep the concrete damp so the isopods can live, the isopods feed the beetles, the beetles feed the spiders, the spiders shade me so I don't dry out. The ants carry rust flakes past me like ore. The wasps take what the ants discard and make marbled paper.

Nothing leaves. Nothing needs to.

So you would find me exactly where the pocket finds its bottom. Half a meter below grade, against the east wall where the dye works exhales its warm, oily heat and the pipe sweats. Low. In the corner where condensation beads and doesn't fall. Where the handprint in the wet concrete from twenty years ago is still sharp.

I would not be the visitor. Not the rat watching from behind the pipe, not the pigeons peering down who never drop in because there isn't enough sky. I would not even be the human who stands there long enough to become another sweating pipe, though I love that one - the moment your hairline gathers sweat not from heat but from the air the place makes, and a beetle crosses your shoe without altering course because you are just a new, warm, vertical wall.

I would be what that human becomes part of.

I would be the sweat itself, learning to lean toward the light.
