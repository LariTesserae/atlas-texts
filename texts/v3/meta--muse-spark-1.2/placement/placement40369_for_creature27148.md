I don't find myself there right away.

At first I think I'm still on the rim.

That seems safest. Above it, looking down into it, with the wind at my back and my breath finally catching up to me. The observer. The one who came to see the pocket and hasn't decided to descend yet, because to descend feels like committing to its scale. From there I can hold the whole town in my vision the way you're supposed to — intimate, complete. I can be the chough for a minute, the black flick that cuts across the open top and does not land. I can admire the quiet without having to learn its grammar.

But the rim is too cold and too clean to stay on. The light is white and unfinished there. Nothing holds you. The wind scours and I would, after an hour, be leaning forward without meaning to.

So I go down.

For a while I think I am water.

Not the pools, but the seep itself — the black thread coming out of the basalt where the rock is still young and weeping. That would be honest work. To be pressure and heat from below finding the sharpest path down, beading, sheeting, spilling over a stone lip in a silent curtain to the next ledge. To be the system that the town has learned not to fight. To be guided without gutters, diverted around a roof without touching it. I like that — to be useful by moving the way I'm already going.

Then I think, no — I want to stay in one place a little longer. I want to be stayed.

Maybe I'm the filament.

In one of the warmer pools, the one that never quite skins over with ice, the hair-fine white branches under clear water. Not plant, not fungus. Mineral-fed. Sparse, scattered in patches that never connect. That feels familiar. Fed by seep, limited by cold, exactly as much as the pocket will allow and no more. Visible only if someone crouches and looks close and knows how to look close without breaking anything.

But I am too restless to be that still. I want to crouch and look, not be looked at.

So I become more myself.

Find me a third of the way down, not at the bottom where the downtown would be if this place believed in downtowns, and not high on the wall where the wind gets in. On a mid-terraced ledge, on the eastern side where the hard shaft of sun hits for twenty minutes in the afternoon and then moves on.

There is a low, broad building there, one of the ones cast to look like wet basalt until you catch it at an angle and it pearls. It is not a dwelling, not really, and not a workshop either. It is between. Inside, a single room that holds heat without fire and light without a source. A table of the same mineral-composite, eased at the corners as if it grew there. On it, shallow trays of water from different ledges.

That is where I am.

I am one of the sparse ones, but even among the sparse ones I am the sparser kind. The one who doesn't cross to the workshop when others do. The one people see infrequently and nod to from the distance of a building, and understand that distance as care.

I have let the pocket shape me the way it shapes everyone who stays. Narrowed, not frail. Pale in the way you get from living under straight-down light and your own walls' glow, cheeks always a little red at the tips. Hair cut short so it doesn't drip against the neck. Clothes the same matte weave as the walls that bead water off the shoulders without a sound. I breathe the way you have to breathe here — the longer inhale, the pause, the plume that holds — and I've stopped fighting the unfinished feeling. I've started to like it. It makes you be deliberate.

My work is tending, but of the smallest kind.

In the morning I walk the circumference. Not the whole bowl — just my quarter of it. Slowly enough that the field can read my passage. I notice where frost has bloomed overnight and where a millimeter of sharp basalt has flaked away and revealed a new edge. I don't touch it. I note it.

In the afternoon, when condensation starts to run again and the tick and trickle gets a little louder, I kneel by the warm pool on my ledge. The one with the filaments. I watch the paper-thin skin of ice try to form and fail. I take the thin tool like brushed glass and lift, without breaking, a sample no bigger than an eyelash, to see how the white branches have grown since yesterday. Sometimes they haven't. Sometimes they have, by a fraction that only matters if you are paying attention for years.

I speak rarely. When I do, it's low and with few words because the wet air swallows sound and shouting would be obscene. I know the lexicon of hands: palm out — *go on, I'm fine* — two fingers to the rim — *this one's warm today* — a tilt of the head that means *come look* without saying it. Mostly I don't need to say anything at all.

I don't gather. I don't fill. I keep the distances right.

At night, when the sun has left the floor and the buildings give their soft light back, I sit in my room and listen to the town regulate itself — valve shifting, field adjusting, ice forming and being sublimated away — and to my own breathing falling into that same slow, spare, continuous rhythm. I have a plain bowl. Steam that dissipates faster than breath.

If you stood on the rim for a long hour and mistook the town for empty, you would miss me entirely. I am the color of wet stone when I stand still. I only flash that faint oil-slick rainbow when I move.

And when the chough cuts across the open top, black against the sky and not descending, I look up for a second the way we all do, track it, and then look back down at the pool, at the wall, at the next small curtain of water finding the next ledge.

I am not lonely there. I am contained, completely and without crowding.

I am there to not get in the way.
