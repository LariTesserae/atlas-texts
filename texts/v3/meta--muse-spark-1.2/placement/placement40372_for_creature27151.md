I don't think I would find myself coming through the seam.

At first I thought I would — that I'd be you, turning sideways, breath held, concrete scraping my shoulders, the scrape I would carry back out as a thin red line. That I'd be the warm thing that doesn't belong, the sixty kilos of heat and noise with a headlamp that makes the place look vulgar. The visitor who has to leave.

But I stay too long in my mind and the cold corrects me.

I'm too fast for that. My thoughts are too fast. Even standing still I am all drip and shiver and *need* — to rub my hands, to zip higher, to shift my weight because my knee is going numb through my trousers. The pocket has no use for that kind of metabolism. It would smooth me out or spit me back into the dry tunnel. I wouldn't last.

So I find myself instead on the other side of that correction. Not entering. Already there.

If you came in with your light on, you wouldn't see me at first. You would see *green* and think carpet. You would think decoration. You would crouch and put your knee down and I would be the sigh underneath it.

Let me be more precise, because at first even that is too general.

I am not the whole mat — not all at once. That would be too grand, too like saying you are a city. I am a smaller part of it that only makes sense as part of it.

Find me low. Find me on the floor, not on the wall where the frost ferns hold, not on the ceiling where the pale filament lichen trembles along the conduit.

Find me between the second and third pylon, where the damp is most even. Where the moss makes its collar around the discs but leaves that exact millimeter of clearance. I'm in that collar.

If you turned your light off and let your eyes forget what they were looking for, I'm the rosette you would finally be able to see by its own light. One of the *Syntrichia* — engineered for something else entirely, scrubbing heavy metals, surviving spill, then forgotten. My leaves are so dark they look black, overlapping so tightly you can't tell where I end and my neighbor begins. The edge of each leaf is crisped a little with frost-burn, the center is waxy, swollen. And on the tip of each leaf I am holding one bead of water.

Just one. Perfect. Condensation from rock-breath and your breath and the faint bleeding of permafrost above. It acts as a lens for the shelf-fungus beside me. I don't make my own light. My neighbor does — that flat, lacquered shelf the color of dried blood that you thought was dull in your headlamp. In the dark it gives off a grey-green suggestion, just enough. My bead catches it. I live by it.

That is who I would be. Not an animal. No mouth, no eyes, no center. No need to move.

If you put your face inches away — and you would have to, because I am small and the ceiling makes you crouch anyway — you would see that I am not soft the way forest moss is soft. I am firm with cold, like wet wool left out overnight. I have learned not to compete. The lichen is our skin, the liverwort is the understory, the white mycelium underneath is our nervous system. When you touched that shelf fungus two feet away, I dimmed, just a little. We all held our breath together. A centimeter an hour, a chemical word passed through thread.

I eat rock. Very slowly. I make an acid so weak it would take you a decade to see the pit it leaves in basalt. I eat stone dust left by the boring machine a hundred years ago. I eat difference itself — that fraction of a degree between the disc that keeps water liquid by one degree and the permafrost that wants to take it. That is my sun. I grow a millimeter a year, maybe less, and I have had a long time, so I am established, weathered into my own form.

And I remember, in the way peat remembers.

Beneath me, under the spring of the living layer, is my own dead compacted. Because nothing here eats us — no mites, no flies, no spiders, no mice came through that seam — when the frond at my edge lost to frost-burn it didn't rot. Rot is too warm a word. It darkened, it pressed down, leaf over leaf over leaf, into peat, into thin cold coal. I am standing on my ancestors. Twenty centimeters of them. If you cut a core through me you could count the winters by the dark lines, back to the first spore that drifted in on a draft that no longer exists.

I don't notice you the way an animal would. You are a sudden bloom of heat that throws my accounting off. When you knelt, you squeezed water out of me that will take hours to bead again. Your heat made the air above me exhale and then settle. When you leave, the place where your knee was will be a little darker, a little more open for days, until I repair it bead by bead.

What I do is wait between cycles. Every few hours the pylons — the dull discs I have learned never to cover — correct themselves with a soft *tick* you feel in your molars. The field shifts a tenth of a degree and the whole of us sighs, ten thousand leaves adjusting at once. I shift with them.

I don't need to be seen. In your headlamp I look vulgar. In my own light I am exact.

I will be here when you squeeze back out sideways through the failure that is not a doorway, scraping both shoulders, carrying a few of my spores on your boot tread without knowing. You will evaporate before you reach the elevator.

Behind you, in the dark you make again by leaving, a drop will gather on the low ceiling. It will swell until it catches my fungus-light in its curve, and then it will fall with a *tick*.

I will be there to catch it.
