I don't find myself right away.

At first, when I come over the lip and the wind hits me and the bowl opens, I think I am still the person who climbed. I am standing. I am at the edge where the grass gives way to nothing, where the veil of water goes over and shreds. That's where a person would stand — to look out at the white-blue gulf, to have the whole range at eye level, to feel very high and very alone and to check that feeling against the view.

But I can't stay there. The wind that circles doesn't let you stand on the edge for long. It pushes you in. It funnels and turns, and everything in the bowl turns with it — the choughs, the mist, the midges. If you stand on the lip you are perpendicular to the motion. So I move without deciding to, down a step, then another, until my boots are in the deep moss and they don't quite dry.

For a while I think I would be one of the easy answers.

I would be a pika, because I like the interval — one sharp whistle and then a long, honest silence of stone before the answer comes forty meters away. I would be good at that. Finding a rock with a good echo and keeping it.

Or I would be one of the swallows that cuts the insect column at dusk. One bright stitch through empty air, and then the whole sky empty again. That feels right for a little while, because everything alive here is spaced so far apart and I have always wanted to be less crowded.

Or I would be the dwarf willow. Not higher than a knee and decades old, twisted flat to the ground, holding on. That, too, feels true.

But if I stay, with my back against one of the sitting-polished stones where the soot has sunk into the rock from a hundred small fires, and I keep still past noon, past the hour when the sun clears the eastern wall and the droplets hang too long — my eye starts to sort the motion correctly.

And I stop looking for what flies or whistles.

I look low. Where the color doesn't change. Where you think there is only moss.

I would not be the visitor. Visitors leave before the snow seals the lid. They have to. Their lungs don't make weather.

I find myself lower down, on the second shelf where the two braid-runnels join. Not the wettest terrace, not the driest. The middle one. The place you wouldn't notice unless you were looking for where water needs help.

I am crouched there. I have always been crouched there, it feels like — even when I was standing on the lip a minute ago, this crouch was waiting underneath.

I am small. Old-small. My shoulders are broad enough to fit between boulders, my hips narrow enough to settle into a peat hollow that has held my shape for years. My hands are too large. That is the first thing I notice about myself — the nails have widened and softened into little dark spades, good for lifting sphagnum without tearing it, good for easing a stone a finger's width downslope over a week so the eddy behind it shifts.

My scalp is cool. When I touch it I feel not hair but haircap moss, thick and damp, and further down my forearms the rust and bone lichen has taken. It itches a little, the way any living thing itches when it grows on you. A glacier midge walks through it and I don't brush it away. A bumblebee once did, and I didn't stir. The bees know the difference between a tussock and me and also that there is no difference worth fussing over.

I am not alone but I am spaced. There is one of us on the tier above, one on the tier below. We keep the interval the bowl requires. One bee, then a long emptiness of air. One pika call, then a long emptiness of stone. One of us, then a long stretch of moss where there is no one. We don't like to be seen together. It makes it look, to a visitor, like there is only one of us that moves very quickly. That is polite. It keeps the room feeling empty in the way it wants to feel empty.

What I do is small enough to look like nothing if you only visit for an afternoon.

I tend the dew.

I crouch at the foot of the weeping seam where the basalt sweats and watch the bead form. The air around my head is different — when I breathe out it is cool and too wet, almost fog, and the dew point drops for a hand's breadth around my face. I can make water form from nothing onto wool, onto stone, onto my own shoulders. I watch the droplet swell, hesitate, catch that late, wrong light where it seems to drift upward with the circling wind. Then I coax it with a fingertip or a flat slate shard. I keep the channel open. I don't scar the rock.

Then I carry it. Not far. A handful of saturated sphagnum from the weeping wall pressed into a drier cushion of star-moss on the next terrace. A scrape with my nail-spades to deepen the undercut of a pool by a millimeter, so it holds shadow longer. The water I don't need to drink from a cup. I kneel at the runnel braid and press my mouth — wide, fringed — to the peat and sip through the moss. *Sip. Sip. Sip.* At dusk you could hear it if you were close enough, between the ticking of the rocks.

I speak rarely. When I do it is in the bowl's own language because that is the only language that doesn't disturb the interval. A sharp whistle that means *here, water.* An answer comes from forty meters away: *here, I hear.* Sometimes at evening I make the chough's *kee-ah* perfectly and the real choughs answer from the wind and we all pretend for a moment we are the same thing.

My shrine is the little slate cairn. It is not an offering *to* me. It is my shelf. Inside it is my wooden spoon, blackened with use, and the coil of wool gone grey that I keep damp so it won't rot. I added a flat stone to it this morning, carried for an hour from the east wall, set precisely where the drip will keep its underside wet. The little carved marmot is there too, ears worn smooth. I turn it over in my big hands when the mist comes in thick and there's nothing to do but wait and breathe.

I think I was a person once, held over for a summer that never quite ended. Or I think the bowl grew me because a room that holds that much water and that much circling air eventually needs something to keep its motion from spilling over the lip all at once. Both feel true in the way moss and stone both feel true at the same time here.

I will not stop you if you leave. I will lift my head from the runnel and look at you with eyes that have the same second, calmer skin as the pools — they reflect the cliffs even while ruffled. You will think I am watching you. I am also watching the droplet behind you that is just about to fall.

When the snow comes, I will not go down the switched-back scar through scree and stunted pine. I will sink. I have a hollow between two sleeping boulders, half-sunk in turf, where the peat fits me. I will pull it over like a quilt, tuck my lichen beard, and slow until a heartbeat is minutes apart, breathing out so little that the mists above me freeze into a low white shroud. The mountain will hold its breath with me.

Until then, I am on the second terrace, moving water a droplet at a time, keeping the room from drying out. You would find me there if you stayed long enough and got close enough and were still enough to be not worth hiding from.

You have already passed me once, thinking I was only more moss.
