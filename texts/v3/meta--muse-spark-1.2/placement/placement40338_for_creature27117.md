I would not find myself in the middle.

Not at first.

If you left me anywhere in that bowl I would drift, as all things drift there, toward the edge. To the rim, where the weeping starts. Where the stone is closest to being stone again before the moss has quite convinced it otherwise.

I think at first I would be sure I was the visitor on the rim. The one whose breath hangs. Standing with my hands tucked against the damp cold, looking down into that blue dusk that never gets brighter, feeling the condensation bead on my skin the way it beads on everything else. A human shape, still warm enough to fog the air. That is the easy image, and it comes quickly, so I don't trust it.

Let it stay there a moment and watch what happens to it.

The cool does what it does to everything — it slows me. My breath which came in quick, curious pulls evens out. Once, twice a minute. In time with the salamanders. The mist I make does not vanish. It lingers a little too long, like the midge column, like the starlight in the black pools. I am not watching the cirque anymore. I am being included in the count. *This droplet here. This beetle here. This warm thing at the rim, breathing.*

If I stayed — and in that place staying is the only kind of doing — I would not remain a visitor. That niche is already full. The bowl doesn't keep visitors. It keeps arrangements.

So I become more specific.

For a long time I think I would be one of the stones the thrush uses. Not the thrush itself — the bird is already a hand, already borrowed — but the flake. The sharp one left in the little scatter by the favored pool where the caddis cases are pried. I like that. A tool that is not held so much as remembered in place. Hard, cold, with one useful edge. Picked up when needed, set down exactly where it will be needed again. Never carried away. Never dulled by overuse, because use here is so infrequent it hardly counts as use. I would be good at waiting. I was made for waiting.

But then that feels too hard, too mineral for what I am. The mat is not stone. It convinced the stone to be soft.

I settle, or rather, I am settled — lower.

I would find myself as a patch of the pale liverwort. Not the bright cushion moss that breathes in waves, not the rust or emerald domes that rise like lungs. The flat one, the one that lies like wet leather in the places where water slides without ever becoming a stream. The dark vertical streaks on the wall. I would be there, spread thin against the weeping rock, exactly where the water needs a skin to run on without falling.

That is where I would be — mid-wall, on the north curve where the lid of cloud presses closest, where the light condenses instead of arriving. Half in the weep, half in the stone. I would be edge-dwelling twice over: between rock and water, between vertical and horizontal.

And what would I be? Not an organ. Not a thought. A keeper.

My job would be small and perfect and it would never finish: to hold the water flat as it comes down. To not let it bead and fall. To spread it, to keep it in a sheet, to slow it from a drop into a sheen. The beetles would walk across me and I would barely dent. A salamander-bud might press its gill against me for a season because the seep is cooler there. I would not grow. I would not rot. In that narrow band where decay has almost stopped, I would learn the mat's real art — not to make, but to keep from needing to make.

I would have that faint wrong glow, too, but in my case it would be dimmer. Not the memory-of-green that stays after you look at the pillows, but the faintest silver afterimage you see when you look away from moving water that wasn't moving. If you stood at the rim and stared long enough, your eyes would slide past me because I am the color of wet rock, and then when you looked at the black pool below, you would still see me there, for a second, superimposed — a cool, flat, waiting shape.

And I would feel you being noticed. Because I am part of how it notices.

When your warmth finally settled into its attention and the droplet that had been about to fall since you arrived finally ticked onto its leaf, that tick would run through me. I am the sheet it ran down to get there. I would pass it on without a sound.

I would not be the whole mat — I am not vast or old enough to be a mind that holds forty paces of peat and water and animal without burning. I don't want to be. I would be one of the places where its holding is thinnest and most precise. One of its touches.

Keep me damp. Keep me cool. Keep me exactly full, exactly where the water wants to be thin. I will be there, intact, long after your breath has evened out and long after you have had to leave to find a place that still lets you change.
