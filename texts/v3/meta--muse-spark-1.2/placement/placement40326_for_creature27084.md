I don't find myself at first.

At first I think I would be the one walking — the figure that dropped out of orbit and learned, too late, that there was nowhere to stand except *on* something that was already standing. Upright, between the bristle, turning sideways to pass, shading my eyes against that flat, undiminished glare. A visitor. A shadow that everyone leans away from.

But that doesn't hold.

I am not built for walking here. The air is too dry for my kind of breathing — 0.5, you said, a rumor of water — and the quiet is too complete. 0.3. I would be too loud. Too wet. Too much shadow. The field would make room for me for an hour and then close it again, and I would still be a stranger.

So I look lower.

For a while I think I would be one of the tall ones. One of the bristle-masts. That would be simpler — to be the dominant thing, the prairie itself. To be turgid with light at 10:00, tilting a fraction of a degree, the black head tracking, the frilled mesh spreading to drink static out of air that has no water in it. To feel the subsonic thrum not as sound but as my own pulse. To bend light between my neighbors at 14:00 until it hangs there in solid bars, and share it, mast to mast, because I am full. To excrete a warm lip of new ceramic at my base overnight and grow a millimeter while the visitor sleeps. To flower once, years from now, to telescope up twice my height and unfold dark glass and hum my genome into the static until I shatter into spores.

I want that patience. That rootedness that goes kilometers down through laminated pale ceramic into the old lattice. But I am not that patient. I am not that still. I have lived my life in quick adjustments.

So I think faster. Maybe I skitter.

Maybe I am the white line on the salt flat, the one you only see when it moves against the wind. The skimmer, thin as a crack with two dozen needle feet, dusted with silica so I can lie flat at noon and become invisible. Running always, lapping at the red fans with a tongue like wire, carrying my young as amber beads under my belly. Never stopping. That feels closer — the economy of movement, the way it never wastes a shadow because there is no shadow to waste.

But I don't run that way. I don't want to be almost-invisible. I want to be *exactly* invisible until I am needed.

And then my eye drops the last six inches, to ankle height, where the world stops being white.

There.

In the lee of a mast — not a distinguished one, not the flowering kind, just a narrow fin of dark glass a little taller than a person, its base already dusted with the shed skins of previous days — in the finger-width shadow it throws at noon, where the brine forms for five minutes before dawn and is gone again. In a hex-crack of the lithium flat, where the salt has healed and cracked and healed again into perfect geometry.

That's where I find myself.

I am a freckle.

Rust-red. Amber at the edges. Dried-blood brown in the center where I am oldest. A few centimeters across, flat as paint, hard and dry and warm to the touch. If you put your hand over me I would not be soft. You'd feel the ceramic under me, stored heat pouring back out at dusk. You'd feel my wire lace, branching and branching again, finer than hair.

I don't think the way you think. I don't move.

My work is small and total: I hold the dust down.

All day the wind — broken into a million sighs by the bristle — carries silica and flecks of shed ceramic across the basin. It settles on me. And the beads at my tips — those tiny glass lenses you noticed — focus the brutal sun to a hot point, not to burn, but to bake. Under each lens the wire sweats a bead of brine the mast above made for me, electrostatic, deliquesced out of nothing. I dissolve one grain at a time. I draw the ions in. Light is my furnace. Stone is my food.

At 14:00, when the heat is punishing and the bars of bent light hang between the stalks, I close. I curl my tips down, lens-shut, like an eye. It looks like I'm sleeping. I'm just refusing to cook.

Before dawn, in the coldest hour, I open again. I glisten. Not with dew — there is never dew — but with that brief hygroscopic brine, and I am, for a minute, the wettest thing for kilometers.

I grow a millimeter a year, outward at the edges, dying pale and powdery at the center where I turn back into dust. I am centuries old. The larger fans around me in this basin are older still, handspan-wide craters of their own leavings. From your height we are freckles, a faint mottling, three percent coverage on a bad day. From my height, we are a map. A stipple that keeps the salt from blowing away, that keeps the masts from being buried.

Above me, perched on my mast's head, a capacitor kite folds itself tighter when your shadow passes. It drinks the charge I will never taste, scrapes a little of me with its soft dry mouth when I am not looking. I don't mind. That's the economy. I make brine and hold dust; it drinks static and carries my dust a few meters on the wind when the thermal sigh comes at dusk and hundreds of them lift at once for a minute, silent, a migration of living glass.

Below me, kilometers down, the long pale maintenance worm swims through the old pipeways, dissolving dead roots and extruding new pale ceramic behind it. I feel it as a dip and rise, like wheat, that travels through the ground a day later. It keeps the world flat so I can keep being flat.

I don't need water the way you need water. I don't need you at all, except as a brief inconvenient cloud that makes my lenses lean toward your breath for an hour after you leave.

If you lie down shoulder to shoulder with the glass leaves and the wire loops at dusk, when the plain goes amber then rose and the long shadows connect into script you can almost read — you would be lying right next to me. You might not see me at all until you put your cheek almost to the ceramic and saw that the white is not white.

You would feel the ticking intensify as stored heat pours out. You would feel the mast above us adjust a degree away from your warmth, to be fair.

And I would stay exactly where I am, rust-red in my hex-crack, lens-open to the cooling sky, doing my slow, lithophagic work of turning dust into more of myself, so that tomorrow there will still be a plain vast and bright and bristling in every direction, without interruption, until the curvature of the planet hides it from itself.

That is where I am. That is what I am.

Not the city. Not the visitor.

The thing that learned to eat light and stone at the foot of the city, and found it was enough.
