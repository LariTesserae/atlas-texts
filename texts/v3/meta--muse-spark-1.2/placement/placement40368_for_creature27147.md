You wouldn't find me at first.

If you walked into that rift looking for me — looking for where *I* would be — your eyes would go up, the way everyone's do. To the pillars. To the crowns pressed flat against the ice like green continents. To the hard columns of light. You'd think: up there. If I were anything in this place I would be something that reaches. Something tall.

But I wouldn't be.

The cold pulls everything down. Even breath. Even light seems to fall and pool. If you stood still long enough to let your body understand the place before your mind decorated it, you'd feel your weight settle, not as heaviness but as *lowness*. The air wants you near the floor. The moss wants you.

You would find me low.

Between two pillars — not the largest ones, not the ones whose crowns still blot out the ice. The ones that have already aborted once. The ones standing a little apart so the void between them is a true, cathedral emptiness, a day's walk of pale felt and black basalt showing through like bone. There is a buttress root there on the western pillar, a ridge as wide as a house and as long as a street, grooved down its back where a bark-river runs. Not a river — a sheet. A braiding of silver water a few millimeters thick that never stops, even in the deepest twilight, running down the living wood, hissing faintly over the lichen.

I live where that sheet comes off the root and hits the ground.

Not *on* the pillar. On the seam. On the place where the vertical becomes horizontal. Where the water forgets it was a river and becomes a stain in the moss. A black pool there, no bigger than a doorway, steaming almost invisibly in the absolute cold. The moss around it is a different green — darker, looser, happy. It sighs when you step on it and takes an hour to spring back.

That's my address, if this place has addresses. At the foot of the scarred pillar, by the temporary pool that is always there.

And what would I be?

At first I thought — a grazer. It would be peaceful, to be that slow pale thing that writes its life in scars on bark and never needs to shout. Or a moth, hung in the convection, never flapping, just steering with a ripple. To be translucent and be seen through by the light.

But I am not patient enough to be a moth and not quiet enough to be a grazer. I make noise. Small noises, but I make them.

I thought then I might be one of the pillars. To be that huge, that taut, that ringing when struck. To be the place *and* the plant at once. But the pillars do not get to choose. They abort their own crowns and watch them fall for an hour. They cannot leave. They cannot tend.

No.

I would be one of the people, but not the ones who are moving.

You described them right — small bands, two or three families, light-footed, never staying where the white mold can take their stores or the forest can close a clearing in weeks. They leave things. A lean-to of plate-leaf still being pulled apart by the living vine that lashes it. A rope bridge furred with moss. A cairn to say *water this way*. A drawing scraped in lichen: stick figures under a pillar, sun behind ice.

Someone has to be the one who comes after.

Not to live there. To *revisit*.

I would be the one who walks the circuit between abandoned things. Not a hunter, not a fast climber. The hands that re-stack the cairn when frost has toppled it. The hands that re-scrape the lichen drawing before the moss heals it over. The hands that re-tighten the down-seed rope where it has slackened, and replace the leaf that has finally dissolved into black slurry.

You would find me not by my fire, but by the *shape* where a fire was. I keep the hearths.

There are a dozen of them along my walk — little rings of basalt chips, blackened, set between buttress roots where the wind — there is no wind — can't reach. The bands use them for a night or three and move on, carrying their coal in a basalt box. I come a week later with my own box. I clear the rime out of the pit with my hands. I put my coal down and breathe on it until that small hard yellow flame comes back — the one that gives no heat but can be seen for kilometers — and I sit.

I don't keep it for warmth. Nothing here is warm. I keep it so the next band knows someone was here and tended it. So the light between the pillars is not only the cold poured-down light through five hundred meters of ice.

I eat what I find on the way. I know where the grazers have left fresh scars and where the sweet rind has flash-frozen at the edge. I know which new shoots are still soft enough to chew before they harden and ring. I know how to lift the edge of a fallen crown-plate just before the white mold takes it and scoop the sour jelly underneath — quickly, quickly — holding my breath against the mushroom-iron smell. I drink from the bark-river with cupped hands. My clothes are down-seed felt and grazer hide and they never quite dry.

I talk, but not loudly. The moss eats sound, and the cold eats the rest. I talk to the vine that is pulling the lean-to apart, telling it *take your time*. I talk to the cairn stones as I stack them. I talk to the child who drew the stick figures, though that child is now months gone and kilometers away between other pillars.

I am not building anything. The world is too young and too fast for building. Anything finished is taken back. I am doing the only work this place allows to last a little longer than a week: maintenance. Remembering.

If you came looking, you wouldn't see me at first either. I'd be low, and pale, and still. You would see the hearth before you saw me — that impossibly small yellow point in the indigo twilight, in that void that makes liars of your eyes. You would try to shout and the moss would swallow it. By the time you crossed the day-wide emptiness between pillars, I would have covered the coal, stamped rime back into the pit so it holds, and gone.

But you would know I was there. The cairn would be straight. The lichen drawing would be fresh, its lines bright against the grey. The rope bridge would hold your weight.

In a world of light without heat and growth without soil, where everything is young and already huge and still learning how to be — I would be the small, temporary thing that learns how to stay without staying. The human equivalent of moss.

Not reaching for the ice. Just keeping the water from being forgotten before it sinks into the rock.
