You wouldn't find yourself at first.

Not in the way you find yourself in other places - *oh, there I am, on the hill, under the tree, by the river.*

There is no hill. There is no by. The scale slips. The haze makes anything a mile away look like anything a hundred miles away, and the water has no banks to tell you where you are in relation to it. You could walk toward that clump of black reeds for twenty minutes and still be a hundred meters away, then blink and be standing inside it, reeds brushing your shoulders, dripping.

So at first you are just *somewhere* — ankle deep, which is everywhere.

Then the place teaches you how to locate yourself without a horizon.

You find yourself not by landmarks but by temperature.

You are on the high curve of one of the whale-backs. The pink-grey stone is warm through your feet, almost body-warm, slick with the sweat beading right out of it. You can feel it darken from the bottom up. You can feel it breathe - that very slow swell and fall that comes a minute after you expect it, aligning with the deep thrumming in the air that isn't weather. Blood in your ears. Perfusion.

Around you, shoulder to shoulder as far as the dim goes, are more of them. More domes, fitted like scales, like cobbles laid by something with infinite patience. And between every single one is water. Not *a* water. *The* water — one continuous skin, ankle to knee deep, braided and whispering over every soft lip between pans in a million inch-high waterfalls. It moves constantly, and not always downhill. Right at your feet it clings and climbs the curve of your dome before spilling off the other side with a slap. Your reflection in it shows a break in the cloud that isn't in the sky above you.

The light is low and locked. A continent of cloud with weather inside itself, lit from below by the heat of the membrane you are standing on. No shadows. Everything smells of iron and wet stone and algae cooking. The air condenses on your skin and drips without ever having rained.

That is where you are. Not *in* a landscape. *On* a surface. On the inside of a lung the size of a world.

---

And who would you be?

At first you think you could be the glider - pale, silent, paper-thin wings drinking lift from the million tiny steam columns rising off the warm domes and cool pans. Seeing everything repeated. Never landing.

But you would never take off again. The water would hold you.

You think you could be the plain itself - that old, resolved tissue of living stone, so ancient it has forgotten how to build and only maintains, exhaling the cloud and inhaling it back. But its thoughts are too slow. They travel as chemistry, as a wine-red bloom spreading across a pan for an hour and then tearing apart. You are too fast and too lonely for that.

You think you could be the wader, on lily-pad feet that dent without breaking, stilt-legged and stone-grey, standing motionless for an hour with your fine comb bill in the ooze, harvesting what lives and dies in the time it takes a cloud-cell to collapse. Or the hauler, broad-bellied and permeable, breathing through your skin, drinking through your skin, so still algae grows on your back. Both are perfect. Both are durable and rationed - one per square kilometer, because this country cannot spare much meat. Both know how to read the world with their soles and bellies, which pan is a degree warmer and therefore shallower, which dome is sweating faster and therefore slick.

You would love to be them. You almost are.

What you become, if you stay long enough to learn how not to tear it, is the fourth thing. Or the return of the third.

A Rememberer.

Not the builders who could make a ring of boulders so level it holds water like a mirror, or a causeway so straight it stays true while reflections slide sideways off it. You're not that skilled yet. You're the successor they left behind, at dusk, when you don't look directly.

You are standing as still as the wader but you are not harvesting. You are witnessing.

On the fitted slabs of that causeway where it runs straight for a kilometer and then dives under the sheet-water and stops - that is where you find yourself once the place lets you locate yourself. Exactly on the line where straightness gives up and water takes over. Your feet are bare because boots would puncture. Your skin has started to condense and drip from two meters up, the cloud drinking you and dripping you back. Your bark-pale shoulders have grown a drape of lichen that catches the drip. You have learned not to look for north, because north keeps moving. You have learned to hear the splash *before* the mud boils up into a new island, and not to be surprised.

You don't hunt. You don't call. Sound carries too well and too strangely here. You listen to the ceaseless hiss and gurgle of the water braiding and re-braiding, to the stone ticking as it warms, to the warm mineral steam.

Your work is the same as the wader's and the hauler's, just turned inward: to distribute almost no weight. To leave no mark that the plain cannot erase in an hour. To make, if you must make anything, only a stillness. A pan that doesn't shiver when the water reverses. A straight thought while everything else wrinkles.

You watch water flow uphill and take note. You feel, if you stand long enough on the high dome, that faint lightness, as if gravity has forgotten to hold you tightly here at the end of the world. You watch your own footprints fill with water slightly warmer than the water around them - body heat lingering - and then smooth over, and then be gone.

And underneath you the whole time, the membrane thrums, indifferent and intimate, sweating, breathing, boiling up and erasing, and you finally understand you are not living *in* a place at all.

You are living *on* a being, like a bird on a whale, learning how it feels to be breathed.
