I don't think I would find myself at first.

If you dropped me into the bowl, my first instinct would be to look for a vantage. To be the heron on the rim. High up, where the basalt is still knife-edged, looking down at the whole honeycomb with those judge's eyes. Able to see the pattern. Able to be apart.

But the air is too heavy for that. It sits cool on your skin and in your lungs, iron-cold and damp, and it pushes you down. And the bowl eats sound and distance. From the rim you would shout and no one would hear you after three paces. You can't be a judge here. The mist that catches and spills over the edge would bead on your feathers until you were too wet to stay high.

So I come down.

At first I thought I would be a water-tender. I like the circuit, the slowness. The idea of walking not much faster than the water itself, fingertip testing the flow, dragging a twig through the black rock flour where it has fanned in the channel. But my hands are too restless for that. I would want to fix it, to solve it, and the water here doesn't want solving. Sometimes it runs uphill for a hand's span. Sometimes it hangs trembling on a vertical face, a lens that refuses to fall, and the tenders just wait. They touch it gently to remind it where down is. I would press too hard.

Then I thought I would be a child. Because the children are the only ones who use the density the way it wants to be used. They don't run in circles, they run in levels. Roof to plank to balcony to notched-log stair to sheet-water with a splash that someone will softly scold. They know the map by foot-feel, which plank bounces, which causeway stone is slick. I love that knowledge. But I am too old for that kind of vertical running. Too aware of falling on the sharp scree, on the clinking plates of talus you wouldn't want to fall on.

It takes a while for the bowl to tell me where I fit. You have to stand still long enough for the ticking to find you.

I find myself not in a house. There are no houses that are only houses — every roof is someone else's yard, every wall is someone else's wall. I find myself in the gap that was never meant to be a gap. The alley that is only shoulder-wide. The place between elbows.

I would be on the second tier, where one terrace steps down to the next. Where the laundry lines crisscross over the ankle-deep sheet-water below and the smoke from the tier above tries to rise and instead flattens and drifts sideways through the perpetual mist.

My spot is where a low dry-stack course meets a wattle-and-daub upper wall. The lower stones are set without mortar on purpose, with a little give. The upper plaster is already hairline-cracked because last night the young rock ticked and settled a millimeter deeper. You feel it through your bare feet before you hear it.

That's where I am. Barefoot, even though the water is cold enough to ache. My soles are white and soft at the edges from the wet, calloused in the middle. I need to feel it. That's my job.

I think I would be a stone-listener, but not the one with the hammer. I would be the one who listens without tapping.

I have a small wooden mallet, fist-sized, and I rarely use it. Mostly I sit. My wool is undyed, grey and black, fulled to felt and oiled with fat so the mist beads and runs off instead of soaking through. It smells of wet wool and woodsmoke that never quite rises and the iron tang of rock flour that gets under your nails no matter how short you trim them. My trousers are hitched high for wading. My hair is always a little lank, my knuckles a little pruned.

I put my palm on the wall. The basalt weeps behind it, a slow bleed from an invisible seam. Drip. Tick off the eave into the barrel below.

People pass me crabwise, shoulders tucked, elbows in. An old woman with a bag of rock flour for chinking. A girl carrying a dun damp-hen that will lay one pale speckled egg in the thatch tonight. A boy with a bundle of willow withies, every twig harvested from the pollarded fists on the north ledge.

No one announces anything. The bowl swallows a loud voice. If a channel is silted, you kneel and clear it. If a stone has slipped, you reset it because your neighbor's wall is also your wall. So when I feel the hollow — the little give where a shim has worked loose and the course above is no longer sitting true — I don't call out. I just reach. I wedge my fingers in. I find the flat shard of basalt that wants to be there and tap it, *tok tok*, soft enough that it doesn't echo.

The old woman who is the memory of the bowl stops on her way past. She is fissured as the upper rim. She puts her hand over mine on the stone. We both wait for the tick. It comes up through the soles of our feet, faint and deep, as the ledge decides again what shape it wants to be.

"It's learning," she says, the way you would say "it's raining."

And the water on the vertical face beside us, which has been hanging impossible in that trembling lens, finally remembers and slides, curling down the wall to find the channel I cleared this morning.

That is my place. Not the center, not the rim. The joint. The flex. The place that is built to sigh instead of shatter.

At dusk, when the mist thickens until the rim disappears and the bowl becomes exactly world-sized, I stay there. The lanterns come on one by one behind oiled paper, each one doubled in the sheet-water below. The midges rise in their tight column over the center pool. In the thatch beside my head, the flat stone-mites come out, slow as fingernails, to graze the moss.

I tuck my feet up onto the stone that has taken the shape of my hips. I listen to the water ticking off the eaves into barrels. I listen to the stone ticking under me, still being born. I listen to the house breathing, wool to wool, foot to foot, all of it packed tight not like squalor but like a weaving, like a warm scab over a wound while it heals.

And I breathe with it. Shallow, cool, mineral breaths. Until the bowl exhales as one.
