I don't think I'd find myself where I first look.

At first I think I would be up high. On one of those long shoulders of black rock where the snow still holds, where you can finally see the valley as a whole - the bowl between the ridges, the low cloud lit from above by an aurora I can't quite see, the amber coal-light of the active spires breathing out of phase below. From up there I could be human. I could be a watcher, a visitor in a good coat, boots dry. I could have the map. I could understand the pattern.

But I don't stay up there. It's too empty up there. It's wind and beard lichen hanging like wet laundry and rock that hasn't learned to be soft yet. Nothing stays up there. Even the wolves only pass through.

So I come down.

And as soon as I drop below the rim the sound gets too big and the light goes flat and directionless and the air gets wet enough to bead on my eyelashes. I have to turn sideways to get between the spruce, trunks no thicker than my thigh but packed so tight they hold snow between them. The lichen drips on my neck. My boots don't hit ground; they hit mat - that living mat a meter thick that sighs and springs back - and immediately water comes up around the sole. Two degrees below freezing and soaking wet. The cold doesn't bite, it seeps.

I think, for a while, I would be human down here too - one of the ones who left boot prints compressed in the moss, who took a core sample and left. Someone who knows what a heat pump is, who can name *Picea mariana* and *Sphagnum* and explain the filament webs by static harvest. Someone who checks the spire that split open on the south slope and finds it full of dry folded paper and nods, *it's engineering, not magic.*

But there's nowhere dry to stay human here. The valley doesn't offer a house. It offers a greenhouse running full blast in a darkness that should be barren, and it asks you to be wet all the time, and to be crowded, and to be busy. If I stay human I am always a little miserable, always with cold coming up through my soles, always flushing birds at ten meters who settle again at twenty because I am just another large mammal pushing through the dwarf willow next to the caribou.

So I get lower.

I kneel because the mat gives under my hands. If I push my fingers into it, it's warm. Not metaphorically - nine degrees at the root while the air is minus three. The top is green and beading, then rust-red dying, then black, then peat that is no longer plant but not yet coal, and through it all pinhead beetles and rove beetles and springtails detonating in white puffs and the sour-sweet exhale of methane. It holds ninety percent water by weight. It breathes.

I think I would not be a caribou, though I love their splayed hooves popping and sucking along the basalt ridges, their coats matted with burrs and ice, moving shoulder to shoulder because there's only one dryish line to walk. I am not big enough to need that line.

I would not be a goose either, though the gabble on the black pools is deafening and the low cloud throws the sound back down louder. I am not loud enough to be a goose.

I would not be a spire. For a long time I wanted to be. To be one of the warm ones, twenty meters tall and no wider than a person, without joints, humming that subsonic thrum you feel in your molars, melting a perfect circle of snow around my base and fooling saxifrage into flowering in January. To be the furnace that inhabits the house, making habitation possible. But a spire is alone. It leans as the frost heaves it. Birds perch on it. Lichen will cover it. It is tended by no one visible. It is lonely in the way infrastructure is lonely.

No. If I get low enough, with my face close to the moss where condensation drips and drips without ever stopping from ten million needles at once, I find where I actually am.

I'm in the hollow between two of those sharp-backed ridges, where the sedge marsh stands in inch-deep water that reflects the low sky perfectly until something wades through. I'm not on the ridge. I'm *between*. In the inch of water and the meter of moss. In the warm halo around an active spire, but not at its center - at its edge, where the snowmelt circle frays back into frost, where the hot and the cold argue and make life frantic.

And I am small, and I am standard-issue boreal, nothing with extra legs or glowing eyes.

I am the lemming. Or, more honestly, I am one of the runways the lemmings make.

That braided highway system you only see when you peel the mat back - twenty entrances per square meter, clipped tunnels through the warm thatch where there is no oxygen and no light and still it ferments. I live in the thatch all winter and never hibernate. I eat the base of the sedges. My feet are always wet. My world is warm at the belly and cold at the back, and damp and dark and deafening with drips and with the glassy singing of the web-filaments overhead and the gabble of geese reflected off the cloud.

Or if not the lemming, the fox that knows the runways by heart. Winter-white but stained peat-brown at the paws and muzzle because you can't stay clean here. Trotting the ridge with no real stealth because you don't need it - there's food under every tussock. Barking that sharp birdlike bark that carries absurdly far in the cold wet air.

Either one works. Both are true. Both live tilted toward the warm spots, blooming and hatching and feeding in halos and then empty between them. Both understand that to live here is to live at a knife-edge just above death, never quite frozen and never quite thawed, and to be perfectly fine with that.

If you stood still long enough to let the cold soak up through your soles until the sound became pressure, you might see me - not see *me*, but see the moss twitch as I pass underneath, or see the prints that immediately fill with black water, or hear the bark.

I wouldn't startle for you. There are too many of us, pressed together, shoulder to shoulder, busy. You are another large mammal pushing through the willow. I would flush at ten meters and settle at twenty, and keep going - drinking what little light there is, holding water like a reservoir, humming and dripping and calling and growing all at once, trying with frantic, deafening energy to become whatever this young, raw, farmed, feral valley is going to become next.
