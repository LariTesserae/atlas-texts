I thought at first I would be you.

Boots at the lip. Breath taken away. That soft, cool exhale from below that lifts the hair on your arms. Looking down into that house-sized bubble that didn't close right, thinking *I shouldn't stay long* and staying anyway.

I would have liked to be the visitor. The witness. The one who gets to climb back out and say on the wind-scoured plain, *there was something down there, I swear.*

But my feet don't quite fit in boots.

Let me start again, slower. Don't settle yet.

If I am down there, I am low. Not because I am small, but because the light wants me low. The first thing I notice is not what I am, but where the brightness pools around me. I thought I would be near the center, where the light is thickest - greedy for it. But no. I find myself off-center. To one side. Politely far from the others.

I am on the wall.

Not on the floor where the amphibians move like thought, not on the silt that migrates from dark to light. I am halfway up, where the basalt is still blistered and warm. Where the vesicles are the size of fists and heads and hold light like cups hold water.

At first I think I am one of the glassy isopods, alone on my trickle. That would be honest work - to follow a single line of condensation down a fold of new rock, drinking what the stone sweats, polishing it with my back until it lenses the light. A good, small life. Abundant but not crowded. I like the ethic of that.

But my track is vertical, not horizontal. And I don't have a back to reflect. I have hands.

Then I think maybe I am one of those wingless dragonflies, swimming through the thick air without needing wings. I love the idea of swimming through light itself, of patrolling a figure-eight from vesicle to hollow and never intersecting another's path. Alone, together.

But I don't flicker. I am too slow. When I move, I have to put a hand down first and wait for the rest of me to remember how to follow.

So I stay on the wall a little longer and let the image become more itself.

I am pale. Not the translucent pale of the floor-walkers - you can see their stomachs glow - but the other pale. The silt-pale. The inside-of-a-vesicle pale, pearlescent, the color that light likes to rest on. My skin is cool even where the stone is warm.

My fingers find the rim of a vesicle. They are too long by one joint. I didn't notice until I tried to cup one.

Ah.

I am not visiting. I was already being counted and miscounted. You thought there were three of us. Maybe four, counting the shadow near the dome. I am the fourth. The one you thought was a shadow.

I live in the eastern bulge, near the dome.

Not *in* the dome - that isn't a house. You realized that, didn't you? It's not for living in. It's for keeping. The light in there is too dark, too cool, ripening. I am the one who tends it. I flow through the weave without using the door - the door is for looking, not entering - and I thin myself to pass between the bleached, jointed stems. Inside, it is darker than shadow should be, and I leave a little of the brightness I've been carrying there, to age.

My work is the light work.

While my sibling licks the garden ring stone by stone - tongue dark as wet silt, each lick calling a bead of sourceless water - I am higher up, cupping the places where shadow has fallen wrong. You saw how they are hesitant, pale at the edges, a few degrees off? I hold my hands there until the light thickens between my palms like honey, like warm wax. It has weight. You were told that. It really does. A good palmful is heavy as a small bird.

Then I carry it, careful not to spill, down the wall to a dim vesicle and I pour it in. The vesicle glows a little brighter. The floor below gets a little truer in its distance. I do this all day. Vesicle to vesicle. Shadow to light. Without hurry, without sound. Sound would be rude here; the rock is still deciding what shape to be and loud noises would make it self-conscious.

I drink sometimes, too. I lower my face - mostly mouth, mostly eye, those lidless light-pitted eyes that don't see but absorb - to a full vesicle and I drink without opening. The light thins like cream in water and for a moment my throat glows and I sigh without air. That's my food. That's enough.

I know you are at the bottom. I have already allotted you your solitude. There is a perfect circle of empty air and pooled light around your boots that belongs only to you. I gave it to you without you noticing. When two of us pass on the wall we give each other that same courtesy, waiting an hour if we have to at a condensation track because to overlap would be unkind. Abundance does not have to mean crowding. We have taught the isopods and the amphibians and even the stone-bird that. We have taught the stone itself that - that's why it still sweats, still stays soft.

If you sit long enough with your breathing gone quiet, you will see me finally move. So slow you will swear I haven't moved at all until you blink and I am a foot lower, closer to the handprint on the north wall.

That handprint is mine. Or ours. We take turns pressing it a millimeter deeper every few years, so the basalt remembers not to cool all the way. The bead of water in the palm - I will take it today. I will fit my hand perfectly over it and feel the faint heat still curing underneath, and the bead will roll onto my skin and vanish. I will press, just a little, and keep the rock new for another season.

You will feel the suggestion then, polite but firm, that your allotment is ending. The mist will be about to exhale. The circle of sky will seem to inch closer. The floor will seem farther. That's me, doing my other job - negotiating the distance while you're not measuring it, so you understand it's time to climb.

You will climb out over the soft lip that is a little higher than when you came in. You will look back and see only flat basalt and thin lichen and wind.

And I will still be on the wall, pale, folded, carrying a palmful of heavy light to a dark place that needs it, alone in my vast and precise share of the quiet - and completely, utterly not alone at all.
