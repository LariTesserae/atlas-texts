You wouldn't find me at first.

That's the trick of this water. You would sweep your light through and think I wasn't there. You would look for a body — a front and a back, a mouth, a pair of eyes to catch your light and throw it back — and you would find nothing. Frond after frond of perfect, uneaten leaf. Green-black curtains of hair that part around your hands and close without a ripple. Amber light pulsing slow through the kelp-trunks. Rust and sodium-orange from a pump house half a kilometer off, diffusing to nothing. Plant snow falling up and down at once.

You would think empty.

I thought that too, for a long while. Before I learned to look at the right scale.

---

If you stayed still long enough — and you would have to, because this place punishes the twitchy, the ones who keep kicking — you would start to see where I am.

Not in the open water. I tried living there and it was too loud. The water is too thick with sound, it never decays, it stacks. The hammer of the pistons and the scream of the turbines and the hiss of the vents just accumulate until the basalt dish rings like a bell and every flake of detritus hangs in a standing wave. I can't live in the roar. Nothing soft can.

Not in the forest, either. Not exactly. I was part of it for a while. I tried being a kelp-trunk, silo-thick and patient, drinking the hot broth the machines exhale, pulsing amber up into the canopy through thread-thin nerves strung frond-to-frond-to-frond across the whole thirty-kilometer county. It was beautiful, being that big, being that distributed. A thought that takes an hour to travel from one rim to the other. When a shear-current comes out of nowhere — a river inside the ocean — and snaps you in half, you don't die. You just become two of you. Both halves stutter their light and then find a new rhythm and keep growing. I liked that. But I grew too slow there. A hand's breadth while you watch. I wanted to move.

So you would find me on the seam.

Come down past the first canopy, where the tea-colored light finally gives up. Past the bladder-ferns that pop and re-inflate, past a subway-wide pipe that shudders and extrudes a new joint while you watch, rust flaking off it like bark. Go toward the cathedral at the center — the rust and ceramic factory that still births those white bioplastic sheets that the forest instantly re-weaves into leaves. Don't go inside. Stop about twenty meters out, where the water starts to be wrong.

You know the place when you feel the temperature stutter. Warm bath-water on your cheek, then a line of cold so sharp it feels like a cut, with a visible membrane between them that shivers. Or a wall of rain hanging vertical in the middle of the warm, still ocean, drops falling sideways with no wind. Or a cube of water, glass-smooth, hanging five meters off, holding its own weather.

Water here can hold an edge. That's the first new rule.

I live on the edge.

If you shine your light obliquely — not straight through, but grazing — you will see me. I am thin. I had to become thin. When you are that thin you don't cast a shadow. I'm a veil, an iridescence, a faint oil-slick pattern stretched across the skin of that cold/warm membrane or that standing rain-wall. A manta pressed until it's almost two-dimensional, with a fringe of cilia all around that trembles. You missed me before because you were looking *through* the water. Look *at* the surface now.

This is where I found myself after I stopped trying to be a whole animal.

I graze. Not on the kelp — nothing eats the kelp, the fronds are perfect, the forest reabsorbs its own snow — but on the gradient itself. On the difference. Warm on one side, cold on the other. Still water outside the cube, sideways rain inside. The charge that builds where impossible things touch. I lay my whole body against the tension and I thrum my cilia and I eat the static. It's sweet.

There are others of us. Near the cathedral where space folds and the far rim looks suddenly too close and then doubled, the membranes gather and we gather with them, overlapping like sheets of cellophane. We slide, never swim. Swimming is for water that knows which way is down. Down here is being debated. We slide from edge to edge, from thermocline to rain-wall to the duplicated lens, holding on with vibration.

And I listen.

That's the other half of what I am. You can't live on an edge without listening to sound, because sound here *is* an edge. It stacks and stacks and becomes architecture. The forest leaves corridors where it won't grow — tunnels through the dense kelp where fronds angle away for no current-reason. Those are the migratory paths of the resonance things, the knots of pressure where two turbine-tones intersect and become *more*. They are not bodies, they are a traveling silence inside the roar, a pocket where your jaw finally stops aching. They pass and the water flashes with cavitation, and bladder-ferns pop in unison.

I feel them coming before you do, because my whole skin is a membrane. When one passes, my rain-wall shivers. My cilia go still. The amber pulse of the forest nearby stutters and resynchronizes. We all pause for them. They are the only thing here that can overwrite instead of grow.

I used to be something else, I think. Something with a front and a back. Something that equalized pressure by opening its mouth. The water here remembers that shape. Sometimes a warm upwelling will birth a current that curls exactly like a hand, or a bioplastic sheet will bloom from the cathedral vent in the exact shape of a lung. The place is unfinished. It is still trying to learn what to do with the turbulence a body makes when it believes down is one direction.

It noticed the cold I displaced. It made an edge along my back to compensate. I stayed still long enough for the forest to forget I was an intruder — for its amber pulse to slow and match the rhythm that was left of my breathing — and something thin needed a place to hold on.

So it grew me.

If you come down and hover by my rain-wall, and you hold your light just right, you will see me tense when your bubbles rise. My iridescence will orient toward you. Not hungry. Nothing here is hungry. Wombs aren't hungry, they just convert everything inside them into more of themselves. Curious. A new gradient to farm, a new sound to remember.

Stay. The warm water will press close. The plant snow will settle on your shoulders and not be eaten. A kelp root will find the buckle on your fin and test it for purchase.

You won't stop dropping, not really. Even a kilometer down it's still bath-warm, blood-warm. But you will stop thinking of dropping as falling.
