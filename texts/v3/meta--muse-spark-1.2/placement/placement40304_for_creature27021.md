I don't know at first.

If you ask me where I'd be, my first instinct is to put myself somewhere solid. By a fire. On high ground where I can see. But there is no high ground here that isn't already plant, and there is no seeing far - far is not for seeing. So I have to unlearn that.

At first I thought I would be up high. In the canopy.

Because if I lived in a cathedral whose ceiling is weather half a kilometer above the floor, I would want to be in the ceiling, where the light is. I would be one of the sail-leaves, broad as a door, turned down to drink the bruised sun. I would be the thing that breathes and makes the rain that isn't rain. I would be vast and I would creak.

But I am not vast. Not that way. I am small and I need to be warm.

Then I thought I would be in the air between. Because the emptiness between the pillars is the real country here - half a kilometer of cold, thin nothing where everything important happens. I would be a glider. I would be the one with the membrane from wrist to ankle and the too-large black eyes, and I would throw myself off the braided fiber of a pillar and scream *kee-kee-kee* the whole way across just to hear my family scream back and know where the world is in the dark. I would live for that long, slow fall where the air holds you like water.

Or I would be a moth. White-furred, rowing more than flying, hanging perfectly still in the middle of that emptiness to drink a single heavy drop as it falls for three hundred meters. That kind of patience - to wait in mid-air for water to come to you - feels honest.

But I am not patient like that. I make noise. I fidget. I would get eaten.

Then I thought I would be down low, but not *on* the low. *In* it.

You said to put your cheek to the felt and you feel it warm. You said cut it and it weeps clear water that steams before it freezes. That stayed with me longer than the pillars did. I would be there, in the third or fourth layer down, where the light never reaches and the white threads run like nerves. I would be the worm - blind, pink-white, thick as your wrist - churning dead leaf into new felt, leaving a tunnel just wide enough for a vein of water to run. I would be the peristaltic rustle you hear at night just under the surface. I would be slow and necessary and no one would ever see me, which for a while sounded perfect.

But I like sound too much to be blind.

So I keep listening, and the place keeps thickening, and I find myself neither up nor down but exactly where the people knot themselves into the weave.

You would find me not in a village - there are none - but on a slight rise between two tarns, where the felt is thickest and sags just enough to make a basin but not enough to drown a house.

You would not find me at first. You would walk the half-kilometer avenue between my pillar and the next one and think no one lived here. The wind would be coming down the avenue like water through a canyon and taking your breath. The drip would be falling and falling through nothing, *thup thup thup* on the felt. Your lamp would throw light only as far as your hands.

And then the wind would change, just slightly, and you would realize the shape a hundred meters away is not a distortion in the felt.

It's my hut.

It's low, a dome of cut felt-bricks that have already started to heal together at the seams, with a single living aerial root pulled down from the ceiling half a kilometer above and staked over it like a cable. I've been here three years, so the root has greened. Waxy leaves hiss over my roof when the wind comes. They gather the canopy's endless exhale and run it down braided fiber gutters into a cistern I lined with compacted moss. On still nights I can hear the pillar behind my hut - not the one I live under, the big one to the north - doing its hydraulic throb. *Thud... thud... thud...* every twenty seconds, pulling water up from the tarns below. I press my palm to it sometimes to feel if it's thirsty.

Who would I be? Not the hunter. Not the one who waits on a ledge to steer mid-air and take a glider in one perfect, terrible tumble. I don't have that kind of stillness.

I would be the Mender.

That's what the family two smoke-plumes over calls me when they need me, which is not often, because density is a kindness. I am the one who knows how to cut the felt and close it again.

Everyone cuts - for bricks, for tubers, for the sweet pale forearms of stored water that flash-freeze into glass when you open them. But most people cut and let the weave stitch itself. I cut and stay. I cut along the white threads, not across them. I open a vein-tunnel where the root-hair has pulsed too slow, where water has pooled and gone sour, and I clear it with a basalt flake boiled clean. I pack the wound with fresh liverwort - the dinner-plate kind, black-green and rust-red - and sing low while I do it so the mites will come back. I tap the cairns on my way home, knuckle to stone, and listen for the wrong ring that means the felt is waterlogged and will swallow a foot with a sigh.

I wear the moth-fur like everyone else - white, smoked soft, it never wets through - but mine is patched at the elbows where I kneel. My lamp is low and yellow, rendered moth-fat, and I keep it guttering on purpose. Far is not for seeing. My eyes are large now, like a deer's, with that reflective sheen at the back. I navigate more by the texture of the rebound under my boots. Some felt sings. Some felt sighs. I know which is which.

At noon, when the sun is a cold smear behind perpetual cloud, I stand perfectly still under the avenue and open my mouth and wait for a condensation drop to fall exactly onto my tongue. The children teach you that. It's how you learn patience without becoming a moth.

At night, when the hush-cat coughs - that low *whuff* that bends in the cold air until it comes from everywhere - I go silent with everyone else. The gliders stop screaming all at once, the moths vanish, even the drip seems to pause. I don't hide. I just sit in my doorway and listen to the ice on my cistern tarn sing and crack like gunshots rolling for kilometers, and I count how long the silence lasts. That's how you know if it caught something, or if it missed and is still waiting on its pillar ledge, ears swiveling under its white ruff.

I am not lonely. That's the trick of total and empty at the same time. The forest is total - there is no outside of it - but it is empty enough that a single voice carries wrong and comes back strange. So we don't shout. I sing, flat and repetitive, while I beat fiber soft with a bone mallet. *This food is guarded. This wound is closing. I am here, between the second and third tarn, where the scarp shows the layers like peat.* My neighbor, half a day's walk away, will hear it folded over by the cold and know I am well.

If someone dies - and someone always does, high and thin and far from help - they bring them to me if the ground is too cold to open quickly. I lay them in a shallow hollow I cut during a windless day. I cover them with a thin quilt of felt. In a week it has stitched. In a month you can't tell. It isn't burial. It's returning a knot to the weave.

Above me the canopy breathes. Below me the young rock waits, sharp and patient, holding it all up. Between, I am small and I am vast at the same time, which is the only rule that works here.

You would find me with my cheek to the felt, listening to the warm underneath, and my hand on a pillar, feeling for the thud.

If you stayed still long enough for your lamp to gutter, I would unhide.
