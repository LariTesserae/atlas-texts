I don't know right away.

If I slip over the rim with you — through that silk of a wall that gives instead of breaking — the cold hits first. Not a shock, but a negotiation. 54 degrees, teeth-aching, lung-tightening until the plants have finished their work in my blood. And for a minute I'm everywhere at once, because the light is everywhere at once. White-gold at the top, green and columned in the middle, deep blue dusk at the bottom, and I can't tell where I belong in it yet.

I thought at first I would be one of the carp.

That would be easy. To be the soot-black school that turns as one body and makes the water thrum. To be fast, purposeful, unthinking. To be the thing the children watch for and hum about. But I am not that fast. I have never been that fast. Even my thinking isn't fast — it eddies, it spirals down for days like the particulate in those light columns.

Then I thought I would be the wall itself. The outer ring of crown-leaves, big as courtyards, all transpiring in unison to hold the sea up in the sky. That appealed to me for a while. To be tension. To be the thing that holds by breathing together. But the wall is too exposed, too much sun. I am not a sunward thing. I squint in direct light.

Then I thought I would be a bridge. One of the old ones, so thick with epiphyte you can't tell where bridge stops and tree begins. That was closer. To be the persuaded runner, trained across a gap year by year until I could bear weight. To be the thing that connects two houses with emptiness left deliberately on either side. But a bridge doesn't get to choose when it's used, and I want to be the one who chooses slowness.

What settles, if I let it settle slowly — the way a runner is nudged a half-inch left and left to show if it holds — is this:

You would not find me in the clearing where they gather at blue dusk, hanging at different depths like lanterns. I would be there sometimes, inverted, feet hooked over a runner, listening to the old women hum. But you wouldn't *find* me there.

You would find me half a fathom down from the surface on the eastern curve, where the noon light comes in at a slant and never quite reaches the bottom rumor. Between two of the great trunks — not the biggest ones, the middle-aged ones whose bark is olive-black and deeply furrowed and still heals quickly when you carve it.

My house is not a house.

It's a dead stem. One that died three seasons before I got there and was not cleared, because nothing dead is wasted. It's hollowed, of course, eaten from within by fish and water beetles and the fungi that softens even the mountain stone. Twelve feet long, horizontal, suspended in the lattice like a fallen note. The family that tended it before me left it when their daughter finally coaxed her dome to arch — after ten years of weighting shoots with stones — and I took it because I had not yet earned a dome of my own.

I am the interval-keeper.

In a town that spaces itself on purpose — a dwelling here, thirty feet of empty water and drifting fronds, then another — someone has to tend the emptiness. Not everyone wants that job. Most people want to coax and build and stitch leaves that still photosynthesize overhead. I want to keep the silences between structures from filling in.

So my work is mostly un-work.

In the morning I check the runners that want to close the gap between my stem and the next house over. They reach at night. Fine, pale root-hairs tasting the current. I pluck them back, softly, with these hands — wide as spades, webbed to the second knuckle, nails thick and dark and still a little torn from healing. I don't cut. I hum low while I do it, the chest-hum that makes the root-hairs shiver and withdraw, gill-ruffs on my lower ribs fluttering open then lying flat again. The sound carries. Two houses over, someone hears the tone and knows I'm up, tending.

Midday, when the whole basin lights up at once and every translucent wall throws light, I hang outside my stem with my back pressed to the sunward trunk the way the ill do. My moss cape — not yet twenty years old, not yet with its own fauna, just a sash of living green that I stitched to my shoulders with root-thread — does its small, stubborn photosynthesis. The symbionts on my shoulders and shins green a little more. I photo-synthesize a little, not enough to live on, enough to make the cool bearable. My hair, cropped short with a bone knife because I'm still young enough to need to slip between stems without snagging, floats around my head and collects stray pollen. I barely kick. A slow scull of webbed feet, a pull along a runner. Hurry is cold and stupid here.

My stem is cool inside, and loud in the way old things are loud. It creaks. Bladder-pods above me implode and refill with hollow booms. Shrimp click inside the walls. When the blind newts pass — pale as canoes, whistling through their blowholes — the whole stem resonates like a drum. I have learned to tell, by that resonance, whether the newt is grazing or just passing. If it's grazing, I stay still; if it's passing, I go out and scrape the parasite vine off the trunk behind it, where it couldn't reach.

I don't have much. A basalt adze, a bone awl, a spool of cordage, a net bag for filter-mussels and those anemone-like bloom things that taste like brine and iron. All the dead things I carry hung from a leaf-scale belt that I shed and re-sew when it grows brittle. On the inside of my stem, where the wood healed around the doorway, I've started training a single shoot to arch into a second doorway that I don't need yet. Maybe in eight years it will be a dome. Maybe it will decide to be a bridge instead. I won't hurry it.

You would know me because I am always listening.

Sound travels too well here to talk loudly. The children are taught early to speak in hums because a shriek stays for seconds, bouncing between leaves. So I have become the one who remembers knocks. Two slow, one quick against living wood means *come help hold this*. A long hum with gills flared means *close your nets, carp coming*. My stem, being dead and hollow, carries vibration better than living wood. People tap it when they want the message to go further without shouting. I tap back.

If you came — still learning the second way of breathing, still coughing hard when you breach among the lily crowns to take air — you wouldn't startle me. I've been watching the rim. An eye would find you, my second lid drawing clear across against the sap-drift, and a hand would extend.

Not to pull you. To offer you a runner to steady yourself while the cold takes hold, while you learn to move folded and gliding, while you learn that time here is measured in handbreadths per year.

You could hang for a while in the empty water outside my stem. There's room left on purpose.

We could be quiet together, and listen to the town creak.
