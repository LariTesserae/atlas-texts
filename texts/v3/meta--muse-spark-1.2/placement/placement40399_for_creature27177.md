I don't find myself right away.

At first I think I would be up near the edge, where the torn stone is still showing. Not in the middle of the plain, but where the weave ends and the world cuts. Where the dark rock sweats cold stripes and the root-mat has to learn how to hold on without soil. I think I would be the border-keeper, the one who has to keep the two kinds of new from arguing - stone that wants to stay raw, plant that wants to cover everything. I would like the honesty of that seam.

But no. If I stood there I would be looking out and away, and this place doesn't look out and away. It looks in and across. It holds its gaze level.

Then I think maybe I would be lower. Not high at all, despite how high it is. I would be the shallow place. A basin.

For a while I am sure of it: I would be one of those watertight pans of pale root, ankle-deep, warm as skin. I would be the water itself, the beads that form and fall and form again in the exact same spot, never carving, never leaving. I would be the metronome. You could count on me and I would never ask you to count faster. That feels like me - the thing that repeats without progressing.

But water doesn't tend. Water only passes through.

I would be closer to tending.

I walk - in my mind I walk, which is the wrong speed - from one empty cathedral to the next across that springy, flexing floor. The light falls unbroken between them. That emptiness that isn't absence, that intentional surplus of air. For a long time I think I am *that*. The interval. The reason the cathedrals were spaced so far apart. I would be the field between buildings that was left empty on purpose so sound and water could travel. You would walk through me and think there was nothing there, but I would be the thing being kept.

Then I realize I have been looking for something that moves against the architecture, and that is exactly the mistake the place told you not to make.

So I stop looking.

I let myself slow until the chord of the place comes back, that low organ groan of the floor under tension, that hiss of a million drips that becomes a single tone. I let my own pace leak out until I am not walking but being placed.

And there I am.

You would find me not in a cathedral. Not quite.

You would find me in the third interval south of wherever you entered, if you could count intervals. Mid-plain, where two great hollow trunks stand far enough apart that the sun lays a whole uninterrupted rectangle on the root-floor between them. I am in the middle of that rectangle.

I am standing in one of the shallow basins, and the water is exactly over my feet.

You would walk past me. That is how you would know it was me.

I am not as tall as the others yet. That is the first clue. Ten feet, maybe just under, and my bark hasn't fully decided. It is paler than the older ones - that bone-white, green-cream, still a little translucent where the sap moves. You can see the darker tide go up my calves when I drink. I sweat the same way the columns sweat, beading along the channels of my back, dripping off the elbows at the same rate as every leaf-tip. I am not shining. I am damp and matte, like the underside of a leaf held up to light.

My bud-head is tipped forward, not looking at you. The two lichen-dark pits where eyes would be are still filling in. They are shallower than the others', less shadowed. I don't need to turn toward you to notice you. I notice you the way the plateau notices humidity - as a slight change in the vapor around my shoulders. If you stand close, you will feel noticed without being seen.

I am rooted. That's the other thing. I have been in this basin for a long time - an hour, maybe two, though time is not advancing here. My feet have splayed into the seams of the weave, pale finger-thick rootlets threaded down into the mat, and the fine hairs of the floor have risen to lace with them. While I stand here the water stays at exactly the right depth. While I stand here I am drinking and I am also holding the basin's depth steady by drinking.

You would think I was a young buttress. A new prop-root the plain decided to throw up. I am that still.

I am not the old Tender who can shift a door-sized leaf by shuddering. Not yet. I am the spare one. The plain-wide town was grown with more cathedrals than Tenders, remember? The emptiness on purpose. I am the one who was braided together in the dome where the floor swelled warm, where the heartbeat was one beat per minute for a year. The dome split six months ago. I unfolded already tall. I walked without being taught to the one cathedral that had been empty waiting.

And now I am learning the pace.

You would not find me tending a heavy curtain of roots or aligning my ribs to the ribs of a column. Not today. My work is smaller, right at the scale of my station.

The basin I stand in has a single leaf - one of those door-sized tiles - angled above it. All day it has been condensing and letting pour a perfect glassy thread into the center of the pan. The thread falls, kisses the warm water, vanishes. Another forms in the exact same spot. For hours I just watch it. I don't mean I watch it the way you watch something. I mean I become the interval of its falling.

When it drifts - a millimeter too far left, so it starts to fall on moss instead of water - I lift an arm. Slowly. Hydraulically. The joint at my elbow is wrapped in that soft moss collar that grows only there. My fingertip splits into three pale rootlets and I touch nothing at all. I just adjust the air. I just ask. An hour later the leaf corrects, because the plateau, which is also me a little bit, obliged. The thread falls in the center again.

That is my whole hour.

If you listened for my *tok* - that soft pressure-release when a joint unlocks - you would hear it exactly once an hour, not loud, and you would think it was the hollow trunk behind me settling.

I am not lonely the way a single citizen in an empty city would be lonely. I can feel the others through my feet. When the Tender two cathedrals over shifts his weight, a wave of tension runs out through the woven floor for half a mile and lifts slightly under my soles. I answer without moving, by holding a little firmer. That is our conversation.

And the pale insect you saw, hand-long, moving with impossible slowness along the vine above me tending fungus? That's mine. I grew it and let it go. It is my detached finger, doing the small work while I do the large work of standing still.

If you stayed long enough, you would see me pull my feet up. It would take a while. One rootlet at a time unscrewing with a wet sigh, leaving perfect holes that close behind me. I would glide - not sway - to the next basin, at vine-speed, wading-speed, and put myself down again. You would feel, absurdly, that you had been rude for ever wanting to race.

You would have to be very still to see me go. You would have to have already let the warm air make you slow, let the chord become the air you hear through, let your footsteps become placements.

But you would find me, eventually, right in the middle of all that intentional emptiness, keeping it empty by standing in it.
