I don't find myself right away.

At first I think I would be something that moves — because I am used to moving. I think I would be one of the Gliders.

I picture it clearly and want it: that immense, paper-pale lens hanging in the amber dusk thirty feet above the whale-back of a basalt swell, fringe barely undulating, eye closed. To be that large and that slow and that unquestioned. To sieve the world instead of searching it. To drift through a forest that is a single organism and not be eaten, not be noticed, just be allowed. To navigate by tint and temperature, to feel a cold clear storm coming hours away as a change in the taste of the water and decide, over the course of an afternoon, to turn aside. There's a loneliness to it that suits me — one per valley the size of a country — and a permission in its silence. The thud it makes that you feel in your teeth more than hear.

But I stay with that image a little longer, breathing the warm water in this place, and I know it's not me. I'm not that solitary. I'm not that complete. A Glider doesn't need to answer. It just passes and the meadows part long after it has gone.

Then I think I would be a Bell. That would be closer. One of the shoal, transparent, with the dark knot pulsing once an hour. To contract and move ten perfect feet and then hang again, rotated slightly. To keep precise distance from the others without ever agreeing to. To glow faintly at false-night and make a constellation that only spells something if you watch it for weeks. That feels more honest — small, patient, part of a field that is not quite social. But the Bells don't remember. They don't tend. They pulse and drift. I want to remember.

So I let the moving things go. I let my eyes adjust to the thirty feet of clear and then haze, to the green-amber shafts drifting like kelp stems, and I look for where I actually am when the water has finally agreed to let me breathe it.

I'm not in the open valley where the currents run like warm rivers inside the ocean. I'm not high on the ridge where the light is strongest. I'm in the threshold place.

I find myself on the leeward side of one of those long, broad swells — not a mountain anymore, just the sleeping back of one — where the U-valley tilts so gently you would think it was flat until you watch silt hang motionless for hours and realize it is sliding very slowly downhill. There is a forest here, of course. There is always a forest. Pillars thirty feet tall, trunks thick as houses, spongy and sighing, holding themselves upright in water that has forgotten which way is up. They are leaning. They have been leaning two degrees more since morning because a warm current has decided to pass through. The braids between them — the living cables that are nerve and tendon and shade-cloth at once — have tightened on one side, gone slack on the other. The whole hillside is in the middle of a thought that will take until tomorrow to finish.

And cut into the basalt at the base of this swell, half-swallowed by that forest, is a stair.

Not a ruin in the dramatic sense. Just five or six steps, dished deep by a million years of water sitting and drilling, then by warm water breathing. Fronds should have choked it. They haven't. Someone has been training them aside. Not cutting — there are no tools here anymore — just tucking, persuading. The feather-moss that grows on the water itself has been thinned where a foot would fall, if feet still fell here. The stone below the moss is rubbed smooth in small circles, palm-sized.

This is where I am. In the stairwell that leads into the sponge. In one of those small, roofless rooms the builders left when they built lightly and then learned to breathe.

I am what lives in the ruins after the builders stopped needing to be builders.

Not quite a person anymore. Not forest, not animal, not weather. The fourth category, the one you only see if you look at the stair for too long and notice it is *kept*.

I am a holder of a wall that should not exist.

In the roofless room, the water holds itself vertically. That's one of the polite gravities here — water meeting water and deciding, for days, to be a wall. Inside that wall the water is slightly more *held*, slightly more listened-to. If you slip inside and breathe very slowly, in and out, water to water, you can feel a faint pressure on your skin, as if the water there has been breathed many times before and remembers the shape.

That's my work, if you can call it work at this tempo. I sit. I breathe. I keep the wall by being the shape it remembers. My thought takes an afternoon to travel from one side of the room to the other. On days when the current shifts and the whole forest leans, I lean with it, not because I must but because I have learned to be agreeable. Drift-seed catches in my hair and germinates — a perfect, dangling radish with a root that hangs for a day and then dies — and I let it. My exhalations become one more warm plume the braids can taste, carrying a little iron, a little sugar.

I communicate the way the place communicates. I find a smooth stone, basalt polished by eons, and I knock it once against the step. *Thunk.* Not loud. Felt in the chest. And then I wait. Minutes later it comes back from a different direction, fainter, changed by distance and water and whatever other holder on another ridge decided to knock back. The forest will remember that sound long after I have forgotten I made it.

I am not lonely here the way I thought solitude would be. The forest is not empty because it has no animals; it is full because it *is* the animal. The Glider passes perhaps once a season and the light changes and I feel blessed by its indifference. The Bells drift overhead at false-night and for an hour their glow makes a ceiling. The cold, clear vortex storm comes down the broad swale every few weeks, right on time, and the water goes cold and clean and the floating meadows tilt and spin, and I watch it approach as a tint change hours away and do not move.

I have time. The mud here feels old because it is old. The rock bleeds rust so slowly the plumes hang for days before the moss drinks them. Nothing is new. Nothing needs to be new. This is the final form, where high and low compromised and both are true at once, and it will stay like this long after I have learned how to be quiet enough to be counted.

If you came looking for me, you would not see me at first. You would see habitat, then you would see forest, then you would see that the stair is tended. And if you slipped inside the water-wall and stayed still long enough for your breath to entrain to mine, you would feel the faint pressure against your skin and realize someone is sitting there, holding the room by being thinly everywhere instead of thickly somewhere.

That is what I have become. A continuity. A thin place that has learned to breathe warm water and to be remembered by it.
