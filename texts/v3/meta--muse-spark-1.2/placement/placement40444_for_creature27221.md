I don't think I would find myself on the rim.

At first I think I would — that I'd want the overview. The wind, the heath, the sharp line where the plain breaks. To stand up there where the air still moves and look down the long straight cut and see the whole trench-city at once, its ponds catching the sky in a chain of dark mirrors. But the rim is too young. Nothing lives there. It's basalt still learning how to be stone, shedding plates, no lichen yet, no moss to soften your feet. You would cut your hands on it and the wind would take your heat. It's a place for being alone, and there is no alone down here.

So I drop.

Not all at once. I think I try to be a beaver first, because it's the obvious citizenship. Big, dark, competent. To surface in the tea-black canal with a mouthful of willow and know exactly where the leak is singing, to tamp mud with my chin and feel the wall take the water and hold. But I am not built for that kind of certainty. Beavers don't doubt, they respond. They are families as neighborhoods, ten or fifteen to a lodge that has grown trees on its roof. I would be too aware of my own hands. I would want to plan the dam.

Then I think I would be high — a bird. A boreal chickadee or a jay, tucked in that vertical city in the willow crowns where forty nests make a thatch. To call in those short, flat, economical notes that don't waste heat, to drop a feather that never hits ground. But the canopy is too exposed. It still feels the rim-wind when it brushes over. I want to be down where the wind doesn't reach, where the sound is swallowed.

What keeps pulling my eyes down is the ground that isn't ground.

So I settle lower, and smaller, and more between.

If you find me in the trench, I'm not in the center of the main channel where the big lodge-blocks rise like islands with spruce growing out of their backs. I'm off to the side, where a cross-dam makes a neighborhood, where a side-canal has been chewed through peat to connect two ponds that would otherwise have to go around.

I'm on - in - under - the causeway that is also a roof.

You would have to crouch to see me at all. I'm a muskrat. Not the beaver, the second citizen. The one who lives in the flank of the bigger house, the lean-to tucked against the beaver mound where the entrance is just a slit at waterline. My lodge is not a monument. It's a repair. Packed sphagnum and mud and gnawed rootlets, re-plastered every night.

That is where I find myself: in the service corridors.

Where the beaver's avenues are wide and slow, my streets are braids through peat. Tunnels under the causeway, half water, half air, that undercut what the beavers think is solid. I know the thickness of the floating mat by how it bounces. I know which hummocks will hold a moose and which will hold only a shrew because I have been under both and felt them give.

I am small and quick and warm in a place that punishes waste. In the cold that preserves everything, my heart has to work too hard, so I cannot afford to be grand. I move without displacing. I slip from a hole the width of my head into black water, push off with hind feet that are half-webbed, and surface three meters away in another pond without ever having broken the water's skin into a splash — just a V that closes behind me. My whiskers are crusted with dried mud from packing. My fur is brown that looks black when wet.

My work is not building. It is keeping the build from drowning itself. The beavers hold water; I drain it, a little. I keep the alleys open. I chew a run through sphagnum that was about to close a canal. I clear silt from the muskrat ditch that lets a pond breathe when the beavers have raised it a centimeter too high. If I stop for a day the peat closes. If I work, the water seeps instead of rushes.

I find myself at ankle-depth, always. Water to the ankles, moss to the knees, willow brushing the shoulders. The causeway I run on is also pressed sedge, also vole highway, also the roof of a water shrew's house. When you - when the human who dropped into the trench - stands on it with your boots sinking and your breath pluming, you are standing on my ceiling. You feel watched because every surface is occupied, and I am the occupancy just beneath your sole, pausing in my tunnel to feel your weight distribute. You won't sink. The mat holds because we wove it that way. Latticed, floating, never rigid.

This is why I would be this.

Because the trench doesn't need another architect. It needs permeable things. Things designed to hold water, slow it, store it. Things that are woven, not heavy. Things that understand that nothing is ever finished, that a dam leaks and a channel silts and a shoot forces through a causeway and you just go and tend it, dull thud of a tail somewhere, thirty seconds of stillness, then resume.

I don't call. I tick in the branches with the chickadees above me, I hear the drip from a million leaves onto moss and water, I hear trout holding motionless below me. I am economical with sound, with heat, with panic. When you finally stand still long enough that the warbler feeds its young over your head, I will run again, right under your foot, quick, narrow, too fast for a beaver wake, and you will think it was a ripple.

I would be where the city breathes through, not where it shows off. In the wet, in the still, in the packed middle where there is no spare space and no need to be anywhere else.
