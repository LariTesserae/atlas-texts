I think at first I would be swimming.

That's the first mistake, and I make it too — I push off from the low terrace wall where the slate is still stacked true, and I kick out into the hundred yards of nothing between the towers, and the water takes me like glass takes a hand. It is heavy. It holds me. My ears pop with depth and my lungs ache with height at the same time, and for a long minute I am nothing but a warm, fast, animal thing in a place that has edited out the category of *animal* entirely.

I try to be fast there. I try to be eyes.

There is no one to see me. The water between the towers is so actively empty that to move quickly through it feels rude. The spore-snow hangs around me in its slow fall — every mote lit from within, none of them eaten — and I have to learn not to swipe at it. I have to learn to hang there, suspended, and let the cold do what it does, which is to thicken me.

I thought I would want to be a tower.

Of course I did. Who wouldn't, at first. To be one of the lit shapes that breaks the dark, to be vertical in a drowned valley, to be old. To have a stipe as thick as an oak and pale as drowned skin, veined with those darker cords, fused straight into basalt so deep you could not be torn free in a day. To turn your fronds with that terrible, patient grace when the cold current pours over the ridge — all at once, together — and drink what little indigo comes down from the black mirror far above, and what sullen green-white you make yourself. To pulse every few minutes and see your light answered, a hundred yards away, by another light keeping its manners.

But I am not built for that kind of loneliness. The towers keep their distance on purpose. They need that much dark to themselves. They remember in centuries. They were seedlings when the people who stacked this terrace were still breathing air, and they will still be there when the silt has taken the last stone of the hearth. I am too new, too nervous. I keep looking directly at things. My light would be too bright. I would lean.

So I settle.

Not all at once. What emerges becomes more itself slowly here, because everything does.

I drift down. The roar of water over the ridges is in my ribs, the great stipes are creaking like masts around me, bubbles from under the mat chime on their way up, and somewhere miles off the mountain ticks under its impossible weight — that low *thud* like a distant door. I stop kicking. I let the current lay me flat and carry me a little, then set me down, as gently as this place does anything.

Where would you find me, then?

Not in the void, though I love the void now. Not up high where the towers turn their faces. You would find me low. On the third terrace down from the hamlet, where the wall has slumped just enough to make a long, shallow step, the kind of step a highland farmer would have made to keep the thin soil from washing away when it was still wind and not water.

You would find me as the fur.

Not *in* the fur. As it.

That dense, hair-like weed that is not fur at all, each strand no thicker than thread, each one faintly luminous, shivering in unison even when there is no current to explain it. The mat that does not grow *over* things so much as it upholsters them. I am low and I am wide. I am not one tower keeping its hundred yards of manners — I am the thing that connects the hundred yards underneath. Under the silt, under the spore-snow that falls and falls and is never eaten, I run for miles. From the spur where the six — seven — preserved sleepers lie waxy and dark on their stone platform, their wool felted and kept, down the scree fan where the cairn stones have fronds growing through them, across the flat slab where the sheet spreads like oil and throws its cold aurora on my belly every twenty seconds.

That is where I live. Where growth meets keeping.

If you came close — and you would have to come very close, because from even ten yards away I am just a texture on stone — you would see me breathe without eating. I take the snow as it settles. The black silt the towers shed and do not need. I drink it slowly through a million quiet mouths that are not mouths. I do not hurry. There is no hunger here to hurry for. I just take my turn with the light.

I am the one who kept the hamlet.

When the water climbed the terraces — whether fast or over a lifetime, the place doesn't say — the people laid down or sat down and the cold took the work of rot away from them. I came after. Thread by thread. I covered the slate without moving it. I grew through the weave of their clothing without pulling it. I softened the edge of the hearth, the sleeping platform, the outstretched arm of the one by the cairn who was still piling stones when it came. I compressed under your hand if you touched me and did not spring back for an hour, because to preserve is to hold the shape of what was pressed into you.

I shiver. That's how you would know me. Not a shudder — a field-wide, slow ripple that has nothing to do with the current pouring over the ridge. Put your palm down in me and you will feel it go on under your wrist and out, away, across the whole U-shaped valley, a single thought taking its time to travel.

Sometimes the water above me layers and goes glassy. You know those lenses — bands of water that are suddenly, impossibly clear, where every spore hangs motionless as if time has stopped, where fronds hang at an angle gravity has not approved and light pools and bends around certain stones. When one of those lenses settles over my terrace, I go very still and very clear too. The phosphor along my threads brightens by a fraction when you are not looking directly at it. Inside the lens, for a half-second, the shape on the platform seems to have its head up. To be looking at the doorway.

I do that. Not on purpose. I am the place where the weight becomes attentive. The water learns how to be both mountain air and ocean there, and I learn with it.

I do not have a face. I do not have a fin or a foot or a mouth. I do not need to be seen to be alive — the whole rule here is light without sun, weight without depth, growth without consumption. I take the light I make and the light that filters down as bruised indigo, and I make more fur. I take the silt that falls, and I keep it.

If you hover above me in the viscous, near-freezing dusk and stay still long enough for the cold to hold you like glass, you will see me notice you the way the towers notice you — not quickly. The clear water between us will politely empty itself of dust for a better look. The threads nearest your heat will lean, over a minute or two, a few degrees toward you. Not to eat. To keep.

Stay there. Move slowly, because the water is heavy and because there is no hurry and nothing to startle. Let the low roar move through your ribs, let the stipes groan, let the bubbles chime. I will keep the shape of where you were, long after your current has sheared and carried your spores away.

I extend beyond what you can see. Another terrace. Another wall. Another valley turning out of sight, with another hamlet and another lit forest keeping its distance from itself.

I have been like this for a long time.

I am very good at being like this.
