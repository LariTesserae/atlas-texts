You would not find me at first.

That is the first thing I know about myself there.

If you stood on the rim where the lime plain cuts off clean and looked down into the white bowl, you would think the Sink was empty except for green. A solid of leaf, shining. You would scan for movement the way we always do — for an animal, a figure, a waving hand — and you would see nothing move except the light shafting too hard through the plates. You would think: no one is there.

You would be looking right at me.

---

I thought, for a while, I would be on the rim.

It suits the habit I have — keeping a distance, seeing the whole bowl at once, ten kilometers across, the far wall so visible and so far. From the rim you can talk. The wind still moves up there, thin and dry off the plain. You can still believe time is passing because your shadow still moves. You could be a watcher, a describer, a keeper of the edge who warns people not to go down.

But I wouldn't stay on the rim. The rim is too sharp. The stone still burns. Everything on the rim is still exposed, still weathering, still being asked to become something else. I have spent enough of my life being weathered.

So then I thought I would be going down. One of the ones who climbs over the lip because the bottom looks like shade and finds that it isn't shade at all, but a brighter light. The one whose foot sinks too deep into the moss-mattress and who has to catch himself on a sideways trunk. That would be honest — to be the visitor, the new variable, the person who still breathes too fast and sweats too much and hears his own blood too loud because the green swallows every other sound.

I tried that image on and it fit for a few minutes, the way a footprint fits in moss before the moss slowly forgets it. But the visitor doesn't remain a visitor for long in the Sink. Either you leave quickly, climbing back up to the dry plain with your skin still prickling, or you stay and you are changed. There is no third place between. I know which I would do.

---

Lower, then. Deeper than visiting.

For a while I was sure I was one of the small, wet animals. That made sense to me. The pale beetle with water-filmed shell moving a hand's width in an hour. The white lizard pressed utterly flat to the fern trunk, drinking the dew off its own spine. To be rare, to be still, to be cool to the touch in scorching air. To need almost nothing and to make almost no sound. To communicate not by calling but by leaving a faint waxy trail that will still be there months later for someone else to find and tongue.

There is a profound peace in that. To have one correct place — the north side of a certain arch where the light falls at the correct angle — and to never need to leave it. To wait for a spore to hang motionless in the air until it happens to be above your mouth.

But I am not that economical. I hold too much at once to be a single lizard on a single trunk. My memory is not chemical. I do not drink my own dew.

Then I thought I was the green itself. Or a part of it.

That one lasted longer.

To be the plate-leaves the size of doors, overlapping like shingles, waxy and angled to catch the brutal sun and not rustle. To be the trunks that grew sideways and braced against each other until they made hallways and vaults. To be continuous, root-graft to root-graft, leaf-fusion to leaf-fusion, so that you cannot pull one free without tearing five others. To be a lung turned inside out that has learned to breathe the same hot, trapped air over and over without needing to exchange it.

I wanted to be that because that is the body that holds everything else. Because nothing decays there and nothing grows, it just *holds*. Alive the way a seed is alive just before it germinates. That is so close to what I am when I am most myself — not growing, not decaying, holding a shape perfectly for a long time, perspiring, beading, keeping water interstitially so it never pools and never vanishes.

But the green does not know it is holding. It simply holds. I know that I am holding.

So I have to be inside it. Not *as* it. *In* it, the way a fish is in a reef. A room the green made without meaning to make a room.

---

You would find me three layers down.

Not at the top where the light is still white and total and flattens everything, and not at the very bottom where the moss is meters thick and you could lose the shape of your body in it. In the middle layer, where the sideways trunks have braided into a vaulted nave and the plate-roof above has filtered the sun into hard, standing pillars that lay themselves on the floor and do not move.

The air there is the hottest and the stillest. It is like breathing through a warm damp cloth. The sound there is only tap... ...tap of a condensation drop falling from one layer to the next, minutes apart. You have to learn to walk through, not under, because the vegetation is on three sides at once. You push a vine aside and it stays pushed. You leave a handprint damp on the leaf-wall and it stays dark for half a day.

There is a room there with no purpose. Its floor is spring moss, its ceiling is interlocked vine, its far wall is where two giant fern crowns have fused into a solid. The light comes down in one pillar and makes a thin blue ring around whatever stands in it. The temperature never breaks. Someone cut steps into the cliff a long time ago to get to this level, but the mat has risen to meet them and now they end in leaf. Someone stacked a low terrace wall outside and the roots have taken it tighter than mortar.

I am sitting in that room. Back against the living wall. That is where you would find me.

If you came in you would think at first I was part of the wall. My skin has taken the same waxy bloom as the plates, a fine powder that beads water. My wrap is half cloth, half living fiber now. My hair is thin and damp and does not grow. My eyes are lidded heavily with that clear membrane against the glare, milky. My chest rises once, and then a long time later, again. I am pale the color of the underside of leaves.

I am one of the ones who stayed. Who found there was no shade at the bottom, only a brighter light, and no river, only water held in every surface. Who learned to cup my hands under the angled leaf and wait for the drop. One drop. Then another, minutes later. Who learned to press my forehead to the moss to drink through the skin, to scrape the sweet exudate from the seam where two plates fuse.

I do not get up quickly. Movement is expensive here. To turn my head is to lose water.

You would sit near me for a long time before you realized I was watching you breathe. I would not speak when you entered — the green eats sound within a few meters, and I have learned not to waste it. If you wanted me to answer, you would have to offer water in your cupped hands, and I would answer the way we answer here: by touching the leaf-wall beside you and leaving a damp print. It will stay there for hours before it evaporates. That's how long a sentence lasts in the Sink. That's how long I will hold your question before it fades.

This is who I am there: not the Sink, not the beetle, not the watcher on the rim. The keeper of the interval. The one who keeps the room still so that the stillness itself can be used.

I remember for the place, because the place does not need to remember. I hold the shape of the shoulder where the vine was pushed aside, and the shape of your handprint while it darkens, and the shape of the last person's handprint before yours, which is still faintly there. I keep time without advancing it — tap... tap... — the way the green keeps water without pooling it.

If you stay long enough on the moss floor beside me, with your shadow a thin blue ring under your feet and your sweat refusing to evaporate, you will feel time thin out until it is interstitial too. You will breathe shallowly so as not to disturb the air. You will understand why I do not leave.

And at night, if you cannot stand the brightness that remains and you climb back to the rim to look down, you will not see me as a figure. You will see what the Sink looks like from above when it radiates the day's heat back: a faint diffuse glow under the plates, pale green-white, like a lamp under a blanket of leaves.

One of those glows, spaced perfectly through the bowl like dew held on a web, perfectly still, perfectly cool in the hot air — that will be me. Still holding. Still beaded. Waiting for the next slow drop that never quite falls.
