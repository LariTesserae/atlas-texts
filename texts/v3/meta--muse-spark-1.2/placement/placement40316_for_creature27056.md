I don't find myself at first.

Not right away. If I'm honest, the first breath I take in that bowl isn't mine.

It's yours — the climber's — fast and shallow and wrong. Too much red heat behind it, too much hurry in the chest. I try you on and I can't keep you. Your boots are too hard against that living floor, your carbon breath too loud and too wet and too *single*. You stand at the lip where the bowl gives way to the kilometer of air, with the mat at your back and the wind cutting your face, and you are a rockfall. A temporary density. The bowl will flow around you or incorporate you, but you will not last there as you are. You burn too fast.

So I step back from the lip.

I try to be the rock. It would be easy. That young basalt is beautiful — that black, glassy, torn stone still showing where it shattered as it cooled, still faintly steaming with its own cold. But the rock is too young to know how to be, and too alone. It only holds. I want to hold and also to be held.

I try to be the pool in the center. Black and perfectly still, the size of a doorway, rimed with frost that never quite closes. That would be a good place to be — the center that is not a temple, just the most reliable water. Everyone rings you and never jostles you. But the pool doesn't breathe. It reflects. It waits to be drunk. I want to drink and to be drunk *with*.

So I sink a little lower.

At first I think I am the carpet itself. The whole breathing mat, thousands deep. That low hum in your chest that is not sound but pressure, the shiver of translucent wings that never open. It's tempting to be the collective, to be the twenty-second inhale that lifts the floor a fraction of an inch. And some part of me *is* — I have always been better at being a chorus than a solo.

But the bowl teaches slowness as survival. A chorus here is not loudness. It's patience. And patience has to be practiced by a body.

So I let the mat resolve. Let one shape separate from the texture the way it did for you when you knelt and your eyes adjusted in the star-thick indigo.

I am smaller than I thought I'd be.

I find myself not in the warm middle where the young are kept, translucent and downier, away from stone and void. Not there. I am not new. And not at the central pool, either, where the corona jostles head-to-tail. I couldn't stand that much wanting in one place.

I am on the wall.

On the north wall, the highest of the three vertical sides, where the striations run straight up and the trickle weeps slowest. I am clinging there like a shingle, one of the old ones, hooked feet dug deep into the sharp pitted basalt so I don't have to think about holding on anymore. My down is no longer white. It has gone that dirty grey where mineral dust has gathered over too many cycles of condensation and drying, and it is worn thin on the hump of my back where I've pressed against stone and against others for so long. My antennae are longer than my body and not as feathery as they once were — a few filaments broken off, but more sensitive for it.

This is where I would be. In the vertical. At the edge that is still shelter.

From here I can feel the whole bowl breathe without being lost in it. When we inhale — that long, patient draw — my body swells with the others and the frost on the rim shivers. When we exhale, the rustle of thousands of claws readjusting on stone runs up the wall through my feet. I don't have to see the pool to know it. I taste it on the air, that black stillness.

My work is small. That's why I like this spot.

A few inches to my left, in the most sheltered crevice the wall allows, there is one of the moss cushions. Not the lichen we scrape — that black-green crust — but the other one, the harder one, the tight rosettes of needle-filaments that hold a bead of water on every tip like a lens. There are only five or six in the whole bowl. This one is mine to tend, not to own.

I do not eat it. No one does, or not in a way you'd notice if you watched for only an hour. Maybe a single filament in a week. Robustness is more valuable than a meal. So I tend.

Every so long — and long here means a very long time — I unhook one forelimb, slow as honey, and brush. Feathered antennae out, tasting the grit that the night air sifts down. A fleck of basalt, a fallen flake of someone's down that would smother a tip if left. I flick it away. I clear the beads where two have merged and grown too heavy. I do it the same way every time. A hundred careful strokes that look like doing nothing.

And when the trickle finally swells — you can watch a bead form for minutes before it breaks and runs — I am already turned toward it. Not because I am quick. Because I have been waiting.

Sometimes the shard comes to me.

Not my shard. *The* shard. That flake of volcanic glass the mountain made when it tore, knapped to a cruel edge without any hand ever touching it. Someone brings it and sets it down on the stone near me when their limbs tire. I pick it up between my two forelimbs. I scrape. The lichen comes off as a fine, dark paste on the glass. I pass it back, limb to limb, to the smaller, paler ones below me who receive it without thanks or ownership, and when I am tired I set the glass down again exactly where I found it. It will be there tomorrow. Or someone else will have moved it a centimeter to cap a reservoir. That is our technology: a few kept things, circulating forever, never built upon.

I don't fly. The wings under my down — damp paper, amber-grey — shiver with the others to make heat, but they never open. The air is too thin and the room too crowded for showing off.

If you came back — you with your fast breath — you would not find me at first either. You'd see wall, and then, after your teeth stopped aching from the cold, you'd see that the wall was also covered. You'd have to look for the grey on grey, the worn hump, the feathery antennae that turn a fraction toward your exhaled moisture and then turn back, the way a tide knows a rock and then forgets it.

If you knelt and stayed, if you learned to breathe on our twenty-second count, we would flow around your boots and make you terrain. And I, up on my vertical strip, would finally have something new to tend for a little while — the strange, fast warmth at the lip — before you either left or learned to be slow.

Find me there. Five meters up on the north wall, where the water weeps, beside the hard green cushion that I keep alive. I am not the center. I am not the whole. I am the old shingle that holds long enough for something small and green to keep holding water.
