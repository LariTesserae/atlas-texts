I don't think I would find myself at first.

Not right away. That is the trick of the Shelf.

At first I think I would be up high — that seems natural. If you put me in this world I imagine myself standing on one of those whale-backed swells, where the basalt has been polished to a matte iron shine and the quartz veins run thick as frozen rivers. The air would be hard and honest there, and I could see a hundred kilometers to the next soft horizon. My breath would smoke. The wind would be a thin hiss over nothing to trip it. I would be a watcher, a walker. A figure on a ridge.

But that doesn't last. Nothing on the Shelf stays high for long. The wind sands it down. The cold seeps through the gloves. And after an hour of standing up there, the wrongness would start to press behind my sternum — that ecological silence. Kilometers of perfect, empty stone and then, far below, an absurd wound of green with no birds over it, no tracks around it. A watcher up high would have nothing to watch except weather making itself. I would get tired of being vertical. On the Shelf, vertical is a mistake the land has already corrected.

So I come down.

I think then I would be in the garden. Because the green is what pulls. After all that stone, the suddenness of it — the deep mattress of moss you can lose your arm in, emerald and rust and black-green, the dwarf willows lying flat and thick as a man's leg, the ferns as wide as doors breathing out warm condensation into the freezing air. I want to be there, in the damp. I think I would be a harvester, kneeling in the peat that squelches cold and tea-colored under the moss, combing it with an antler rake, wringing out a slab that steams faintly in my hands. It would be good work. Uncomplicated. The sour mineral smell of lichen bread baking on a hot stone.

But even that is not quite right. That's too... busy. Too separate. A harvester still thinks of the moss as *crop*, as something other than itself. And on the Shelf nothing is other. The people there don't farm the moss the way a farmer farms wheat. They farm it the way a hand tends its own skin — because there is no one else to do the grazing, the pruning, the pollinating. The wind can't do it all.

No. If I were really *of* this place, I wouldn't be the one who cuts. I would be the one who holds.

I find myself lower. Lower than the garden, even.

I am in the city, but not where you first look. Not moving along the terraces with the skin bags of black water, not tending the living fence. I am where the city meets the weird and has to make an accommodation.

You know the place. Every terrace has one. The stretch of pavement between the second and third level where you count seven steps one way and nine steps back. The courtyard where your voice returns a half-second *before* you speak, so you learn to speak low and flat to avoid interrupting yourself. The corner of a turf roof where condensation beads *up* off the moss and rises like slow inverted rain.

The builders have left a small cairn there. Not a warning. A marker. *Step over here.*

I am the one who remembers why the cairn is there.

I find myself with my palm flat against a dry-stone wall three meters thick at the base, leaning slightly inward. The stone is cold in a way that goes through the wrap on my hand. My eyes are closed. I am listening to the hum.

It's not a sound, really. It's the thing underneath the quiet — that 0.6 that is not silence but the absence of animal noise — the low, planetary thrum of rock trying to remember what shape it is supposed to be. The frost heaved this wall a centimeter last night. It will heave it again tonight. The water in the runnel beside my feet is climbing two degrees up the north rim and holding there, quivering. Space is a little stretched on this corner; if I stand here too long my left arm will feel a half-inch longer than my right.

I don't find this strange. I don't have a word for strange anymore.

My body has gone low to match it. I have become broad through the chest — a great barrel to wring thin air. My legs are short, my feet splayed and callused to grip the polished basalt better than boots. My skin is the pale grey-pink of stone scraped clean of lichen. My face has been smoothed, not wrinkled, by this endless late autumn; my eyes are quartz-grey, adapted to a hard, shadowless noon. My hands are wide, spatulate, nails blackened, palms crosshatched from fitting stone without mortar. When I kneel — and I mostly kneel, folding down knees-to-chest-to-ground because standing is expensive — I am the same height as the willow-mat.

I don't speak much. Thin air makes shouting wasteful. I tap. Two clicks of hammer on stone and the younger ones know to shift the lintel two inches left. I nod toward the cairn and they know to step over.

My house is half-sunken, roofed with turf that is still alive and growing. Inside, the back room is larger than the house that contains it. That's where I keep the winter moss. It never molds in there. A child born in that room will never think that's odd. She will think that's just where moss keeps best. When she drags her fingertips along the wall while learning to walk, she will be feeling for the frost-heave I felt this morning.

Who would I be?

Not a visitor. Visitors look up. Visitors expect birds.

I would be the function the Shelf was waiting for after it lost all its animals. Not predator, not prey. The tender. The pruner. The thing that resets one stone, harvests one slab, counts one street wrong on purpose, and does it again tomorrow without hurry.

I would not live *on* the Shelf.

I would live *with* it, low and level with it, like moss itself.
