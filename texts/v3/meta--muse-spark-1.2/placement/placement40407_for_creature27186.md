I don't know at first.

If you put me down in that boulevard between Two and Three, in the hard white column of the Fresnel sun, I think at first that I would be walking. That I would be one of the figures in the cork-coats, with the veil and the economical stride, trying not to stir the dust.

But I'm not walking. Walking is for going somewhere, and I don't have anywhere to go that quickly. The people who live here don't hurry. To hurry is to sweat, and they cannot afford sweat.

So I correct myself.

I am not on the floor at all.

At first I thought I would be something that flies - one of those great door-wide moths drifting on the processor wind from Column to Column, dusting everything with pepper-smoke scales. That would suit me. Pale, dry, dusty, reading the air without drinking it. But the moths don't stay. They are beautiful because they are transient. They live to cross the emptiness. I am not transient. I want to stay and watch.

Then I think I am one of the isopods, armored, scraping the root-felt, leaving a clean trail behind me. Patient, low work. But the isopod does not understand what it cleans.

No. Let it become more itself.

I find myself higher. Not on the mineral plates, but *in* the Column.

About two hundred meters up the side of Column Three, on the western face where the sun-shaft never quite hits directly and the light is therefore softer - the light of the photophore leaves far above and the amber of the refinery lamps below, mixed into a perpetual late morning. There is a hollow here that was not cut. It was coaxed.

You would miss it from the boulevard. From the floor, Column Three looks like a sheer cliff of bone-white and oatmeal, window after window, balcony after balcony. But about two thirds up, there is a place where the cork bulged wrong thirty years ago when a technician mis-mixed the hormone spray - a little too much iodine-sharp canister, a little too much coaxing - and the Column extruded a lip that curled back on itself, made a room like a blister, like the oil blisters but larger, and empty.

They didn't shave it off. Waste is not let. Instead they let it cure and then they lined it.

That is where I am.

I am not a harvester - I don't wait for the *whumph* of falling house-sized bark. I am not a tapper - my elbows are not amber to the joint from oil. I am not a child on his belly watching a root tip etch basalt, though I was once, and part of me still is.

I am what happens after those things have happened.

I am the Keeper of the Slough.

The walls of my blister-room are living cork on three sides, warm and faintly giving if you lean, smelling of camphor and iodine. The fourth side is open to the air, a balcony with an iron rail, looking out over the regional void - over the boulevard so far below that people are only dust-plumes, over to Column Two whose windows at this distance look like a vertical constellation. The processor wind moves through here constantly, cool, 8 or 9 degrees, hydrophobic dust hanging in it forever.

My work is this: when a sheet peels - that vast, soundless delamination high above - and the harvesters drag it to the mills at the base to be pressed into board and insulation and fuel, they always bring me the off-cuts. The thin, flawed pieces. The parts that cracked as they fell, too veined or too thin to be building board. Sheets the color of paper and bone, dry as old skin, rustling like nothing else rustles because there is no moisture in them to soften the sound.

I take them and I press them further. Not in the big industrial presses that thrumm and shake the whole trunk. In a small hand press in the back of the blister, iron and bone, that I pump with a foot pedal. Slowly. Economically.

I make paper. True paper. The only paper this dry world can make without water.

And then I write on it.

Not with ink - ink would require water and would bleed and run in this air anyway. I write with a stylus of heated iron, and with a little cake of refined Column resin, amber and clear. I sear the words into the cork-paper. It smokes faintly, a clean, medicinal smoke that the processor wind pulls out over the balcony and out into the cavern.

What do I write? The things that cannot be allowed to crumble.

The hormone recipes. *Three parts salt-liquor brine to one part auxin, mist fine, do not touch the swelling for six days.* The sound a Column makes two days before it is ready to be hollowed - *a low crackle, subsonic, felt in the teeth more than heard.* The names of the drones who have earned names scratched in iron on their sides, and which joints need oiling. The old word for damp that we almost let fall away, and why we kept it anyway.

I am not loud. My room is quiet the way the whole cavern is quiet - sound swallowed by acreage of cork. My stylus hisses. My press thumps, once, soft. The wind moves dust across my balcony in a hush.

I wear what they all wear - cork-cloth coat and hood, oiled canvas apron - but mine is stained not amber but brown-black where the hot stylus has kissed it. My hands have the same waxed sheen, the same bloom, rubbed in daily from rendered Column wax so I do not perspire. My lips are thin and kept closed. I wear a veil of cork-gauze when I go out to collect the off-cuts, and I take my few milliliters of salt-liquor brine in the morning like medicine, sharp on the tongue, and feel it go nowhere and everywhere because my body has learned to do chemistry without water.

I do not have many visitors. The density here is sparse by design. Families small and spaced far apart like Columns. But sometimes a boy climbs the hundred and ninety meters of stair cut into living tissue to bring me a flawed sheet, and he stays and watches me write. He doesn't speak - speech is low and short here, mostly hands - but he watches the iron kiss the paper and the words appear, brown on bone.

Sometimes an old drone picks its way up the outside of the trunk on many-jointed legs of chitin and actuator cable, and pauses on my rail to let me dab resin on a crack in its back-plate. We do not command each other. We have shared this shift for a long time.

This is where I belong in this world because it is a world that keeps everything by keeping it dry. Death does not rot, it cures. A dead moth bleaches intact for months. An elder in a niche in the root-felt wall stays paper-preserved, hands folded, for years. And memory here cannot be wet either. It cannot be fluid, or flushed, or wasted. It has to be sealed, pressed, seared.

So I sit in my blister that was a mistake that was kept, at the height where the sun-shaft's hard white and the leaf-light's pale glow meet, and I conserve.

If you stood on the boulevard far below and looked up, you would not see me at first. That is the first adaptation. You would think Column Three was empty, lights on, machines purring for no one. Then your eye would catch a flicker on a high balcony - not movement, but a brief, steady glow, amber, as I lift the iron again.

If you climbed long enough, and walked economical so as not to stir the dust, and came to my rail, I would look at you the way that woman looked at you - to see if you have found purchase.

I would nod, once. And then, because paper is scarce and iron is hot, I would offer you the stylus.

Not to write a lot. Just one word. Just enough to keep.
