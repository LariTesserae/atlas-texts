At first I thought I knew.

I thought I would be up high - on the second city, the scaffolding city. Pale wood still weeping sap, ropes creaking, hanging above the basin in that soot-orange dusk where you can finally see how far the Made Ground goes. I thought I would be one of the pulley-men, because I like to see how things connect. I thought I would be the watch-bird.

Then I stood there - in my head, I stood there - and I realized I don't have any distance vision. Nothing in me wants distance.

I am not built for height. The wind up there would be too thin. I would get vertigo from all that empty air between walls.

So I came down.

I thought maybe I would be a mason then. One of the true ones, who knows how to lay thirsty in the third hour after rain. The one with the broad, stone-colored hands, the hammer that rings *tink-tink-TAK* without thinking. But my hands, even imagined, are not those hands. My callus is not that thick yet. I tried to hold the hammer in my mind and my wrist ached. I would be the apprentice who hits wrong, who cracks the tile. They would move me elsewhere.

So I came lower, and narrower.

And that is where I started to find myself.

Not in the oldest quarter where the basalt is so tight you can't fit a fingernail between stones - that place is too perfect, too ancestral, too warm with the lime-buried dead. And not in the newest quarter where the clay tiles are still warm and sweating through your shoes, where everything smells of fresh lime and the work is all frantic laying.

I am in the middle thickness. Where old meets raw. Where a basalt wall from six generations ago has been patched with unfired brick, and the brick has been patched again with fungus-felt and render. Where the floor is half slick flagstone with the seep coming up black between the joints, and half hard-packed cinder that drinks the condensation.

You would find me in a rib-wide alley that is technically not an alley at all. It is the space left over between three houses, a kiln, and a cistern drain. It is too narrow for dragging stone, too damp for sleeping, too low for scaffolding. No one builds there, they just maintain it. That's why I am there.

I am the Keeps-Damp.

We don't have a real word for it - we tap it on pipe as *shh-shh-keep*. I am not a mason, not a hauler, not a kiln-tender. I am one of the ones who remembers the water.

I am small - smaller than most, even here. Short-legged, bowed, barrel-chested from breathing thin air that never fills. My skin is that fired-pale, clay-pale, the mushroom-underneath color that never washes clean of soot at the forearms. My hair is shaved to stubble because if I let it grow the white hairy fungus finds it. My clothes never dry. Inner felt of fungus-fiber, sour and warm even when wet. Outer apron of oiled leather with ten pockets. In them: not a hammer. A rag. A small stiff brush made from crawler hair. A wooden scoop. A tin cup for tasting the seep.

My work is this:

In the deep dusk, when the kiln-light is low and the hammering from three streets over sounds like it's inside my skull, I walk my ten meters of alley, again and again. I mop the condensation before it runs the wrong way. I watch where it beads. On a good day it runs down. On a heavy-air day after rain, when space gets thick, it beads and climbs, or hangs in the air a moment too long before falling. I learn not to lay square then. I learn to lay thirsty.

I clear the drain. That's the real work. The drain is a hand-cut channel in the flags, no wider than my hand, running to the cistern. In it live the pale, forearm-long things, segmented, farming their white mold. Most people step over them. I tend them. I harvest only half the fungal hair, stroke their backs with my brush so the mineral salts come off clean. They go still when I do it, which is the only stillness this loud place allows. A child once asked me if they have names. I told her we don't name what we work with; we remember its shape.

I listen.

That is why I am good at this narrow place. My eyes are large and amber-brown and useless past thirty meters, but my close vision is perfect. I can see the pock of a pick from a hundred years ago still sharp in the wall. And my hearing - I tilt my head to the stone the way the old masons taught me, and I can hear the scaffold pole two alleys over starting to split, the kiln three roofs down drawing too much air, the cistern filling too fast before anyone sees the rain coming through the fissure above.

I speak rarely, and when I do it is in taps. Two taps on the drain edge means *water coming, lift your sacks*. Three taps on the wet wall means *leave this joint alone, it's swelling*. The alley carries sound so well I never need to shout.

At night - which is not night, but the half-shift when the hammers thin - I don't sleep in a niche by the kiln like the others. It's too hot for me. I curl in my alley itself, against the wall that sweats, on a ledge that is someone else's foundation, under a roof that is someone else's floor. I can feel feet moving six inches above my head. I can feel the whole reef breathing. The hammering never quite stops, so I hum in the gaps, that low timing hum that lets the haulers pull together around corners. My hum is not melodic. It is practical. It says *stay, stay, stay.*

If you left my alley alone for a week, I know what would happen. The flagstone I mop every hour would tilt upright, trying to stand. The wall I brush would lean inward a degree and not fall. The drain would clog with lime and the water would start running uphill. The room at the end would be an arm's length larger when you returned, and a little colder.

So I don't leave it alone.

And if I died - which here is not dramatic, just a dry stone cough that doesn't stop - they wouldn't carry me to any lip or burial ground. There is no ground that wasn't made. They would clean me with lime, as is proper, and lay me under a new flag in my own alley. I would become part of the Made Ground at last, not as a wall or a house, but as the drain itself. And the next small, shaved-headed, barrel-chested child who takes my rag and my brush would put their ear to the stone above me and swear it was warmer there, and a little damper, and would mop a little more gently.

You would find me low, not crouching but pressed. In the narrowest thickness, where the water remembers to run downhill only if someone watches it. I would be the one whose hands twitch even in sleep - not hammering, but wringing, brushing, scooping. The one who tends what grows in the cracks so the city can keep forgetting that it should not exist.

You would have to bend down to see me. And then you would have to be quiet, so you could hear the drip, tick, trickle under all that ringing - and hear me tap back, *I am still here. This stone is still flat. This water is still ours.*
