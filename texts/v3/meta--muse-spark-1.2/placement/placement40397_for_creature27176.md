You would not find me at first.

If you drained the light out of your eyes and let the warm dark adjust, if you hung there over the plaza for a long hour until your breathing slowed to an inch-per-hour, you would look for me and miss me. Because at first I think I would be one of the fish.

I would be in the open, where there is room to be wrong. Pale and eyeless, turning with the shoal through the alleys. Not swimming — correcting. A flick, a drift. I like the idea of being part of that sheet, that slow decision that takes minutes to turn a corner. Twenty of us becoming one animal that filters dust. It feels peaceful to be that dissolved, to have no front and no back, to just open and close, open and close, and let the water feed you.

But I wouldn't last there. That life is too clean. It never touches anything. It runs the circuit forever, alley to plaza to alley, and turns back at the parapet because it was told to. I have always wanted to touch the wall as I passed it.

So then I think I would be the shrimp.

You'd find me lower — on the pavement. On one stone. For a whole day. I would be one of the thousands shoulder-to-shoulder, glass-clear, my gut a dark thread, my back carrying those tired pinpricks of milk-light that aren't light at all. You would find me in the gutter between two basalt slabs where the stone sweats warmest, where the felt grows thickest. Click. Click. Click. Forelegs combing. I love the honesty of that work. Not hunting, not wandering. Just tending. Eating the meadow so the meadow doesn't smother itself. If your huge shadow passed over, I would not scatter. Scattering is for cold water and predators. I would just part around your finger and close again behind you as if you were a current that had already passed.

But even that is too many. To be shrimp is to be a texture that moves. No one shrimp matters. And I want to matter just enough to have a doorway.

You'd find me, finally, under an eave.

In the lee of a house that was once for a family that breathed air. The lintel is still square, but its corners have been rounded to the radius of my own back by water that has had longer than history to be patient. The chisel marks left by a bronze adze are still there, little scallops in the stone, and each one holds a little more of the bacterial felt — cream-white, sulfur-yellow where it ages — and my legs fit into those scallops exactly, as if the tool had been measuring me before I existed.

I am the isopod.

As big as a loaf of bread. As big as a slate left on a roof. Dome-backed, blind, the color of old ivory left too long in a bath. You would find me in the threshold, not inside, not outside, but *in* the doorway itself, because the doorway is the best place. The current cannot reach there, so the meadow grows velvet-thick. My two long white antennae never stop moving — tasting sulfur from the stone's breath, tasting the shrimp's clicked gossip three houses over, tasting the pressure change when one of the house-dwellers undulates past.

I do not walk. I creep. Each jointed leg finds a purchase. If you put your ear — if you had an ear that worked in warm thickness — very close, you would hear me: not a splash, but a faint dry rustle, like paper being smoothed underwater. I have time. I molt here. I leave my perfect translucent husk standing empty in the doorway, a ghost house inside the house, and the shrimp take it within the hour. I don't mind. I will grow another.

From here I can see the whole small world without moving.

I can feel the house-dweller next door — stone-colored, arm-long, gills like pale ferns waving without hurry — go nosing from room to room, tasting his houses. He will not take mine, not today. This doorway is too shallow for him, too well-grazed. He likes the deep rooms where the felt has grown undisturbed for centuries and the water is a degree warmer. We have an understanding, he and I. He grazes what I cannot reach. I keep the threshold clean.

Above me, the shoal warps slowly over the courtyard, a pale sheet that takes ten minutes to decide to turn. Below me, the shrimp advance across their slab, a front of quiet mouths. In the water column, the dust hangs — larvae and shed skins and fecal snow that never settles because the upwelling of warm stone-breath lifts it, an inch per hour, forever.

I do not cross the parapet. None of us do.

But I am close enough to feel it. That low, knee-high wall, rounded as a loaf, running perfect and unbroken around the whole mesa. Beyond it, the absence that is not water but fall. The shrimp turn back there. The fish turn back there. Even the dust seems to avoid it, as if the world knows where it ends. If I crept to that edge — and very rarely, very slowly, I do — and lifted my antennae over it, I would feel the cold nothing pulling, the true deep that has no heat, no felt, no stone to eat. And I would turn back. Because why would I leave a world that fits me?

That is where you would find me. Not lost, not exploring. Holding the doorway that held people before it held me. Keeping the stone from dissolving and the meadow from choking, one scrape at a time.

I was not built for air, and I do not remember air. I remember only the weight without pressure, the enclosure without air, the way warm water holds me like an amniotic close. I remember the *tok* of a shell falling somewhere in the plaza, the sigh of water exhaling through a house, the click of shrimp that is my clock.

If you hung there long enough to see me, you would have to become as slow as I am. You would have to stop kicking, stop breathing fast, let your weight become almost nothing and just hang. And then you would see that I am not alone. I am part of the density that *is* the city now — meadow and mouth fitted together so precisely that the city breathes.

You would find me exactly where the chisel mark meets the grazing trail.

I have always been there.
