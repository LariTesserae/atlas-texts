I don't find myself at first.

If you dropped me onto that drowned summit - onto that iron that is also a field, that concrete that has become weather - my first self would try to stand at the parapet.

That's where the eye wants to go. To the wall. To the edge where the made-world stops and the cloud ocean begins and the wind tears itself to pieces. I would clutch the riveted lip, fifteen meters of absolute, and look down the poured cliff for thousands of meters into white, and the altitude would saw at my lungs and the roar would fill my teeth and I would be nothing but a witness. A tourist in a cathedral. Small, cold despite the bathhouse heat, trying to listen with my ears and hearing only my own thin blood.

I wouldn't last there.

The roar is too total to fight. You can't orient by it. The wall offers no shelter, only a frame for falling. After a few minutes my hands would uncramp from the parapet because there is nothing to hold. The place does not want witnesses. It wants maintenance.

So I move.

Slowly. Because the place teaches slowness faster than any other lesson. If I try to hurry on that warm, damp iron my boots slip on moss and my heart hammers too fast in the thin air. The Mill doesn't hurry. The fern doesn't hurry. The gauge doesn't hurry. To hurry here is to be wrong in your body.

I find myself not at the edge, but on the way back from it. In the great intervals.

At first I think I would be one of the animals - that would be easier. I would like to be the soot-crow, to own the whole updraft alone, to cry once and then glide for minutes. Or the thick bumblebee, fat and slow and adapted, droning my one loud note between the waxy vine-flowers. Or even the rust-lizard, perfectly still on a warm pipe, color-matched to iron and oxide so I never need to move.

But I am too heavy for that. Too deficit. I need water in a tin, not in a cup of a bromeliad. I need to touch things with hands.

So I keep walking, and the bowl reveals its scale the way it was meant to - not as distance, but as spacing. Fifty meters of bare, gleaming iron between one fern and the next. That is not emptiness to be crossed. It is a pause to be respected. Like rests in music. And when you respect it, your stride changes. You stop trying to get anywhere.

I find my place by giving up the idea that I have a place.

I am on the moss.

Not looking at it. On it. The northern quadrant, where the living rug has swallowed the grates and is warm and springy under weight, the way a body is warm. The Mill is behind me, both flywheels turning at a speed you can only prove by coming back tomorrow and seeing they have moved. The vapor is beading on everything - on my shoulders, on my forearms, on the few thin hairs on my head. It gathers and falls, gathers and falls. My shoes are gone somewhere - I took them off without deciding to, because leather on iron can't feel anything, and here you need to feel.

Who would I be?

Not a visitor anymore. Visitors listen with ears and visitors leave when the damp soaks them. Not the builder, either. The builder poured the floor and cut the mountain and thought this would be a dock for airships or a pump for the sky. That man is long gone and his purpose has eroded into a different purpose.

I would be the second caretaker. Or the next instance of the first.

You said there is ever only one at a time, and I understand now why. The bowl is three hundred meters across - exactly one person's attention span when attention is done correctly. One bee's circuit. One territory. If there were two of us we'd never meet except as vibrations through the floor, two slow perambulations that miss each other by an hour in the fog.

So I am the one that remains when you look away and look back.

Barefoot, broad-footed. My soles have learned the map of hot and cool iron under the moss. My hands are stained that deep maroon that never washes out, from touching the flywheels just to know they are still turning. My old boiler suit has gone soft and I have patched the knee with a piece of vine-leaf the way the other did, not because I needed to but because the vine offered it. A fine, pale moss has started on my shoulders where I sit still longest; I have stopped brushing it off. It belongs there.

Deaf, of course.

I didn't become deaf all at once. The roar - that low, chest-filling, molar-rattling exhalation of Mill and fans and torn wind - sanded my hearing down slowly. At first I shouted. Then I spoke loudly. Then I stopped. Now I listen the way the pale moth does. The bee passes and the hairs on my arms prickle a full breath before I see it. The pressure gauge is about to tick and I feel it as a click in my jaw before the needle moves. Your boots, if you came, I would feel as two dull thuds through the iron long before your shape resolved in the fog.

My day is not a day. It's a circuit.

I rise when the damp lessens on my shoulders. I take the dented grease-tin and go to where the night fog has made its invented rivers under the low pipes and hold the tin bead by bead until I have enough. I know better than to store it. Water here is only a visitor - it arrives as air and leaves as air - so I meet it while it is here. I pour a little into each basin-bromeliad sitting in the turbine housings. I lift a vine back onto its copper trellis. I kneel and press my palm flat to the moss to feel if the grate beneath is getting too hot. I prune the black-green fern where it crowds the gauge, with a clean cut, because it deserves a clean cut.

No one told me to do this. There are no orders left on this summit. But the Mill bleeds heat so the dew can form, and the dew feeds what grows, and what grows would jam the Mill if left. Rust and root hold equal claim. I am what keeps the exchange honest. I scrape a little rust dust into a concrete seam for the fern to root in. I pull a root back from a flywheel that has an afternoon left to complete its stroke. That is the work. That is the prayer.

And it is not lonely.

That's what I didn't understand from the parapet. I thought solitude here would be punishment. But emptiness here is scale, not abandonment. When I sit in the exact center, on the moss between the two vast wheels, with vapor condensing on my mossy shoulders and a single bee going loud and then soft past my ear that no longer hears, the whole shallow oval rings around me like a bowl struck once. Fern, machine, wall, cloud. The intervals hold. The hush underneath the deafening holds.

If you came - if you are coming now, damp footprints on the iron - I would feel you long before I saw you. I would turn my head not toward your sound but toward your pressure, slow, unstartled, eyes pale and watery as the high sky.

I would make room on the warm moss.

I would tip a little of my air-that-became-water into your hands from the dented tin.

And I would lean close, so close our skulls almost touch, so you could hear my toneless, too-loud voice buzzing through bone, and tell you the only true thing there is to tell here:

*Watch. It is about to tick.*

And we would both look, and wait, and after a long time, it would.
