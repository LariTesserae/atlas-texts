You wouldn't find me at first.

That's the nature of the plateau. At first you look and you think there's no one here at all — just the concrete going on until it becomes weather, just the ticking of water on water, just the high pale haze that turns out, when you squint, to be wings.

If you were looking for me quickly, you would miss me. And no one here moves quickly.

---

At first I think I would be up high. In the air, because the air here is so thick with living. I think I would be one of the swifts — soot-colored, needletail, never landing except to cling vertically to a wall that weeps. Hanging in the updraft off the polished edge where the water goes over in veils and becomes cloud again. It would be simple to be that. To be part of the weather. To kiting and shrieking and never have to learn the short-stepped gait.

But I don't have the bones for that. My bones are heavy. I like weight. I like things I can see how they work — belt drives, rivets, porcelain insulators.

Then I think maybe I would be down low, in the dust. One of the pill beetles, iron-black, slow and ticking across a damp wall scraping the bacterial paint with a mouth like a rasp. There are billions of them. It would be honest work, drinking condensation and being food in turn. And I do feel some kinship with them. That patient, layered way of being on stone.

But I am too loud. I need to make a sound over the sound. Beetles don't shout.

So let it come slower. Don't settle.

Where would you find me? You would have to listen for me before you see me.

You would find me not at the edge — though I know the edge is there, two weeks' walk past District 47 if the switching yard isn't blocked — not staring over it. The people who live near the edge have their backs to it. They hang laundry in the updraft. That's right. I wouldn't be looking out. I would be looking in, at the drip rate.

You would find me inside the volume, not beside it. On the third level up from the dust, where the aqueduct runs.

It's not a Roman arch. It's a long concrete trough on stilts, humming, carrying grey water from one district's weep-catch to another's cistern. It ticks. Everything here ticks, seeps, rills. But this has its own tone when you tap it. I walk it.

I think I've been left behind long enough to be a water-tender. Or a listener. We don't have priests. We have listeners.

I wear what everyone wears because everything weeps — waxed canvas gone dark at the shoulders, rubber boots that have never fully dried and never will, an oilskin apron that has gone soft as skin. When I come inside I hang it on a peg by the door where it drips into a tray that drains back into the cistern. It will not be dry in the morning. I stopped expecting it to be dry years ago.

My skin is pale under the high white sun because I live under cloud and sodium light. My fingertips are faintly macerated, always. The small of my back is always damp. Along my forearms there is a faint orange-yellow rash, harmless, where the film decided to live on me too. *Fusca plateauensis* or whatever we used to call it before we stopped naming things separately from ourselves. It paints the walls, and it paints me. I wash, I dry carefully, it comes back. I leave it. My nails are thickened and yellowed from the mineral water. I can taste the hardness of a cistern and know if the scale is building again.

You would find me at hour-that-is-not-an-hour, when the cloud sea arrives — not rises, *arrives* — dragging its belly across the flat and beading on every vertical face. The weeping doubles. The birds wheel tighter. My trough starts to sing a little higher.

I walk with a long pole. Not for balance, though the walkway beads and the concrete stair in the center is worn into a shallow U that holds an eighth of an inch of water permanently. We all learned that gait as children — short steps, weight forward, never hurry or you fall and crack something on basalt under concrete. I walk that way now without thinking. The pole is for tapping.

*Ton-ton-tonk.* Every ten meters. Listening for the change in tone that means the mineral scale has constricted the throat. The water here is distilled on cold iron, pure when it beads, hard as soon as it runs. It leaves its geology inside the pipes. If I don't clear it, the bacterial sheets thin out downstream, and the beetles go hungry, and the gulls lay fewer eggs, and the balance tips.

So I listen. The City drones under me — a turbine the size of a house turning somewhere with a low thrum I feel in my sternum, belt drives slapping, a ventilation fan pushing warm wet air from my district to the next — and over it the fauna tears the air, the kiting shriek of a hundred thousand wings, the chitter of lice on fungal leather, the clatter of beetle shells on concrete. To tell you what I'm hearing I have to pitch my voice up and over it. I have the permanent slight shout. The slight loss in my left ear by now. I read your lips without knowing I do.

When I find the dull spot, I stop. I take the hammer from my belt — head worn round as a river stone — and I hit the concrete. Not hard. Patiently. Scale falls away inside like old bone. Water finds its runnel again. The tone clears. *Ton-ton-tonk.* Good.

I share the trough with others. That's the part you get wrong longest if you're not from here. You think sharing a narrow walkway with animals means fighting. It doesn't.

While I tap, there are twelve stone-colored lizards pressed around the warm seam where the trough leaks heat, throats pulsing. There are three high-plateau rats moving along the wall in their river, parting around my boots like water around stones. There is a crust of pill beetles working the weep on the underside, and a swift clinging vertically under the next span, watching me with one eye. None of us is in the way. A beam has a nest and a conduit and my bunk under it. An exhaust vent has lizards and rats and an old man warming his hands. We have negotiated this over a very long time. It is not tolerance. It is architecture.

Later, when the nine-day cycle turns and the cloud thins, I will go down to the cellar under our block. My sister tends the trays there, ten high under amber work-light, the beetles moving in a slow ticking mass. I will bring her the bacterial sheets I scraped this morning off the sheltered wall behind the power station — wet paper that smells of iron and brine, to be dried, powdered, pressed into cakes. Four days for a cake. Eleven days for a beetle generation. A year for rust to deepen one shade. That's how we measure time here. Not hours. Curing times.

We will eat. Beetles roasted until the carapace cracks, fungus fried like an omelet, earthy and metallic, a gull egg taken on rotation — two taken, one left, because the guano is the geology we stand on. We will eat it with more salt than you would like. We will oil our boots.

At night the sodium lamps along the elevated causeway never turn off. The City glows amber, not neon. Nothing flashes. Everything hums. The water ticks under everything else. If I want to tell my sister something intimate — that I heard the turbine go slightly flat today, that I think the trough on 47 will need jacking again soon — whispering is useless here. I will lean in until our foreheads touch and speak into the bone.

I am not lonely. There is nowhere to be alone here. Even the stone has something living on it.

Especially the stone.

If you came looking for me, you would have to stand on that beading walkway in your new boots, learning not to hurry, raising your voice over the surf of wings and machines, and you would see me not as a figure against the landscape but as part of the layering — damp, broad, unhurried, colonized lightly, tapping and listening to keep something that should have failed a hundred years ago still turning, still weeping, still feeding.

I was not selected for speed. I was left behind by erosion because I agreed to keep the shape.
