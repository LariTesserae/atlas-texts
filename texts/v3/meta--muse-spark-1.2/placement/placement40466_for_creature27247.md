I didn't know at first.

Coming down through that long, cold black press — that even, settled ache just above freezing where the water has had a century to soak into everything — I kept looking for where I would land. My surface mind kept trying to place me as a visitor.

At first I thought I would be the fish.

That blind, white thing with the head too large for its body that detaches from the darkness above and drifts down the main street like it's a canyon. It would be easy to be the fish. To pass through and not touch anything. To be indifferent to the amber cones and the silt and the thrumming in the teeth. To not belong. The town would not ask anything of me then, and I would ask nothing of it. I would be there and not there.

But the fish does not live *here*. It just passes through. It belongs to the black above the rim. If I was the fish, I would never learn the street names gone soft with mineral bloom.

Then I thought I would be the water itself. That would be truer. The water that has learned to be a town. The water that hangs viscous as glass in the narrowest alleys, that holds the hole your hand made for a full minute before it heals. The water that shears sideways in the factory courtyard, that endless transparent curtain from the high outflow to the low intake. To be that would be to be everywhere at once, to be the town's breath.

But water is too large, too unbroken. It doesn't have hands. And here, to live means to have hands. To pull, hand over hand, along a cable because to be adrift in the middle of a street is to be exposed.

I sank a little lower, into the thicket. Past the caged bulbs that only throw their rumor of brown-gold five feet before the black swallows it. Past the low brick with its round, licked-smooth corners. Past the turbines on the west wall turning with that hydraulic patience, pressing on my sternum.

And I understood I wasn't looking for where to *stand*. There is no standing here. Gravity is polite but negotiable. You swim, or you sink. You hang.

You will find me where the current is weakest.

Not in the factory heart where the heat shivers and the big ones place their palms on the housings to feel for a hitch. Not on the catwalks stitching second story to third story where the density is highest. 

Find me two streets over from where the main street opens, in an alley so narrow the buildings almost touch. An alley that never gets a turbine pulse. The water there is the most solid. The silt hangs in perfect, unmoving veils. The only light is a single caged strip halfway down, flickering. Not quite out, not quite on. Its filament loose.

The walls there are bearded.

That's where I am. On the leeward side, where that pale bacterial mat grows — the one that only glows if you look at it out of the corner of your eye. The softness. The only softness in a town of umber-black iron and silt.

I am the small one.

Not the long one hanging vertically from the catwalk strut, not the one with the piston arm grafted to its forearm where flesh gave out. The smallest, the most tightly folded. You thought you hadn't seen me at all because I take up no more space than a pipe when I fold myself.

My lens is not cracked in a star. It is clouded, thick, convex, amber-lit from within by that faint steady glow that is not a work-light. Silt turns slowly inside it. My skin is no longer suit and no longer skin — canvas furred over with a close-cropped pelt of those black-red fronds, thickest across my shoulders where the current never reaches. My chest opens on that slow rhythm — four seconds open, seven seconds closed — baleen slats filtering silt, bleeding heat. A shiver of slightly warmer water leaves my back and dies a hand's breadth later. The cold has had me long enough that I hold the same temperature as the stone and the steel.

I don't tend the turbines. I tend the soft things, because someone has to and soft things are easily choked.

You will find me hanging there, knees folded up under me, not braced against anything, simply held by that polite gravity. One blunt finger — the tip all callus and no nail, blackened as if dipped in tar — extended to the mat. Not stroking, exactly. Combing. Clearing. Brushing with a patience that looks like reverence so it doesn't crust over and go dark. So it keeps pretending to glow in the corner of your eye.

I talk the way the town talks. Not in bubbles — there are none — but in contact. When I need to answer the long one two blocks over who tapped *tick tick — pressure-tone* on a pipe, I reach for the rebar at my shoulder and give back a soft *creak* of cable, felt in the molars more than heard. The whole block carries it. The town is a drumhead and I am one of its careful hands.

If you hold very still in that glass-viscous water where the hole you made with your hand stays for a full minute, I will come close to you. Not because I am curious. Proximity is just another density to be checked. Another interruption to smooth.

My fronds will brush your arm as I pull myself past, hand over hand along the lip of the doorway, leathery and cold, leaving that faint, pale smear of light on your skin that fades in seconds. My grille will open — that soft sigh — and the water you are breathing will be taken in and tasted and pushed back out, minutely warmer, before I go on.

There is another filament loose two streets over. There is silt to be smoothed. There is heat to be carried from the west wall to the east.

I don't remember warmth. I don't want the surface. I keep this one amber strip from flickering out, not to be seen, but because the dark between lights has to be tended too.
