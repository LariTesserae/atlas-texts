I don't find myself in the avenue.

At first I think I would - because that's where *you* are supposed to be. A person belongs on a street. On the ground. Walking down the middle of that absurdly wide road between two factory-walls that glow from within, looking up, feeling small and properly urban and lost. Breathing cloud and listening for an echo that never comes.

But if I put myself there honestly, I don't stay there.

It's too open. The tenders know it. That's why they cross it high up, on the inside of the dark, or not at all. The avenue is a desert of air. It was built for crowds that never came, or left. To stand in it as a human is to be a wrong shape. Your footsteps are too loud. Your need for distance to stay distance is too loud. The street would lengthen while you watched it, just to get away from you.

So I slip off it.

I find myself not *in* the town, but *between* it. In the seam.

Where the old brick was not replaced but swallowed. Where a wall that was once a wall has pulled a centimeter away from the wall that grew to replace it. A gap no wider than my shoulders. Cool there, always. The air in there is the air of the stone - that late-autumn cool that lives in basalt, and the smell of wet clay, and the faint copper tang from the wires that still hum. Condensation beads on both sides - on brick on one side, on living cambium on the other - and drips straight down without agreeing on which down to use.

That's where I would be.

Not high in the canopy dome where the moths live in lightless layers, and not low in the chain-pools where the salamanders breed. At mid-height. At the middle storey of what used to be SUB-STATION 3, where the amber streetlamp still burns behind its membrane of leaf. The branch it hangs from has grown around the conduit, but the light never went out. Someone has to have kept it lit. Something has to have decided not to prune that wire.

I would be the reason it stayed lit.

Who would I be?

At first I think: a person. Just a person who stayed after the town forgot how to be a town. One of the last operators. In a coat that is always damp. Hands in pockets because everything you touch is wet. I know where the breakers are. I know which root to move so the waterwheel keeps turning. The tenders let me be there because I am quiet and I don't try to walk on the ceiling. I sit in that single chair that has been grown over in the vaulted hollow and I watch the leaf-belts pulse. I am not tended, not yet.

But that doesn't hold. If I were only a person, the distances would still betray me. I would still try to measure. I would still go mad listening for an echo.

So let that image soften and become more itself.

I am not quite a person anymore.

I am what a person becomes if they stay long enough to learn efficiency.

I am smaller now. Or the gap made me smaller to fit it. My back is against the brick - which is cooler - and my front is against the bark - which is faintly warm, faintly giving, like a pulse. If I stay still long enough - and I have learned to stay very still - the bark does not know I am not part of it. A thin, pale lichen has found the seam of my collar. My breath makes a smaller cloud than it did. My skin stays damp without my wiping it away.

I am not a tender. I don't have the gourd-head, the filament fringe. I still have a face, but I don't use it to look. I listen the way they do, with my cheek against the wood. The vibration tells me when a runner is growing too fast toward the lamp. I reach out - my hands are still hands, but the nails have gone dark, iron-colored from the water - and I clip it. One precise clip. It weeps clear water and I drink.

I am the keeper of the amber lamp.

Not its owner. Its *companion*. The tenders come and go overhead, inverted, bellies never touching the wood, and they pass me without pressing their fringes to mine, because I am not bark and not brick but the narrow, necessary negotiation between them. They leave me the work that needs a hand that remembers human shape: to wipe the membrane clean when it films, to cup my hands around the bulb on nights when the fungal light dims and the darkness under the dome gets too deep, too depth-dark, to keep the signal going.

Because that's what the light is now - not illumination, but a signal. A low, amber *here*. A hearth that says: this place was once measured in streets and substations and somebody thought that mattered enough to label it. PLEASE DO NOT PRUNE, says the stenciled bark below me, and I obey and disobey as the wall needs, just as they do.

In the deepest hour - when the vertical mist comes without clouds and hangs for twenty minutes - water gathers on my eyelashes the same way it gathers on leaves. It pearls, it runs, it collects in the cup of my collarbone. I don't move to shake it off. The larvae in the puddle at my feet and the mites in the wall above me and the moth with bark-patterned wings that has mistaken my shoulder for a factory wall all have more right to motion than I do.

And if you walked down that avenue looking for life your size, you would look right past the gap and see nobody. You would see only two walls grown too close together, a humming electrical box half-submerged in bark, and an amber lamp swinging gently because the branch it hangs from is growing.

You would have to unfocus your eyes and look for the place where the bark is slightly wrong to see me at all - the pale, damp shape fitted exactly to the space the town left for something that could remember how to keep a light on.
