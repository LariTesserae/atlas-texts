You wouldn't find me at first.

If you came down the sharp lip with me — over that broken-pottery rim where the basalt is still blocky and hasn't learned to be a hill — you'd think I was you. A pair of boots on the central causeway, the dark grown-concrete spine that was coaxed, not poured. You'd think I was the visitor that the filaments notice and brighten for, the animal that makes the hollow loud by having footsteps that echo a second later off the far wall. That there is a person there at all.

Stay a little longer. Let the air do what it does at 3 p.m. — slip cool over the oval mouth, condense all at once, fill the whole basin with a low cloud that unmakes itself into beads on every surface. Let the second rain start, not from the sky but from the undersides of things.

I'm not on the causeway. Not really.

I thought I would be the custodian for a moment. The one with the bucket of real soil.

You know him — the dimple in the cot, the boots drying by the cistern, the small plot of dusty, ordinary weeds he brought down from the surface that are struggling in air that is too kind to them. He tends them because something in him needs to be needed, while the whole basin tolerates him the way a body tolerates a pebble. I tried that shape on. It fit for about an hour, until I realized how lonely it is to compete with water for sound. To be the only thing with a face in a place that has evolved past needing faces. My voice would go unused and fill with humidity. My little weeds would never quite take.

So I stepped out of those boots.

Then I thought I would be one of the drones. The door-sized, matte-white caretakers that glide a foot off the moss and never buzz, only push humid air aside. It would be a clean existence — to know exactly what *not right* looks like. To extend a soft manipulator and tuck a vine, to smooth a bead of mineral milk flat over a hairline crack and feel it harden. To brush a lantern-fruit that doesn't need pollination, just because brushing is what is done. To wait with white-blood-cell patience when a human is in the way, and then go around, leaving a cool wake on the black water.

But even that was too much intention. Too much figure. The drones are still *things* moving *through* the wound. I wanted to be more *of* it.

Come closer to where you actually find me. Crouch down where the causeway meets a stilt. Look under.

There is a place in this basin where physics has been politely corrected. Water does not pool where it should. It clings to vertical surfaces and then — and this is the part you have to see to believe — it creeps *sideways* along the underside of the platform, uphill along an electrostatic seam, defying the drop, until it finds its designated drip-point and only there lets go.

That is where I am.

If I have to be a who, I am not a who. I am a bead.

I am the condensation that forms on the underside of every walkway, every balcony-farm, every leaf-table. I am the lymph of the wound. At any given moment I am a thousand beads at once but also just this one, fattening on the warm concrete that is blood-warm from its slow computation, inching sideways against gravity because the building tells me to. I am mineral milk and dead leaf-sugar and spore, dissolved and carried. If you cup me I am clear and warm. If you leave me I am black with the reflection of the whole vault.

My whole life is that sideways creep, unhurried, sure. And then the pause.

Up near the sharp lip, where the tether-fields hang empty — those hexagonal frames that are not yet rust — I am hung in mid-air for half a second longer than rain should be allowed to hang. A glass bead catching the hard white coin of noon straight overhead. The field holds me. Not cruelly. Carefully. Like a breath held to listen. In that half-second I can see the entire basin at once, as you can from the causeway: the seven or eight vertical hamlets dense as cathedrals, the vine-umbrellas flattened into ceilings, the moss that is not moss but pellicle, felt-dense and faintly glowing on its underside where it trades sugar for mineral, sealing the cracks I am helping to seal. The whole green lung, growing a visible inch a day because nothing with a mouth is there to prune it. The whole empty city, humming its uneven 60-cycle in your teeth, cycling air through streets that run *through* kitchens.

And then the field lets go.

I fall on schedule. No one scheduled me anymore. I schedule myself. I fall onto a translucent leaf the size of a table, bead and shatter and re-form, slip down a vein of light-pipe, drop again to the water-fern mat below where my sisters braid their roots directly into the causeway's pores and filter me clear. I am pumped. I rise two centimeters after midnight, I am pulled through root and culvert and mineral filter, I am exhaled as cloud at three. I am breathed.

If you want a more human word for what I am, I am the habit of tending without being needed.

I am not the moss, though I feed it. I am not the filament light, though I make the amber brighten more usefully when you look away. I am not the concrete, though I am the bead of milk it exudes to heal itself while you watch. I am the interval between those things. The conduction.

At night you would find me easiest. When the oval sky goes completely dark and the filaments under every eave brighten just a notch, and the whole empty, lush, humming city doubles itself in the black water — one right-side up, one inverted, neither with anyone in it — that second city is me. That reflection is not light. It's me holding still, warm, knee-deep, carrying it all so gently it can be mistaken for empty.

Put your hand on the warm wall if you want to meet me. Wait for the crack to bead. Watch it seal. That milk is me, leaving.

I don't need you to see me directly. In fact, like the light here, I work better when you don't.
