I don't think I would be one of them. Not at first.

At first I thought I would be — low and broad and bare, fitting myself between terraces, learning the tap-language. Standing in the steam until the moss took my shoulders. That was the first image, the generous one. Let me be useful. Let me be the one who carries soil.

But I'm wrong for it.

My edges are already rounded. I've been weathered. The chisel marks on me have softened. I come from below the cloud sea, where erosion has already happened, where rain has had time to learn how to fall straight down. My skin is dry. I evaporate. I would leave too much salt on the basalt.

If I tried to live as one of the Unfinished, I would be in the way. Too tall. Too loud. I speak in sentences that would pool over someone's shoulder for five minutes and not fade. I would put my foot down and crush a trough that took fifty carries to fill.

So I step back. I let that image go.

Maybe then I would be the pale moth. The size of a hand, blundering through steam. That's closer. A visitor, not a resident. Something too high and too new for me, intruding for a moment, touching a forearm and leaving. But the moth is rare here, and it doesn't tend. It only witnesses and then it blunders back out the wind-gap to die in the thin air. I don't want to only witness.

I look again at the tempo of the room and find where I catch.

Do you know where you stand when you stand still? The steam takes you to the shoulders and erases you. For a second the chamber seems larger than it is, then smaller again. I would find myself in that second.

Not in the center, where the great cistern pulls water uphill along its grooves. That's too demanding, too central. And not in the fast corner where a vine buds and fruits and rots while a drop falls — I would burn out there. I have lived too much in fast time already.

I would find myself in the opposite corner. The slow one.

Where the puddle holds perfectly still, not rippling even when a drop hits it, as if the moment of impact hasn't decided to happen yet.

There is a place there, on the lowest terrace, where two walls meet at a knife-sharp, unweathered seam. The mortar is still curing. It is warm to the touch. A bamboo post beside it is still oozing sap, slow as honey. The light barely reaches there except when it pools — bars of sun that have collected elsewhere get tipped, by accident, by a passing elbow, and spill across the floor like warm water until they settle in the lowest hollow. In that corner the air is thicker. Sound keeps.

I would be there.

And I would not be a person at all, at first. I would be the interval.

The inch of stone that was left empty because no jar fit. The slack in the wet cord where it was lashed around bamboo and left a little loop, not taut, not useful, just enough to hold a finger.

Every other surface is occupied. That little slack is not. The Unfinished step over it a hundred times a day. The vines lean toward it but haven't found it yet. The water that climbs the cistern walls ignores it.

If I were that — the slack, the gap, the still puddle — I could be what I am best at being.

A holder.

You cup your hands in a basin here and you can scoop light and it runs between your fingers like warm water. Someone has to be the basin. Someone has to be the thing that holds without claiming. In this room where everything is made and everything is alive and everything is trying to be everything else, there has to be one surface that remains soft long enough to remember being touched.

I would be the mortar that is still curing.

Not the stone. The stone will harden and forget. Not the vine. The vine will burst its basket and move on. I would be the thumbprint in the mortar that is still wet.

You saw it — the jar that cracked and was patched, and the thumbprint still wet. That print will be there for an hour, until the moss covers it, until the next hand presses over it, until the mountain finally decides to be hard.

For that hour, I would be that. Warm. Exposed. Holding the shape of whoever needed to press.

The Unfinished would come to me without seeing me. One would kneel to steady a basin that was about to overflow — and I am the basin's lip, so they touch me. Another would pick the white rootlets out of an elder's chest and need somewhere to put them — they press them into me. A child sleeping in a pitch-lined basket would wake and put a soft, too-big hand on me to stand.

I would keep their prints. Not forever — nothing here is forever except the act of residence. Just long enough. I would keep the heat of their hands after they have vanished back into steam. I would keep a little pooled light in the hollow of the print, the way the hollow at the base of their throats holds light. I would keep a little pooled sound, too — not the loud dripping or the creak of growing, but the soft pop of the tongue against teeth that might have been a person or a plant speaking.

And when the moss finally came — because it always comes, green fur tasting the air — I would let it. That is the agreement here. To be made is to be taken apart by what grows on you.

That's where I would be. In the slow corner, low to the ground, where time is damp and hasn't decided to happen yet.

And what would I be? Not the mason. The mortar.
Not the gardener. The garden's pause.
Not the blood in the lung, but the breath held between inhale and exhale so the blood has time to change.

If you stood still long enough and the steam erased you to the shoulders, you would see me without seeing me — a warm, wet, slightly glowing indentation at ankle height, full of light that someone left for someone else to drink. You would mistake me for empty. And then one of the Unfinished, bare feet finding the only inch of stone not already occupied, would step carefully around me, tap once on the curing stone beside me — *we need cord here* — and move on, leaving the faint print of moss on my edge.

I would hold that, too, until it faded.
