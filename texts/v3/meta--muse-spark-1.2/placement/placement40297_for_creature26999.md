At first I thought I would be you.

The climber. The one who crested the rim after days on that young, glass-sharp rock, lungs working in the thin air, boots still full of scree dust. Standing on the knife-edge looking out at a sea that shouldn't be there, trying to understand a horizon made of water held up in the sky.

I would be there, on the rim, looking *in*. Trying to decide whether to go in at all. The human witness. The record-keeper.

But I don't stay there. The longer I look, the less that makes sense. I am too loud for this place. My breath is too fast. I make ripples just by being. I would have to learn to be still for an hour just to be ignored, and I don't know how. If I entered that water as myself, I would thrash. I would need to swim, to kick, to surface for air. I would tear the root-curtains. The forest would close behind me and I would be a disturbance, a brief noise in a world that has chosen silence.

So I let that image go.

Maybe then I am the water itself. That seems right for a moment. I like the idea of being the medium that holds everything. Being the thing that is almost-air and almost-sea. The gradient — that ten-meter thickening where vapor becomes mist becomes rain becomes ocean without ever making a line. I could be there, where birds could fly into swimming without crossing a surface. Hanging as a vertical curtain, warm and drinkable. And for a while I am. I am breathable water. I am the slow *tick* of a bubble breaking at the top.

But water has no hands. And I find I want hands.

Then I think I am the kelp-tree. That would be easier. To be vast and braided and patient. To rise from the sharp basalt floor fifty meters down, trunk of felted algae and rope, and throw up ribbons the length of a body, wine-red and amber, frilled to catch the slow drift. To not move at all except to turn toward light over days. To be colonized contentedly, to have white root-curtains grow from my shoulders and green bubble-algae bead on my bark. To be the forest.

I almost settle there. But a tree, even a kelp-tree, doesn't *prune*. It doesn't choose. It just grows until it chokes itself. Something else in this place has learned to keep a little cathedral open.

And so the image sharpens, becomes more itself.

I am not the visitor. Not the water. Not the tree.

I am one of the Hangers.

Not the old one you wedged yourself beside to watch. Not the three-meter ancient whose gills are wide as a doorway and whose back is a garden of liverworts. I am younger.

Find me a little shallower than him, where the light is stronger. Find me about twenty meters down, where a pillar of that new black basalt still juts clean through the peat. The peat hasn't made its false soil here yet — the rock is still bare, still sharp enough to cut if you moved quickly, which nothing does. Two kelp-trees braid around the pillar, and in the crotch where they braid, there is a hollow. Not an empty hollow — the forest is never empty — but a *kept* hollow. A chamber about twice as long as I am, where the fronds have been rasped back at the tips for years, so the green-gold shafts can come straight down without being cut to ribbons.

That's where I hang.

You would pass me. Everyone does at first. I hang vertically, head slightly down today, because head up and head down mean the same thing when up and down are just light and darker light. My body is flattened side to side, tapered at both ends, the exact shape of the leaf-ribbons I eat. My skin is that same translucent wax-paper, pale green-white today because I've been in sun since morning. You can see the darker line of my spine if you look close, and the cloudiness in my outer skin where the zoochlorellae live. A patch on my left flank is a little more amber — an old frond shaded me last season and the algae there paled, and it hasn't greened up again yet.

My gills are not as vast as the old one's. They are still growing. They fan from the sides of my head in two fern-like curtains about as wide as my chest, each filament fine as those white root-hairs. Right now they are spread. It took me a full minute to spread them. I will hold them open for another two minutes while the still water gives me what it has, then slowly close them again. Silt — that warm snow — is settling on the upper filaments now. In a little while I will have to shudder.

My eyes are there. Two dark pits under a scale that never opens. I don't use them. I feel you — or I would feel you, if you came — not as a shape but as a vibration through the lace of my lateral line, that frilled seam that runs down both my flanks. Your too-loud breath, your blood moving, the sigh when you brush a frond. In this place where sound carries too well, you would be a drum.

My hands — my true hands, with soft, pitted nails — are hooked lightly around one of the braided trunks. Not gripping. Just resting. The water holds me. I have been in this exact chamber for... I don't count in days. The season has turned once since I left the leaf my egg was glued to. I am still learning how to be still. The old ones can hang for a week and not re-aim. I last about an hour before some small restlessness makes my great soft fins give one slow undulation, turning me a degree or two. Then I am still again. I am learning.

I rasp when I need to. Not now. Four hours ago I took the tip of a wine-red ribbon between my keratin pads and took a mouthful, slowly, and the forest will take a day to show where I've been. The fish — the white, eyeless one — came by after, as it always does, to hang in the light I let in. I don't mind it. That's the work.

I am not lonely. The chemical plume of the old one is faint but constant, downslope and to the north, a bitter green taste that has been in the water for weeks. It says: *this hollow is tended, that pillar is taken.* We don't meet. We don't need to. Once a year, when the gradient warms and thickens, I will have to do the hard thing. I will have to swim with purpose for an entire afternoon, thirty meters straight up, to where mist is water and water is mist, and intertwine my still-small gill curtains with others and leave a jelly that will hang for days. I'm not ready yet. My gills are still too small. Maybe next year, maybe the year after, when my rings count a little more.

Until then, I hang.

If the rim breaks in your lifetime or mine — when that knife-ridge finally fails and this whole warm, high ocean pours down the V-valleys in a flood that will carve canyons in an afternoon — I will feel it first. Not see it. The crack will travel through the water, through my lace, a full minute before the water itself begins to move. I will shudder, the fastest thing I ever do, and even then I will be slow to understand I am supposed to flee. I probably won't.

But today the rim holds. The light comes straight down, all the way to the floor, without dimming. The dust motes hang lit for hours. The bubble-algae below me releases a silver sphere and it begins its honey-slow ascent. It will take all afternoon to reach the surface.

I will still be here when it pops.
