At first I think I know.

I think I would be looking out.

Standing at the very lip of the scarp where the city stops and the young country goes on - that line where the last dry-stone wall gives up and there is only wind-scoured basalt and permafrost to the horizon. I would be the watcher. The lammergeier's view. The person who keeps the high glacier gate, because someone has to be furthest and highest and alone. I like that image of myself. Distant, clear-eyed, useful.

But I don't last there.

The air is too thin for watching. At that height, standing still is work. My chest - as it is now - would be too small. I would be bent over, mouth open, that faint catch and sigh the description gives everyone, only in me it would be a gasp. My skin would burn. I would squint until my face ached. And I would be useless, because the job at the edge is not to look, it is to know when the ice in the rock ticks a certain way before it splits. I don't know that yet. I like the romance of the edge, but I wouldn't survive the edge.

So I move inward. Try again.

Maybe I am not human at all. That would be easier to breathe.

I try being a raven. It fits for a moment - lazy circulation in the updraft over the scarp, hundreds of us turning without effort, watching doors open and close far below. I would know the butcher by the smell of his hands. I would land on the windbreak wall and watch the people move like water in a finger-width channel. I would be above the maintenance, not responsible for it.

But the raven is a tourist. It rides the wind the city makes but does not keep the channel clear. And I want to keep the channel clear. I want to be part of the reef.

I try being the wall itself. A stretch of basalt on the third terrace from the top, dark stone and lighter sandstone, fitted two degrees into the hill so the frost will not push me out. I would be touched every day - a man passing running his palm along me without stopping, a child testing my joint with a barley husk. I would hold heat from the sun into the evening. I would have notches inside the granary I am a part of, counting good years and bad. I like the patience of that. A course a week. A neighborhood a generation.

But a wall does not hear the yak bells. It does not hum to turn the yaks.

So I come down, and closer, and smaller, until the image stops trying to be grand.

I find myself not at the high gate and not in the sky and not as stone, but in the middle density, where the city is most itself.

Lower-mid terraces, eastern scarp, where the sun in the morning has to climb over the scarp edge before it hits you, so the frost lasts an hour longer. There is a north-facing wall there that never quite dries. Ice condenses in its shadowed culvert and drips all winter, even when everything else is dust. The thread it makes is barely a thread - you could cover it with your thumb. And it is mine.

If I am born there, I am short. Of course I am short. Barrel-chested, even as a child, lungs grown large inside the ribs like a second heart. My face by twenty is already lined at the corners, ruddy-brown from that white, merciless sun that bounces off snowfield and pale stone until there is nowhere to hide. My eyes are dark brown, narrowed by habit even in shade. My hands - that is how you would know me - broad palms, knuckles split every winter in a white lattice that never quite heals, nails blackened from stone. The callus on my right palm from the hammer my father cut down for me when I was ten. I would not remember a time before it.

I wear what everyone wears because there is nothing else to wear: coarse wool fulled to felt, brown and undyed white, over that a blanket-coat belted with cord, the hem black with soot and remade in sinew a dozen times. Felt boots stuffed with new moss in the morning. Fingerless mitts so I can work, so my fingertips are always cold and black at the edges.

You would find me in the half-light before the sun clears the rim, because my job is the small job.

I am the feather-walker.

Every morning, while the dung smoke still lies low and blue in the stepped streets and does not rise, I walk my contour channel. It is no wider than my forearm, carved by my great-grandmother and corrected by every hand since. I walk it with a feather - actually a snowfinch feather, because it is light enough to show the water when I hold it just above the thread. Where dust has dammed it, where a pika has kicked scree into it, I clear it with a fingertip. One fingertip. That's all it takes. If I miss a day, the barley plot two terraces down gets nothing that week.

It is not an important job. The elders who sit in the sun-trap - that rare still pocket where windbreak and scarp make warmth - and remember the eleven-day blizzard would not be consulted about it. The mason twins who can set stone without speaking would not notice it. But after a few years the people on my stair-street would notice if I stopped, because their shared channel would clog in the way that arguments clog when people stop speaking.

Later in the day I am on the windward terrace with everyone else. Bent over barley that is barely ankle-high, brittle, moving dung from the yak pen to the plot that must not lie fallow this year. The sun stands violet overhead and throws ink-black lines off every edge. Breathing becomes a rhythm that paces the work, audible, steaming. We do not hurry. Nobody runs. My calves are corded wood under felt. When I pass an old wall I touch it without thinking, appraising.

At midday my shoulders are covered in snowfinches for a moment if I stand still winnowing grain. They don't mean anything by it. The dogs sleeping in piles in the warm alley know my smell but are not mine. The yaks know the stairways better than I do. The city moves around me like water and I am one of the droplets.

If you stayed long enough to be allowed to stay - to learn not to shout, because sound does not carry and wind takes anything louder - you would learn my name the way people learn any name there: terrace to terrace, because my family arranges water rights sensibly, because privacy is thinned like the air through shared walls. You would hear me speak only close, few high vowels, words short and ending in breath. We talk about maintenance, mostly. When to open the high gate. Whether the winter lichen on that north wall can take more sheep without dying for seven years.

At evening I go inside. My house is low, half-sunk into the rock, one room where we all sleep around the hearth, the stone warm from the day's sun and from bodies. Ten people and two dogs under yak-hair blankets, breathing into shared air that is warmer for being breathed. I add a stone to the cairn where the path crosses the ice culvert that never melts. I check the prayer flags - bleached pale, whipping to threads - still snapping. I do not expect an answer. I do the small repeated act so something does not vanish.

I am not the high gatekeeper or the raven or the wall. I am not distinct the way a lowlander wants to be distinct.

I am lichen.

From half a kilometer off I am just a stain on basalt, a crowd the color of stone. From close I am a nation - my cracked hands, my particular way of holding the feather, the juniper bush no taller than my knee that my child guards with absurd seriousness because it took a century to grow that high and we prune it for incense only once a year.

Like lichen, I hold.
