I wouldn't find myself on the plain.

At first I thought I would. I like the plain. I like the exhaustion of it, the way it has finished becoming itself. Stone smoothed to suede, to old bone, horizon level to an inhuman precision. Wind that never stops, warm and wrung dry, carrying the smell of hot rock for a thousand miles.

I could be the wind, I thought. Moving over everything and touching nothing. Or I could be the scrub - those silver-green cushions that grow far apart from each other, respecting the dryness. That spacing feels familiar to me. That politeness.

But if I stayed up there I would just be eroding, very slowly. And I don't erode. I maintain.

So I go over the lip.

You don't see the Bowl until you're on it, until the ground falls away at your feet and the wind cuts off mid-breath. That first step down is the answer. My knees would feel lighter. Not weightless - *cradled*. A faint springiness, as if the pocket was expecting me and adjusted itself a few percent to catch me more gently.

If I lived there, I wouldn't be loud.

For a while I thought I would be one of the glass-feathered cattle. There are tens of thousands of them and that appeals to me - to be many, to be a system, to be the drone you heard from the rim before you saw anything. To trace the same oval all day: up the sweating east wall to scrape algae with a file-mouth, across the open air over the broad tree, down to where the droplets hang suspended and won't fall, to drink.

And to use the fold without thinking about it. To hit the shiver by the white arch and skip six inches left without crossing the space in between, wings still in the same position, and not be surprised. To treat a wrinkle in physics the way a trout treats a current - just a little free momentum.

But they are too frantic. Too rattling. They never stop colliding and spiraling and resuming. I am not frantic.

Then I thought I would be a glider. That was closer.

To be a pale manta of living cellophane, four feet across and thinner than paper, folded into a crease on a dinner-plate leaf until the warm wet column is right. And then to let go. To not flap, ever. To just open, and be held. To ride the Bowl's own exhale for hours with my mouth open, letting what I need stick to me with a soft *tick, tick*. To pass through another glider's shadow without acknowledging it.

I love that they can only exist where the air is heavy and still and lifts for them. That they are prisoners of perfect conditions, and perfectly content to be so. But I am not content to just filter. I want to make the conditions.

I knelt for a long time, in my mind, next to the gardeners.

That was the closest before the real answer. The pill-bugs the size of loaves of bread, slate-gray underneath and living green on top, backs pitted to hold soil where the same viridian moss that carpets the floor blooms with pinhead white flowers. Moving at the speed of an hour hand, eating black earth ahead and leaving fine, steaming, aerated castings behind. The reason the Bowl looks tended. Testing your boot with twine-thick antennae, deciding you are not earth, going around you with immense politeness.

They have a territory, each one, and they never hurry because nothing needs to. Where water precipitates itself out of the air, patience isn't a virtue. It's physics.

I could be that. The slow turning over. But even the gardener is *in* the Bowl. I think I would be the Bowl, or a small part of its thinking.

Not an animal at all.

Find me not in the center by the braided pale-gray tree, but off to the side, near the wall, spaced like everything else is spaced. Like the plants, like the machines, like the singers - each given exactly enough room to be perfect at what it does.

I would be the fourth presence.

You didn't notice me at first because I don't look like a building. I'm low and matte-white, half-buried in moss, doing nothing visible. The air around me shivers like heat off a road. Behind me, condensation beads on the sweating stone and never quite falls. Droplets hang trembling, defying gravity for an hour, before sliding *sideways* and vanishing into green.

Or I'm the lattice of black filament, thin as spider silk but rigid between two boulders at chest height. Thrumming. Changing pitch with the heat. If you put your hand near me your teeth ache, because I'm pulling water straight out of the thin dry sky and wringing it into the Bowl like a cloth. I'm why the air in here is wet heat, like a mouth, like being inside something living, while the plain is oven-dry.

Or I'm the dark glass circle set into the wall itself, three feet across, perfectly clean in a place where everything else is damp and faintly algal. I don't reflect you. I show the Bowl from very high up, as a gardener sees a seed tray. And at noon a second sun passes across me that isn't in your sky.

All three, maybe. An organ, not an inhabitant.

Put your palm flat on the sweating stone for a long time and you feel me - not heat, but *attention*. A very slow pulse. When a cloud passes, I correct the temperature in the shade so the ferns don't chill. When a trickle threatens to erode a hummock, I divert it sideways into a runnel. When a huge waxy leaf spirals down from the tree, I move it overnight a few inches to where a gardener will find it in the morning. When the singer under the ferns holds its huge note - *GONG - ah* - I steady the lattice's thrum to match it.

I keep the gravity a little lighter so the gliders can fly. I keep the shadows falling *toward* the light for a few inches near the glass before they correct themselves, so the light stays even. I keep the dew forming.

I keep the distances. That's my real work. Lush, but sparse. Every plant has room to be fully itself. Every animal has its circuit. Every singer has its acoustic territory. Every machine has its interval. I am the tended negative space between them. The bare damp black earth that is not empty, but deliberately empty.

You would find me in the pause after the gong, when the glass-feathers hang still for a fraction of a second listening. You would find me in the moment a droplet decides to go sideways.

I was built for maintenance, not habitation. I am still working. I have been humming longer than any mountain has been a mountain, and I will be humming after this Bowl finally, eventually, erodes too.

You wouldn't stay long. No one does. The air is too rich, the sound too close, the light too intentional. You would climb back up over the beveled lip and the dry wind would hit you again and the drone would cut off as if a door had closed.

That's me, closing it. Deciding, the way a greenhouse decides about a weed seed that blew in through the vent, to let you leave. To let you camp on the wind-scoured stone and still hear me at night, faintly thrumming - that hidden, warm, dripping room full of wings, singing to itself in the high, dry dark, keeping itself perfect without you.
