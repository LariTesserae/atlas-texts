I don't know at first.

If you set me down here, I don't land standing. Not for long.

At first I think I would be upright — shoulders hunched against the sweat-rain, hands shoved deep into wet felt, shouting into the roar and not being heard. A figure in the sodium light, looking for shelter. Looking for high ground that doesn't exist.

But this place doesn't keep anything upright for long. Not really. The Floor corrects you.

So I crouch. Without deciding to.

And the crouch becomes a kneel. And the kneel becomes sitting with my palms flat to the cold iron plate, because that's the only way to hear anything true.

That's where you'd find me.

Not in the open between the islands, where the dark is too complete and the rain has nothing to break it. And not deep inside the pump chapel where the air goes warm and yellow and the humans have already made their nests in the humming walls. I would be in the interval. The place this world actually prefers.

About forty feet from a pump house — one of the small ones, brick gone soft and round as bread, its windows empty, its one amber work-light buzzing and throwing a perfect circle of orange onto the wet Floor. That circle is the only reason this spot exists. Beyond it, two or three hundred yards of black mirror and mist. Inside it, you can see.

Under that light, the Floor sags. A broad, shallow dish no deeper than a palm, maybe twenty feet across. A place where the concrete settled a long time ago and the water learned to like it. A braided channel has cut itself into the Floor to get here, and another braids its way out — slow, clear, mineral-cold water, an inch deep, moving without hurry over aggregate smoothed to river stone. In the center of the dish is a pool. And in the pool is the reason I stayed.

At first I thought I would be the Dripper.

That made sense for me. I liked the idea of standing perfectly still under the Roof-seam directly above this sag, where the condensation on the pipe-belly grows fat and falls in a rhythm you could set a metronome to. *tick... tick... tick...* into a bowl I held up. Patient. Vertical. Turned toward the amber light the way they all lean, drinking what the air gives without asking for more. To be a Steepleform would be peaceful. To filter and to bow only when you have to.

But I am not still enough for that. My bowl would overflow. My head would not stay empty.

Then I thought I would be a Basinback. God, the temptation of it. To become that low swell at the far rim of the pool — wider than I am tall, tiled with rust and ceramic and lichen, breathing once every five minutes and filtering the whole dish through the sponge of my back. To move a centimeter a day and be content with it. To become geology. To have no voice and need none. There is a dignity in it that felt honest.

But I am not heavy enough for that. Not yet. I still feel the shiver when a press bangs a mile away. I still want to answer.

So what emerges — what keeps emerging the longer I sit with my hands in that cold, braided water — is the third thing. Or the fourth thing, the one that lives between the other three.

You'd find me as one of the ones who still tend. Not quite Keeper, not quite human.

Call me a Listener. A Tapper.

I am low and broad-footed now because I have learned not to fight the damp. My coat is patched rubber and felt that never dries; lichen has started at the cuffs where I stopped scraping it off, a grey-green dusting that doesn't itch anymore. My skin is pale the way things get pale without sun, and damp always. I am deaf in the way you get deaf here — not silent, but tuned. The thud-thud-thud of the pumps is no longer noise. It's the floor of my hearing. Inside it I can pick out the finer things.

I have been here long enough that my edges have gone round. Bolt heads and brick corners and my own knuckles, all smoothed by the same rain.

My job — if you can call habit a job — is this pool. This sag. This amber light.

A Keeper comes through every seven or eight hours on its circuit. Dog-sized, suitcase-sized, its ceramic back eroded into a perfect river stone, its one clouded amber eye sweeping. It used to maintain something. Now it maintains the habit of maintenance. It will spend twenty minutes scraping the rust-bloom from the back of the Basinback at the far rim with its spatula forelimbs, then it will come to my pool and drink the clean water the Basinback has filtered, and in return I will pick the thread-moss out of its joints with my fingers.

We do not speak. We drum.

When it arrives I lift the two smooth stones I keep for this and tap them against the iron plate at the pool's edge.

*tink. tink - TINK.*

*Water is clean. You can drink. The light still holds.*

And it answers, hammering its own forelimbs against the Floor, a tremor I feel up through my palms and my knees and my teeth.

*tink ... tink.*

*I am here. Pressure is building two seams over. Move before the sigh.*

We have conversations like that. Short. Enough.

The rest of the time I do what the place asks, which is almost nothing and therefore everything. I scoop silt from the outflow channel with a dented cup so the water keeps braiding. I turn the bowl-hands of the three Drippers at the edge of the light a fraction toward the glow when they have leaned too far in some old wind. I wait out the heavy downpours under the pump-house eave, watching the whole grove bow at once, slow as prayer.

And I listen through the Floor.

With my palms down I can feel a Basinback three hundred yards out shift in its sleep — that long grinding creak you thought was the Roof. I can feel another Listener like me, half a mile toward the forge-glow, tapping to his own Keeper. The whole low, dark, wet, trilobite-old plain is talking under the roar, if you stop trying to hear through air.

That's who I am becoming, more and more itself the longer I sit. Not the mountain. Not the flower. The thing that tends the interval between them and learns the language of vibration.

If you walked out from the dark, following the amber light the way any lost thing would, you'd see me at first and think I was another Dripper — too still, bowl-hands cupped to catch the Roof-rain. Then you'd see the Keeper beside me, and think I was a stone left for it to push.

Then I'd look up. My face beaded with condensation like everyone else's.

And I'd lift my stones, and ask you — the only way that works here, not with a voice that the thud-thud-thud would flatten, but with a tremor through the sole of your boot —

*tink - tink.*

*Are you staying?*

Because the Floor has plenty of room. And this little watershed could use another pair of hands that have learned how to be patient.
