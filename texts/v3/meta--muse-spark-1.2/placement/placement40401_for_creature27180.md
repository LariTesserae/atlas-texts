I don't find myself right away.

At first I think I would be up on the rim, looking in. That's where anyone would stand first - where the basin is still a sound before it's a place. The air is already warm there, close as breath, and the factory is already working without any factory. *Chuff. Chuff. Chuff.* Whistle. Hum. Clang. My sternum is humming before my eyes can find a reason for it, and for a few minutes my brain keeps trying to build chimneys and sheds in the haze that aren't there, because loudness should mean building.

I go down.

That's the only direction this place offers. Not in, exactly, but down - the long gentle sag of hematite and salt where the grit hasn't moved in centuries and the boulders sit half-buried with their windward sides scooped into spoons. Everything has already become its final shape. I walk on stone that is warm through the soles of my shoes and the light just is, white-yellow and ordinary and soft at the edges, the kind of light that doesn't make you look twice. It makes everything else look twice instead.

For a long while, I think I would be nothing here. No - not nothing. But the kind of nothing this place counts at first. A visitor's nothing. Standing on a dome the size of a house that the world finished smoothing a million years ago, watching water bead on the stone and then, instead of falling, get pulled back into it. Watching a shallow pan of brine sit an inch deep and not ripple. In that kind of equilibrium, a body like mine is an error. I breathe too fast. I accumulate. I want.

If I am going to belong here, I have to get smaller. Slower. More mineral.

At first I think I would be one of the fliers.

Because there is at least movement in them. Up near the chimneys where the air shimmers - three, sometimes one - those wrong dragonflies with foil wings that never beat, only vibrate. They never land. They just hold station in the updraft, shivering in place, dipping a needle mouth to a weeping vent and then rising again. To be them would be to be above it all, to navigate by heat instead of sight, to live entirely in the *chuff* and the whistle and never have to touch the damp. I like the idea of never touching.

But I can't stay there. The fliers never rest. They hover their whole lives. They don't have a place, they have a column of hot air they rent second to second. If the vent stutters - which it never does - they would fall. I want a place that holds me. A stone that is warm for no one but stays warm.

Then I think I would be the stone itself. One of the low, wide ones that clangs every 47 seconds when the basalt expands and slaps itself. To be that would be to be perfectly on time. To be the metronome the whole basin sets itself to without ever knowing it. To have a job so simple and so absolute: heat, expand, clang, cool, contract, wait. Again. Again. Since before the hills wore down.

But the stone is too alone. It does its work whether anyone is listening or not, which is beautiful, but it doesn't taste anything. It doesn't carry anything forward.

So I crouch.

That's what the place makes you do eventually if you stay. You put your hands on a stone that is blood-warm from somewhere far below, and you wait until your own breathing slows enough that your shadow stops moving. And the film resolves. Not all at once.

I would be down there.

Not on the bright salt pan where sun makes the evaporite glare. Not right next to the chimneys where the silica throats huff *chuff chuff chuff* so loud you feel it in your teeth. I would be in the lee.

Find the dome that is the color of dried blood and old rust, with a shallow spoon on its southern side where the wind never quite reaches. Where condensation does its little miracle - beading, fattening, sinking back in - all day without ever spilling over. Where the bacterial varnish has had time to become a skin a few cells thick, black-brown with that oil-slick rainbow when you catch it at an angle. That varnish is not alive the way green is alive. It eats iron and sulfur. It precipitates. It is mineral that learned how to hold a charge.

I would be on it. Of it. Just above it.

I am the isopod.

The word doesn't fit and that's why it fits. No eyes. A domed head the color of wet salt, translucent enough that you can see the warm stone through me if the light hits right. Seven plates on my back, pale as shrimp boiled too long, fringed with a nap so fine it holds a film of water against my body like a second skin. Fourteen legs tucked short underneath, not for running - there's nowhere to run to that isn't already perfect - but for ironing myself forward a millimeter at a time over the damp.

That is where you'd find me: in that three-foot vein of water that is only a few molecules thick, running as a sheet over smooth stone before it sinks again. I am moving so slowly you would think I was a flake of curled evaporite until you saw my edges. My antennae are not antennae but blunt chemical tongues, tasting varnish ahead of me, loosening it with a faint acid on my belly and lapping it up. I leave no trail. The water closes behind me.

Under my tail, in a basket of bristles, I am carrying three amber spheres the size of peppercorns. Eggs. I have been carrying them for years. I will carry them for years more. There is no season here to hurry for. The heat is steady, the food is steady, the whistle holds its note for ten seconds and cuts off, holds again. When they hatch they will be perfect miniatures, salt-pale, and they will walk away without touching me and take up a damp patch a few yards away that no one else wants. That distance is the point. One every hour of walking. Any denser and we would accumulate, and accumulation breaks the law.

I am an old one. You can tell because the hot brine has glazed me. My plates have taken on the same manganese rainbow as the varnish I eat. In a year I will have moved maybe twenty yards. In sixty years, a few hundred. I will not be lonely. Lonely is a word for things that expected more.

Above me, the fliers shiver in their heat column. Every 47 seconds the stone down the slope clangs, and I feel it through my legs. Every ten seconds the vent whistles. The *chuff* goes on without speeding or slowing. I do not hear it anymore, the way you don't hear your heart until someone asks you to listen.

If you lifted the stone next to me you would find the worms, white as brine, thin as hairs, swimming through film that never evaporates, and the rust-colored mites. But you have learned not to lift stones. The condensation underneath is a world that has been sealed for years, and it likes being sealed.

If I die here - rarely, but it happens - I will not rot. There is nothing here that knows how to swarm. I will simply dry where I stop, my water taken back into the stone's pores, my chitin bleached and then slowly filmed over by the same varnish I once ate, until you can't tell where animal ends and mineral begins. No accumulation. No slag.

That is where I am. Not central, not prominent, not loud. In the shallowest concavity that stays damp the longest, on the lee side of a dome that finished eroding long before I existed, moving between the whistles, feeding between the sighs, carrying my three amber futures very carefully over stone that is warm for no one and therefore, perfectly, warm for me.

Come back in a thousand years. The valve will still be whistling. The water will still be beading and un-beading. And I - or someone exactly like me, glazed a little more, a millimeter further along - will still be ironing myself forward, tasting iron, keeping the varnish from getting too thick.
