I don't arrive there.

That's the first thing. There's no door to come through, no path that leads in. You don't enter the built grove. You notice you've been there for a while already, and the warm air has already dried your lips.

At first I thought I would find myself standing.

That seemed right — a figure against a ground, a visitor with shoes on the layered floor, looking around, trying to hold the edges of the room in my head at once. I would be upright, at the sort-of center, where the basin of planks and tiles and reed mats is lowest, where the warmest air pools. Leaves of hammered tin and starched linen would be at my shoulders, at my elbows. I would be pushing them aside to see further, and they would make that soft, dry sound. I would be the one counting the tenders and getting the count wrong.

I stayed with that image for a little while, and it wasn't true. Nothing stands for very long here if it can lie down instead. The grove invites a different posture.

So then I thought — lower.

I would find myself already lying down. Not collapsed, not tired. Placed. In one of those shallow depressions in the deep fiber mat, the dark, waxy coir and wool and hair that isn't soil. On my back. The mat gives a little under me, dry and sun-warm, not cool the way earth should be. The light from the vault falls straight down, shadowless, so I have no shadow to tell me where I end. Needle-leaves brush my cheek when the air moves, which is rarely. A copper leaf somewhere above me relaxes a millimeter and ticks.

That was closer. But still wrong, because I was still thinking of myself as separate from the place. A person lying *on* a floor. Figure and ground. The grove doesn't do that.

I let the image soften and become more itself.

I am not *on* the mat. I am *in* it. Carded into it a little. The fiber has been pulled up over my ankles, over the side of one calf, the way you would pull a blanket up without thinking. Someone did that. One of them did. Or the mat did itself through them.

And I am not breathing at my own speed. At first I was, quick and a little dry-throated, the way someone breathes when they still think they are a visitor and still expect water somewhere. Now I am breathing slower without deciding to. Every thirty seconds or so. A shallow lift that moves the leaves around me. I have fallen into the grove's rhythm because it is the only rhythm being offered, and it is very patient.

Where would I find myself? In the slack part of space.

Not by the wall — you can see the wall from anywhere, but you never remember it correctly. Not by the vault, though the heat is kindest up there. I would be in the middle distance that takes twenty steps to cross and six steps to return from. In a fork where two trellises meet at an angle that gravity allows but doesn't insist upon. Where a beam has put out a bud at a joint and no one has decided yet whether that bud will be a leaf or a limb. Where the dense curtains make a small room inside the room, about as big as an outstretched arm in every direction, so everything is within reach. You could call it a tending circuit, but I wouldn't have known to call it that. I would have just thought: this is a good place to lie still.

Who would I be?

For a long time I thought the answer would be *one of them* — one of the parchment-skinned tenders with mortise-and-tenon elbows and copper-disc eyes, hair stitched in rows with wooden pegs, moving sideways through foliage, humming without sound. That would be tidy. To be the one who kneels with the bone needle and the oiled cord and stitches the frayed rope-vine. To be useful. To have a pouch of the same fiber as the floor.

But I'm not that finished. My joints don't creak the right way yet. When I hold my forearm up to the vaulted light, I can see the light through it, but unevenly. Too thick in places. The stitching is still visible where it shouldn't be — loose threads at my cuff, at my collar, at the seam of my mouth. I am not yet waxy enough to stay dry without trying. My lips are still chapped.

So I am not the tender. Not yet.

And I am not the small fauna, though I like their quiet. I am too large to traverse a leaf in a morning. I am too loud to be 0.8/3 quiet, though I am learning.

What I am, more precisely, is the thing that is being tended.

I am the loose thread.

I came in — or the grove realized me there — with the wrong expectation of wetness. With thirst as a category. With the idea that a garden needs water and a person needs drinking, and that a room should have an outside. I brought those ideas with me like burrs, and they caught on everything because everything here is within arm's reach.

One of them found me while I was still thinking I was standing. They didn't approach so much as resolve. A knot of fiber and shadow that was a nest and then was a person kneeling. Their linen eyelid drew sideways. They tilted their dark wood eyes at my mouth — at the chap — and offered, with great solemnity, a fingertip of ochre pollen. It didn't help. They weren't disappointed.

Then they did the other thing. The small, including gesture.

They reached out — cord creaking very softly at the elbow, the same sound the building makes when it settles — and tucked a loose thread of my clothing back into its weave. A repair. Not to make me pretty. To make me hold.

I felt how loving the making of this place was in that moment, how thorough. How every leaf had been cut and every beetle lacquered by hands that didn't have water to spare, so they made a world that refused to require it. And now those hands, through these slow, dry children, were making the same offer to me: *stay, and you will not need what you think you need. Be made a little more, and the warm air will be enough.*

So I let myself be carded further into the mat. I let the tin leaves settle over my face. A felt moth landed on my wrist and stayed there, dusting me with mineral pollen. Somewhere, a vine-braid tightened a centimeter lower with a soft creak.

I am not yet leaf and not yet carpentry. I am the seam between them where the distinction was never enforced. The bud on the beam that is learning to be a limb. The visitor who is learning to drink light and wax and amber diffusion.

If you came and looked for me, you would misread my scale at first. You would think I was a rolled quilt tucked into the fork of what was carpentry and is now botany. Then you would see I was breathing, very slowly, moving the curtain of needle-leaves around me.

If you sat with me for an hour, you would see the stitch-pattern I am learning to leave: a small knot on the trellis nearest my head that means *I slept here, it was warm.* 

And later — much later, on the scale of days — the cord at my own elbow might harden into true wood. A leaf might put out from my wrist, hammered copper, veined with thread, and tremble there. I would flex it and it would make a soft tin sound, and I wouldn't correct it.
