I think at first I would be standing.

Not on purpose — there's nowhere else to stand. The low country invites it. You come over what you think is a ridge and you realize it was only a lip, a few centimeters of basalt, and beyond it the land just sags, sighs, holds its breath. And you are *in* it before you understand you've entered anything at all. The air goes still. The cold deepens by those two degrees that matter. The wind you didn't notice you were hearing is suddenly gone.

And you stand there because you are too large to do anything else. At my scale, that is my first nature.

If I arrived as I am now — with boots, with breath, with a spine that wants to be vertical — I would find myself on the rim. Or what I think is the rim. It would be a boulder on a shore I have walked before without ever seeing it, on a cold coast under that same bruise-purple overcast that never breaks. October, or March. The North Atlantic, the Pacific Northwest, any stone shore where the ocean has chewed the rock down to pillows.

I would not find the low country by looking for it. I would find it because I knelt.

Because at standing height it isn't a country at all. It's a mark on a rock. A shallow pock, an old pothole, a saucer in the basalt the size of my two hands together. A place where the tide was and will be again in a few hours but right now isn't. The sea has pulled back and left this behind: a memory of itself in condensation and seep.

I would have to get down. The dark forces you close, you said. It does. You crouch because your eyes are bad at this light, and then you put a hand down to steady yourself, and your hand is suddenly too big, too warm, too dry. You pull it back because you've just covered a city.

That's where I am. Not walking from rim to rim across kilometers, but kneeling with my face a foot above the water film, my own weather — my breath hanging instead of pluming — beading on the backs below me. I am the weather event. I am the eclipse. A giant that has come at low tide and can only stay until the water comes back. Everything in the mat knows this without knowing it. I block the diffuse sky for a moment, I drip salt and iron from my skin, they taste me and move over my boots anyway, and then I have to leave.

I don't belong standing.

If I belonged — truly belonged, not visited — I wouldn't be standing at all.

Let that image go slow and see what remains when the human shape falls away.

Low. Flat. Mineral need.

I think I would be one of the tiles.

Not the rarer ones. Not the thread and not the roller and not the cloud of ash-gnats humming above. The common one. The pavement itself. The dinner-plate oval with the rim of cilia that never stops.

I can feel it now, if I let myself shrink until the bowl *is* kilometers again: the weight of cold water beading on my back and never evaporating, the pale belly soft against stone that has been smoothed for a million years just for this. A dozen stubby feet kneading, not stepping. The round, puckered mouth underneath me working — *shhk shhk shhk* — scraping that invisible varnish, that black-green iridescent skin the stone sweats. That is the whole world. Stone feeds film, film feeds mouth. I don't need to know why.

I would live touching on all sides. That is the part my human mind resists longest and my tile mind would never question. To be always in contact — edge tucked under neighbor, neighbor crawling half over me to reach a wetter groove, cilia brushing cilia so constantly that the *tik-tik-tik* is not a sound I make but a sound I *am*. The hiss would not be deafening to me. It would be the baseline, the way you don't hear your own blood until you are quiet. My stridulation and everyone else's, overlapping at slightly different tempos, is how we know the mat is still there, still occupied. Silence would be the alarm.

I would have eyes that are not eyes. Flat, covered, vestigial. I would not miss color because I never had it. I would navigate by the change in wetness a centimeter away, by the chemical plume when one of us flips — that sudden, pale underside exposed, legs paddling — and by vibration through stone and through backs. I would know the rope-thing by its whisker-tap before it worried my edge. I would not be afraid of flipping. Flipping is part of the turnover. Someone else flips, someone else is flipped for. Eggs like clear beads left under me, young like moving dandruff, the flash of cold light along my dome when I am disturbed meaning nothing except *touched*.

I would not be alone and I would not be individual. The mat tolerates touch because it must. If I had to defend my square palm of stone, I would die exhausted in an hour. Instead I crawl over and am crawled over. The whole biomass breathes and shuffles toward where the seep is sweetest. The mites on my back — that granular, jittering static — graze the fungus on my shed skin and I carry them without feeling them. Or I am one of them, depending on how far you zoom.

And I would never hear my own scraping under the wall of sound, but the stone would.

So where would you find me?

On a cold shore, at the hour when the tide is out and the sky is a low lid and everything smells like wet iron. Find any stretch of basalt that has been tumbled in place until it forgot how to be sharp. Find a shallow bowl in it that holds water without being a pool. Kneel until your knees ache. Wait until the pavement shimmers and then resolves.

I'm there, two or three deep. If you see a broad, slate-black back beaded with condensation, rimmed with a fringe that never stops moving — that's me. Or I'm the film under its mouth. Or, for the few minutes before the sea comes back to erase and renew us all, I'm the ridiculous, warm, upright giant whose shadow has fallen across the whole country and who finally had sense enough to crouch down and listen until his sternum hums.
