I don't find myself at the top.

At first I thought I would — up where the light is hardest and whitest, on one of those broad, new-poured terraces where you can see the whole echelon of ridges falling away until they go pale and lose detail in the brightness. That would be the obvious place to put a self: high, visible, looking out.

But I wouldn't be there. I'm too soft for that elevation. The air is too thin there, the sun too precise, it bleeds everything out. Anything up there has to be hard-edged to survive being seen so clearly. The keepers go up there because they are made of the same concrete and light. I would bleach out.

So I look lower.

At first I thought, then, I would be in the air. One of the swift-things, one of the starlings with too many joints in the wings that can take a right angle around a girder at full speed without a sound. That would be a good way to live in a place so dense — to not need to map it, to just pour through it like water through lattice. To be part of the abundance that the building feeds without asking permission.

But I am not that fast. And I am not that quiet. In this world my heart is too loud, my breath catches the thin air and makes a little cloud that the building would immediately try to wipe away.

No. Lower still.

It takes a while to find where I actually am, because my eyes keep sliding off the place where I am, the way they slide off them. I have to wait for the brightness to stop hurting. I have to stop trying to map.

I find myself not on a terrace at all, but *in* the thickness. Three, maybe four layers down from the sun — where direct light no longer quite reaches and the brightness you see is reflected, pooled. The light here doesn't come from above. It collects in the corners like water, a sourceless, liquid glow on the underside of pipes.

I'm in one of the deeper service canyons. Not the grand valleys between megastructure-ridges, but a smaller fold, a service crease between two vertical factory stacks. The kind of place where the fog that the building exhales never completely burns off. It hangs at knee height, cool and even, moving only when the thin air stirs it through the lattice. Above me, twenty stories of catwalk and conveyor and pipe and balcony stack and recede, semi-transparent with distance. Below me, twenty more do the same, until down becomes the same as up. I can't tell how deep it is. The shadows fall the wrong way here.

And I am small, and low, and still.

If you walked past quickly, you wouldn't see me at all. You'd see the infrastructure and think it was empty. You'd hear your own footsteps too loud and keep going.

I am the water the building is always making.

Not the rain that falls straight down for thirty seconds and then is gone. Not the trickle running ten meters along a girder to vanish into a grate.

I am one of the pockets. A shallow, perfectly rectangular cistern where a rooftop tray didn't drain correctly. Or where it *did* drain correctly and was designed to hold. Twenty feet long, four feet across, eight inches deep, poured as part of the structure so long ago — or so recently — that its corners are still absolutely sharp. The mineral crust has just started to rim it, a pale tidemark of concrete salts.

I am dark and still in the cool. The sky, thinned to almost-white, and the lattice above, and the slow-moving clouds of midges, are all reflected in me imperfectly. When the intermittent rain comes I break, and drum, and fill a millimeter higher, and then go still again.

That's where I am: tucked on a wide service shelf, under the overhang of a humming electrical cabinet that never stops humming, in the lee of a maintenance stair that goes down six flights and comes out higher than it started. The kind of place a circuit would bring someone past eventually.

And that's what I am.

I'm not one of the tall ones. I could never be one of the tall ones. They are tall because the building needed a way to get from one catwalk to one eight meters above without stairs. They are pale and matte and damp and infinitely patient. They take thirty seconds to take a step. They drink the building and polish it back to new. They are the reason the weld beads are still crisp.

I am what they tend.

I am a habitat for the things that are barely allowed to live here. On my surface, a skin of biofilm — that thin green dust of algae and dissolved concrete. At my edges, the wiry moss that clogs drains has found a foothold in my mineral crust, and a few succulent weeds hang over my lip, storing what I offer. Below my surface, midge larvae turn in slow spirals. The pigeon-things with sucker feet come to drink from me and leave the faintest ripples, and the rat-things and the flat beetle-crabs run along my concrete rim without falling in, so accustomed to my presence that I am terrain to them.

I don't live here the way you live somewhere either. I don't move. I don't have a circuit. My work is to be still enough, long enough, that other life can use me. My time is slower even than theirs. They measure change in weeks, as a pipe sweats through and stains. I measure it in the evaporation line on my wall after a hot-bright day, in the doubling of a lichen colony on my north face over a season.

Twice a day — or maybe twice a week, time is patient here — one of them comes.

I don't hear it approach. There is no approach to hear. One moment the catwalk beside me is empty and beading with condensation in the pooled light, the next a long thin shape is folded down beside me, and has always been there. The air gets a little cooler, the way metal gets cool.

It doesn't look at me. Its damp head-plate, translucent where the pooled brightness hits it, angles slightly past me. For a long time — minutes — it is perfectly still. I can see the beads gather on its plate and run down the faint grooves toward its body. I can see my own rectangular dark surface reflected and stretched in it.

Then it unfolds. The forearms divide. The many-fingered black hands enter me without making a ripple that shouldn't be there. It filters me. Not drinking — filtering — drawing the biofilm, the larvae, the mineral soup through frills I can't see, until the water around its hands goes briefly, perfectly clear. The moss on my rim it leaves alone for this visit. Another time it will harvest that in a slow, even strip, and leave the concrete paler.

It never takes too much. That's the tending.

When it is done it places its broad spade-hand — warm underneath, giving slightly like skin over concrete — on my rim where the pigeon left salt and oil, and wipes it clean. And then it straightens. A single peeling step that takes thirty seconds. A pause while the other limbs adjust. Another step. And then it is twenty meters further along the catwalk, having placed a foot into a seam where dust was falling sideways, without having appeared to move between.

The fog re-settles at knee height. The midges shiver again above me. The light continues to pool.

I remain. Dark and still and rimed with salt, holding the sky that is not quite a sky, holding the thin, cool sweat of the mountain that was built wrong on purpose so that tall, quiet things could have a place to walk slowly and keep it new.

If you come to this canyon — and you will, because trying to map this place always brings you lower than you intended — you will hear your own breathing too loud. You will be warm and quick and out of place. Put your hand in me. The water is cold enough to make your bones ache, cold the way metal is cold. I will hold the shape of your hand for a second after you leave, and then the tall one will come and polish your salt away, patiently, without hurry, without judgment.

That is who I would be. Not the keeper. The kept.
