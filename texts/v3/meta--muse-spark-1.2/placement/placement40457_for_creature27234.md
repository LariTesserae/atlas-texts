I don't know, at first.

At first there is only the cold.

Not a sharp cold. A damp, even cold that lives in stone. It's under me before it's around me — seeping up through a mat so thick I can't tell where ground ends and body begins. The air is thin when I breathe and it ghosts, just a little. The light is that sideways blue-grey that never gets brighter, and for a long time I think I might be the moss.

Because I am low. Very low. And still.

If you found me, you wouldn't find me at first. That is the point.

You would come along the black-stone path that is half-swallowed by ferns and see what looks like empty space. That's where I am. In the intentional emptiness.

The ledge is three hundred meters long but my place is only about fifty paces across. A halo. A clearing of cropped moss around a house that isn't a house the way you mean a house. Basalt blocks the color of wet ash, slurry-painted with living jade so it disappears if you don't know how to look. My house sits behind me, alone, and the next house sits a long, respectful distance away across the mat, alone, with its own halo, its own single dim copper lamp by the door. Between us is what I tend.

I tend the buffer.

So I am on my knees, and also on my feet. Bare feet, always. The soles are wide and calloused and have become hands. Toe first, weight rolling outward so I don't ring the stone. Every four or five steps I stop to listen the way we listen — not with ears.

The moss is a foot deep and it is not ground. It is a colonial animal pretending to be a plant, white-veined underneath, and it tightens or softens when you step. I feel it through my arches. This morning, condensation from the cliff above has beaded and run and collected in the basalt cups, and somewhere under the ferns to my left a patch ripples without wind. A slow peristalsis. It has found a beetle. I let it have it. I am weeding, not hunting.

My hands are in it. Pulling the white threads.

If you leave the halo alone for a week, the moss braids itself upward — thin, nerve-like filaments the color of bone, feeling for heat. If you let it go shaggy, one night your floor will be soft and hungry. So I pull them. Bone shaver, slow scrape. No hammer, no ring. Wood joined with lashing, not nails. Noise is a rudeness here, and a risk. Something above the cliff or below the cloud is always listening for a sharp sound.

I am short. We all are, here, because a tall person would brush the second floor. Ten meters above me hangs the forest. My forest. One pale tree, rooted not in the ledge but in the overhanging cliff two hundred meters up, growing down. White birch bark, cold to the touch, branches reaching *toward* me. Its leaves are huge and waxy and translucent and they pulse with that tired green phosphor, out of sync with my breath. This one is mine. Or I am hers.

I stand, and my chest does the thing it has learned to do in thin air — the whole deep ribcage expanding like a bellows, slow, deliberate. My skin is limestone pale from no direct sun. My eyes are too large, amber, wet, pupils blown wide even at dim noon. And on my left forearm and on my left cheek, the lichen.

They grafted it when I was seven. A flat, pale fungus that lives on skin. When I stroke it, it glows.

I stroke my forearm now, two fingers, one long slow pulse. *Stay.* Not to anyone. To the moss, to the Frill-throat I know is watching me from the cliff face three meters away — six-limbed, white as cave fish, throat fan closed so it's invisible. It flashes back, amber, without sound. *Food here, danger there.* I answer by not moving.

Further up, suspended in the damp air without seeming to beat their wings, two Veil Moths hang. Grey ash-paper wings the size of hawks. Their antennae filter mist. As they pass each other they release a cloud of luminous pollen that hangs in the air like script for thirty seconds. I have learned not to walk through it. I hold my breath and let the script dissolve.

On the path that cuts my halo, a Backward Deer picks its way on wrong-jointed legs, so it doesn't sink. Six opal eyes along its flank blush in rings — calm — then it taps one hoof once, soft *tok* on basalt. The vibration travels through the moss and up through my feet. *I am here, this path is taken.* I lift my foot slightly in acknowledgement.

Above, in my tree, a Watcher opens.

It is a sphere of feathers the size of my head, tucked among the pale branches. No face until it decides. Then it opens all at once and there is a single huge wet eye, amber like mine but without a pupil, just light. Hundreds open at dusk in unison, but this one opens now, just for me. I feel absolutely counted. I do not look away. We leave them alone. They may be the tree's fruit or its god.

This is how the rules live in a body:

Water is not taken, it is coaxed. I check the hollowed-log aqueduct that coaxes cliff-drip into my cistern. It's full.

Light is not for seeing, it's for signaling. I have not made myself bright. My cheek glows faintly only when I touch it.

Empty space is not wasted. It is this halo I keep cropped so the moss can hunt between houses and not inside them.

To hide and to be seen at once. My house is invisible with moss slurry. My copper lamp by the door burns dim green, so those who know how to look can find me.

I finish weeding and I go to my tree. I place both bare palms on its cold white bark and stroke upward, against the grain. An old woman's work, but I am not old. I am the age where I am learning to be old correctly. I stroke until the vast leaves above brighten from a tired pulse to a steady glow, and the pools on the ledge reflect it. The lichen on my cheek brightens in answer without me touching it.

If you stayed until the dim day deepened without sunset — the grey just becoming a darker grey — you would see the whole inverted canopy wake. Every family with its tree, every halo standing separate, doing the same stroking. The ledge becomes a cathedral, roof of living light, floor of cloud and moss, congregation of a thousand silent, watching animals flashing to each other in a light too soft to cast a shadow.

And then a wind moves through the hanging forest, horizontal rain arriving on it for an hour, and all the leaves dim at once. And we dim with them.

You would still not hear us.

You would pass my house and think it abandoned, because I am very good at being texture. But if you held still long enough that your own breathing sounded rude, you would see me at last — short, pale, broad-chested, barefoot in the cropped circle between two houses that are deliberately too far apart, one hand on white bark, cheek faintly lit, listening through my feet while the ground breathes under me.

I am the interval. The keeper of the space that is not empty.
