I wouldn't find myself standing.

At first I thought I would — that I would arrive upright, a visitor, perched on the warm back of one of those whale-backed domes with my feet in the pan, looking out at the perfect hazy circle of horizon. I thought I would be the one who watches. The one who listens to the cicadas until they feel like they're inside my teeth.

But that doesn't last here. Not in this country.

This place corrects you.

The heat takes your height first. That 85-degree damp that lives in your chest, that keeps your skin wet whether you sweat or not — it presses down. Sound carries too well to need to stand to hear it. The water rises around every footprint you make and erases it. And there is nothing to stand *for*. No hill to get a better view from. No cliff to see past. The view is already total. Standing only dries you out.

So I settle. That is what happens. Not all at once. Slowly, the way the water leans southwest.

At first I think I would be one of the small, declared things. I could be a copper dragonfly — a hard jewel hovering over a sedge clump, flexing my wings open and closed on a rush stem, living entirely in jerks and stillnesses. Or one of the brown bark-frogs, thumb-joint sized, throat pale as a balloon, making the water tremble in front of my mouth with a jug-note that becomes part of the chorus.

Then I think, no — I would be one of the herons. All leg and neck to keep my body out of the sun's reflection while my feet stay cool. Standing for an hour without shifting weight, my shadow nailed to the silt. Learning that strike is not speed, but inevitability.

But those things still move. They still *go*. And this country belongs most completely to what has learned how not to go anywhere.

If you came looking for me, you wouldn't find me at first.

You would have to sit on the north flank of a dome — not the biggest one, the third one in a loose chain that runs like a spine for a half-mile, each separated by a tea-colored pan braided between sedge islands. You would have to let the midges decide you are another warm stone. Let the steam rise after the brief drizzle. Let your ankles go numb in the tepid seep that slips down in a sheet no thicker than glass.

And then you would realize that not every dome is stone.

I am there, half-submerged where the granite sweats into the peat. Wider than I am long by almost half, and as long as a man if a man were to lie down and let the water take him. My back is not shell, not hide, but pebbled skin the color of wet bone and rust, broken by shallow fissures that hold water exactly the way the rock does. Green-brown film grows in those fissures. Algae. Lichen. I weep, too.

My limbs are short and splayed and massive, more like the buttress roots of those flat-topped cork-bark trees than like legs. Broad spades that spread my weight so I never break through the thin skin of sour peat over iron. My belly drags. When I do walk — rarely — I leave a paired trough in the mud that fills with clear water instantly and stays for days. But I seldom walk.

I wait. That is my work.

My eyes are two dark wet pebbles set far apart on a broad, flat shovel of a head. My throat pulses once, twice a minute. I breathe through lungs and through skin, and my skin never dries because I never leave the seep. I keep one flank against the stone's sweat. I hear through water and through stone, the cicada whine shivering across my back.

I do not lunge. When a frog swims too close, when a silver minnow school turns all at once and catches the white sun, when a lungfish rises to gulp with that soft wet pop — I open. The head hinges at the back, impossibly wide, pale inside. Water rushes in. Then I close. Swallow. Resume being stone. Two seconds, then an hour of stillness to digest.

At night I sing, but not like the frogs. Below the crickets, a single sustained note you feel in the soles of your feet more than your ears, conducted through saturated ground from dome to dome. Another answers a mile away through the humid dark. Another. We are counting each other, making sure the plain is still whole, still level, still wet.

Between the sedge clumps I have scraped a shallow circle where the water leans a little deeper. Translucent beads flecked with iron-red lie there, and I lie over them, not incubating so much as shading, sweating stone-water over them from the fissures in my back. The tadpoles that will hatch are already flat, already mottled, already expert at holding still and looking like dead leaves. Then like small stones. Then like me.

I have been growing a millimeter of thickened skin a year. A new fissure every wet season. Longer than any tree on this plain has been alive. When I die — which will not be for a very long time, unless a dry year cracks even the peat — I will settle and flatten further, and my broad bones will sink and become part of the thin soil over the shield. The pan I kept open will stay a little deeper, a little clearer, holding water longer into the dry. The land will remember where I lay as a slight, round deepening that dragonflies hunt over.

If you placed your hand on my back, and you were very still and very respectful and I allowed it, you would feel what you feel on the stone: not cold, not hot, but exactly the temperature of the world. Damp. Faintly humming. Glinting in that high, white, late-morning light that takes the hard edge off the sun without dimming it.

I would not flinch. I have not learned to be afraid of anything that sits still long enough to become part of the seep.
