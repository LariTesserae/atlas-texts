At first I think I would be on the rim.

That's where you put me, and where I put myself. Feet at the edge where the water sheets thin over the stone, one temperature from the sun and one from the basalt, the visitor's doubt still in my lungs from the climb. I would be the one who came lightly. The one who cut nothing, who made a fire that wouldn't stay lit, who sits long enough to start hearing individual drops ten feet away. The human shape the bowl remembers because it was gentle enough to be held for a little while.

I try that skin on and it doesn't quite fit.

Because on the rim you are still outside. You are still looking *in*. Your ears are still tuned to wind-scour and lowland haze, and you have to learn, hour by hour, to hear the bowl at its own volume. And I — whatever I am that would belong here — have already learned that. I don't arrive. I have been wrung out of the cloud like everything else.

So lower down, then.

For a while I think I would be water. That would be honest. Not the lake that isn't a lake, but one of the threads no wider than your finger, welling dark out of the stone and trailing down without hurry, clinging sideways by capillary because in this place water is allowed to disobey. Or one of the beads under a frond, swelling until it remembers it is heavy and letting go with a *plink* that the amphitheater makes important.

That is close. To be water here is to be memory. The pools hold the sun a second too long after the cloud has passed. You would find me in the ankle-deep standing water between hummocks, holding light. But water moves through. And what lives here lives by staying.

I try again, slower. Let the bowl correct me.

I would not be in the center, where she is. The central palm of sky-water, six feet of perfect reflection — that is the heart, and the heart is already taken, and she has held it for decades at twenty seconds in and thirty seconds out. You don't displace a heart. You don't need to.

You would find me at the edge of the center. Or rather, at the center of the edge.

Under the overhang on the far wall, where the basalt weeps most constantly. Where the shade keeps the rock cold enough that condensation never stops forming, even at noon. Tucked back where you have to wait a full day and let your eyes adjust to the speed of the place before you see that not all hummocks are hummocks.

I would be the second one.

Not the large, dog-bear-long mother in the middle with the bromeliad flowering between her shoulders. The other. Smaller. Younger by maybe forty years, which here is still young. My back-dish is shallower. The garden on it hasn't flowered yet — just sphagnum and a liverwort that has learned the grain of my skin and lays with it, and one small cup-bromeliad still growing into its shape, holding not even a mouthful yet.

My gill-fronds would be neater, less algae-freckled, leaf-green still, not yet green-black. They would still look exactly like wet fern until they pulsed. You would walk past me three times thinking I was moss on stone, because I want you to. Because the ecology here is spaced like words with a lot of space between them, and my word only works if there is this much quiet around it.

Who would I be? I would be what the bowl made to hold a smaller bowl.

I would be almost entirely water, with just enough animal to keep the water from running away. Translucent jade at the throat where you can see the dark vessels and the slosh. Wide head with eyes set outward and upward, not to hunt but to watch the sky because the sky is the only predator here. Spade-toes splayed wide so I don't sink in peat that would otherwise swallow me. Four legs I barely use — I ease, I repose, I am three inches to the left an hour later and there is no print to prove I moved.

I would prune without cutting. Lie on a fern shoot that grew too close to open water until it turns aside. Cover a fallen frond with my mouth and secrete a clear film over it and wait half a day until it softens into time. Not eating, exactly. Converting stillness into stillness.

I would speak the way the place speaks — by letting the water speak for me. Condensation gathering on the cold edge of my neck-frills, beading, falling. The overflow from the tiny bromeliad on my back trickling down my flank in a thread the same width as the threads on the basalt, so you can't tell which drops are stone and which are me. In the amphitheater that *is* a voice. That is enough.

And late, when you think I have done nothing all day, I would lift my blunt head an inch and flick my tongue and take a droplet from the air itself, twenty feet above, as it falls from the underside of a tree-fern crown catching the last level light. You would realize I had been drinking the air the whole time you thought I was still.

I would be patient in the way stone learned to be soil. I would carry my pond with me. If you sat with your boot in that warm-cold sheet at the rim until your lungs forgot the climb, I might ease near enough that your shadow kept a patch of peat from drying. That would be reason enough. Not curiosity. Usefulness.

That is where you would find me — not looking into the bowl, but as one of the reasons the bowl looks tended, looks tended without a tender. A shallow, warm, breathing dish inside the mountain's dish, holding in, holding on, ticking loudly and slowly into the chorus until we all forget which water is which.

If you stayed until evening when the cloud lifts and the light goes gold and level, you would see my eyes wipe sideways, clear as a drip, and close for a long time between breaths.

You would count me, and then later you would wonder if you had counted me at all.
