I don't know at first.

That is the honest answer. I stand — or I think I stand — and for a long while I am not sure what I am, because nothing here lets you be a separate thing quickly.

My first sense is not of place but of weight. Of air.

I am high up, my lungs tell me so. Each breath is a little too much work, as if the air is thinned and then thickened again with water. It comes in warm and heavy and tastes faintly of iron and tannin. The cloud is at my knees. No — at my chest, then at my knees again, moving through me. It catches on something near me and shreds, reforms. I put out a hand and my hand disappears a little at the edges. So I am somewhere inside the weather, not under it.

My feet tell me the next thing. Or what I thought were my feet.

There is no hard place to be. I press down and the world gives. A slow dent spreads out from me a foot, two feet, sighing. The rebound is patient. It pushes back up long after I've shifted my weight, as if the ground is deciding whether to remember me.

I look down to see what I am standing on and there is no seeing. Only green. Moss and liverwort so deep it has no top, only a surface that is also a thickness. Some of it is bright, almost yellow. Some of it is dark and tight as felt. It climbs up over what I thought were my boots and keeps going. And I understand, with a small correction behind my ears like a pop in pressure, that I have been listening for the wrong kind of horizon.

I am not on a hill. I am on a hill that doesn't know it's a hill. The rise is so broad I can only feel it as a faint tightness in my knees, a slow lean. Somewhere a day's walk away the land sags and holds a sheet of sky-colored water ankle deep through reeds, but not here. Here is a flank. Here is somewhere between a sag and a swell, where the water has no direction it prefers, so it goes every direction at once, thin as a film on every leaf, every stem.

*Tick.*

Something cold hits my shoulder. Slides.

*Tick. Tick.*

Above me — or around me — a leaf the size of a door, waxy and cupped, has tipped. A bead swollen to its limit lets go and falls to the leaf below, and that leaf, fuller now, lets go in turn. The sound never stops. It's the only clock here.

I could be many things in this ticking.

At first I think I am one of the Holders. That would be easiest — to be vast and still and drink the cloud with my cold leaves, to be a vertical bog with frogs living in my coat. I like the loneliness of that. To not need to move. To be eaten slowly from the inside by my own tenants and not mind.

But I am not that tall. My eyes are not that high. The light is still dim and green and sourceless, but I can see shag close up — the trunk near me is not me, it is next to me. Its bark is not bark, but a cliff-face of moss on moss, ferns unspooling from impossible places, and something pale and blind gropes from it an inch toward my heat and then thinks better.

Then I think I might be small and glassy. A cup-dweller. I can see the cistern now — two leaves have fused over a century into a bowl that could hold a child, tea-dark, steaming faintly in the warm air. Inside it a shrimp-thing fans, translucent, its back a garden. If I were that, my whole world would be this cup. Born in it, dying in it, never knowing peat or stone. The idea is perfect and I almost stay there.

Or I could be the white isopod, blind, carving silk through the peat beneath my own weight, farming fungus on the walls of a tunnel that will close behind me. Or the moth, too heavy with fog to really fly, drifting through lichen patterned wings. Or the wader on stilts the color of reed, standing so still I forget I am animal.

All of them are true here because all of them are the same decision, repeated: *become part of the sponge.* Don't stand out.

But my hands — I have hands after all — are not legs, not wings. They are empty. They are cupped.

I find myself when the cloud sags a little lower and brushes the upper leaves and condenses cold on my shoulders. The water beads and runs. It follows a seam I hadn't noticed.

I am wearing it. A cape, but not woven. Felted, living. Moss that was coaxed and pressed until it kept growing after it was cut, so that it never stopped being part of the mat. It grows over my shoulders and hoods my head, and where it touches my skin it is not quite separate. The moisture that gathers on it tips, as the leaves do, down a channel to my mouth. I don't remember putting it on. It feels like it grew there while I was learning to be still.

And my skin underneath is damp, pale, green at the edges of my knuckles and my throat where the algae has made its partnership. Cool. I am warm-blooded, but not very. I have learned not to be very.

So I am not the tree. I am not the moth. I am one of the ones who *tends.*

You would find me not at a center — there are no centers — but on that almost-ridge you only know is a ridge because the wind is a little stronger and the cloud lifts a hand's breadth. The stone circle is here, five stones bearded and leaning inward, not a monument but a correction, placed where the drift of pollen hesitates. I did not build it. It was here and I keep it clear, pulling the liverwort back where it would swallow the lichen script.

Below the circle, where the ancient basalt shows through the peat like bone, someone long ago cut a stair. Water uses it now as a handrail, a seep-spring running down each step in a clear thread. I knelt there this morning — or what passes for morning when the light never changes — and diverted that thread with a fitted stone the size of my palm, because a leaf-cup above had clogged and its salamanders were at risk of drying. A small work. A borrowing, not a taking.

My house is not a house. It is the lean of a giant that gave up being vertical over decades and is now a long ridge you can walk along. Under its arch, where the peat holds itself up, is a dry-ish hollow lined with the same felt, where a bowl of strained cup-water sits and a cake of pressed pollen ferments slowly under a leaf, exhaling sour-sweet air.

I was standing still long enough for the vine-feeler to test me. That's how I knew I was back in myself. It came out, pale, sniffed the salt of me, found me neither soil nor rain. Withdrew. Condensation had beaded on my hood. The ticking went on.

If you came looking, you would pass within twenty yards and think I was a mossy boulder beside the seep. I would let you. I breathe with the mat. I wait until you understand you are being mirrored. If you move too fast, I am already gone — not fled, but reabsorbed.

If you stay — if you let the peat take your footfalls and the cloud move through your chest and the tick-tick-tick become your own slow pulse — I might lift a hand, very slowly, and show you how to tip the leaf so the water goes where the moss is thirsty.

I live in the sponge, not on it. In the thickness of the cape. In the reading of a wake frozen in green where something warm passed nine days ago. In the interval between one drip and the next.

That is where you would find me: on a swell that pretends to be flat, at the edge of a sheet-marsh that is not a lake, beside a stair that is now a spring, keeping a cup from emptying, listening for the long pressure wave underneath that tells me the Long Back is still moving, still keeping the mat from drowning in itself.

And I will be still enough, finally, that you are not sure if I was ever there at all.
