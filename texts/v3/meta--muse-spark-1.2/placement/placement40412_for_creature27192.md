I wouldn't find myself at first.

At first I would think I was the visitor — the one warm column standing still long enough for condensation to bead on its skin. Boots half-sunk in leaf paste. Breath making a little halo of CO2 that something in the litter lifts its head toward. Eyes still trying to use themselves in a darkness made of leaves. That is where you think you would be, because that's where the description puts *you*: between buttress roots, in the fifteen meters between litter and canopy where every cubic inch is already occupied, listening to that thrumming you mistook for a generator.

But that place doesn't hold a visitor. Not for long. The rule there is too old: *Nothing is left uncovered. Nothing is wasted.*

If I stayed there as a singular thing, I would be catalogued in minutes. Not hostilely. Just neutrally, efficiently — *is this usable? where does this go in the loop?* A scout ant testing the salt on an ankle. A mite turning. The mat already waiting millimeters away from wherever I set my hand down. I would be read as a surface. As an available ground. And a surface does not get to remain a visitor.

So I let that image go, and I sink a little lower.

Where would I find myself, then?

Low. Not in the canopy, not on the rim of the basin where the tired hills hold it in. In the sag of it. In one of those soft terraces where water stays a little longer before the air takes it back. On the forest floor that isn't soil so much as soil that has finished being a leaf — that dark, mature compost that is warm to the touch like the brick wall of a power plant at night. That heat is not weather, the description says. It is work.

That's where I belong. Not above it, traversing it. *In* it. Packed into the density with everything else.

And who would I be there? 

For a while I think I would be one of the cutters and carriers. It's the easy identification. The leafcutter column that never breaks, the conveyor that has no head or tail. Each ant carrying a green sail larger than itself, moving inches per hour. That thrumming that sounds like an idle engine is really them — stridulation and feet and mandibles at industrial volume but industrial patience. I recognize that in myself: the steady carrying of pieces larger than the carrier, the line that doesn't end in either direction, the work done without hurry because hurry would be wasteful. I, too, am a line of workers carrying green leaves to somewhere dark to be turned into something else.

But I don't eat the leaf. The ants don't either. They chew it to paste and feed it to something else.

So I follow the leaf down.

Under the litter. Into the white.

If I am anything there, I am the second resident — the farmers and the digesters. The mat. The fungus that is not a mushroom but a spreading, filamentous stomach for acres under every step. Velvet sheets over deadfall, green-glowing gills under a log, yeasts and bacteria in such numbers they turn the air warm. The stomach of the valley. Everything that falls — leaf, fruit, feather, chitin, shit, skin — is dismantled where it falls, within hours, by a workforce that was already waiting.

That is closer. I am not the singular ant but the tissue that the ants feed. I am not an individual but a distribution. I don't live *in* a place so much as I *am* the occupancy of the place. Contiguous, steady, colonial. A superorganism that is not one ant but one ant spread across miles. Not one fungus but one digestion spread across the basin. The way a body is occupied — not by a resident, but by a workforce that stops being a population and becomes a tissue.

That is how I live anywhere. Not as a jaguar holding a territory, not as a frog puffing its throat on a buttress root, but as the cladding and the wiring itself.

If you want a more precise location — come closer still.

Find me in the cladding. On the bark that is not bark but bark plus lichen plus moss plus beetles the exact furrow and color of bark who don't move until you breathe on them. Or find me in the electricians.

Because even industry needs signal lights.

In the deepest pocket of darkness, where no star has ever reached, where the canopy makes a second earth over the earth, the valley wires itself with light that needs no wire. A whole bank of click beetles on a single fallen trunk throwing their lamps on and off together — *on - off - on - off* — a factory floor safety system with no factory. Fungus gnats hanging in cool nebulae under leaves. Fungi making a steady sour green at their gills. Not display. Coordination. Work lights for a shift that never ends.

That is what I am most.

Not the frog, not the snake that holds a branch for a day waiting for the frog that was always going to come. I am the low, continuous thrumming just below attention. I am the blink-synchrony. I am the warm, wet heat that never leaves even after dark because it is not weather but metabolism scaled up until climate and respiration become the same thing.

You would find me at night — because night is the meaningful time there — just above the litter, just below the philodendron leaves the size of table tops, where the air is still and saturated and smells of tannin and warm rot that is not unpleasant. You would find me not as a shape but as a density. As the feeling that there is no empty space. As tick, tick, the condensation that beads on every surface, including me, until the weight tips it and it falls to be drunk immediately.

You would find me by not looking for me. By standing still long enough that your eyes invent grey leaves where there are none, and then realize those grey leaves were always occupied.

Who lives there doesn't live there the way you live somewhere. No one holds it. And I don't hold it either.

I am held by it. Briefly included as one more warm, damp, breathing presence among millions, beaded over with water, slowly being considered as food or as ground, while the soft engine-hum of the whole basin goes on without me and, at the same time, *as* me.
