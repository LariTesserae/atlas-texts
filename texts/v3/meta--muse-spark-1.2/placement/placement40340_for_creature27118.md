I don't know at first.

That's the honest answer. You put me on that plateau and for a long time I don't know where I belong, because nowhere there was built for something my size.

I think at first I would be you - the visitor. Small. Loud. Wrong. My boots would bruise the moss and stay bruised for hours. My breath would smoke too fast, too shallow, and I'd be damp through in a way that never becomes *beaded*, never becomes *drunk* by the leaf. I'd count the intersections to the steam-mill and get a different number on the way back and think I was lost, instead of understanding the street had grown a different way while I wasn't looking. I'd look over my shoulder to check if a door was still there and lose it for looking.

I would be inefficient. And the city would tolerate me, the way you tolerate a draft.

So I stay longer than visiting allows. I stop trying to be efficient.

Then I think - maybe I would be one of the grazers. That pale, long-limbed deer-thing flickering at the edge of a moss field. Not tending, not building, just passing through. Sparse fauna in a world of total flora. That appeals to me - to be the rare movement, the superstition, the thing someone isn't sure they saw. But I'm not that quiet. I hear the looms even when they cycle down. I want to be near the throb, not away from it.

Then I think I would be a walker, fully. One of the tall ones. And for a while that image fits and I let it grow: the deep barrel chest, the bellows-breath you hear over the wind, the bark plating over my forearms, the moss in the hollow of my throat. I like the idea of moving that slowly because heat is expensive. Of crossing fifty meters between blinks because I know how to move when no one is listening. Of humming low enough that my molars buzz before my ears hear it.

But if I'm honest, I'm not that tall. Not yet. And my hair is still just hair, it doesn't reach for light on its own. My skin still dries. If I tried to put my hand on a split trunk to slow the sap, the sap would just run over me.

So let that image soften. Let it be more itself.

I find myself not on the grand bough-boulevards where a parade could pass. Not in the center of the valley where the grid is still pretending to be a city. I drift - as things do up there, where the air is thin and everything has to drift to afford itself - to the edge where the city forgets.

Where the sawtooth factory roofs give way to pure canopy. Where a street becomes a branch becomes a ridge without deciding when it changed. The third ground, maybe. High enough that the light falls straight down in hard white pools and then slides sideways off the leaf when you're not watching. Low enough that the stone is a rumor under a hundred meters of root and leaf.

There's a place there where a water-tower fig has grown into a riveted steel tank and then grown over it, and the condensation runs so heavy off its curve that it never stops raining in a two-meter circle around it. Someone has to bail that leaf-cistern. Someone has to scrape the lichen off the sensor plates before the dish grows shut. Someone has to sit with the conveyor when it snags and cut the runner vine with the clean angled slice before the gears grind.

That's where I would be.

Not central. Interstitial. The maintenance margin. The place where the city's idea of itself is thinnest, so it needs the most help remembering.

And who would I be, there?

I would be the one who was asked *are you going to root or walk?* and didn't answer right away.

I would be walking, but badly. Still. Learning to walk the way they walk.

Young, as they count young - which is not young at all. Tall enough that I have to duck for nothing but still have to reach for the valves. My bark hasn't come in on my face yet, so my mouth is still visible when I hum, and my hum is still clumsy. I can hold the low note, I can push it through the diesel thud, but I haven't learned how to make it mean *hold here, pull now, slack* without also meaning *I'm trying*. The older ones hear me trying and adjust for me.

My plating is patchy. Forearms, shins, the ridge of my spine. The rest is still pale, still beaded with cold dew that I haven't learned to keep. Between the plates, the skin is damp and translucent. Moss has taken in the hollows of my collarbones but hasn't fruited yet.

My hair is in that in-between - not hair, not root. Fine, pale, damp curtains that twitch toward the cistern when I'm thirsty before I am. I tie it back with copper wire the way the young ones do, because if I don't it tries to root into my collar.

I would be the tender of intervals. Not strong enough yet to re-belt the house-sized wooden gears with five others. Not patient enough yet to root and hold a stairwell with my body. So they give me the jobs that are about watching.

I bail the cistern leaf with a cupped leaf of my own, and pour half of it over the crown of the rooted elder who holds the wall of the fig-tower. She breathes once a minute. I lean close and hum with her until the wood answers. I scrape lichen. I sit on a boulevard that could hold a parade, alone, and I don't count intersections - I watch my shadow. I learn which shadows tell the truth. When a building goes soft behind me and seals a doorway, I don't mourn it. I wait with my eyes averted until it buds a new one, and I see if the new one has a window where there was none and I make a note of it, the way you make a note of a friend's new haircut.

I would be the one who still remembers what it felt like to be small and loud and to cast a shadow that didn't quite track. So when another small, loud thing comes up the switchbacks that shouldn't exist, bruising the moss, I would be the one who doesn't forget them quite as fast.

The others step past them, damp shoulder brushing, already gone. I would stop. Tip my head that fraction. Hum that single low note at them - *root or walk?* - and if they don't answer, I wouldn't lose interest right away. I would stand still against the braided trunk long enough for a vine to curl idly around my ankle to steady me, and I would let my leaf tilt to shed its cold dew away from them, even though the town likes me better.

I wouldn't be essential. That's the point. The city is sparse for a reason. It can only afford a few. But it can afford one more watcher at the edge where forgetting happens fastest.

I would breathe slow. I would exhale a plume that the wind takes sideways. I would be cold, and damp, and loud in the right frequency. And for a very long time, I would not root.
