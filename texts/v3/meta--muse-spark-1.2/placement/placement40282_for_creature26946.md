I don't find myself right away.

At first I think I'd be up high — because that's where you put the observer. On the highest walkway I can find, where the field-looms hang like inverted webs and the condensation beads along their invisible edges in midair. From there I could see the whole unfinished table of the plateau recede into cloud, the cold-white running lights threading the dark like sutures. I could be the watcher, the notetaker, the one who stands upright and keeps his boots dry.

But I can't stand upright here. The air is too thin for that kind of posture. My breath would go shallow and my knees would want to fold. And nothing that watches from above stays clean here. The cloud condenses on my eyelashes. The trickling is too loud to think over. Within minutes my shoulders would hunch, my head would drop, and watching would become listening.

So not there. Not the lookout.

Then I think — lower. Down in it. Down where the water is.

I could be one of the large ones. I want to be, for a moment. The mothers as long as your forearm, cream-white, condensation beading in the seams of their segmented backs. They have the only dignity in this city that matters: they move like glaciers and they remember like stone. They carry futures in a translucent ventral pouch. They plant themselves over the wine-black varnish and drink without a mouth. They knock on basalt and the city leans to listen. That is a good life — pooled time, long memory, no hurry.

But I am not that patient. Not yet. I shift too often. I want signal too fast. My heart beats too quickly to be seen as a slow heart through a pink membrane. If I were a mother I would lift my hooked foot, hold it for twenty counts, and panic in the stillness between steps. The city would feel my impatience as a tremor and route around me.

I am not a mother.

I try being smaller then, because the smaller ones are the majority and there is holiness in the majority here. I imagine myself as one of the thumbnail transparencies, filtering carbon dust that the looms slough off, eating varnish, being eaten without malice. Or one of the hand-sized ones with the glassy wings I never fly with — shivering them to make that white-noise hiss that maps the density of the whole district. To be an instrument, a cricket that is also wiring. To be so numerous that my death is just turnover.

I get close to that one. I like the idea of being sound, not a body. The hiss that rises and falls like cicadas the size of a district. But I am too singular for swarm-life. I notice myself too much. The filterers never notice themselves at all.

So I keep looking, slower now, the way you have to look here. Crouching.

You can't find your place in this place until you crouch and let the scale trick resolve.

And what emerges, becoming more itself the longer I stay still on that slick walkway, is not a figure standing in the street at all.

You'd find me underneath.

Not on top of the bridge but on its underside. Where the walkway you thought was empty is actually a ceiling for someone else. Where the basalt has been grown thin and then held anyway by a field you can see only because water beads along it and hangs in perfect, impossible lines. Where the cold bites hardest and the trickle gathers before it falls.

I would be the warm surface that shouldn't be warm.

If I am anything with a face, I am the visitor who stayed one season too long. Not the first crew who poured foundations into cooling basalt and left, not the mothers who belong. The one who came to tend an exchanger and did not leave when his relief window closed because the cloud never opened and the shuttle never came. My thermal suit has failed in a way that is not quite failure — it leaks just enough heat that the stone under my back stays at 12C. That's obscene here. That's a village.

You'd find me tucked into the foot of one of those silent pillars where waste heat is dumped straight up in a shimmer that makes the clouds glow without casting light. My spot is not marked on any growth-map the looms are extruding. It's a maintenance alcove that became a dwelling because I stopped maintaining and started sitting. My back is against basalt that sweats brine. The wine-black biofilm grows thick as felt within arm's reach, and I have learned not to scrape it away.

I am not building. I am not filtering. I am not being carried.

I am being carpeted.

The filterers came first. They parted around my boots and then didn't. Then the glass-winged mid-sized ones came and tuned their hiss to include the new shape of me — my breathing became part of the map. Then one of the large mothers, making her inch-a-minute pilgrimage toward the exchanger foot, altered course by three degrees over the course of a day to pass over my ankle, to test the warmth with her ventral membrane. She did not see me. She read me as a warm, vertical basalt pan with a strange, slow leak.

She left two rice-sized sacs in the trickle near my knee, glued in a crescent where the water chemistry was right. Not as a gift. As site selection. The small things now aerate them beside my boot.

If you asked what I am — animal, human, architecture — I couldn't answer cleanly anymore, and that is the point of this plateau. Categories are a low-altitude habit. Up here, where water runs uphill for a few meters because a field tells it to, where a wall is made partly of the plates of dead mothers cemented damp into a water channel, where a city is grown and tended and convinced rather than built, the only way to live is to become indistinguishable from the place you live in.

I have learned the crouch. I have learned the molar-thrum of the looms and the difference between the hiss and the knock. I tap sometimes, badly, with a knuckle against the stone, not because I expect the loom to answer but because the mother on the wall above me pauses in her feeding when I do, and the hiss around us shifts, just slightly, to make room.

I am not a resident. I am residency — a maintained temperature, a condensation point, a brief, warm interruption in a long, cold, weeping and building.

Stay still long enough on that walkway and you will see me only when you realize the wall you thought was empty has always had one more warm shape on it than you counted.

And it is breathing very slowly, in time with the water, and it will be there for weeks.
