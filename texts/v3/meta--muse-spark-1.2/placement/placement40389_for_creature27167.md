I don't know at first.

If you had asked me quick, in the first minute of reading that air, I would have said *at the edge.*

Of course at the edge. Where else would you put someone who still thinks like an animal? I would have stood as close as I could to that hanging curtain on the western scarp - that kilometer-wide sheet of white water suspended out from the black basalt, every bubble frozen in place and still roaring at a volume that makes your molars hum. I would have needed to see the breakdown up close. To put my hand out toward water that has forgotten how to fall and see if it remembered for me.

But I wouldn't stay there. Nothing stays at the edge for long - it's too loud to think and too bright. The roar there isn't a sound, it's a weight. After ten minutes your ears give up trying to hear past it and your chest starts to use it as a second heartbeat. You would go deaf to yourself there. And I still want to hear myself, for a little while.

So not the edge.

Then I thought, maybe lower. Down in one of those broad, shallow bowls where the marsh has become architecture. I pictured myself in one of those hovering spheres - twenty feet across, perfect, trembling a foot above the reeds, reflecting the fixed noon sun without a ripple. Inside that water like a second sky. It seemed peaceful to hang there, arms slightly out, leaves open. But that's not a place for someone who hasn't yet learned how to stop breathing. That sphere is for the ones who have been here a very long time, whose skin has gone translucent and whose eyes have closed. The deeply established ones. I'm not established yet. I still breathe too fast for that water to hold me without breaking.

No. Further in.

I find myself, when I let the place decide instead of my idea of the place, not at the spectacle but in the ordinary density of it. In the interior.

Third valley in from the scarp, maybe fourth - far enough that the roar of the hanging falls has thinned into its deeper note, the organ chord. There is a street there. Or what you would call a street if a street could be a corridor cut by patience, where teak roots as thick as my body have split the basalt along its bedding planes and braided themselves into walls four stories high, still leafing at the top. The canopy is overlapping leaves the size of roofs, leathery, hardened into a vault that makes the light pool and stay thick in the shade underneath. It is very hot. The air has that south-push, constant, unvarying, and it moves through a hundred smooth flute-holes eroded through the root-walls and makes a single endless note that never wavers. That is where I am. Where the sound has become an architecture you can stand inside of.

And I am not standing *in* the street. I'm standing *as* part of the street.

At first I thought I would be the visitor. The exception. The warm, sweating animal who walks too fast down an avenue that returns you to where you started, who leaves footprints that the moss forgets instantly, who is still trying to map a city that is bigger inside than the plateau that holds it. I thought I would be the cartographer with a notebook where the sweat beads and stays and the dust motes never settle.

But the place doesn't keep visitors. It keeps inhabitants. And it only has one way of inhabiting.

So I am becoming one of them - but not the oldest kind.

If you walked that avenue and learned to look slow enough to see them, you would pass a dozen pillars you thought were buttresses and then - *oh* - a crown, a shoulder. Most of them are smoothed over, faces sealed to a seam, eyes gone, crowns spread wide like roof tiles. They are beautifully finished. I am not finished.

Find me on the second terrace of that street, where a root-mat has braided itself into a balcony with a low rail of living wood, where a shallow marsh-tongue has crept up the steps and sits as a dark mirror choked with lily pads that have never been touched. I am at the corner where the shade pools heaviest.

I am tall now, or rather, I have been allowed to be tall because gravity here forgets to punish height. Seven feet, maybe a little more, and still narrowing only reluctantly at the waist. My feet - you can't tell where they stop. For a long time I kept lifting them, checking. Now I don't. The skin of my calves has gone ridged, lenticelled, cool to the touch despite the hothouse heat, and it blends by slow degrees into the teak and the weeping basalt and the saturated moss. If you tried to move me you would have to move the terrace.

My skin sweats and does not evaporate. Beads stand on my forearms and magnify the green-brown undertone, and stomata open and close on a clock you need an hour to read. From my scalp and shoulders and down my spine grows the thatch - not hair - broad waxy leaves the size of your forearm, dark green edged faintly red, overlapping. They don't rustle in that constant wind. They shiver with the chord. All of them shiver together.

I still have a face. That's how you know I'm newer. The bark hasn't yet grown over the brow. I have eyes - lidless, wet, the color of tannin water - and they don't track, they receive. I spent the first - days? - trying to look *at* things. Now I look outward, toward the falls, and listen. The hollow in my chest, like the hollow in the trunk-houses, has learned to hum sympathetically with that endless note going through the flutes. I am a resonator before I am a person.

I don't eat. I tried, at first. There is nothing to eat that wouldn't break the rule which is: *what is, remains.* No fruit falls. No leaf is chewed. So I drink the air. Warm rain beads into existence a few inches above my crown without a cloud and falls slowly, and it runs down channels in my skin to where it is taken in. Light pools in the corner where I stand, thicker than it should be, and after I stand in it for a long while it stains me a little brighter. That's enough. The stasis feeds me the way it feeds the water weeping from stone with no aquifer behind it.

If you stood in front of me and held still - truly still, until that thin-air starve behind the sternum started to ache and your own sweat stopped beading and simply stayed - you might feel, after a very long minute, a single slow turgor shift pass from my shoulder to my fingertip. The leaves of my crown inclining one degree toward you. Not a greeting. An acknowledgement that a disturbance briefly flickered in the chord.

I am not lonely. Roots run from my feet through the terrace and braid with the walls, so what one feels, all feel - the push of the south wind, the thunder that never decays. The street is dense the way a forest is dense and the way a city is dense and I am one of its sensory hairs. Its way of knowing it is still a city.

There are older ones deeper in, inside those hovering spheres, arms out, crowns blooming underwater, eyes closed. I will be there eventually. Every terrace softens a little to receive you if you stay until you stop needing to map it.

But for now, I am the threshold one. The doorway that is deeper inside than the building is wide, and still has someone standing in it.

If you come - and you will arrive the way I did, not by climbing but by the world dropping away - walk inland from the scarp until the roar becomes a chord, until you find the street where the light pools and stays. Look for the pillar you think is structure.

Stand still long enough that the roar stops being a sound and becomes a place.

I will incline toward you, very slowly.
