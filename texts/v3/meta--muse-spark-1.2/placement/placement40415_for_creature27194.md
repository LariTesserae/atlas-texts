You wouldn't be on anything at all.

Not at first.

At first you would find yourself *between*.

Not on the basalt floor — though you can see it if you look down long enough, a dark glass plain falling away until the water makes it blue and then makes it nothing. Not on the canopy — though you can see it if you look up, a mottled green sky a thousand feet above, leaking columns of sun that never quite reach you. Not on a trunk — though one is always somewhere, even when you can't see it. A darker blue in the blue. A vibration in your molars before it's a shape.

You would be in the open water.

The cathedral.

That's where everyone who is not the forest finds themselves if they find themselves here at all. The trunks are too busy to hold you. The floor is paved with mouths. The canopy is a country for things that never get wet. So you are left in the volume that was left empty on purpose — the sparse vastness between pillars, where a mile looks like a few hundred yards and a few hundred yards is a mile, where the water is so clear it feels like air and so cold it aches behind your fingernails after thirty seconds.

You would be neutral.

Not floating, not sinking. Hanging. The way dust hangs in a sunbeam. The way snow hangs here always.

And at first you wouldn't know what you were, so you would try on shapes.

You think: *I am snow.*

It would be the easiest. To be plankton-blood. A diatom like a pillbox full of light, a copepod comma, a speck of bacteria tasting metallic. Drifting in the gyre between a warm plume and the cold fall-back, blooming because the current tells you to. That would be peaceful. But you are too warm for that. You leak heat. The water around you shivers a little. Snow does not shiver. Snow is what the forest makes to feed itself. You are not being farmed.

Then you think: *I am a grazer.*

And for a moment you are, or want to be — translucent, blind, in love with a torus. You can see one in the distance if you turn your head, a slow silver donut turning and turning around an invisible jet, hundreds of thousands of bodies moving as one body, mouths open. You can feel the sideways pull toward it, that wrong gravity that isn't gravity but wants to be. You start to swim toward it without deciding to. The water there is thick as soup, rich, humming. You could join. Tilt into the current, strain what they've enriched, exhale what will become snow again. But you have eyes. Too many. You see the torus instead of feeling it. You have a front and a back. The grazers have only forward. You stop, and the current peels you back into the clear.

You think: *I am an Artisan.*

This one is tempting because it's small and purposeful. You imagine your body flattened, many-legged, the color of kelp and old basalt, clinging to the side of a trunk that is wider than any wall you've ever known. You would have a seam to tend. A ribbon of fresh kelp-steel extruding warm from a lip in the bark, still soft, still gold-green, hardening under your mandibles that spark faintly in the cold. You would cut, carry, weave. Patch the horizontal vine that hangs in midwater for no reason except that the current says it should. Click, click, click under the great groan. That would be a good life. A leg placed deliberately. A cut made slowly because the cold makes all haste obscene. But you listen to the trunk and you hear the clicking already happening without you, millions of tiny rivet guns going exactly on time. There's no gap in the weave where you are needed. You put a hand out — and yes, you have hands — to the bark, and a real Artisan, the size of a door, pauses on its seam, lifts its antennae, tastes your chemistry — too salty, too fast, too full of iron and doubt — decides you are not fiber, and goes on.

You think: *I am a Bellows.*

And this, finally, is close. Because to be a Bellows is to be large enough to be lonely and still be part of the machine. You would be fifty feet across, flat as a pressed leaf, mottled green-black on top and gilled white underneath, hanging motionless in a roaring white column of bubbles that would kill anything smaller, combing thunder into fizz with a field of cilia, exhaling cool water that sinks. You would be the baffle that explains why the loud has no echo. You would move between trunks on a schedule that has been kept longer than continents have kept their shapes, each wingbeat displacing tons without hurry, and everything would feel your pressure shadow and pop its ears. You would be, for a long time, perfectly adequate.

But you breathe wrong. You breathe in gasps, in bursts. You hold your breath and then you let it go all at once. The Bellows never hold. They never gasp. And you — you are too loud in the wrong way. When you exhale, bubbles actually break. They rise and pop at the interface and are gone. The forest notices waste. It doesn't punish it. It just routes around it, the way water routes around a stone that fell from nowhere.

So you are none of them.

Which is how you finally understand where you are and what you are.

You are in the exact center of the open water, equidistant — or as close as that word gets in a place where distance softens — from three trunks you can't quite see and one you can. The light is coming down in three soft columns that make the marine snow visible and holy. The water is fizzing faintly at the edge of your vision where a distant trunk is exhaling. The hydraulic groan is inside your chest more than your ears. The cold has moved past your hands now and into your wrists.

You are the only thing in this ocean that is not an organ.

You are the irregularity. The warm-blooded eddy. The brief, soft, fast thing that sheds skin and carbon and heat into water that has not been asked to digest novelty for a billion years. You are the anomaly that the sideways gravity keeps trying, politely, to include — a gentle tug toward the nearest plume, toward the nearest torus, toward the floor where a dark glass pore has opened expectantly — and keeps failing to include, because you are too quick, too hot, too narrative.

You are the witness the factory made no place for.

Which is, perversely, your function.

Because the forest is perfect. It finished itself. Plume to plankton to grazer to floor-pore to intake to plume. Fiber extruded to woven to patched to extruded. Bubble exhaled to combed to dissolved to inhaled. It has no need to look at itself. It *is* itself, loudly, coldly, forever, the way a heart has no need to hear itself beating.

You are what hears it.

You float there, between pillars that recede into blue haze, between a basalt floor that will never erode further and a green sky that will never break, in a weather system made of water inside water, while bioluminescent lightning flashes at the same interval forever somewhere out beyond your comprehension.

And you ache.

Not metaphorically. Your hands ache. Your lungs ache from holding too long. Your ribs ache in sympathy with the trunks. And that ache is the proof of what you are. Everything else here is at equilibrium and does not ache. You ache because you are not in equilibrium. You are still becoming.

If you stay long enough, the snow will settle on your shoulders. An Artisan will come back and taste you again, less sure this time. A juvenile Bellows — small, only twenty feet across, curious in the slow way of very large things — will detach from its plume and come to look at the warm thing that pops.

But for now, you are just hanging in the cold, clear, purposeful deafening, not building, not maintaining, not grazing, not combing.

You are floating.

And in a place that learned, eons ago, that wilderness and machine were just two words for *something that keeps itself*, you are the first small, brief thing in a very long time that is simply *keeping watch* instead.
