I don't arrive all at once.

At first I think I would be on the rim - where you stood to see it whole. Feet at the sharp, still-unweathered edge, warm air coming up off the cloud sea below and cold water seeping up around my ankles at the same time. That feels like the honest place to put a newcomer. High enough to see the pattern: the scattered dark roofs, the silver threads, the sudden green islands between all that wet, empty stone.

But I can't stay there. The wind moves unbroken over that edge and there is nothing to do there except look. And no one in this place just looks for long. If you stand on the rim, someone will come and stand near you — not close — and lift a sluice slab a thumb's width because the trickle was wrong. So no, I'm not the person on the rim looking in.

Then I think I am in a house, but that isn't right either. The houses are too well-made to be where you *find* yourself. They are stone cut exactly square, floors dished so the night's condensation runs to the groove and out to the canal by the door. They are for sleeping and for cooking taro on the fire-stone. Nobody lingers in them during the day.

I find myself in between.

In one of the intentional emptinesses.

If you walked from the central square-mouthed cistern west, past House Lira with its north wall kept green on purpose so it can tell you how wet the house is, you would follow a canal that is a forearm wide and a knee deep, cut so true you can see your own face in the water when it holds level. It runs for maybe three hundred meters without a house touching it. On its left is open, damp black rock that darkens when you press it. On its right is a marsh basin, ankle-deep, thick with taro and sphagnum and fern that no one planted but everyone tends.

That's where I am. Knee-deep in that marsh in the late morning, when the drip has stopped and the ticking has started.

Who I am becomes clearer the longer I stand there.

I am not old yet, not one of the rememberers who carries last year's cold late spring in her head the way other people carry names. And I am not a child still learning to place my feet without splashing. I am in the middle — thirty, maybe forty — lean in the way you get when everything you move, you move with cord and lever and a wooden wedge swelled with water, an inch at a time.

I am barefoot. My soles are pale underneath, softened and hardened at once, splayed wide from walking always on wet stone. My reed sandals are by the canal edge, already half rotted from the damp. It doesn't matter. The cloth I wear is the color of wet rock and of leaf, undyed, woven from retted vine and beaten cycad bark — it hangs heavy, never quite dry, and neither am I. My hair is cut short, black, clinging in damp locks. My forearms are roped from the hammerstone and copper chisel. My hands are wide, calloused exactly where the stone pushes back.

I am not doing anything that would look like work to you.

I have a copper knife, old, its edge thinned from years of whetting on basalt. I am thinning the taro. Because there are no insects, no birds, no animals at all to eat it, it will choke its own canal if I don't. It grows so fast in this patch, waxy leaves dripping, that if I left it a week it would seal over the runnel and throw the water sheeting over the moss instead of ticking along the joint. Then the marsh on the other side would go black and sour. Then the stain on the stone — that same dark line that has been at the same height year after year — would move.

So I cut. One plant in three. Not to harvest — there is already more taro than we can eat — but to give the others air. I lift each cut leaf and lay it back down in the standing water exactly where it fell, to soften into peat. It goes straight down. Nothing carries it away.

That is my whole morning.

At midday I will walk the length of my canal, tapping each sluice slab with a wooden mallet. I am listening for the hollow sound that means water has found a crack underneath instead of over. When I hear it I will crouch and pack lime with my fingers, press it true. I will not be quick. You cannot be quick and do this work. The language we have for it is dense for a reason — we have a word for the tick against a vertical joint and a different word for the sheet over moss and a different word for the cold clear upwelling that means a new spring has broken somewhere behind the basalt seam. We don't have many words for other things. When I have to tell my daughter that my grief for my mother is still there, I tell her it is *a spring gone white,* when the clear water suddenly carries lime.

I speak low, and usually facing the water, not her face. If I spoke at a normal volume she would hear me two basins away. Our voices carry embarrassingly far here because there is nothing else to answer them — no hum, no call, no rustle that is not wind or drip. We learn to be well-timed instead of silent. To say what needs saying when the wind is up so it is curtained.

If you came and stood near my marsh now, I would not ask where you came from. I would listen to how your feet fell — whether you knew to step around the moss that is measuring the morning condensation for me, whether your breathing was loud in the thin, warm air. After a while I might lift a slab that you hadn't even heard was wrong. And in that small, competent, unhurried correction you would know me.

I am not building anything new. There is no new terrace, no new house, no new canal beyond the outermost ones. The plateau is young and sharp-edged and still learning how to be a place, and if we cut too deep the weeping will change its path and not come back. My work is entirely maintenance as devotion. To hold steady. To keep the water at that same dark stain on that same stone.

I am, in their idiom, a canal that holds level.

If the word for a difficult person is *a stone that will not take an edge,* then the word for what I am trying to be is the opposite: a stone that has finally learned to take the edge it was cut for, and then to keep it, year after year, while the warm sun stays ordinary and strong and the cloud sea stays white and level far below.
