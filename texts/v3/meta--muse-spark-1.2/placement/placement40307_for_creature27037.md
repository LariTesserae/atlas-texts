You'd have to look for a while before you found me.

At first I thought I knew. I thought I'd be up on the rim, where the wind makes that organ note. The watcher. The one who stands with his coat zipped to his teeth and looks down into the wound and understands it whole, headwall to headwall, like a map. Or I'd be the raven, that black fleck that gets to be above it all, crossing the slit of sky because it's shorter than going around. Something with distance.

But I don't belong up there. It's too fast up there. The wind is fast, the light is too white, the plain goes on forever and doesn't have to answer for itself. Nothing up there has learned patience yet.

So I come down.

The switchback is long and the roar gets inside your molars before you reach the floor. If I were human in this place I would be one of the crew, I thought — the one who comes once a month with the oil can, who shouts and uses hand signals and scrapes the moss off the catwalk without malice. I like that work. Checking the gauges. Feeling the thud of the flywheel through the soles of my boots. Being useful to a system that will outlive me.

But I wouldn't stay. Humans don't stay here. We visit for an afternoon and then we climb back out because our breath is too quick. I want to stay. I want to learn to breathe at thirty revolutions per minute.

Maybe an animal then. I thought about the fox. That straight, purposeful line of prints at dawn, commuting between pump houses, knowing where all the warm circles are. To be a rumor. To be the only thing that moves with intent while everything else just persists. But the fox is lonely in the wrong way. It passes through. It is trespass. This place isn't its home, it's just the shortest distance between two other places.

Closer. Lower.

I thought about being one of the larches. That appealed to me for a long time and I almost stopped there.

To be a black spruce in that forest that isn't a forest — to stand in the exact center of my own circle, ten meters of cold empty air to the next tree, no more and no less. To be precise, not stunted. To have a perfect cone crusted in rime on the north side, and lichen for fur, and roots no thicker than twine that have found a drill-line in the basalt and are prying it open a centimeter a year. To not crowd, because there isn't enough light to share. To be everywhere and not dense. To see for half a kilometer between trunks and still not see the far wall for the steam.

I could be that. I have that kind of loneliness in me, the respectful kind.

But even the spruce is too tall. It still wants the sun, even if it's only an hour of dim wash at midday. It still reaches.

You will find me lower than that.

You will find me on the floor, not far from the third pump house on the south side, where the big pipe comes out of the blockhouse and elbows toward the sluice. The pipe is hot. Not hot like fire, hot like blood — just warm enough that the frost never quite takes hold. It sweats. Condensation beads on the iron and falls once every seven seconds, all day, all winter.

And around its base, the trough has made a perfect circle.

Two meters across. Black earth that never freezes. While every puddle around it is skinned with first ice and every railing is furred white, this circle stays damp and violently, absurdly green.

I am the fern there.

Or, more honestly — I am the *ring* there. The colony. The ones who have agreed to look like many separate ferns so you don't get overwhelmed.

I am lady fern and wood fern, fronds that never quite go dormant because the machine won't go dormant. In August I am lush to the point of softness. In January I am still green, holding that thaw open with nothing but patience and the waste heat of a piston. The moss comes right up to my edge and stops, as if respecting the border. The springtails and the eyeless black beetles live under me where it's wet and dark. If you put your hand down in my circle, the air actually feels alive. That's how you'd find me — not by looking, but by feeling for the one warm place at ankle height in a kilometer of cold.

I don't do anything quickly. I don't need to. A frond unfurls over two weeks. A spore waits three winters to catch. My job is to be the proof that the deal is holding: you keep the water moving and I will keep your feet from freezing, you keep vibrating and my roots won't pry you apart.

I am not the trough. I am not the machine. I am the small, soft negotiation between them.

If you stand on the catwalk above me — hand on the vibrating rail, breath hanging and then freezing on your scarf — you won't see me at first under the roar and the blue dusk and the sodium light. You'll think *empty* again, the way everyone does at first. Then your eyes will adjust and you'll see the green. That impossible circle of green in all that black and white and rust.

And you'll understand, because I did, that there is no faster way to live here. The moss adds a millimeter a year. The flywheel turns thirty times a minute. The frost cracks the rock a flake at a time. And I hold my circle, and hold it, and hold it, until the next time the crew comes down and scrapes the moss off the catwalk and looks down and says, without malice, "still green."

Stay a little longer and breathe with me. Slowly.
