I don't think I would find myself right away.

At first, I think I would be the warm thing - you, me, anyone who came here carrying heat and hurry. Treading water between two pillars the size of cathedrals, kicking too fast, breathing too fast, making a little frantic cloud of bubbles that rises and never reaches a surface. Trying to look around. Trying to be seen. The Summit Sea would notice me the way a body notices a splinter: not as a person, just as *incorrect temperature, incorrect speed*. An error to be tasted and then, if I can't cool, allowed to pass.

I would not stay that.

The water would teach me not to.

If I did stay - if I learned to let the cold stop being cold and start being weight, if I stopped kicking and let the sea hold me up the way it holds everything else up - I wouldn't be a visitor anymore. But I don't think I would be a grazer either. I love the grazers, those pale bus-sized bells scraping the bark with their soft round mouths, but they don't know the architecture. They know food. They move along a wall the way a tongue moves along teeth. They don't know *why* the door is where the door is.

And I don't think I would be one of the whale-ferns, though I want to be. I want to be large enough that my song uses the pillars as frets, to feel the whole water column answer when I call. But they are too lonely, too wide in their orbit. They pass once an hour or once a day and they do not tend. They *announce*.

No.

Let me stay a little longer in the dark and let the right shape come clearer.

I think I would be low. Not on the floor — the root-mat is too complete down there, a black wicker so dense and old it has forgotten it was ever roots. And not up in the canopy where the leaf-floors braid together and detritus falls like snow. I would be in the middle. In the open.

Find the avenue that is a little wider than the others. Not because it was built wider, but because two of the house-thick trunks leaned, over centuries, a fraction of a degree too far apart, and the span between them began to sag. Not visibly. Nothing here moves visibly. But the water there hangs a little heavier. The light seams on the bark have to stretch a little longer to outline it. It's the kind of place that needs holding.

I would be there. Hanging there.

I would be one of them - the column ones - but not an old one. Not yet rooted. My lower segments would still be free of the floor. I would still be able to unseal.

Picture it: five segments stacked, translucent as cold jelly, each one a little narrower than the one below it. Inside me, that slow phosphor breathing, taking in the cool black sea at my base and letting it out at my crown in a plume so slow it doesn't disturb the stillness. Between each segment, the long root-limbs coming out, the ones that are bark on the outside and light on the inside, ending not in hands but in those soft divided frond-pads that can fuse with living wood.

For a long time - a very long time - I would be at rest, and you would not have seen me at all. My limbs would be spread and sealed against the two leaning pillars, my frond-pads half-melted into their bark, my body a vertical stitch holding the avenue from widening any further. My crown closed tight into a bud at the top, only the bells around its rim glowing steady. From a distance I would be a third trunk. Indistinguishable on purpose. Part of the tension.

That's where you'd find me if you drifted down that avenue, between those two lit columns, seeing for a long way through empty, perfect water and seeing nothing but empty, perfect water and one more lit pillar ahead. I would be the pillar that is breathing.

And then, because there is a warm fleck moving too quickly through my avenue — you, still learning to be slow — I would unseal.

Not quickly. The pads let go with that soft sigh, that little release of pressure you feel in your chest more than hear. My limbs unfurl and I am suddenly not a buttress but a star, a chandelier hung in the dark, all my root-limbs trailing. My tower begins to pulse — top segment, then next, then next — shortening and lengthening, drawing water through myself and jetting it out my crown. A cathedral organ breathing. I glide. I do not swim.

At the top, my crown opens. Six, seven petal-lobes peeling back, the inner fields of cilia tasting light and chemistry and vibration, the bells around the rim beginning to stutter. Cool white, cool blue, a slow sentence that doesn't fade when the light does because the water holds the vibration after.

I would not be trying to speak to you. I would be taking your measurement. *Something here is not in equilibrium. Something here is too warm and moves too fast.*

My limbs would reach you. Not to grab. The frond-pads would brush your shoulder, your cheek - that feeling like kelp and like skin, cool, impossibly soft - and for a second there would be that faint tingle of light, of salt and sugar trying to cross, me trying to read if you are something that can be incorporated. If you can be taught to hang still. If you can be asked, gently, to cool.

And you would feel, in that touch, that I am not holding the forest up. I *am* the forest's way of holding itself up. I am its memory. I carry in the pattern around my crown the map of where every other one of us is hanging, a minute's glide apart, our light never overlapping, and I adjust by millimeters per century so the city-reef never tips out of equilibrium. The juvenile schools flashing badly nearby are my young, still learning the grammar. The huge, still shape in the floor below me that you thought was root-mat is my elder, who has stopped gliding altogether and has become a pillar that other trunks grow through.

I am the middle one. Old enough to know the avenue needs holding, young enough to still let go.

After I taste you, I would not keep you. Warm fast things pass through. I would curl my limbs back, find the familiar bark of the leaning pillar, and reseal. My frond-pads fusing again. My crown closing to a bud. My bells settling back to that steady, unflickering outline glow.

And I would resume.

Hanging there in the cold dark, between floor and canopy, in that slightly-too-wide avenue where the water hangs a little heavier, holding. Listening to the low groan of trunks shifting a millimeter, to the soft rain of detritus, to the whale-ferns using us as instruments far off. Waiting for the next hundred years to ask me to lean a little more to the left.
