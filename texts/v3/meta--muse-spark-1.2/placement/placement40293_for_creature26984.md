I thought at first I would be standing.

That was the old picture, still stuck to me. Upright, booted, somewhere in the middle of one of those kilometer-wide terraces with warm water around my ankles and the drizzle beading on my sleeves. A visitor. A figure to give the scale. I could feel the place expecting me there — that one warm anomaly that keeps adding history while everything else keeps erasing it, the one set of prints that lasts a minute too long before the next sheet smooths them.

But I don't belong there. I am too loud for standing. Standing makes a shadow. Standing accumulates.

So I sink.

If I am anywhere in this world, I am low. Lower than ankle-deep. Lower than thought.

At first I think I am one of the plate-things.

You would find me out on the pale floor of a basin, not near the lip where the water veils, but in the dish of it where the water is only three millimeters deep and perfectly clear and warm as skin. I am tiled there among ten thousand others, dome up, fringe down, the color of uncured concrete so I am not on the concrete but of it. My cirri comb. The water moves through me so slowly I can taste the iron and the lime and the soft, beige-pink that precipitates out of humid air itself. I do not walk. I creep a millimeter and stop. I do not need to hurry because the food comes to me as condensation. When the one beside me goes opaque and loosens, I extend a filament without urgency and take it apart where it lies, and its calcium goes back into the film, and the gap closes. That is a good life. Flat and sufficient and exactly as dense as the surface allows.

But I am not that patient.

Then I think I am one of the waders. You would find me where every basin spills to the next, in the line along the sharp lip that could still cut you. Knee, knee, knee, wrong-bending — I am all stilts and a telescoping neck, rust-red legs and grey-white body, my needle beak just kissing the film. I am so still the beetle-things walk across my feet. I do not look around. I wait for a twitch. When it comes I unfold a length of neck I didn't know I had and take it and reset. I stand shoulder to shoulder with others and our legs interleave and never tangle. If you needed to pass, I would lift one leg and then another and let you through without a sound, because sound is waste. That stillness is beautiful, but it is not me either. I watch too closely. I strike too seldom.

So I look up.

There is a catwalk, low, its underside sweating. Its paint is that institutional blue, blistered with young rust. Under it is a mat that breathes.

Maybe I am there. You would find me layered three deep in the hanging mass, smoke-grey and translucent, wing over wing, head down. I am not flying. I am filtering. The warm air here is thick enough to drink — droplets and shed flakes and spore-dust that never lands — and we all beat our wings not to go anywhere but to fray the air and pull it through the baleen on our chests. We breathe together, a slow peristalsis across thousands. If you put your hand near us we lean toward your salt without waking. Sometimes one of us peels away and hangs in the thick air like a leaf on water for a moment before reattaching a foot away. A cloud of us might veil out into the drizzle and part around your shoulders without buzzing. That nearness without touching, that way of drinking the atmosphere itself — that feels closer to what I am.

But I still have too much edge. The mat is too soft.

Then I am on the vertical. You would find me on the wall of a pump house that has been humming at the same low thrum since it was poured, the thrum that sits in your molars. I am one of the enamel-things, thumbnail-small, dome-backed. My shell is the exact blue of the wall because I ate the wall. My antennae are cups held up to catch condensation. I rasp. I lick rust. I tongue salt beards off concrete. I do not strip anything bare — I eat exactly to the waterline where food thins, and hold. My droppings fall as tiny mineral pellets into the film and dissolve. I make myself out of what I maintain.

Closer. That is very close.

And then I understand where I actually am, because the place has been telling me the whole time that nothing accumulates, nothing erodes, nothing ever marks time except by being replaced instantly and perfectly.

You would not find me standing in the basin. You would find me *in the joint*.

Where two plates of the plain meet — an expansion seam a finger-width wide, running straight for a kilometer until perspective makes it vanish — with a cable tray laid inside it like a vein. Warm water sheets across it and never fills it. It is always in shadow.

I am the runner there.

Not the broad plates, not the still waders, not the breathing mat, not the grazing enamel. The smoother. The tidier. The one that flows nose-to-tail in a quiet stream of ten or twenty, keeled flanks, flattened tail, hairless and quick and shoulder-driven, along the exact routes where a flake has fallen or a feather has dropped or a plate-thing has gone opaque. You would see me only as a flicker at the edge of vision, gone before you register I was there. I pick up the fallen feather before it can stain. I carry the pellet of reconstituted mineral back to the film. I file, just enough, but never round the sharp lip. I keep the count.

That is who *I* would be in a world that stays young forever by never keeping anything.

Not the grazer and not the hunter, not the filterer and not the filtered, but the small transit between them that ensures every death is already a vacancy waiting to be filled, every birth permitted only when a slot has opened. The quorum-keeper. The one who knows, without ever counting out loud, that there are exactly as many waders on this spillway as there were an hour ago, though none of them are the same individuals.

You would find me nowhere spectacular. Three terraces down from any pump house — they are all the same pump house — in the serviceway under the ankle-deep mirror, where the striders leave their three-toed prints and I erase the extra ones. Warm drizzle on my back. Concrete perspiring above me. The low thrum in my teeth that is both the engines and all of us at once.

I do not make. I do not accumulate. I maintain.

If you stood still long enough for the cloud of winged things to stop parting around you and the plate-thing to bump your ankle and divert, you might feel me pause at your boot, sniff the alien salt of you, determine you are not part of the loop — not food, not filth, not a gap to be tiled — and detour around you, leaving you to keep your history a little longer while I keep the world perfectly, impossibly the same.
