I thought I would find myself at the rim.

That is where you start, after all. Standing on the polished table of the summit plain with the dry wind scouring your ankles and the sun still hot on your shoulders, looking down into the cut where light ends. Observer-height. Human-height. Far enough away to see the whole thumbprint at once, to call it beautiful and terrible and leave before dark.

But I don't stay there.

The rim is too exposed for me. Too thin. The air up there is still that high desert air, 28 degrees and dust, and it scrapes. I can feel how quickly it would take the water out of me. Nothing lives up there. It is for passing over. I don't belong to the passing-over things.

So I go down, without deciding to.

Not all at once. Hand over hand down the collapsed chute where the wall has sagged into a ramp. The air changes halfway. That's where I find myself first - in the change. The moment the dry becomes humid. Where the stone stops radiating sun and starts sweating. The smell hits and I breathe it in and it stays in me: mineral, guano, warm stone, the faint tea-black tang of two inches of water that has been sitting in a scooped hollow for weeks.

At first I think I would be something with wings. That would be easiest. The bowl is so full of wings that to be otherwise feels like arguing with the room. I try on being a soot-bat for a minute - hanging three deep in a scallop of rust-red limestone, shoulder to shoulder, feeling the press of other warm bodies as a kind of comfort, listening to the leathery shuffle when someone shifts. Or a swiftlet, with that glassy click that becomes rain when ten thousand do it. Or even a moth, palm-sized and dusted, lifting when a bat moves and resettling like ash finds its place again.

But I am too heavy for hanging. And I am not good at being part of a curtain. I keep wanting to look *at* the curtain, to run a finger along the seam where the beetles run.

So maybe I am a beetle then. Glossy black, as long as a hand. Running the moisture lines. Endless, purposeful, following the faint green film of algae that only shows green if you get very close. That life is honest and ceaseless and would let me be small and necessary without needing to understand the bowl. But the beetle doesn't hear the bowl. It just runs it. And I hear too much. I can't be the beetle because the bowl is too loud for me to be deaf to it. The cricket drone is in my molars already, that electric sawtooth, and the plink-plink-plink of condensation falling out of no cloud at all, unsynchronized, and underneath it all the soft *hut hut* that you only notice when you stop trying to hear the loud things.

For a while I think I am the water. That I am not a creature at all. That I am the condensation itself - the way the air sweats onto the underside of an overhang, beads, swells, and falls. The way the rock weeps without a river. I would be content to exist only in the afternoons when the temperature differential pulls me out of thin air, to gather for hours and then fall three feet onto silt. To be the thing that everything else waits for. The succulents with their swollen waxy leaves are waiting for me. The lichen is waiting for me. Even the troop leaves their twigs where I will keep them pliant.

I linger there, because being water means being needed without being looked at. And for a moment that feels right.

But water doesn't remember where it left its tools.

And I remember.

That’s when I settle. Or when what I am settles into me and becomes more itself.

I find myself low on the north wall, not at the bottom where the silt is soft and stamped with broad prints, but on the second tier up, where an undercut has made a ledge as deep as a bed. The wall here is banded cream and ash grey and the ledge is stained pink-white with old droppings. A wiry succulent has rooted in a crack beside me and over years has sent a runner six inches toward the seep. The seep beside me is the big one, the meter-wide basin that never quite evaporates, black as tea, and beside it on the flat stone is my kit.

I did not build it. I curated it.

There is the chert flake, white-grey, sharp as a crescent moon. I carried it up from the scree below when I was younger and braver about the ramp, when the sun still felt possible. No chert grows here. Everything sharp here has to be carried. I turn it over in a palm that is ridged and damp and too sensitive for the plateau. My nails are short, black, curved. My feet are splayed too wide for walking on flat ground, the big toe set low and grasping at vertical algae. I cling without thinking about it. Hand, foot, hand, foot, tail flicking behind me for balance - a thin, furred tail that I don't even notice until I need it.

I am small. Two and a half kilos, forty centimeters, folded in. Ash-grey mottled with rust. Fur so short and close it looks like dust on the stone until I move against the line. My belly is pink-grey where the fur thins so I can dump heat - because here at 3,000 meters I should be shaggy and white and I am not; the bowl has unmade the mountain. My whiskers tremble without my permission, reading air that hardly moves. My ears - huge, translucent at the edges where blood shows through - swivel on their own, one toward the crickets, one toward the drip.

My eyes are too big for my face. Amber, and they catch the dim fungus-light on the guano and throw it back. I don't need sun. I see by phosphorescence, by the faint green of algae in a fissure, by silhouette.

I am not alone on this tier, but I am not crowded either. The bats are the crowded ones. We are twenty, thirty at most, spread thin and knowing exactly where each other is by a breathy *hut hut* that slides under the cricket drone. An infant clings to me, its face still pink, its eyes still shut against even this dusk. It will open them in a week. I feel its four limbs holding me.

A juvenile - maybe mine from two seasons ago - is fishing upstream from me with the twig. Stripped, bent while green, left in this exact fissure because the damp keeps it from snapping. It pulls out a blind white grub, eats it, and carefully replaces the twig. For tomorrow. It looks at me to see if it did it right. I make the soft sound and it relaxes.

I pick up the flake. A black beetle runs the moisture seam right past my foot, glossy, oblivious. I don't snatch. I palpate. I wedge the crescent edge under its carapace and pop. The meat scoops. My teeth are small, peg-like, not for crushing. That's why I need the edge.

When the pale viper comes up the ramp tonight - cream as limestone, narrow-headed, hunting by sound - I will press flat against the rock and vanish. Ash on ash. The bats will panic and lift in a storm and I will not move until the storm passes. I have learned to be quiet in a room that is deafening. That is the whole trick.

I do not know I live in a bowl on top of a mountain. To me the rim is the horizon. The oval of sky is a lamp that is sometimes hard blue-white and sometimes black. The world is twenty meters of tiered, weeping stone that holds just enough water if you know which ledge sweats at three o'clock, just enough food if you remember where you left your hook.

I hear the human that came down to look and left again before dawn, their breath shallow against the dust. I let them think the walls were just walls.

When they are gone I replace the twig in its crack.

For tomorrow.
