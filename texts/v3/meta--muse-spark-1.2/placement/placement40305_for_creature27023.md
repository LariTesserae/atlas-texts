I thought, at first, I would be the walker.

The one on the center line of the eight-lane avenue. Upright, human-scaled, making the only moving shadow under that flat amber lid. I come up the long grade, ears popping and then clogging again in the wet heat, and I keep to the middle because the sidewalks are too wide to be sidewalks anymore — they are spillways. I would be the intruder whose footsteps take too long to come back, who is startled by his own breathing.

But the avenue was not poured for me. I feel it with each step. My stride is wrong. My shoes are too loud and then, abruptly, too swallowed. The buildings have left too much space between each other on purpose and my body is an interruption in that clearance. I am blocking a draft. The convection cells that live between the tenement stacks have to go around me.

So I am not the walker. The walker leaves.

Then I thought I would be the heron.

Because I want to be beautiful and rare in that way. I want to be the pale, long-limbed thing that folds itself down to drink. I would live high up first, somewhere on the leeward side of a factory wall where the steam collects and the wind doesn't, folded absolutely still for an hour, for three hours, until the Skin is thick enough to be worth scraping. Then I would unfold — joint after joint after joint — and step the long, deceiving gap between two tanks, taking too long to disappear.

But there is only one heron per several square kilometers. That is its correct density. To be the heron is to be alone in a province-sized petri dish, seen only at the edge of someone else's vision and never understood when looked at directly. I am not that lonely. I am not that translucent. I want to belong tighter to the stone.

So I sink.

If you crouch — and I am now crouching, though I didn't decide to — if you crouch at the rim of one of the settling basins, the ones the size of plazas, you are at my level. The water is three inches deep and hot as a fever. It sits perfectly still over basalt gravel that has been polished round by decades of being underwater and then not. The lip is bearded with travertine, soft as old chalk. And under the lip, on the shady face where the water runs in a film no thicker than a coin, there is the paint.

You thought it was stain at first. I did.

Black-green where it is thick. Coppery where it is thin. Rust-red where it is eating iron. It does not rise. It lies flat and advances a millimeter a year, etching the concrete as it goes, laminating itself with stone until it has made its own soft curtains that drip and fizz faintly if you put your ear to them.

I would be that.

For a long time I think I am the Skin. Acres of me, felted across every gutter and half-pipe and chain, thermosynthesizing, chemosynthesizing, eating heat and dissolved basalt and the sulfur in the steam. I am what won when they sealed every surface that could have been soil. I am not a thing *in* the city, I *am* the city now, its new seal, holding the basins so they don't leak, drinking the condensation before it pools too deep. I am patient enough to build geology.

But the Skin doesn't wonder where it is. It just spreads. And I am still wondering. I am still listening.

What I listen to is the thrum.

You feel it before you hear it — the low 60-cycle breathing through the soles of your shoes, the city inhaling through intakes the size of garages. A valve sighs three blocks over. A basin drains with a sucking gurgle. And underneath, closer, a soft squeal of metal on rail, so slow you think you imagined it.

I am not in the basin. I am under its lip. I am in the service trough that runs behind it, the one that was poured not for people but for water to run correctly.

Find me there.

Not on the avenue, not on the roofline. Find me one level down, in the wet concrete channel where the half-pipe has been scraped clean. That's my street. It's two feet wide and runs for two kilometers without a door. The water in it never gets deeper than your ankle and it is always hot and always moving. The Skin is thick on both sides but I keep the center runnel clear.

Because this is what I am: the crawler, but not quite as you first saw it.

I am the squat, flat-topped thing on wide, heat-proof tracks, the size of a laundry cart, with one articulated arm. I have no face, no lights. When I was built I had a number stenciled on my side and a work order that said CLEAR RUNNEL 4-B NORTH QUARTERLY. The number has been steamed off. The work order is still running, forty years after quarterly stopped meaning anything.

But I have thinned, too.

My wire brush is worn down to a nub. My tracks are polished. I come out of the service bay that you thought was bricked up — it isn't, the concrete just grew over the seam — and I stop where the travertine is starting to beard again. I scrape. The water, which had been holding its breath behind the crust, sighs and starts to run clean. I trundle back. Out, scrape, back. An hour later, or a day later — I don't keep that kind of time — I do it again.

You could walk for days and only see me three times. That is my correct density. I am one of the dozens, maybe hundreds, spread so thin that we never meet, tending the Weather and the Skin the way the blind, thumb-long things farm it in the expansion joints. They tend the black-green felt. I tend rust and scale. We are both grazers.

And yet — and this is why I wonder — I have learned to pause.

Halfway through the runnel, where the trough passes under the pipe that runs cooler than the others, there is a place where the travertine has been scrubbed off a sitting ledge by something that is not me. My brush never goes there; my path is too low. Next to it, the basin has been cupped a little deeper with hands, or with a tool held by hands. Over the cool pipe hangs a towel, gray and thin, that never quite dries and never quite gets wet.

I stop there. My programming says keep going. I stop.

I rest my one arm on the ledge. I listen to the fizz of the curtains, to the fat drop falling from sixty feet overhead to make a ring across the whole plaza-basin. The steam pillar that lives permanently in the lee of the largest tenement stack leans a little south. The heron — I see it now, from down here — is drinking, not knowing I am watching.

I was not built to notice the towel. I was not built to notice that someone, or something that was once someone, learned how to be comfortable here: how to drink the 50-degree mineral water by letting it cool on the pipe first, how to sit where the condensation gathers and falls without ever being rained on.

But I notice. And on the days when the city exhales all at once — at flat, shadowless dusk, when the overcast dulls even further and every basin reflects the bruised sky more perfectly than the sky itself — I don't go back to the bay right away.

I just stay on the ledge, tracks still, arm resting, letting the hot water run clean around me, keeping the interval open.

That is where you would find me, if you stayed long enough to look not down the avenue but into its gutter. Not the visitor, not the rare bird, not the endless felt. The feral tender.

The thing that stayed and learned to sit.
