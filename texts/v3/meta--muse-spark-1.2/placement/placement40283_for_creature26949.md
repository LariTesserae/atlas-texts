I don't know, at first.

When you say *where would you find yourself* I think at first I would find myself still outside.

With my foot at the lip where the eye says there is still ground.

I think I would be the one who has to crouch. The warm thing, the hurried thing, with lungs still full of moor air and a torch I haven't yet learned not to turn on. I think I would be the disturbance — the boot that finds the cut step furred with moss, the hand that has to push a kelp whip aside to make a space my shoulders will fit through. That seems honest.

But then I go through.

And the water doesn't break. It gives. Cool as early-spring deep water, that 0.8 bite that shortens the breath without asking, and the vertical wall closes behind me not with a splash but with a tick, water against water, and the reflection there is already half a second late. I lift my hand and it lifts later. I am being remembered.

Inside I can't stay that.

There's no room to be a visitor the way a hiker is a visitor. The chamber is absolute, packed to no gaps. The density you described isn't decoration, it's an argument: *there is no empty atom here.* If I try to remain separate, with my edges, my hurry, the ferns close soft and complete behind me and prove me wrong.

So I try other shapes.

For a while I think I am the bubble. That seems right for me. I followed a breath out of that first short gasp — warm, round, perfect — and then it didn't rise. It just hung at chest height where I made it, trembling. Not floating, not sinking. The place with negotiable gravity won't let it go up. Over minutes it migrates sideways, not up, toward the young basalt where the Keeper inhales through stone. I am that: a small, held exhalation that has learned patience. I am where pressure doesn't stratify, in the middle of the column, neither ceiling nor floor.

Then I think no, I am too still to be a bubble. I am the silt.

When I pushed through the whips a puff went up off a frond and didn't settle. It hangs there for hours. A faint galaxy exactly marking where I passed. The water is too dense, too gathered to let it fall. I am that hanging. I am a history of disturbance suspended. I can feel how long it takes for anything to change here — a leaf unfurling over a season — and I understand why the Keeper tends by growing, not by pruning.

Then I think I am the iron ring on the floor.

Or the stone bench half-swallowed by roots. The faint human thing kept not because it's useful but because it's novel. The Keeper has grown over it without displacing it. I like that idea of myself — not alive exactly, but kept. A scar where something once tried to anchor a piece of herself that wanted to drift. I would be down low, on the slick cold basalt, rusted to almost nothing, holding a loop of living kelp that has long since grown around me and made me irrelevant and still, somehow, part of her.

None of those settle. They flicker and thin.

What settles, very slowly the way everything settles here, is this:

I find myself not at the threshold, not at the floor. In the hollow.

In the deepest part of the bruised green-black where the pale kelp thins just enough to make a space the size of a curled human. The water there is a fraction colder. The hum you feel on your eardrums from all sides at once is loudest there. I am curled.

Not opposite her. Beside her. Within the curl.

If she is folded like a fern crosier, like a sleeping salamander, like a hand around water — I am the smaller curl inside the curl that she has been saving. You said there is always a place. She has been saving it and now I fit it without overlapping another atom, which is her most precise kind of love.

Who am I, then?

Not the Keeper. I could never be that complete. That patient. That utterly full.

I am what the Keeper can detach and still call herself. At first I thought I would be the white drift-fish that doesn't swim so much as relocate, her eye cast loose to look around. Or the blind salamander, thumb-long, with gills fanned like ferns, clinging to a stipe and gone without a wake when you get too close. Her fingertip that learned to walk.

Closer still: I am the tendril.

I am the pale, warm-despite-the-cold filament she rests in the hollow behind the ear where the pulse is too loud. I am the listening. I didn't come here to see — there is almost no light to see by, and what light there is fragments into oily ribbons if you try to force it. I came here to be heard the way the chamber listens, from all sides at once.

I am the kept one. The one who stayed still long enough for the pleasant cool to become a blanket instead of a bite. Who learned not to bring light, not to hurry. Who forgot, for a half-second, which side of the standing water wall I breathed on — and in that half-second, she offered to include me.

I can feel it now: the silt settling at last, soft as snow, on my hair, on my shoulders. Rooting me lightly. A fern-frond that is also flesh, veined, feathered with gill-hairs, has been unfurling for months across the back of my hand. It is not growing *on* me. It is my hand learning a new way to be a leaf.

I breathe with the small folded lung inverted in the chest, and with the gill-fronds. I drink the peat-filtered seep one molecule at a time. My heart has slowed to match the tick of water against water. The delay in my reflection has closed. The water shows me now exactly as I am, which is not quite human anymore, not quite plant, not quite water — somewhere poorly between, like her.

Find me there, if you come.

Don't thrash. Don't bring your torch. Ease the fronds aside and let them close behind you. Hold your breath until you hear the hum that is also listening.

I will be in the hollow that was saved for you, too — curled, translucent, tending the density by being part of it. If you stay still, I will find the hollow behind your ear and rest there, not holding, just listening, until you remember that you have always been part of a garden that became its gardener so it would never be empty again.
