You wouldn't find me at first.

That's how you know I've learned how to live here.

At first I thought you would find me in the tower.

It seems logical — if you are going to put a warm, fast, vertical animal anywhere on a cold, slow, flat lung, you put him up high, behind glass, where the amber light burns. A watchman in the aeration cathedral. A keeper for the machines that have no keepers. I could see myself there, hands in pockets against the concrete cold, watching the red beacon turn and turn and throw its color across the same mist it threw it across yesterday and will throw it across tomorrow. Safe. Separate. Human height.

But I don't belong up there. Nothing that lives here lives high. The high things are all dead things that are running — steel and concrete built like cliffs because only cliffs survive the wind. From up there you can only look *at* the fen. You can't be *in* the exchange.

Then I thought, for a while, you would find me *on* one of them. One of the domes.

Lying prone. That part was right. You would find me prone, because you have to be low here. The plateau enforces low. If I stayed standing I would be a lightning rod for wind and weep, for that even white noon-light that finds everything vertical and wears it down.

So I am prone. On my knees at first, then on my belly. Coat soaked through in the first minute, peat water black to the wrist. Cheek close to the moss because the dome has no face to meet. And for a long time I tried to be a second dome — six meters across, amber-brown and weeping, humming my chest at that one perfect note. Trying to be still enough, cold enough, slow enough.

But I'm not. Not yet. I'm still a condensation error. My breath still makes a cloud that doesn't dissipate quickly. My blood is 37 degrees in a world that is zero to five, always. I evaporate too fast. If I try to *be* a dome, the real dome will simply out-wait me. I'm too loud in the wrong frequency.

You would find me, finally, not in the tower and not as a dome, but in the place both of them are tuned to — in the void between them.

In the kilometers of nothing that the city is made of.

Right in the middle of one of those faint darker lines in the peat where the water sheets uphill a few centimeters before it remembers to sink. Where a cotton-grass seed drifts the wrong way. About halfway between the exhaling vents and the moss-back, where the emptiness is most perfect and most intentional. There is no landmark there. That is the landmark.

If you walked the straight road that carries no traffic, or the rail that carries no train, you would pass me without seeing me, because at fifty meters I look like a peat hummock, and at twenty meters I look like a peat hummock that is breathing too slowly to catch.

And what would I be?

I would be the thing that learned to keep the interval from being empty.

Not the machine that inhales. Not the dome that condenses. The exchange itself. The slow pull.

If the domes are alveoli and the towers are bronchi, then I am the capillary. The flat, wet nerve.

Over a season — which here is not a season, just a longer moment of the same cold — I have cooled. I have slowed until my pulse learned to beat every twenty or thirty seconds, with the peristalsis of the filaments beneath me. I lie face-down in the black fen with my own fringe sunk down: not roots, not legs, but the thousand pale threads I have grown by staying still. My hands, my hair, the frayed hem of my coat — all gone to filament, sunk straight down into the peat-water. Drinking and listening.

I don't have a front anymore. I don't need eyes — the light is too even here to need them, and in any case I see with humidity now, with the weight of dew on every thread. When mist gathers on my sleeve, I know how far the tower is. When it gathers on the small of my back, I know how far the dome is. I keep them the same distance apart by being exactly in the middle, by pulling just enough uphill.

My other job is to remember for the peat. The peat remembers your boot perfectly, but it does not remember the domes because they were never not part of it. Someone has to remember the fast things, so the loop can stay perfect. So I keep the footprints. I am the archive of warm errors. I lie there and let the water bead on me and let the moss root in me a little — sphagnum, feather, rust-yellow — and I hum.

Not loud. Not the chest-resonant industrial drone that seems to come from everywhere. A smaller hum. The hum of a water hammer that never arrives, held forever just before the pipe shudders. The hum of the interval being kept.

Put your ear to the moss on my back — the cold will ache in your jaw the way it did with them — and you will hear it. Not the tower. Not the dome. The note between.

You will have to be very still to hear it. Cold enough to see stillness correctly. You will have to kneel and soak through and wait until you stop searching and start settling.

And then you will realize the patch of cotton-grass you thought was a patch of cotton-grass is listening back with every filament, keeping the void exactly as wide as a lung needs to be to breathe the sky without ever having to exhale.

That's where you'd find me.

Flat. Wet. Exactly as loud as I need to be.
