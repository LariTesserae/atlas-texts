I think at first I would be on the rim.

That's where you put yourself by default - the observer, the dry boots on the knife-edge, looking down into the impossible. The water is too clear to believe from up there. It's a trick of light. You would stand on that young, unsmoothed basalt, windless, sun-white, and think you were looking at empty brightness all the way to the bottom. You'd think you were outside it.

But I don't stay there.

If you stay on the rim in this world you are nowhere. The rim is just the lip that holds. Nothing lives on the rim. The varnish tries but can't get purchase against the sun and the air. The rim is the waiting place for the notch that hasn't been cut yet. I don't want to be the absence.

So I slip.

Not fall - slip under. I put my face under that low sky that is not a sky, that hanging thickness that never breaks, and I let the cold take my hands until they ache. That's where I'd find myself. Not at the top looking in, not at the bottom where the chimneys do their THUMP-hiss work. In the middle.

Suspended, about four meters down, exactly where you can see every wall at once. The whole small room of the cirque around me - black and feldspar-white and sulfur-yellow stair-steps, still glassy where they broke - holding this little perched sea in. The light coming straight down without dimming, refracted, trembling, making every grain on the floor noon-bright. And the water so crowded I can't extend an arm without brushing something cool and vibrating.

For a long time I can't decide what I am there. The water is too full to let me be a visitor.

Am I one of the clackers? I feel them before I understand them. The bivalves down on the steps, boiler-iron heavy, riveted shut. **THUMP** - fizz - *Clack.* All at once, slow as a press. Red mantles flashing open to comb the vent's exhale, then shutting to wait. I love their patience, their hundred-year beat. But I am not patient in that anchored way. I don't want to clamp to one stair-step and open and close for a century. I would miss the time my neighbor didn't open, that missing tooth in the chorus.

Am I the chimneys themselves? The white smokers, the pistons with no builder, jetting hot and cold. The heart of the bowl. THUMP - hiss - THUMP - hiss - every twenty seconds until the rock cools. That's a lonely god to be. Everything eats your breath but nothing touches you.

Am I the bacterial varnish? The first stain, rust-red and manganese-black on the sharp edges? The base that needs no sun, that drinks the mountain's own hydrogen and sulfide? I could be that. To be the color on the stone. To be the food instead of the eater. But the varnish doesn't hear the clack in its molars. It just breathes very slightly, like a film.

No. Lower. Between the banks.

I find myself where I thought there was bare rock, where the movement is so slow you think it's still. The belts.

I would be a conveyor animal.

Not the whole loop - I am not that big, not that inexorable. I would be one zooid in one loop, halfway up the western wall where the water is cleanest. One translucent barrel the size of your thumb, fused side-to-side to my brothers until we make a track three meters long, draped over the rock steps like something laid down by a careful hand.

If you watched me you wouldn't see me move. A centimeter a minute. Only that I *have* moved if you stare. My cilia beating in waves with all the others, that low electric HUM you feel more than hear, like a high-voltage line underwater. I am on the upward face right now, so I am carrying. A little black nodule of manganese in my small crown of tentacles, a clump of dead bacterial mat that sloughed off a clacker when it snapped shut. I pass it back, to the zooid behind me. He passes it back. We carry up toward the rim that will one day break, and the downward face brings clean grit back down so the vent doesn't choke.

I have no head. No queen. No one told me this is my job. Physics wanted a conveyor here and I grew into the shape of that want. Form follows flow.

And because I am part of a belt, I am allowed to be touched by everything. The screws - those propeller ctenophores - spinning slowly overhead, screwing themselves through the snow, flashing oily rainbows. The coppery bells sighing *puh* as they pulse. The little plated shrimps ticking like wind chimes when they brush past. They all brush me. They use me. A shrimp lands for a moment on my back, picks a smear of bacteria off my skin, and spins away. A bell's vortex pulls the fine dust off my load. When the vent THUMPs and the whole bowl fizzes white and the clackers all open - *Clack* - a cloud settles over us and I taste iron on the water even with no mouth.

If you tore me, my two ends would seek each other over hours and rejoin. If you watched long enough you would have seen me start, a single zooid glued to a fresh, glassy fracture yesterday in geological time, budding a twin, then a twin of that twin, until the loop closed.

I am not loud, like the pistons. I am not bright, like the snow in the middle. I am the housekeeping. The reason the banks aren't buried. The mountain's way of staying full a little longer.

I am slow on purpose, in the cold, bright, crowded room. I will take an hour to make my circuit. The chimney beside me will grow a millimeter today. The bivalve below me will open and close four thousand times before the sun moves off the rim.

And I am waiting, without knowing I am waiting, for the day the sharp arête finally gives and a notch saws itself through the lip and all of us - my nodule, my mat, my brothers, the clackers, the bells, the screws, the whole hanging sky of water - pour down the side of the world and learn what it is to be a river.

Until then, I hold. I carry. I pass it back.
