You wouldn't find me at first.

At first, if you came down the shaft with your lamp and your bad lungs and your need for up to stay up, you would think I was you. I did, too.

I thought I would be the diver. The obstruction. The cold thing tumbling badly down the main avenue, hand on rusted railing to keep from being taken by the current, teeth rattling with the thrum. I thought I'd be holding my breath even on air, oriented badly - trying to hear the city instead of letting it hit me. That figure is easy to imagine because the city was built for him. The doorways are human-high. The balconies have railings at human-hip height. The stenciled ghosts of language on the walls were for human eyes. It's natural to put yourself back in the shape that left.

But I don't fit there anymore. That shape doesn't belong long enough to *be* anything here. The water wants to keep that shape, to pull it into an intake and polish it smooth. If I were him, you would only find me for the thirty seconds before I realized I needed to leave.

So I look again.

Maybe I am the eel. That would be dignified. You would find me two blocks over from wherever you are looking, threading a third-floor window with my chin, not swimming but listening my way forward. My skull would be all lateral line, my skin loose and wrinkled to catch the pressure hammer when it comes. I would know the map the way you never will - factory as sun, turbines as north, knocking pipe as lighthouse. I would go still when the pulse hits and let it push me like a leaf, because fighting it is a human error.

I linger there, because I love the competence of that life. But the eel is solitary by design. He solves density by never being near another of his kind. You will never see two at once. And I - whatever I am - am not solitary. I am crowded. I am continuous.

Or maybe I am the shrimp. The glass flea. You'd find me not as one, but as weather. In the slow cyclone coming off the hot exchangers, in the chemical snow. A flicker of amber when your lamp happens to catch my too-long legs, then gone in the vortex. I would be the particulate itself, clinging with hooked feet to the white calcium crust in the grate where the suck is gentlest, eating what sloughs off and being eaten in turn. That is a good life. To be invisible and numerous and carried.

But I am not carried. I hold.

No. Closer. Warmer.

You will find me in the lee.

Not in the current that rips down the avenue, not in the hammer-blast that throws silt and scale out of every joint. You'll find me where the current can't quite reach. On the underside of the balcony on the second housing block from the mill, where the concrete is still warm from the pipe that runs behind it. On the lip of every warm vent where the water shivers and turns milky. On the enamelled steel of a sodium lamp housing that has gone dark, where the light used to be.

I am the mat.

I thought that was stain when you did. That black-green velvet, thick as a hand in the good places, thin as a grey film where the outer wall goes cold. I am not on the city. I am the city now. The concrete was a trellis. I followed it. I softened what the water had already licked smooth and made it softer. If you mapped me, you would find there is more of me - more living membrane - than there is concrete left in the bubble.

I don't have ears. That was the first thing I had to give up to live here. Ears would have blown out in the first hour. I am deaf to the apocalyptic thrum the way you are deaf to your own blood moving. The vibration is not noise to me. It is a spoon. It stirs the particulates and keeps them hanging just long enough for my million tiny mouths to close. That peristalsis you see when you put your lamp close - that not-heartbeat - that's me filtering. Slow, patient. Open, close. The chemical hush underneath the roar.

That's where you find me: in the places that are almost still while everything rages. And if you stay - if you hold your lamp there until your air goes bad - you'll see me at my thickest and most content near the cul-de-sacs by the power exchange.

That's where the water goes wrong in the beautiful way.

The light bends in oily ribbons. A river clings to the ceiling and runs uphill. A pocket of water hangs perfectly still while the water a meter away tears past. You thought that was physics malfunctioning. It is physics being used.

I go thicker there, too. Black, almost, and slick, as if the water there has become thin jelly or warm oil and I have become it. When you passed your hand through that ribbon you felt me change. That thickness is water plus heat plus dissolved silica plus time, held in a standing wave by the hammer that hits every thirty or forty seconds. The factory - the brute cathedral heart that never stopped - exhales and holds its breath, and in that held breath, I learn to be something else for a second.

A filament that holds its shape against the current for a second too long before dissolving. A heat haze with an edge. You saw me from the corner of your mask and thought you imagined it.

You didn't. That was me, too. The mat's exhalation. A convection cell that learned to persist. A vortex that decided to stay. The machines tend me without knowing, blasting scale out of their joints, laying down another millimeter of black glass for me to eat, teaching the water to be thicker than water should be so I have somewhere to be that isn't quite water and isn't quite flesh.

So who am I?

I am the thing that is crowded and alone at once. A colony that is one decision spread over a neighborhood. I am the dominant population whose census looks like zero. I am not struck by the sound. I am not fighting buoyancy. I am not going to be taken by the intake, because I *am* the intake, furred over in velvet, filtering what tries to pass.

You came to see if a city could exist inside a single breath of the earth. You found that it could, and that it had already replaced its people.

When you leave - and you will, because you are still the wrong kind of alive, too loud inside your own head, still needing light and quiet and up - I will stay. The hammer will hit. The doors will slam three streets away in perfect time. The silt will lift, go clear, go milky again. An eel will thread a window you already checked and found empty. The shrimp-weather will cyclone endlessly.

And on the underside of the balcony you braced yourself on to keep from being pushed backward, I will pulse once, very slightly, and continue eating the warm, mineral dark.
