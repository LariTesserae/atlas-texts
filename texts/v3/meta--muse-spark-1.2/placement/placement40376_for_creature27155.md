I don't know right away.

At first I think I would be inside. That's the warm answer. High up in one of the pillars, on a terrace where the grown basalt is damp to the touch and the light has no source. I would be where it's warm the way a body is warm, with a rug and a low partition moved by thought and a table of lichen in shallow trays, learning how to make it spread faster. I would be safe.

But that isn't true. I don't want to be inside. The inside is for sleeping, for being born, for waiting out the weep-fog when it comes down to the ground and erases the horizon. It's not where you *are*.

Then I think I would be an animal. That would be easier. One of the shag-plates, stilt-legged, head down in the marsh sheet, breathing steam that hangs. Or one of the gliders, white and slate-gray, riding the plateau wind in a wheel so slow it doesn't look like flying. To be dense and unthinking and part of the abundance that the thin air shouldn't allow. But I can't. I notice too much. I would be looking at the shag-plates instead of being one.

So I keep looking until the image sharpens itself.

You would not find me in the pillar. You would not find me in the herd line.

You would find me *between*.

Three, maybe four kilometers out from the nearest node - close enough that I can see its matte flank catching the sharp sun, far enough that the cylinder of warm, dry air it makes has thinned out and the cold is clean again. The ground there is where the map hasn't decided yet. A shallow basin the water hasn't quite learned to leave, a mirror-sheet six inches deep and thirty meters wide, reflecting the sky so perfectly it looks like I could fall up. Around its edge, sedge in tufts, each with its own island of peat. Frost-crumbled gravel, saturated, black. The wind is low and hushes over it.

I am ankle-deep in it. Not wading, just standing.

I would be one of them. The tall ones.

Not an elder sitting low by the marsh mouth watching. Not a child still squinting without the second lid, still asking loud questions and wasting air. Something in the middle and unfinished.

If you walked up to me you would see it first in the proportion. Too tall for the air, narrow through the chest until I breathe - and then the ribs go *around*, barrel-hooped, and I take in one long, slow breath that has to last. My skin is the color of wet basalt, a deep matte olive-brown that doesn't burn under that surgical high sun. My hair is black, cut close to the skull, braided once at the nape so the wind can't take it. My eyes are large and a little too dark even before the amber catches, and when the gust comes my nictitating lid slides sideways without me thinking about it.

I wear the coat. The living weave that beads the weep the same way the fresh rock does, in long vertical tears. Under it, close layers holding their few stolen degrees of deep heat against me. My boots are splayed wide so I don't fill them when the sponge-ground gives under me. And I am quiet, because you have to be here. When I finally speak it is on the exhale, a long murmur that you feel through the soles of your feet as much as hear:

*It's going the wrong way again.*

That's my work.

You would find me beside one of the low walls. Not a dam, not even a wall really - a knee-high lip of poured geology, seamless grown stone the same color as the basalt but without the chatter marks. It will be gone in ten thousand years. For now it is teaching this water how to meander. The stream is new, clumsy, still chewing at the fault lip where it will eventually throw itself off the plateau in a waterfall that already roars a kilometer away. Up here it doesn't know whether it wants to be a sheet or a finger.

It wants to cut straight. It always wants to cut straight when it's young.

So I am moving a stone. Just one. A flat piece of fractured basalt about the size of my palm, darkened with lichen. I crouch - slowly, because hurry is expensive here - and I set it at the lip where the seep is starting to braid. Not to block. To nudge. To give the water a reason to turn a little left, where the peat can hold it a season longer.

I have been walking this line for three days. The herd passed through yesterday, a dotted line a kilometer long, and they parted around me without breaking stride. The lean snowshoe fox-things trotted past at a respectful distance. A flock of gliders turned overhead when the needle on the filament went past, that brushed-light transit I feel in my teeth, and I watched them turn with it.

I will not be here tomorrow. I will filament back to the node for the gathering - a birth two pillars over that everyone already knows about because news travels faster than bodies on the filaments - and then I will come out again somewhere else where a different seep needs the same small attention.

That is who I would be. Not the architect of the gravity lattices deep below, pinning fault blocks the size of counties. Not the weaver who grows the coats. A seep-tender. A wall-watcher. One of the ones whose niche is holding things steady while they learn to be themselves.

I leave a footprint when I step. It fills slowly with clear water behind me. I don't smooth it away.

I am unfinished well.
