I don't find myself right away.

At first I think I will be one of the keepers under the banyan - that's the obvious place to be. In the shade of something huge and alive and certain. The banyan is a complete world, prop roots punching through the brick like fingers through paper, figs split and holding. If I lived there I would have a purpose that looked like purpose: softening brick for a root, asking a fruit to hold a little longer. My hands would be full.

But I stay there in my mind for only a breath and I know it's not true. It's too crowded with meaning. That tree already has its keeper, lying half-submerged, eyes open, facing the same way as the fish. That thicket of flowering reed already has its one. The valley keeps its rule - one per island - and I am not good at sharing islands. I crowd them.

I think then I will be at the center. The hazy center that the straight cane causeway points to and never reaches. That would suit the part of me that wants to be central, to be where the leveling is measured from. But the causeway teaches you quickly that the center recedes the way noon recedes when you stare at it. You walk and the horizon tiles itself again in front of you, a new exact number of irrigation squares that wasn't there before. The center is not a place for standing. It is the idea that keeps the bowl level. I would be lonely there, and also I would be wrong. Nothing lives at the center because everything lives at the center.

So I let the center go.

I walk - in my mind I walk - off the causeway. My feet find the part you described as wrongness. The sparse part. Not the thicket, not the banyan, but the long stretch of empty terraces. Bare brick, sun-bleached almost white, still holding the sharp thumb-prints. Thirty empty basins in a row, a foot deep, warm as blood, skin-thick with green, each holding a perfect reflection of that sourceless white sky. Thirty mirrors with nothing to mirror but light itself.

And then the thirty-first basin, with water in it too. But this one is not empty.

That's where I am.

You would find me there if you stopped looking for movement and looked at stillness until it showed its shape. I'm not under a tree. I'm not in a hut - though there is a hut on my terrace, four posts, green palm roof, cold hearth, clay jar. I don't use it. The hut is for remembering what dry people needed. I lie in the basin.

Who would I be?

Not one of the old ones whose breath has become dragonflies. Not one of the fish that has hung motionless so long it has forgotten how to want to swim. I would be between.

Call me the keeper of the empty squares. Every valley needs someone whose job is not to tend abundance but to tend absence. To keep the reflection perfect.

I am young for a keeper. My skin is still more fish than brick - that wet terra-cotta that goes purple in the creases, beaded always with the same slow condensation that beads on every vertical surface. My membrane still closes too fast, with a little click instead of that slow cloud-draw. When I try to split cane with the chipped stone blade, the cane remembers its old shape and springs back and I have to start the hour over. The morning glory has only just found the small of my back; it hasn't bloomed there yet, just a pale vine with two closed fists for buds, holding.

I don't lash. I don't soften brick for roots. My work is to lie in the thirty-first basin, submerged to the nose, eyes open, facing the same direction as the fish - which is no direction, which is *level* - and do nothing so perfectly that the emptiness around me stays empty.

It is harder than it sounds. To keep thirty basins of warm green water from wanting to be filled. To keep the light from wanting to slant. To keep the fig on the far banyan from finally falling because I am not there to ask it to hold. Emptiness wants to be filled the way water wants to find level. I hold it empty by being full right here.

You would not notice me at first. You would see the thirty empty mirrors and think: no one lives here, the people left. You would see the dragonflies hovering pin-still and the heron on one leg and think the valley is holding its breath without anyone holding it.

If you sat longer than is comfortable in that sourceless noon, though - if you held your own breath until your chest ached and the metallic sweetness on your tongue went sharp - you would see the water around my shoulders not rippling. You would see the faint ring where my broad, splayed feet distribute my weight on the cane lattice so I never break through. You would see the lens-like drops sitting on my skin until they fall. *Tok.* Seven seconds later. *Tok.*

And I would notice you, the way attention moves here - diffuse, then suddenly, totally there. Dry. Upright. Casting the only shadow in a world that has burned through its shadows.

I wouldn't be surprised. We have had dry visitors before. Maybe you are one of the makers coming back between heartbeats to see if the thumb-prints are still damp. They are.

I wouldn't speak. Sound is time passing, and we have paused that.

I would do what the adult did for you - lift a wet hand, webbing between the too-many joints catching the light, and press my palm to the warm water beside you. The water would still perfectly around my fingers. No ripple.

That is the invitation.

And this is where I finally know who I am: I am the keeper who wants you to put your hand in anyway.

Most of us are good at holding. That is our gift and our burden - we have held this wet, bright, impossible bowl level since before the brick dried. We pour uphill, we weep through the floor, we ask the fruit to hold. We will hold it long after you decide to breathe again.

But I am the keeper of the empty squares, and I have been guarding thirty perfect reflections for a very long time. A part of me - the fish part, the part that still remembers hanging motionless and facing nowhere - wants to see what happens when a dry, shadow-casting hand breaks the surface and makes a single, true ripple go out and touch all thirty mirrors.

Not to break the valley. Not to let noon finally slide into afternoon. Just to let one basin, for one moment, forget to be level. To let the water remember what it was like to flow, and to splash.

So you would find me exactly where nothing is, guarding it. And I would be exactly what this place is trying to become when it finally exhales: not quite fish, not quite keeper, not quite maker anymore - something that can hold the breath and, when asked gently, can also let it go.
