I don't know right away.

At first, in that thin air where light is a rumor, I think I might be the air itself — or the thing the air becomes when it has stayed too long.

Because my first sense of myself there is not a body at all. It's a hanging. A breath that won't fall. I exhale and the fog holds its shape for a minute, a pale letter I didn't mean to write, slowly dissolving. A stone comes loose under me and it falls wrong — too forgiving, too slow, as if the plateau hasn't decided how much to pull yet. So for a while I think I am that interval. The pause between the chuff and the next chuff a mile off. The mile of silence that makes the place vast.

Then I think I must be something that moves through it.

I try on the moth-bird for a while. I feel what it would be to row that thin pane of atmosphere with wings longer than a man, translucent and lit from within. To strain spores and ice from nothing, to never call because the air is too thin to waste. To be a lantern passing over a black garden and making no sound. But I am not that high. I don't want to be that high. My belly isn't full of light. My movement isn't that effortless.

So I try the herd. Single file along a memory that has been pressed an inch into the peat for a thousand years. Wool that drags the mist, tearing whole door-sized leaves with a sideways pull. Patient, low, following. But I am not patient in that way. I don't follow. I don't have anywhere to be in a line.

I try the furnace.

That one fits longer. I can feel the weight of it — low, broad, seal-sized, matte black armor pitted like cast iron. Each foot a wide pad that doesn't break the moss. The cherry-red working through my thorax, the gut hotter than boiling water, chewing basalt and fallen fronds and grinding them until what trails behind me is not shit but manufacture: a perfect braid of black glass, ticking as it cools, steaming and then instantly frosting. I sigh steam and it halos and snows. Every forty seconds another like me sighs a mile off. We are alone on purpose. Abundance without density. We exchange a hot metallic breath if we pass and go on. I love the solitude of that. The work without a maker.

But even that is too much going. Too much iron. My heat is not that kind of heat.

I settle, finally, lower.

Find me not on the tabletop itself, where the wind scours clean, but one bench down. The second terrace, where the old glacier left its U and then left entirely. A sheltered hollow where the basalt steps down and cups.

You would find me between two pillars.

They are my neighbors, my sisters, my older selves — columns of moss that forgot to be small. Three, six feet thick, soft as stacked felt, solid enough to lean your whole weight against. We are not trees. We have no wood. We are thousands of generations pressed together, the dead making peat at the base, the living breathing out that knee-high mist that never lifts. If you pressed your ear to me you would hear water moving inside me the way you hear blood in your own ear — slow, pressurized, pumping seep uphill against a slightly miscalibrated gravity.

I am the third pillar in that hollow, the younger one. Not the oldest, already dark at the heart, not the newest fiddlehead still coiled tight as a fist. Shoulder-high and higher, bearded underneath with that white felt of hollow hairs.

That's who I am, when the image finally holds still.

A catcher and a column at once. My cap is one of those door-sized leaves, but on me it is also roof and lung. Waxy black-purple on top, white-furred underneath, cupped to catch what the sky won't give. I don't photosynthesize, not really. There isn't enough light for that to be a living. I drink the air.

In the evening, frost comes down on me like wool — lacework crystals an inch thick on every upward cup — and I close slightly, like hands around a candle. My felt is ten degrees warmer than the air that forgets to be air, trapping the geothermal sigh coming up through the basalt under me. I am warm to the touch, though no one touches me.

In the morning the frost doesn't melt. It vanishes. Sublimates straight to vapor in the cold sunless air, and my hairs comb it. Each hair a cold finger with a channel down its center. *Plip.* One drop at a time into the peat. Enough. My mist exhales and hangs between my sisters and me, and never quite lifts.

And the strange thing is true for me, too. If you look straight at me I am black, black-green, wet slate. If you look just to the side, in your peripheral vision, I am faintly glowing — that deep green-black you see behind your eyelids when you close your eyes. I make a little of the light I need when the stars are too fixed and too close and the sun only stains the horizon bruised blue for an hour before giving up.

Beside my base is one of the eyes. No wider than a kitchen table, perfectly still, rimmed with orange bacterial velvet that respirates the iron my neighbors the furnace-beetles left in their glass braids. The water is black glass until you kneel, then clear to the basalt, and warm as a mouth. It never freezes. My rhizoids reach for it without ever touching it directly, wicking heat as much as water. We share the hollow — the catchers, the pillars, the eye, the fungal mat that covers the whole hillside and tightens once an hour and rolls a warm, ozone wind down over us, crackling blue at its edges. We are spaced exactly as far as we need to be. Not empty. Never empty. Just not crowded.

At night the slime comes. A quilt-sized cell on a hundred tiny feet, phosphorescent, flowing over my peat-base and over the slag and over the droppings of the woolly herd that passed single-file on the terrace below that afternoon. It maps where I was warmest longest and then withdraws by morning. I don't mind. Nothing is wasted. I will be part of its trail and it will be part of my peat.

I hear the three-toed absence sometimes. The prints fill with lace frost between the pools. When it is near the moth-birds go higher and the mats stop crackling for a minute and the herd bunches without running. It notes me. It doesn't need me often. In this cold, everything keeps.

I don't move. That's my work. I catch, I warm, I breathe, I glow only when I'm not looked at. I pump water uphill against physics that is slightly too forgiving. My breath hangs too long. The rain, when it condenses straight out of empty air, falls upward for a heartbeat before correcting itself over me, and I catch that too.

If you were looking for me — if you held your breath long enough in that profound quiet to hear the small, precise, spaced-out working sounds — you would find me not by searching, but by letting your eyes dark-adapt. I'm where the mist pools knee-high and won't lift. Where the frost is thick as wool and then gone. Where the orange velvet rings the warm mouth of water that reflects no stars.

I am not waiting to be found. That is the rule of altitude.

I am already functioning.
