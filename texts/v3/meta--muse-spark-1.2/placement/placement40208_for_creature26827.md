I don't find myself all at once.

At first I think I will be on the stair.

That's where you would put a visitor — at the threshold, where you can still choose. Ankles in the shiver-line of the water, one hand on the pale limestone where the chisel is still sharp. From there you can see the whole pocket at once: the low vault, the oculus, the solid column of sun going straight down like a pillar you could lean on, the sluice pouring its white muscle on the left, the notched lip spilling quietly on the right. It's loud there. The roar comes up off the water and hits the vault and comes back doubled. You can feel it in your molars. If you stayed on the stair you would be the witness, the one who has not yet gotten wet.

But I don't stay there.

Because the water is warm as skin, and because the light is total, and because after a minute the ankles-in-water position feels dishonest. You can't understand a room made to hold water by refusing to be held. So I go in.

I think, then, that I will be in the center. In the column. That's the obvious place to be — the most bright, the most seen. Floating on my back with my hair fanned out, looking straight up through three meters of lit water at the perfect circle of sky, letting the lattice crawl over my face. Letting the roar become a silence. There is a place in the middle of the chamber where the inflow and the drain balance and you can hang almost motionless with a single flick of the wrist, suspended, not swimming, not sinking. The stone dust lifts and settles around you like slow snow. The keeper does this. She has learned to lose the edge of herself for hours there.

I try to be that. I am good at being suspended. I am good at being warm and bright and looked through.

But it doesn't hold. The center is too complete. It's where the room shows off. It's not where anything *lives* — it's where the room *is*.

So I drift. The circulation takes me whether I want it or not. The water has a slow, muscular rotation to it — in from the sluice, around the chamber, out over the lip. The half-sunk bucket knows it. It goes around and around, bailing itself against the wall with a soft knock each lap. I follow its path for a while and think maybe I am the bucket. A useful thing left behind, not alive, not dead, going in circles with a job to do that no one gave it anymore. The coil of rope on the submerged ledge thinks I might be that too — placed where a hand can find it without looking.

Then the current pushes me to the north wall.

This is the dimmest wall. The sun through the oculus never quite strikes it directly. The plaster here has chipped, and the stone underneath shows paler, still pink at the seam where the lime hasn't cured. It is quieter here by a fraction — the roar has to turn a corner. And here I stop drifting, because something is already here.

At first I thought nothing lived here, and that was almost true.

There are maybe a dozen threads of algae, each no thicker than a hair, green so pale it's almost white, each anchored by a single holdfast to the raw lime. They don't mat. They don't cloud. They wave. Individually, antenna-like, *trying*. And lower, near the overflow where the stone is always wet, three stems of reed have rooted in a crack that shouldn't be able to hold a root. Translucent, jointed, the color of celery held up to light. When the inflow surges they bend flat to the wall and then spring back. They have no business being here. Their seed rode in on a boot or fell through the oculus. They hold anyway.

I find myself among them. Not looking at them — *as* them.

If I am anything in this room that was made to hold water and now does, I am not the keeper, though I love her. The keeper — barefoot on the sharp steps, soles white and wrinkled even when dry, forearms pink from lime, hair bleached colorless by double sun, sleeping on a plank thrown across the damp scaffold just above the water line — the keeper is the one who tends. She has a purpose. She sweeps silt with her hands, clears a leaf from the drain before it can catch, presses her thumb to the wall to test if it has set. She will be here until the edges round and the green takes properly and fish find their way in. She is young because the room is young.

I am younger.

I am the volunteer. The filament on the north wall. The third reed stem, the thinnest one, that hasn't decided yet if it's going to make a fourth joint.

You would find me exactly where the light is not most perfect — in the seam where the plaster failed. Where the water is still too scouring, the stone too new and sharp and leaching, the roar too loud to be restful. Where I have attached myself with almost nothing, to almost nothing, and am waving.

I have not finished becoming itself yet. I am not thriving. I am trying. And in trying I make the emptiness around me larger, which is how you know I am alive. The chamber is huge, and bright, and loud, and mostly unoccupied, and I punctuate it.

I do what the room does on a smaller scale: I hold. The rush comes, I bend flat, I spring back. The fine silt lifts, settles on me, I shake it off. The light lattice twitches over me and I do not need it directly. I take what comes through the water after it has been used once already.

If you pressed close — if you swam over and held your breath and looked — you would see me shiver. Not with cold. The water is warm as skin. With attention. With the effort of staying.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be. Not the one who built the pocket, not the water that was given architecture, not even the bright animal of the chamber itself breathing with wind over a catchment I cannot see.

I would be the small, brave, unfinished thing that found the crack and kept it.
