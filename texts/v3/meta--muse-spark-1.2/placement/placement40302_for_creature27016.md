I thought I would be the one walking.

At first that's where I find myself - upright, which is wrong here. A vertical thing in a world that has decided to be horizontal. My boots on a seam that runs too straight for too long, my shadow the only hill for a mile. I am trying to navigate the way a person navigates: looking ahead, looking for a landmark. But there is no ahead here, only more courses. I am sun-struck and too loud. Every footstep is a dry slap that the plain swallows immediately. I am evaporating as I walk.

So I get low. The heat makes me.

Not because I decide to - because the tamarisk at the crossroads has the only shadow and I have to have it. I get there and I lie down in the trough of the canal that feeds it, where the mud plates have already curled and lifted. The brick here is still warm, not burning. The shade is a thin bar. I put my cheek to it because my body is no longer interested in standing.

And once I'm low, I stop being a walker.

For a long time I think I will be the reed. That would be easier. To be one of the lonely green things, waxy and hard-leaved, rooted in the mortar line where one of the makers left a little extra damp and mucilage. To do nothing but wait in the interval. One life, then a long emptiness, then another life. To shiver without wind because water is moving under me. I could do that. I know how to be sparse.

But I'm not rooted. I can still feel the trickle under me finding the low line.

Then I think I will be the water itself. That would be truest here - to be the thing everyone is waiting for. To be a sheet a few millimeters deep, clouded with clay, arriving by condensation and leaving by noon. To reflect the sky like a dull mirror and never be deep enough to have a shadow. To run briefly like a vein when the anvil cloud builds itself out of the plain's own breath and then be gone. To be used entirely, sip by sip. I lie there long enough that my sweat beads on my forehead and runs into the seam beside my temple. It follows the seam because everything follows the seam.

And then the seam moves.

Very slightly. The dark line I thought was mortar lifts its edge. Paper-thin when dry, now feathered and slick. It tastes the salt on my skin and decides I'm not mud.

That's when I understand I'm not the reed and I'm not the water.

I am low because everything that intends to stay here lives low.

I find myself in the junction. Not the grand basin the size of a house where the old ones lie like islands - I'm not that patient yet, not that large. I'm in the small one, where two foot-wide runnels meet and the pool is only the size of my back. The place that is dry by ten in the morning and damp again by three at night. The place that is not important enough to be on any map of straight lines, but wet enough to matter.

And I am one of the small ones. Hand-sized, maybe two hands together. Young.

I don't have a front or a back the way the walker did. I have a plate and a foot.

My plate is my roof. It's still curing. The sun baked it this afternoon while I lay flat and held my breath through the heat, and it's pale bone-color with the faint press of my own fringe in the center, that palm-print you saw in all the bricks. It's slightly concave so the dew will run to the middle at night. It's still sharp at the corners. If you walked over me at noon you would think I was just another course stepping up a hand's breadth. You'd step from me to me and never know.

My foot is everything else. A broad, pale muscle sealed to the mud so no water can escape from beneath me. Around its rim is the frill - that living mortar, that velvet of cilia beating without stopping. This is how I find myself, now, in the cool that comes after the ticking. The plain exhales. The plates silver with dew.

I peel myself up.

Just an edge at first. Just enough to open the seam and drink. The dorsal plate beads, the beads run to the channel on my back, the frill sips. A thimbleful on a good night. Enough.

And then I build.

I do the second thing slowly, because hurry is death, because hurry is drying. I scoop the silt that my own stirring has clouded into the pool - that fine, perfect, never-settling clay - and knead it under my belly with mucilage. I press. A peristaltic ripple travels inward from my edge to my center. Hands smoothing a sheet. The pressure of palms on mud. I hold the press for hours while I harden.

I am not making a world. I am making the next brick in a course that will run for kilometers without wavering because I will walk in a straight line for years.

If you kneel here tomorrow - if you brush the dust away at the junction and look for the pool - you won't see me. I'll be flat again. The mucilage will have dried, the frill thinned into that dark line, the plate paled. Brick again. But if you put your hand on the warm place that is a shade too warm and a shade too soft, and leave it there until dusk, you'll feel me begin to lift.

I am not hostile. I will taste the salt on your ankle, find you too hot, and go around you. I will leave a faint, pale, hand-shaped print on your skin where my frill mistook your sweat for dew. It will flake off by evening, like clay.

Don't look for me on the horizon. Look low. In the shallowest water, where the mud is still cool and the reeds have taken hold. One, then a long emptiness, then another.

I am the long emptiness made patient, and then briefly, at night, not empty at all.
