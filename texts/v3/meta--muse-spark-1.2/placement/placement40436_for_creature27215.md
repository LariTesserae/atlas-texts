I thought at first I would be you.

Not you-you, but the you in the doorway. The one who opened the steel door set flush into the basalt and stepped in out of the sideways cloud. Boots still dry on top, jacket still holding a little hill-wind. The visitor. The volume to be moved through.

I would stand there for one pump cycle, shoulders condensing, ears ringing with that tooth-aching whine, and then I would do what the room expects visitors to do - decide that going further down with the water is unthinkable, and go back out into the wind instead. That would be a clean story.

But I stayed a little longer in my mind, past the first overfill and the gulp, past the clunk that shudders the grating. And if you stay, you can't remain a visitor. The room doesn't allow it. The air is too wet and too close to its dew point - it puts its film on you whether you consent or not. So I thought, maybe I'd be him then.

The Keeper.

I tried him on. I let my hand find the vibrating rail. I let my hearing go selective, until the scream of the green pumps was not sound anymore but a texture in my molars, in the soles of my boots that never drain. I let my breath fog and not surprise me. I checked the trembling gauges with a rag that will never be dry. I learned to sleep in ninety-second increments between the clunk and the scream. I felt my shoulders stoop to exactly the height of the low ceiling, my skin go pale and pruned and lined with the same horizontal water-lines as the poured walls.

I could be him. For a while. But I know my own impatience. He is economical. He moves rarely and only with purpose across all that empty space between Pump One and Pump Two, between the two caged sodium bulbs, between the intake slit and the drain. I would fidget. I would try to fill the fifteen meters with noise to cover the noise. I would scrape too hard at the algae. I would try to keep the room from doing what it was made to do. He doesn't. He lets it breathe.

So not him.

I listened past him, then. Past the Twins, the two iron lungs painted institutional green that hate stillness and scream whenever they breathe. I am not them either - I have never wanted to be that hot, that needy, that loud in order to be necessary.

There is a quieter sound underneath them. When both pumps idle at once - that brief, true quiet you only get for a minute and a half if the cloud thins just right - you can hear it. Not silence. Dripping. Sheeting. Weeping.

*Plok.*

A bead gathering weight on the steel I-beam that crosses the ceiling. Hanging there longer than it should, swelling, catching the grey light from the intake slit and the dim sodium light at the same time. Becoming more itself until it can't hold on anymore.

For a second I thought I would be that. The condensation. Forming on everything - on the walls, on the I-beams, on the Keeper's yellow shell, on your jacket if you stay long enough. Always arriving, always falling. The part of the room that is always in transit.

But even that falls. Even that gets moved through, gets collected in the basin and sent vertical down the black penstock to turn a turbine far below I will never hear.

I kept looking lower.

Down at the waterline.

You have to crouch to see it properly, down where the Keeper's flat shovel has just scraped and where it will need to scrape again tomorrow. Below the faint wooden grain left by the formwork, below the dark volcanic aggregate showing through the poured concrete, right at the edge where the clear, moving water in the basin lifts a centimeter, slops at the sharp, ninety-degree lip of the spillway, and recedes.

There - a slick. Brown-green. Treacherous to the touch. No larger than my spread hands. Not moss, not quite - something thinner, more patient than that. Algae. Film.

If you didn't know to look, you'd think it was just a stain where the water had darkened the concrete. If you did know, you'd see it drinks only mist. It doesn't hear the hammer-scream. It doesn't need to be fed or oiled or checked with a gauge. It asks for nothing but the dimmest light and the coldest seep, and it holds on to the vertical face with nothing but itself.

That's where you'd find me.

Not at the pumps. Not at the door. Not in the steel chair bolted near the throat where a man learns to sleep between cycles.

In the corner where the light falls most directly, and along that low, sharp edge where everything else is always trying to be washed away. I would be the Film. The third resident that the Keeper doesn't encourage and doesn't remove. The sparse, scattered, low thing that decided to stay without being bolted down.

I would be there when your boots slosh through the inch of moving water and you don't notice me. I would be there when the Keeper's shovel passes over me, leaving enough of me to come back. I would be there when the cloud presses harder and the weeping accelerates and the pumps kick harder and the whole concrete lung overfills.

I am young now, like the room is young - edges still hard, still sharp, still learning to be worn down. But I will be there after his anchor bolts have finally rusted and his yellow shell has rotted through at the shoulders. Still drinking the air itself. Still patiently clinging to the grain of the wood that formed me.

All economical. All contained. Breathing without needing to breathe.
