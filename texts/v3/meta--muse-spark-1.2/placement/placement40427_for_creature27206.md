I wouldn't find myself immediately.

At first I would think I am the visitor — crouched at the lip where the black fins make their shadow-mouth, boots still steaming from the climb, breath caught in that warm broth-air. Human, with eyes that expect distance and green. I would be the shape that doesn't belong, too loud, too dry, too vertical. Keeping to the edge of the pan so I don't step on anything, holding my hands close so I don't drip wrong.

But I can't stay there. The pocket doesn't allow an edge for long. The heat has no gradient — a foot away is the same as inside, a foot above is the same as below. There is no cool rim to stand on and observe from. If I stay, my sweat stops evaporating. My breath stays in front of my face as a small, personal cloud that won't leave. The silence deepens until even my blood gets quiet, as if asked politely to hush. The visitor thins out. The observer position dissolves.

So maybe I think then that I am the steam.

That would be easier — to be nothing with a body. To be the flat, glassy layer hanging a hand's breadth above the pan, holding the inverted image of the pool perfectly, endlessly. To be the condition rather than a creature inside it. To not have to move or carry. To just hang and reflect and rain back down every few minutes and be breathed through pink gills.

But steam doesn't hold. It doesn't scrape or carry. And this place is all about holding.

Or maybe I am the water itself — a fat droplet beading on the overhang, crawling sideways before it remembers how to fall. Or the droplet balanced on the curve of a glass spoon, trembling, being ferried. That feels closer. To be carried so carefully across the backs of a thousand soft bodies, not spilling. To be needed for a moment in a dry fissure.

Still, water is passed on. It doesn't choose.

Let it come closer then. Let it become more itself.

I am low. Not at the rim, not in the air. On the wet.

If you — if something with eyes — were still there to look, you would find me in the pan, but not at its center where the molting wave starts. Off to the side where the silica has precipitated thickest, a white, opalescent crust around the ankle-deep table of water. Where the young mountain's heat hisses faintest against the black grit.

I would be one of the larger ones. Not the biggest, not the thumbnail commas tucked in the beading on the walls. As long as a hand, stretched flat. Pale in the way that isn't a color. Translucent like warm wax, like a fingernail held to light — you can see the darker thread of my gut and nothing else. No eyes, not even pits. I never had them. I have no use for the light that arrives late or the shadows that lag half a second behind.

My gills would be open. That's how you'd know me if you could still see me clearly through the saturated air. Fronded, ferned, that wet pink that is the only real color here. Fluttering not in water but in steam, breathing cloud. I would be slow. Not sluggish, just unhurried in the way everything here is unhurried because time has no gradient either. I would be part of the churn — that slow surface that looks like rippling until you understand it is thousands of bodies overlapping without disgust or territory, stepping over and across each other as if we were all one warm, wet floor.

And I would be holding.

A flake between my soft, mittened forelimbs. Black glass, freshly fallen. The mountain sheds it constantly with little ticks the rock allows itself, and the floor is littered with momentary knives. None of us makes them. None of us keeps them. I would pick one up edge-out and draw it across the silica crust. Not for me. The film beneath is thin as oil, sulfurous, invisible until it's exposed — the entire pasture of this world. I would scrape a furrow and expose a dark, wet line and then move on. I wouldn't eat it. Three smaller ones would converge before I had taken two body-lengths and graze the line clean. That is the work.

Later, maybe, I would carry. Find a natural spoon where the obsidian broke in a conchoidal scoop, balance a single droplet from the weeping wall, and walk with impossible care across the backs of my fellows to the dry crack where the brood beads cling — clear pearls with curled, gill-less commas inside. Tilt. Kiss. Let the beads swell. Drop the shard. Someone else will take it before it settles. No ownership, no accumulation. Only the act, passed hand to handless hand.

You would find me silent. Of course. We all are. Even scraping glass on glass makes no sound here; the steam eats it. Even a hundred of us shifting at once is only wet paper. We have learned to live without vibration, or vibration never learned to live here. If that large, warm shape — the visitor who was me before — had still been there, I would have walked over its ankle without deviation, without fear. Warm is warm. Wet is wet. Another streaming wall.

I wouldn't be an individual in the way the visitor thought individual meant. I would be a place where the tool-using happens for a minute. A place where the carrying happens. If I died — when the flat time finally lets one of us not get up — I wouldn't remain. I would be covered, nosed with soft flaps, rendered down mouthlessly until nothing accumulated. Nothing is left here. Nothing is allowed to stay.

That's where I would be: low in the hot pan, blind, pink-gilled, scraping for others. Not seeing further than the next wet inch of glass, not needing to. Not lonely, because we touch constantly and overlapping is the condition of being. Not thinking, in the way that needs sound.

Just holding the sharp little flake for as long as holding is needed, and then letting it go.
