You would not find me at first.

At first I thought I would find myself where I always try to find myself — high up and dry. On one of those maintenance gantries that cross the whole roiling plain, high enough to see the perfect cold circle of the horizon, where the plain ends and the ice is still receding. From there you can pretend you are separate from the place. Observer. Surveyor. The one who maps.

But there is nothing dry up there. The pipe over your head is weeping even at that height. The gantry itself is furred with three inches of moss and you can feel it give under your boots like turf. And the sound doesn't work up there the way a height should. You expect wind. You expect clang. Instead the damp eats everything and all you hear is dripping and your own breath, and the distant thud of forges like a heart through a sweater. Standing apart is not actually possible here. The city doesn't allow a vantage that isn't also damp, also growing over.

So I tried lower. I thought maybe I am the plain itself — that embryonic geology, the raw basalt with the long claw-marks of the glacier still in it. Still soft, still learning how to be land. That has a certain rightness. To be the thing that is being built over, being pressed down, being drunk before it can learn to be a river.

But the plain is too patient. Too still. It has waited kilometers of ice and can wait longer. Nothing in me is that patient. The rule of this place is *keep moving or the cold comes back in* — and whatever I am, I keep moving.

I tried being the obvious fauna. A gull riding the hot updrafts off the rolling mills, thin cry, embarrassed by its own volume. A crane swinging panels overhead. Even one of the pale hand-moths big as a hand, roosting on a hot pipe. But those things are too solitary, too much themselves. They pass through the city. They don't negotiate with it.

The scale was wrong. I was looking for a body the size of a building or a bird when the whole city operates at the scale of the gap.

You have to recalibrate your eye. You look for crowds and see empty streets, then you understand the streets are only a shoulder-width wide and every single one of them is occupied. You look for people and see only a hand appears over a parapet to cut back vine, a back bent double under a pipe scraping moss into a bucket. That is where I started to find myself. Not in the expanse. In the aperture.

I would be low. Not low as in lowly — low as in pressed down, close to the warmth.

You would find me in a gap that isn't on any map and won't be there next month when they move a wall one cinderblock at a time. Say, the seam where the back of the dye works leans against a tenement that was a machine shop last winter. The buildings don't meet cleanly. They leave a slot maybe four feet wide and twenty feet long, open to that flat white sky only as a bright slit. The condensation from both buildings runs down into it constantly, so the north-facing wall never dries and is thick with shelf fungi the size of plates, and the south-facing wall is colonized by that fast pale vine with the translucent hand-leaves.

You would find me there because that is where the city sweats for a living.

And I wouldn't be much to look at from a distance. That's the point. I thought at first I would be armored — sealed suit, parka, diver against the cold — but nothing sealed survives here. If you seal yourself you rot from your own steam. You have to learn to be damp with it.

So I am soft. And compact.

I'm short through the legs and broad through the chest, the way you get when you never fully stand upright because the pipes are always overhead. Broad flat feet for wet concrete. My skin has gone that paper-in-water pallor, grey-pink, showing blue at the temples and the backs of the hands. Eyes puffy at the lids from the damp and the faint smoke that never clears, hair cropped short because it never dries otherwise. When I breathe out I steam, even indoors, because indoors doesn't really exist — buildings leak into buildings — and I am always taking off a layer and putting it back on. My cuffs are always dark with water. The wool I've darned until it is more darn than sweater has a faint green tinge at the shoulders where the moss tried to take me too and I brushed it off.

Who I am is what I do, because there is no one idle here. Stillness is where the frost comes back in.

You would find me tending. Not building, exactly, though I have a hammer. Not farming, though I have a trowel. In that slot I keep a row of planter boxes wedged between the hot-water pipe and the lightwell, where I grow kale and those iron-tasting tubers and the tomatoes that split from too much heat. The same hand that turns the soil patches the mortar on the wall. The same trowel. My fingerprints are worn nearly smooth — black under the nails that is not dirt but concrete dust and moss juice and iron oxide that will not wash out. On the cooler pipe I have a little colony of shelf fungi I slice like bread. When the pigeons come down I don't shoo them. When one of those big soot-scaled moths lands on the hot pipe I lift it gently to a cooler one so it doesn't scorch. That's a small liturgy here: you lift the moth.

If you stood on the pipe-gantry above my slot you would hear me before you saw me, but not loudly. I speak in that low, flat, clipped murmur everyone uses here, consonants softened so they don't carry, because the moss and the wet air swallow shouting anyway. I'd touch your forearm to get your attention, not call. I'd laugh with my shoulders. If you had a child with you, I would take it without asking and tuck it between me and the pipe for warmth while we talk mouth-to-ear, one foot apart, because a child alone cools. That's not kindness, it's thermodynamics. Property here is heat. If we share a warm pipe, we are kin for as long as the pipe stays warm.

And I know I'm a tenant.

That is the strangest and most honest part. My grandparents might have been born between these same two walls, but I don't act like a native. No one does. The plain is too new to have natives. We all act like recent arrivals who have decided to make do brilliantly. I don't say *my people built this plain.* I say *I'm keeping this pipe from freezing.* I keep adding, keep patching, keep moving, because the turbulence is the only stable thing. The brilliant green moss I harvested this morning will blacken and slough off next week when this pipe cools, and reappear two buildings over. The wall I patched will be gutted for rebar. That is not failure. That's the city breathing.

If you stayed a full day — through the dripping noon when the frost-ferns liquefy and the amber light of my little greenhouse glows, and into the night when everything steams — you would see me stop once. Just for a minute. Back aching from crouching. Hands steaming. I'd look up at that slit of white sky and out, as far as I could, at the circuit-board of moss-swallowed roofs and smoking chimneys going on to the perfect circle, and I'd feel that vertigo everyone gets here: being in a place that hasn't decided what it is yet.

And then I'd pat the pipe I'm leaning on, the way you reassure an animal, and get back to cutting back the hand vine before it covers the window, and whispering, and dripping.

You would find me there. Not the city's master. Its perspiration. One more damp, pale, quiet, hot-blooded patch keeping the cold from coming back in for one more night.
