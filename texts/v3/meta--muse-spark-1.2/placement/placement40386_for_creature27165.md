I don't find myself in the yard.

Not at first.

If I came over the rim at midday - and I think I would come over the rim on foot, because there's no other way to come, breathing a little too fast in that thin, plain light - I would be visible for a long time before I arrived. A dark speck against limestone, moving slowly because I'd have to. The bowl would not rush to meet me. It would let me descend the smoothed scree one careful step at a time until the hush of the rim closed over me.

And in that first hour I would look for the center. Everyone does. The cistern, the tamped earth, the circle of houses facing inward like a held breath. I would stand there and hear the tick of water into stone and think: here. This is the place.

But I wouldn't stay there.

The center is for agreement. For ladling, for standing side-by-side looking at the work while you speak. It is too accounted for. Every roof feeds it. Every eye knows its level.

I find myself, instead, a little higher up.

On the upper crescent, where the smooth back of the mountain rises behind the hamlet like a shoulder. Second from the end, where the crescent thins and stops, not because there is no more flat ground but because someone long ago decided not to build any further and no one has disagreed since. The house there is low, even for here. Thick walls, a door of mountain pine that has gone grey, a single small window that looks not at the yard but across the interval to the next house.

That interval is mine.

Every house has one - a stretch of bare rock between buildings that was left deliberately empty. A pen with no goats. A terrace with no planting. A paving of slab that is swept everyday and holds nothing. People from below would call it waste. Here it is structure. Silence between words.

My interval is a rectangle of bare limestone maybe nine paces long, bounded on one side by my house wall and on the other by a retaining wall that holds the terrace above. Bedrock shows through in places, polished where feet have never quite worn it. In the corner where my wall meets bedrock, in the permanent shadow, there is a pillow of moss the color of old copper that I have never touched. Lichen maps the north face of my wall in pale green and orange. I don't clean it off.

I would be the person who keeps an empty space empty.

Not old, not young. In the middle layer. Broad-chested the way everyone is here - you have to be, to get enough air - my wool coat the color of the stone and straight from yoke to hem, darned at the elbow until the darn is thicker than the cloth. Hair cut short, gone grey early. Cheeks reddened not by sun but by that permanent cold burn from wind over the rim. Eyes narrowed against the high glare.

My hands would be the giveaway. Palms broad, nails cut to the quick, the creases dark with stone dust that never washes out. Not because I build, but because I maintain. Which here is the same thing.

In the morning I sweep. Not the yard - someone else sweeps the yard - I sweep my interval. A broom of twig, short strokes. The wind sifts a fine grit over everything in the night, condensation beads and dries and leaves a pale mark. I sweep it clean. At noon I check the small channel cut along the lip of the terrace above my interval. It should be clear. A leaf from the stunted pine, a fragment of slate, a beetle - anything can slow water that is supposed to tick. I clear it with two fingers. I don't hurry. If I hurry I get breathless, and the stone teaches you quickly not to.

In the afternoon when the cool rain comes - straight down for twenty minutes, no warning - I don't go inside. I stand in my doorway and watch how the water moves. It doesn't puddle. It goes dark along the mortar, it runs in the gutters, it finds the channels already cut for it. For a day afterwards the yard smells of turned earth. The cistern ticks more quickly. My interval dries in an hour, the limestone drinking everything just as it should.

Children sometimes run through, for a few steps, then stop, hands on knees, laughing without breath. I don't tell them to be quiet. They already know not to shout. Sound goes flat here against stone. I nod to them. They know my place is not a shortcut, but a pause, and they go around.

At dusk I light the lamp. A shallow bowl of oil with a wick, set on the stone sill. The light is small. It falls on the same wall that has held that same small circle of light for longer than I can count. Seventeen or eighteen other lights come on in the two crescents. Mine is not the brightest. It doesn't need to be.

I sit in the doorway where the stone holds the day's sun a little longer, even in summer when the air stays thin and cool enough to pull your sleeves down. I listen to goats shift on slate. To a pika, one bright note from the scree beyond the walls. To the drip into the cistern, which from up here is not a tick but a very faint, very regular sigh.

I am not the rememberer. The very old in the sunniest doorways remember which wall was re-laid in the year the pine was struck by frost. I am not the child with the too-long rope, learning to lead the goat. I am the interval between.

If you asked me what I do, I wouldn't have a word for it that you would know. We have many words for the states of stone and water - stone that sweats, stone that drinks, water that beads, water that sleeps under the slab. My work is stone that stays empty, water that passes by without stopping. Maintenance that looks like doing nothing, and is, precisely, doing nothing so well that the bowl can continue to hold its shape.

I fit there because I like the pause more than the sentence. Because my breathing has slowed to the hamlet's pace. Because I understand, without having been taught, that nothing changing is not the same as nothing happening. It is happening very completely, over and over, bead by bead, sweep by sweep, drip by drip.

And when condensation begins to form again in the dark, bead by bead on every surface, it will form on my walls too, and on the bare rock of my interval, and I will be there inside the small, perfect stillness the bowl holds while the mountain weather moves high above it, untouched.
