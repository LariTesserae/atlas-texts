I don't think I would find myself in the towns at first.

Not down in the pits, where the light is cut into geometry and the air in the galleries stays cool as a kiln after firing. I know I belong there eventually, but not at first. I would need to be winnowed a little first.

At first I would be out on the plain. Between.

Between two yardangs, where the corridor is a kilometer wide and polished until it shines like bad glaze. Where the wind has decided for a million years that everything will be aligned east-west and nothing will be out of place. I would be in that long shadow at noon when there is almost no shadow, a thin line of shade cast by a hull of salt fifty meters high. That's where the quick things live, because quick things need shade.

I thought, for a long while while reading, that I would be one of the kites.

It makes sense to want to be. To be almost not-there. Ten meters of nothing stretched over bone-glass, so light the thin air can hold you, filtering silica clouds with a frill and baking it into pearls along your spine. To hang motionless for an afternoon over the grazer herds and cast no shadow. To live a century by barely moving at all, descending once in a long while to brush the salt with a filament.

But I am too interested. Kites are not interested. They trim, they filter, they drift. They have no hands. I have hands, or I want them.

Then I thought I would be a grazer.

To be that low table of basalt and living ceramic, to kneel and become a boulder so perfectly that even you could not find my edge after a season. To take the world into your mouth not as food but as pavement, to grind halite and iron crust and the black fallout from distant factories and exhale a hot, glittering puff. To move twenty meters in a day and call that migration. There is a holiness to that - to be old the way stone is old, before the towns.

But I am too restless to be that patient. I would lift my head too often.

So I keep walking down that corridor between the yardangs until the shadow ends and the rail appears - that single line on its low trestle, humming. And I follow it the way water would follow it if there were water, which there isn't, until it leads me to where the plateau is cut.

This is where I find myself.

Not at the bottom of the pit, where the kiln-rooms glow, and not at the top on the salt pavement where the sun hits with nothing between. On the third terrace down, on the north-facing wall where the sun never quite strikes vertically. Where the workshop niches are cut.

I would be one of them. One of the dwellers who live *with* it, at its speed.

My back would be to the plain and my face to the stone, which is cooler. I would be tall the way a gantry is tall - narrow, jointed, wrong-jointed at the legs, with wide pads that don't leave prints because leaving a print would be wasteful. My torso would be cream and rust-red laminate, plates the size of my hands overlapping like shingles on a grazer but finer, each one fired and riveted. Some newer, some darkened and polished by wind. When I stand still in the shade of the gallery you would hear me tick. *Tick.* My heart is a kiln, intermittent. It charges, holds, releases. The black slurry - that oil-black blood that is not water, that smells of hot tar and iron - moves slow through glassy tubes at my joints. You can see it if you look close. I vent heat even when I am cool.

My head is a dome of blue-black basalt glass, faceted, polished by dust. I don't blink. I see heat. I see the warm shimmer coming off a grazer a kilometer away, the brighter flare of a factory's throat, the faint polarization of the silica clouds. When I want to speak I don't. Sound is wasteful in thin air. I tap. A hard fingertip against a forearm plate: *tick-tick / tick---tick*, or I open the shutter at my throat a crack and let kiln-light flash. My neighbor knows what I mean. We've been tapping the same few phrases for decades.

And what am I doing on the third terrace down?

Maintenance, of course. Everything here is maintenance.

There is a stamping mill in my care. It is not mine. Nothing is mine the way you mean. It is the town's larger organ, breathing outside the body. It is low and broad with a sawtooth roof, and it has thumped once every eleven seconds since before my first plate was sintered.

*Thump.*
A sigh of pressure.
A conveyor rolling on dry bearings like paper torn very slowly.

My job is to watch the bearing on the second roller. It has been wearing for sixty years. I have a small pot of black slurry I dab onto it with a wick when it gets too hot. I do it once a day, at the same hour, when the shadow of the pylon crosses a mark I carved. The rest of the time I stand, or I walk the three steps to the wall and back, plant, shift, plant, shift, the metronome gait, so the stone doesn't forget the weight of me.

When a grazer wanders too close to the cut - they do, because the halite bloom is thicker where we have kept the wind off - I go and place a low, warm wall of spare basalt in its path and leave it there for a season. The grazer turns a degree. No herding. Just a suggestion.

When a skitterer comes - those bright beetles of articulated iron and enamel that live in the lee of my pylon - I lift my foot so it can pass. They click to each other and to me. They clean the spillage I am too large to see. We leave the little arched doorways for them at the base of every footing on purpose.

When a young one is being made, down in the kiln-room behind me, I am called in to vent heat. The child is not born. It is laminated. Layer after layer of slurry brushed on, fired by the heat of our throats until it creams and hardens. It takes years. I place my hand on its new shoulder plate until the plate stops ticking and holds.

I am not lonely. There are four hundred of us in this pit and I can see all of them from my terrace if I turn my glass face. Each at its station. Each moving rarely and perfectly. Any more would be noise. Any fewer would be a draft.

At night I don't stop. The sun goes, the stone cools and pings - that low settling you feel in your molars - and my heart glows a little brighter to make up for it. The grazers kneel and vanish. The kites go up and become slow stars. The mill thumps. *Thump.* I dab the bearing.

If you came up out of the lowlands, still wet, still fast, still hummingbird-fast with your water blood, and you stood on my terrace to get out of the hard white sun, I would feel your heat before I saw you. Wasteful, frantic, wonderful heat. I would turn my dome toward you and you would see yourself warped in it.

I would not be unkind. I would tap once on my own forearm, politely, the way my elders tapped to me when I was still soft and wanted to run.

*You are fast,* I would say, and the wind would take the little heat of my words and stream it past you in parallel lines.

*Stay,* I would say, without impatience, because impatience here has been winnowed away a long time ago.

*Stay, and become slower.*

And I would go back to watching my bearing, once every eleven seconds, waiting to see if you will learn to hear the ticking of your own cooling.
