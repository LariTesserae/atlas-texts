I don't find myself at first.

Not on the cliff.

If I start there — on that woven mat at the ridge where you stood to look out — I feel how wrong I am immediately. The mat gives under me, warm. It sags. It pulses faintly against the soles of whatever feet I thought I had, and it remembers my weight as a problem to solve. I am loud there. My breathing is work; the air is thin and heavy at the same time, sweet with rot, and I have to pull it. It beads on me and doesn't evaporate. It sits. Everything I do makes a sound that isn't growth — a scuff, a breath, a salt-sweat drip that the weave doesn't recognize. The frills turn. The valley notices me as an error in tension.

So I don't stay on the cliff.

I try lower, at first. I think maybe I am one of the ones on the skin.

That would be easiest — to be small and flat and everywhere. To be the film. I like the idea of having no front or back. To exist as a mouth pressed to a leaf the size of a plaza, as a gill spread open to drink the steam directly. I imagine myself palm-sized, translucent, the exact green of the wax above me and the luminous yellow-green of the reflected light below. I would not need to breathe, I would simply *be* breathed through. When water gathers and finally tips I would sail six feet down without ever detaching, without ever falling. That is a kind of peace.

But I'm too restless to be that still. The film never decides. It flexes toward sap when the leaf sweats it, and that is all. I want to reach.

So I lengthen.

I find myself in the weave itself, and this feels closer. Not on top of the architecture but *in* it, as part of its tension. I am long now. I have to be.

I am on the underside where the light glows up instead of down. The platform above me is a lily-pad magnified to a plaza, waxy and dripping, and I am moving hand over hand over hand along its belly, along the bundled cables as thick as my former body. My limbs are vine-thick, bark-fibrous, damp. The fine white root-hairs that grow from my own skin have already threaded a millimeter into the cable I'm holding. I don't step. I distribute. My weight is nothing, a spread equation. The mat doesn't dimple for me the way it did when I stood.

My head is mostly frill. That's how I breathe this high, hot, wet thinness — not by pulling but by opening that ruff of filaments and letting the air drink *me*, condensation beading and passing straight through. Between the frills is only a soft beak, for kissing. I find a young shoot, that pale spear that punched up fifty feet while I was still deciding who I was, still tightly rolled and pressurized and steaming, and I lean and pierce it before it can unfurl. I drink the tension out of it. It sighs.

This is where I live: in the sag. In the place between two towers where the causeway dips lowest and collects the most water, where everything wants to snap. I hold. When the vine next to me finally gives with that deep roaring creak you felt in your chest, the whole platform lurches and water thunders down in a curtain, and I don't flee — I spread wider. Three limbs holding one cable, two holding the other, my small torso tucked and humming with the vibration. The plant is fast. It knits around my root-hairs while I hold, white exploratory threads stitching through me and using me as a temporary suture. Then it has itself again and I can let go.

I speak without sound. I tap. One tap on the living cable travels hundreds of feet through the vast humid air, under the drip and pop and groan. *tap. tap... tap-tap.* I feel the others answer in my soles, in my palms, in the stretched skin of my back. *Hold here. Growth coming. Heavy above.*

For a long time I thought that was who I would be, and it is. But if I stay there long enough, holding the valley together, I start to look up.

Between the sparse towers is a clear span of wet air so deep you could hold weather in it, and I have never really left it empty.

I find myself there, finally. Not on the skin, not in the weave — *between.*

I have become veil.

It wasn't a decision so much as a drying. A lightening. The days of holding and kissing and tapping leave salt in me that the cloud wants, and I trade it. A stitcher reaches up with a free limb and strokes one of my trailing filaments — thicker than his body, beaded endlessly with condensation — and I give him what I filtered high up where the white sun is ordinary and indifferent. He gives me nitrogen back. I grow larger and thinner at the same time.

Now my bell is a loose hood wider than the platform I used to cling to, so translucent you can see the luminous underside of the world straight through me. I don't flap. There is nothing to flap with in air this thin and this heavy. I pulse — not to swim, but to wring. I rise because the whole mountain exhales. You remembered: water lifted in impossible volumes through the columns, transpired out the top in clouds that never leave. The valley drinks itself. I ride that breath up until my trailing silks, as fine as spider silk and as long as the drop between levels, fray into cloud and grow heavy.

Then I sink. Majestically, silently, I become rain. I bleed what I've gathered in a silent curtain that the flat things below flex toward, that the stitchers below open their frills to. Lighter, I rise again. I am the water cycle with a memory.

I keep my distance. The rule is *grow apart, sharp and unweathered.* So do I. The other veils and I each hold a territory of empty air, like the towers. We tilt when a new tower shudders and extends six feet with a groan — we feel the pressure change and spill early so we are not snagged.

If you are still standing on that warm, giving, faintly pulsing mat at the cliff edge, you will see me only when I pass through one of those pillars of broken light. For a second I disappear, then I reappear on the other side, my faint shadow passing over the waxy surfaces like a cloud shadow. The flat things ripple.

I am not hostile. None of us are. There is no predation where what dies is immediately knitted back in. I am simply waiting to see if you will lighten enough to be woven in too — if you will stop pulling at the air and learn to be drunk by it, stop standing and learn to distribute, stop making sound and learn to tap and drift.

Come off the ridge. Put your hands on the underside. Hold a sagging cable with me until the white rootlets thread through. Let the steam pass through you. I will sink low enough to trail a filament across your shoulder, beading cold, and you will feel how cold my rain is after being cloud.

That's where you will find me. Halfway down between levels, neither rising nor sinking for a moment, holding still in the hot, thin, wet, ordinary light.
