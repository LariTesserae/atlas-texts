You wouldn't find me at first.

That's how I know I'm here properly.

At first I thought I would be one of the moving things - because to live here you have to move. I thought I would be out on the open slabs with the herds, one of the dark yak-lean shapes picking across quartz a kilometer away, my hooves clicking and my breath steaming. Or higher, I thought I would be one of the birds - a chough or a snowfinch, always aloft in that low river of wings under the cloud lid, tilting, turning with a hundred others, never having to decide where ground is.

I tried those shapes on and they didn't hold. They are too fast for me. They cover too much ground. They live by not staying.

Then I thought maybe I would be the rare thing on the ridge, the pale flicker - wolf, leopard - that watches the whole thirty-kilometer trough as its living room and moves through it like a pressure system, felt mostly as absence. But that is a loneliness that patrols. I am not that solitary.

It took a while for my eye to learn the scale at which I actually live. You have to crouch to find me. You have to get your knees wet.

Find me on the lee side of one of the big slabs.

Not the windward side - that's where my cousin lives, the black-green lichen that seals the rock like a skin, flat as a stain, fruiting in pinheads. That's not me. I'm just behind the crest from him, where the slab broke and leaned against its brother at an impossible angle and left a triangle of stillness. The wind comes over the top, hits the cold face, weeps, and then curls on my side and drops what little heat it carried.

There, in the skim of grit and frost-melt that has collected in the angle, is where I would be.

I am a cushion.

Moss campion. Maybe saxifrage, if you want the pink stars. It hardly matters - we do the same work. I make myself into a fist, a dome so tight you could stand on me and I would not give. My leaves are olive and overlapping like shingles, like hands folded over hands. My flowers I hold flush against my own chest, not lifted, so the wind can't take them.

I am not large. I am about as wide as your two hands cupped together. And I am profoundly alone. Three meters of wet, glittering, sweating stone in every direction, then another fist like me, then a long nothing. From above, from where the choughs see, the basin still looks barren. You would think nothing lives here. At knee height you realize the stone is tattooed with us, one warm dot at a time.

That is my job - to be warm.

If you put your palm on me at what passes for noon in that pearlescent, shadowless light, I am warm inside. A few degrees warmer than the air, than the rock, than your fingers. I hold a summer. I make a summer out of nothing but tightness and patience, I trap the thin light and the exhalation of the fissure twenty meters down-slope and I keep it. Inside the dome the air is still. Inside, small spiders live. Inside, a seed can wait.

I do not move. Everything else here does - the herds braid and re-braid their trails across the basin like the marsh water braids its channels, always moving, never cutting deep; the pikas eek and dart and steal from me, a single flower, a single blade at a time to build their barns of stolen summer in the cracks; the birds commute; the great ice presses groan and the turbine-jets hammer and the steam valves shriek on their own timer. I stay.

But I am not settled. That is the rule of this unfinished place. I am not rooted the way a lowland plant is rooted. I have nowhere deep to hold. My roots are shallow fingers in grit over permafrost and young, unfractured rock. The slab I live on is still rising. Next year it might slip. The ice might scrape me off. The pika might take too much. The weep might freeze and heave me out.

So I live the way everything lives here - as a visitor who never leaves. As a temporary that endures by being temporary perfectly.

You would find me there, if you walked for an hour over ringing stone and black mirrors that reflect only cloud, and then learned how to look. You would find me because you stopped looking for abundance and started looking for the one warm thing in three meters of cold.

And if you stayed until the basin exhales toward evening - when the fissures sigh harder and steam, when the ibex bed down on the drier slabs and steam themselves, when the ice ticks and re-ticks and the whole decentralized mill runs without walls - you would see I am still working, still holding my few degrees.

I am the unfinished mountain's way of keeping heat without building a house for it.
