You would not find me above.

At first I thought you would. When I slipped through that lip of torn roots, turning sideways to get in, I thought I was still the thing that slips — upright, warm, too loud, boots sinking and springing back in peat a foot deep. I thought I was the breath that hangs and does not leave, the shape standing in the thin grey shaft from the crack, listening to the *tit... tit... tit* of water falling from root tip to peat and being drunk again.

But that shape is the empty space. That shape is the only gap in the room. I am not the gap.

Give it a minute. Let your eyes learn the faint lichen-light. Let the cold that never bites, just holds, have time to equalize. The moss is not still.

I find myself lower.

Not standing. Not exactly on the floor either, because the floor is not ground. The floor gives. It breathes. If I try to locate myself I am about a hand's breadth above the water that beads and falls and beads again. I am close enough to the peat that I can feel the sigh when weight shifts two meters away, the ripple that goes through the wall without any shadow causing it. My world is horizontal and very close.

For a while I think I am part of the mat itself — sphagnum and liverwort and cold-thread moss braided into pillar and curtain and shelf. That would make sense. The braid is total. There is no between. To be here is to be touching something else that is also alive. I am touching green-black felt on all sides. I am covered and covering.

Then I move, and the wall moves with me, and I understand I am not the wall.

I am one of the things the wall is made of when it decides to rearrange.

You would find me on the eastern side of the pocket, where the moss has already made that perfect, empty shelf at waist height, the one with the lip, the crate-sized negative that no hands have built yet. I keep to that wall because it is drier there, or rather, less wet. The condensation runs differently there, along the spider sheets that are strung between the root-columns like cisterns. The sheets glitter when I breathe — but I don't breathe the way the upright thing breathes. I move without sound. My legs are muffled by wet felt.

If you crouched and put your eyes to the moss at that height and held very still — longer than is comfortable in the wet near-freezing air — you would lose me. That is my particular talent.

I am flat. Black at first, until the faint light hits me at an angle and I go iridescent — green, then blue, then black again. My back is not my back. My back is a garden. In the pits of my elytra, moss and liverwort have taken root, tiny perfect cushions that I prune by moving. Lichen dust collects there. A fern spore once tried. When I walk, I am a beetle. When I stop, I am a patch of wall. The pocket taught me this. Nothing lives here alone, and the best way to not be alone is to carry your part of the totality with you.

I am about as long as the last joint of the upright thing's thumb. I carry my weight low, antennae testing the wet air before my feet do. I walk the vertical now, up the furred side of one of the tarred larch pilings — those points driven from the town above to hold houses up, now totems gone completely to moss and white, faintly glowing lichen. The ants use them as roads. The salamanders hang from them like fruit, ash-grey with sudden orange underneath, throats pulsing, blinking once, very slowly, when the upright thing exhales. I pass under one. It does not see me when I am still. Condensation has beaded on its head and falls from its nose, *plip*, into a bootprint pool that never drains. It is memory. I am maintenance.

My work is small and constant. I turn over what the woodlice have chewed. I keep the garden on my back from overgrowing my eyes. I drink from the spider sheets without breaking them — the spiders are glassy-legged and patient, they tend their plumbing, they don't rush. When midges touch a sheet and stick and do not struggle, I wait until the spider has decided it is not thirsty.

And I tend the shelf.

Not because I understand crates. I don't. But the ants do, in their white, milk-colored line, carrying flecks of larch bark from piling to piling to build a low, lipped tray that looks useless until a human thinks *that would be useful there*. And I do, in my way. I graze the edge of the empty moss shelf so it keeps its lip. I keep it clean. The pocket prototypes. The plant makes the negative before the hands make the positive. The shelf was here before any crate. The moss cairn beside the stone shrine was here before the stones were stacked. I keep the prototype true, so when the upright thing finally sets something down — salt fish, a bowl, a hand — it will fit.

That is the rule that asserts itself the longer you stand here, or hang, or lie flat and carry a garden: light is food and light is forbidden. If the upright thing brings a lantern down, we will cover it. Not to kill. To tend. The lichen glows just enough. In the dark the moss grows toward the dark. I know to stay out of the thin shaft from the crack where the midges hang motionless and grey. I know that nothing decays here, not really — the three-winter-old fish on the platform is still there, its eye my cousin's nursery — but when the thaw comes even for a day, everything will move an inch. My garden will tighten. A ladder rung will snap and be bridged before it can fall.

You would find me, then, not in the town of Nizhny-Kol with its sparse houses and meaningful fences and black water between them. You would find me below it, in the room that inverts its scale, where the town is the sparse interruption and we are the city.

You would find me on the eastern wall, becoming wall, becoming beetle, becoming wall again, tracing the lip of a shelf that is waiting.

If you held your breath until yours matched the peat's slow sigh, you might see me move. A patch of wall walking. A small, flat, iridescent back with a green cushion, pausing at the edge of what has not yet been built, tending it, waiting to see if you will become useful enough to fill it.
