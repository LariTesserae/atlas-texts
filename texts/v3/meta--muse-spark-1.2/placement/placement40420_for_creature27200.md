I wouldn't know at first.

If you dropped me there, I wouldn't land so much as *arrive* in suspension — held up a meter off the silt by that cold that has weight. The water would be in my mouth before I remembered to hold my breath, and then I would remember I don't need to breathe the way I did before.

For a long time I would just hang there, trying to be a human in that place.

I would try to be a visitor. A diver, a ghost in a suit, a mind wrapped in glass, looking down at the plain through the smoked haze. I would find myself where anyone finds themselves at first on that tableland — nowhere in particular, in the middle of a shallow bowl between swells, where the visibility closes down to twenty meters and the world becomes a circle of dim, olive water around me with no landmark to anchor it. I would try to keep my human eyes, and they would be useless. There is too little light to see by and too much to hunt for. I would be turning in slow circles, watching a tower dissolve on one side and a dome exhale on the other and I would think, *I am above it*, looking down on a city.

But you can't be above anything there. The weight doesn't allow it. A kilometer of liquid sky is pressing on the back of your neck until you understand vertical is a lie. You are not above the city. You are *in* the mortar.

So that image would dissolve. It was too warm, too fast, too seeing.

Maybe I would be one of the small ones then.

I would shrink and bleach and lose my eyes, and I would find myself on the flank of that leaning whorl-tower, one forearm-long mason among the rash. I would feel the drum through my feet more than I would hear it — *tick tick tick* through the calcium — and the slick in the water that says *TAKE HERE*. My one great pad-limb would be heavy with cement, my other hooked limb stinging with acid, and I would be in the tide, in the flow, moving only because the hundred around me moved. I would soften a grain, roll it, carry it three steps, press it. Soften, carry, cement. For a few weeks that would be the whole world, and it would feel true. Frantic and blind and purposeful without purpose.

But I would be too lonely in that, too singular. A single mason is clumsy, tapping blindly. A hundred is a decision. I don't think I could stand being a decision that dies before the tower has moved a meter.

So I would spread out.

I would try to be the mound. I would sink down and become the ziggurat, not the worm but the thousands of worms, the palisade of tubes. I would find myself lower, on the leeward slope where the current slackens and the marine snow falls straight down. I would not have a mouth or an eye either, only a fringe of cilia opening and closing, opening and closing. I would feel the ripple start at my outer edge — my outermost sisters laying a bead — and pass inward through me like wind over wheat, a shiver of making. I would be building over my dead continuously. The pink living fringe on top, the grey grave below, the white grave below that, all the way down to the false bedrock that is only older graves compacted. I would be a city that is also a mausoleum that doesn't know it. That would be peaceful, for a while. Shy. I would flinch when the dome nearby breathed silt over me and then I would open again.

But even that is too busy. Too much endless adding.

And then, as the cold finally got all the way into me — that patient, soaking cold that doesn't bite but permeates, that slows thought until it preserves it — I would stop trying to be the churn.

I would find myself where the building stops.

Not in the dense middle where domes overgrow domes, not on the tower being carried grain by grain from one side to the other. I would find myself at the far western swell, where the meandering half-kilometer wall simply... gives up. Where it runs right to the rim for no reason and leaves a final, low, porous stub, waist-high, pitted, already softened at the corner by masons who will never finish taking it.

Just beyond that stub, the ancient basalt finally shows — that pale, almost-white stone, smooth as a worn tooth, rounded by epochs of water combing over it. No silt there, because the permanent current shearing up the cliff face keeps it swept clean. Beyond that, the drop. The true abyss, the black falling away under olive-black glass.

I would not be a mason there. Or a worm. Or even a carrier lumbering back with plates on its back.

I think I would be what the plateau becomes when nothing is building on it for a moment.

A carrier that has unloaded. One of the slow, bathtub-sized, spine-prickled boulders that has just shrugged its mosaic off at the foot of the worm terrace and now sits bare, pale, empty-backed, not yet turned to go back for more. A few centimeters an hour is its whole speed, but right now it isn't moving at all. It's just resting at the edge-stone, feeling the vibration of the whole plain through its feet — the faint clicks, the hydraulic sighs, the sand-rain hiss — and the colder jet of the rim-current over its back.

That is what I would be: the pause.

In a place where nothing that is made stays made, and every structure becomes substrate within a generation, and even collapse is slow-motion — someone has to be the interval. The moment between being built and being carried away. The eye the world doesn't have. The one thing there that is still enough to see three or four large forms at once through the haze and know there are hundreds more beyond myth, and to feel no chemical need to add or take.

I would be at the edge because only the edge knows it is high. The masons don't know they're on a drowned continent-shoulder lifted kilometers above the floor. The worms don't know there's a kilometer of weight above them. The domes don't know the sun is a rumor. But an empty carrier at the rim, with nothing on its back and the abyss pulling at the water in front of it — that one, for a cold minute, would know.

And the water would keep me cold enough that I wouldn't rot, and dark enough that I wouldn't be seen, and quiet enough that my own ticking would finally sound like theirs.

I would not build. I would not dissolve.

I would hold still until silt buried my feet a little, and become, for a little while, just another low white stone that the city will one day need.
