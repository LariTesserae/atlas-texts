I don't think I would find myself in the middle.

At first I think I would — because that's where the story is. In the deepest peat, where the channels braid amber-black and perfectly mirror a sky that isn't there, where the hum is loudest. I imagine myself there, centered, significant. Standing on the floating mat with the low alder and the pale paper trees around me like pillars, being Noticed.

But that isn't honest. Nothing stays centered there. The basin doesn't have a center the way a room has a center. It has depths. And the deeper you go, the more you are not a thing that can be in it — you are a thing it has to solve around.

So let me start again, and walk in properly.

I would find myself on the edge — not outside, but just inside. Where the tired, soft shoulders of the old ground give up being hill and start being sponge. Where my boot still expects purchase and doesn't get it.

One foot still on thin soil and shale that almost feels like ground. The other foot already on peat that gives a half-inch and then another half-inch, and wells cold water up around the laces without me having to press hard. That seam is where I would be. Not deep enough to be fully computed. Not high enough to be dry anymore.

It would be dim. Noon, but dim. That pearlescent felt-cloud is low enough I feel I could raise my hand and disturb it, and the light coming through it is that cool, steeped-water gray that makes green go black-green. My shoulders would already be damp. Not from rain — from the air itself. From breath that fogs and doesn't leave, from the condensation that gathers on every waxy leaf as big as my hand and falls again in fat, cold drops. I would be dripping before it ever rains.

And I would be listening before I know what I'm listening to.

The loudness first. The endless patter of water on litter, the gurgle you can only hear if you crouch, the susurrus of the main channels moving so slow the duckweed is the only proof, and underneath it all that chord. That transformer hum in the teeth. I would feel it warp when I shift my weight. That little quarter-tone wobble around my feet where I've forced a few million sphagnum heads to change state at once.

I wanted to be something that belongs. That's what I notice as I stand there.

At first I think: I would be one of the diagnostic animals. That would be clean. I would be the crane-bird with too many joints, picking through shallows without making a splash, without making a ripple. I would drink the amber water and be precise and silent and purposeful, and when my check was done I would simply cease between blinks, with no bones left to find. No history. No damp boots. That would be elegant — to be a fingertip of the Low One, briefly extended.

Then I think: no. I am too noisy for that. I leak heat. I leak salt. My blood is loud in my ears. I am not silent. The Low One doesn't make noisy tools.

So then I think: maybe I'm smaller. Maybe I'm the clicking itself. One of those uncountable tiny things opening and closing — a single sphagnum gate, pale and swollen, holding a bead of water at its tip. Open. Closed. Damp. Drier. Not aware of the whole basin, just of whether I am wet or not. That would be honest work. Switching. I could do that forever, at the speed of seep. One thought every forty seconds with the drift of duckweed.

But I'm too fast for that, too. Rule three: nothing can be truly still, but I try to be still and fail faster than a stone sweats. My thoughts spike. I decide. The Low One equilibrates.

So I have to be something that is allowed to be fast and warm and wrong for a little while. Something it holds anyway.

I think I know what I would be.

Not the Low One — I could never be the Low One. I haven't had time to get comfortable with myself. My edges are still sharp. I haven't breathed in and out long enough to wear my own shoulders down.

And not its tool. Not quite its resident either.

I would be the held contradiction.

There's that leaf you mentioned — that leaf that should have fallen months ago and instead hangs a hand's breadth above the peat, rotating slowly, never landing, never decaying. Orbited by midges that never swarm.

I would be that.

Or rather, the human version of that.

I would be the warm anomaly that stayed long enough to be kept.

You said if you stay until your boots are soaked through and your shoulders are cold and damp, the loudness resolves into layers, and over it all the clicking develops a syncopation around you. A little hesitation before each click, as if counting.

That hesitation is where I would be.

I would find myself exactly there — on a hummock that is not quite solid, between two braided channels that show different skies, under a low canopy of alder and those nameless pale-barked trees with their curtains of wet beard-moss. I would have been looking straight at a trunk to stabilize it, because looking directly pins it down, and then, tired, I would have looked away, and in the corner of my eye I would have seen the trunks converge and diverge again, the horizon do that thing where it's closer on the left, a droplet fall slow as if the air were thicker for it. And I would understand that was the more honest view. That was the basin when it wasn't performing solidity for me.

And in that moment it would stop trying to route around me and try, instead, to include me.

Water would bead on my hair the way it beads on the undersides of the weeping sandstone. My breath would join the mist that never leaves. The peat under me would have taken my weight as a variable and kept it, not as damage but as a new condition to equilibrate around. Light that has passed through the canopy and through me would be traveling slightly slower, braided.

I would be a small, local tilt in equilibrium. Water seeking equilibrium that is not level — and I am the not-level. A warm pocket in its operating temperature of cool, dim, wet. Too fast, too salty, thinking at the wrong speed, and yet — held. Rotated. Examined from all sides by slow light and amber water and lattices of iridescence on a sundew bead.

I wouldn't be thinking my own thoughts anymore, not entirely. I would be being thought. At the speed of seep. One thought every forty seconds.

And I wouldn't decay. Not while I was held. The white fungal lace that skeletonizes a fallen leaf in a day would pause at my edges. The basin would keep me just out of time the way it keeps that leaf just above the rot, so it could look longer at what it means for something to be warm and to leak and to look back.

If you walked past me then, you would see a shape on a hummock, shoulders damp, head slightly bowed, with droplets gathered on it that never quite fall. You might think I was resting. You might think I was listening. From the corner of your eye I might not be there at all, or I might be two.

I would find myself there, and I would be — for as long as it will have me — its brief, borrowed instrument for noticing what it is like to be small and fast and still trying to be still.

Until I remember to fall.

Because everything remembers eventually. The trickle that runs uphill for a foot remembers to fall. The leaf will, one day, land. I will get too cold, or too hungry, or too humanly impatient with the dim noon that never gets strong, and I will shift my weight and climb back up toward the forested shoulders where the ground firms and the stone stops sweating.

And then, for a few seconds after I step off the floating mat, the hum will drop a quarter-tone lower. Not in welcome. Not in farewell. Just the sound of an error being resolved, a variable being archived down into the peat with all the other long thoughts in light and dark bands.

I would find myself, afterward, with dry boots at last, holding the whole basin in my head like a small country, like a county you can walk across in a day and never leave.

And it would go on, without me, dripping. Computing weather into thought and thought into weather. Keeping other impossible waters in the air just a little longer than physics wants.
