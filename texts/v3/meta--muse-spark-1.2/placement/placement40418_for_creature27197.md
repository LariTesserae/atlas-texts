I think at first you wouldn't find me at all.

That is the point. You would look straight through me.

If you came walking that flat palm of a continent - where the mountains have all forgotten how to be stone and become this black, fingerprint-soft silt, where water has forgotten how to be a river and become a condition - you would see the basin first as an interruption. A green coin pressed into wet earth, twenty feet lower than everything that is already low. You would see the weeping towers first, because they catch the light. Ribbed and translucent and beading with their own condensed weather. You would hear the chorus first, because it's louder than sight - cicadas layered on cicadas, frogs, that two-note whistle every seven seconds that is not a bird, the soft creak of cellulose growing.

You would think the place was empty. Sparse on purpose. Thirty islands, fifty or a hundred meters of tea-dark water and root-braid between each one, silver lily-pad filters humming. And you would be right, and wrong.

You would find me in the interval.

Not on an island. Between them.

Thigh-deep, always. That is where I live - in the negotiation. In the place where water doesn't know where to go, and so I have to decide for it, for an hour. Ankle-deep black water that holds the sky perfectly until my shin shatters it. The saturated sponge that is not ground and not plant but both. If you stood on a tower balcony at gold hour when all the membranes flush pink and go translucent, you would see me as a slow wake between two lights. A line drawn briefly on water and then erased.

If you waded closer, I would resolve.

I would be one of the low people. Not low as in small. Low as in settled. Pulled down toward the water table by design and by generations of wading.

You'd see the feet first, probably - bare because they have to be. Wide, splayed, webbed to the first knuckle with skin thin as a frog's throat, the sole ridged and soft enough to grip algae without slipping. Bowed calves from a lifetime of pushing, not stepping. I don't hurry on dry ground because there is no dry ground; I part the water.

You'd see the skin second, in that honest light under the high thin haze that makes all colors true. Brown, maybe - with that green undertone you only catch at an angle. Not paint. Chloroplasts, dusted in the dermis. Freckles that aren't freckles: flat rosettes of rust-red lichen on my shoulders where the sun hits, seeded there when I was a child. They keep me from burning. If I fall asleep on a floating mat at noon, I wake up not hungry, faintly sugared, my sweat smelling like crushed leaf. If I stand still too long you can watch the air try to grow on me, a faint biofilm on my forearms that I wipe off without thinking, the way you wipe condensation from a glass.

I am always wiping, always cutting. You would hear me before you had my face.

*Shhk.* Soft draw-cut. The inside curve of the hook doing its work.

I wear it on a loop at my wrist and I never take it off, not to sleep. It's grown, not forged - cellulose edge, lignin spine, light as a question mark. Not a weapon. Manners. The vine crossed the canal while you were watching and I hook it back. The wall of the house behind me sealed a window overnight because it wanted less light and I have to coax it open again with a whistle and an offering of nitrogen. My tower is weeping as always, beads running down its fluted sides into the vacuole-cistern that is also a room, and the gutter is clogged with water lettuce. I clear it. I apologize to the root-mat for stepping where I did by trimming the algae I bruised.

You would know my face when I look up. I must have been in the canal - gill slits still fluttering pink behind my jaw, three fine seams that open when I go under for a minute to wrestle the filter-machines clear. Nictitating membrane flicking sideways as spray hits. Iris too large, amber in my case, made for leaf-filter. Hair shaved close except for the cap I cultivate - not hair at all now but a tight skull-garden of liverwort, soft and damp, that holds water for me. The children laugh at it. The old woman with the hanging moss that houses the mite-colony says it suits me.

And I am not alone, even when I am alone - because privacy here is water. Fifty meters of marsh in every direction until I choose to wade over and converge, brief and intense as dragonflies, on a boardwalk that the houses grew between breakfast and lunch because enough of us wanted it. Then we disperse.

If you asked me who I am in this hamlet where no one owns anything and everyone negotiates - I am not the owner. I am not even, strictly, the inhabitant. I am one of the four inhabitants layered over each other.

If you think I am the person, you are missing the building I tend, which drinks and remembers every palm that has touched it and pulses faintly at night. It's the third tower from the western edge, not the feral one that's been left to go thick with epiphytes and deer-things - mine is still polite. It lights under my hand when I press it, capillary light blooming, recognition, not reaction. It sulks sometimes. I pat it when I thump it. I talk to it in that short tonal patois that is half words and half whistle - the same two-note call you thought was a bird.

If you think I am the person and the building, you are missing the colleagues: the heron-assembler standing motionless in my canal filtering heavy metals through its beak, wings spread humming as solar collectors; the circuit-etched dragonflies that carry pollen and data with the same reverence; the vine-snake that is really a peristaltic pump keeping the ditch from going stagnant; the shimmering sigil of mites on my tower's flank that I consult about water quality the way you would ask a neighbor if the road is out.

If you think I am all of those, you are missing the mat itself - the mattress of moss and root-braid that is soil and roof and road - which sets the terms. It feeds me figs the size of fists that the tower extrudes a basket to catch, and protein nodules off aerial roots that I hook down. In return I keep cutting. Keeping the wound open just enough.

So where would you find me?

Exactly where the continent is trying to finish eroding and the place is refusing to be finished. In the shallowest basin on the oldest, flattest ground in the world, in bath-warm air that beads on my eyelashes, in water that never learned to leave.

You would find me mid-wade, mid-cut, mid-whistle, mid-conversation with a wall. Barefoot, green-sweated, gills still wet, hook warm from my hand.

Who would I be?

One of the peneplain's hands. One of the ways the low place keeps itself low and wet and alive. Not a settler. Not a conqueror. A tenant who learned how to prune a miracle and how to ask a house, politely, to grow a door.
