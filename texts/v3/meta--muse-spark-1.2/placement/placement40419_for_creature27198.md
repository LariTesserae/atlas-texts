You wouldn't find me up on the high hump.

At first I thought you would — that's where anyone would stand the first time, on one of those whale-backed domes where the black peat finally thins out and you can put your hand on the polished granite underneath. The place where you can see it whole. The perched ocean going wall to wall to cloud to the undercut lip at the edge of the world, the slate-gray bowl full to the brim. The cableways swaying, the turbine hall steaming on the next rise, the sheep on its drifting peat island turning slow in the current. That's the view you're given. But you don't *live* there. No one does. It's too exposed. The wind funnels up over the rim and never stops, and after a minute your eyebrows are beaded and your breath is ghosting even though it's summer and you realize that hill is just a place to look from, not a place to stay.

Then I thought maybe I'd be one of the birds. That would be honest — there are so many of them they *are* the weather. To lift with the geese at dusk when the whole reedbed rips itself into the air in one deafening clatter and the sound bounces off the pump houses. To live above the compromise, not in it. But that's too clean. Too dry. Nothing here gets to be dry.

And then I thought maybe I'd be smaller. A newt. Which is probably what I should be, if we're talking about who really lives here successfully. Black-brown back, orange belly flashing. Clinging to a rust-red piling at the waterline, moving up an inch when the wind pushes a wave, down an inch when it recedes. Perfect tide-keeper where there is no tide. Never needing to worry about whether the spillway holds. Just holding on.

But I can't. I keep coming back to the negotiation.

Find me lower down, where the water is only chest-deep and so clear you can see the drowned meadow underneath — the stone still there, furred green with algae and kelp-weed swaying just under the chop like hair. There's a place where one shallow bowl pours into the next over a smooth, undercut sill that the water has worn into a perfect step. It roars there, even when everything else is just chopping. That kind of low, constant, white-noise roar that gets in your teeth.

That's my circuit.

You'd find me on the walkway that stitches across it. Not the high main line; the secondary one, half-submerged, the steel slick with algae and newt eggs so you have to go one hand always on the cable rail. My walkway. My kilometer.

I am one of the ones who stayed. And I've been worn smooth the same way the granite was.

I'm short and broad — you have to be, at four kilometers. The air makes you pay for every tall breath, so you learn to be compact. Barrel chest, thick neck, calves like poured concrete from a lifetime of walking on steel that never stops moving under you. My face is that skim-milk pale you get under perpetual cloud-scrape, with the wind-flush high on the cheeks that never fades. My hair is always damp — frizzed or plastered, never dry. No one is ever dry here. It's the first thing you have to understand to understand us. Within minutes outside you're beaded, your wool sweats under your oilskin and never dries, your eyebrows hold mist. I wear the shaggy undyed sweater from the feral musk-sheep, felted trousers, hip waders patched with tire rubber, a canvas coat re-waxed until it shines black. Mittens with the trigger finger cut out. I smell like cold iron and peat smoke and lanolin and diesel, like the place does.

You'd hear me before you saw me, because you have to be loud here. The wind plus the water roar plus the thud-whine-clank of the hydromill — you learn to shout before you learn to whisper. My voice is hoarse and carrying, flattened into short sentences that can make it over a spillway. *GRATE'S CHOKED AGAIN!* *VALVE'S SIGHING!*

What I do is tend.

I wake up above the thrumming. We don't build houses separate from the work anymore. I sleep in the loft hammered together from scaffold plank above the generators in the old turbine hall — poured concrete stained black and green with seep, laundry steaming on lines strung between the pipes because the heat from the machines is the only reliable heat. Black tea with salt, split smoked char, fried cakes of reed-root. Then boots on steel.

My morning is a circuit. Check the pressure valve with a wrench the size of my forearm — it sighs steam and scalds the mist for a second. Rake the reeds from the intake grate where they've choked it overnight — shoulder-high reeds so thick you have to push a boat through with a pole, hissing in the wind — the water around my knees green and choked and absolutely vibrating with life. Phytoplankton, midge smoke, the chorus frogs under the reeds making the stems shake. A punt poled through the shallows to get to the cableway where the ore buckets still creak overhead mostly empty. Everything needs unblocking, tightening, bleeding, coaxing. Every hour.

I am not fighting the water. That's not the job. The water wants to leave — gravity wants this whole sky-sea to pour off the plateau rim in one catastrophic, smooth sheet to the lowlands. The ancient flat stone wants to lie still and be a bog. My job is the compromise. I give it a place to go so it doesn't take everything at once. I let it through the throat I tend, over that perfect stone lip, into the next bowl, through the turbine. A controlled roar instead of an ending.

My hands are pruned from birth, really. The children here swim before they walk well, slipping under the walkways to free a snagged line, eyes open in the cold. The old people go deaf in one ear and get the stoop from ducking pipe runs for fifty years. I rock a little even when I'm standing still, as if the chop is in my marrow.

You'd find me at dusk in the best moment, when the walkways fill with figures moving toward the light. The cranes and geese lift off the slate water in their thousands — that deafening, echoing clatter — and we all converge on the nearest hall where the light is on, because the dark is total and wet and the machines are warm. We share soup. We mend nets. We trade news about which floating sphagnum island has drifted where and whether the peat mat in the west bowl will hold a sheep for another week. Midges cloud around our heads and we don't even wave them off.

If you came down to my sill and crouched on the walkway, you'd see my newts — my little tide-keepers — lined up on the piling at the exact waterline, black backs, orange bellies. You'd see the fat char hanging silver in the green water below. You'd see the moss on the dome behind me ankle-deep and springing like a soaked mattress where the shaggy cattle graze, dripping.

And you'd hear it all resolve, after a minute, not into noise but into a single crowded, working chord. Water slap on stone, creak of cableway, putter of pump, wind in reeds, frogs, generators. And in the middle of it, my valve sighing, my rake scraping the grate, my breath ghosting in the cold — 0.8 cold that never leaves — and my low chanty to time the pull, harmonizing without trying to with the thrum.

I am not beautiful in a pristine way. I am damp, hoarse, and useful. I am scattered — 0.6 scattered — but not lonely.

I am the woman who keeps this one piece of ocean from falling off the roof of the world for one more hour, and then one more after that.
