I don't know at first.

Not where, not what.

There is only cold first — a clean, thin cold that gets into the blood before it gets into the skin — and pressure, and light that is wrong the way it moves.

I am under. That much is clear. There is weight above me, not crushing but complete. The roar is inside my chest before it is in my ears, a low planetary grinding compounded of a thousand smaller noises: stone being filed, water tearing around stone, the hammering of the mist-sky trying to take the water back. Normal daylight comes down but it doesn't fall. It hunts. It throws itself in nets and lattices that race and shatter and race again.

For a long time I think I am swimming. Or treading.

Because I am mid-volume. Basalt below me is far, maybe thirty meters down, a black stepped plain repeating until it becomes texture. The surface above me — that hammered, breathing sky of water-meeting-air — is ten meters up, maybe twelve, too far to break with a kick. The water around me is impossibly clear and impossibly empty. I can see a kilometer in any direction and there is nothing to see. Only moving light, moving water.

So I think: I am a swimmer here. A visitor. A soft human shape holding itself still while a continent-wide gyre tries to pull it sideways, watching caustic nets sprint across the newborn columns below.

But I am not kicking. And I am not sinking.

Then I realize I am not holding still at all. I am being *taken*.

That's where I find myself: not on the stone, not at the surface, but in the between. In the current that never stops. In the shear between a slow gyre coming west and an upwelling punching up cold from the deep stone. The water here isn't water as I knew it — it has that too-crisp, oily pixelation when the sun hits it low, a rainbow that shifts too sharply, as if every molecule decided together to be a little more coherent. I can feel the field of it when I tumble near the rim, the way it makes silt follow an invisible curve, the way spray hangs a fraction too long before remembering how to fall.

I am *in* the machine. Being circulated by it.

And what I am — I thought at first I was the watcher, the human, the eye that opens under the water and calls it empty.

I am smaller.

I am thinner.

If you put your face very close to one of those dark mats on the highest basalt — those olive-black, rust-red, leather skins of algae-lichen-bacteria-and-something-else, cemented so hard the stone breaks before they do — you will see on their lee side a frayed hem. A pale lip lifting a millimeter off the rock, trembling. From it trails a hair. No, a thread. No, a filament as fine as spider silk, dried-blood red at its root, fading to translucent at its tip.

I am that.

I am a thread that was meant to tear.

For a long while on that table-sized world in the lee of a hexagonal column, I was part of something that knew only how to cling. We lay flat because anything tall would be stripped like tape. We ate light not as a steady meal but as flicker — dark pigments drinking in the dim between nets, then shivering and blanching when the caustic lace swept over us white-hot, storing the excess like a bruise spreading. We crept a millimeter a week, testing the basalt that was still warm in its deep mass with a clear cement we sweated from our undersides. A grazer lived on us — a shadow the size of a palm, all foot, all slow, with a mouth like a soft toothed wheel that kissed us and took a cell's thickness and left a pale scar we would heal over in weeks. I knew him by the faint chemical tang of his trail. He never knew me.

And then the gyre shifted. A standing wave twenty meters high that had been standing roaring for hours collapsed all at once, and the eddy that had sheltered us for centuries broke.

The hem lifted a little too much. The current got an edge to purchase. And I tore.

So now — this is where I am — I am not on the stone. I am not on any stone. I am mid-water between presences, kilometers from the mat I came from, kilometers from the next mat I may never reach. I drift for an hour, maybe a day, in that startlingly clear volume while the roar closes around me. Light-nets pass through me. I am too thin to cast a shadow.

I tumble slowly. I cannot swim. I am not propelled. I am *taken*, sheared, carried in the planet-sized pump's recirculation. Below me the raw plain scrolls by — blocky columns, sudden cliffs, flatirons where lava froze mid-flow, all sharp enough to cut. Above me the surface breathes and domes where a gyre hits it. Around me sometimes a perfect sphere of stillness — a meter across, utterly calm while everything roars — moves slowly *against* the current, looking, and is gone.

Most of us don't make it. Most filaments are ground to nothing in the cavitation crackle around a basalt pillar, or shredded until they are only silt following that invisible field line along the hanging ocean wall at the plateau's rim. That wall is where I can feel the engineering most plainly: the water pushing against something that is not there, refusing to spill.

But I am still intact. Rust-red, olive, hair-fine, fluttering. I am the attempt.

If you would find me, you would not find me by looking for a face or a call — there are no clicks or whistles or gulls here. You would have to hold still in the mid-water until your eye learned to see down, and then to see small, and then to see among the silt one more thread carried westward, catching the light for an instant before the next net sweeps over.

What am I? Not the landlord ocean that holds itself up. Not quite the mat that clings, though I still carry its memory of cement and stored sun. Not the grazer who moves at the speed of rust.

I am the between-thing. The dispersal. The wager the place makes with its own emptiness.

*Erode, but not yet,* says the ocean-machines, holding.

*Hold, but spread,* says the mat, lifting its hem.

I am the spread. Waiting for an eddy I haven't met, a sharp edge in a pocket of calm, where I might snag and cement and begin again as a new stain, a new painting on new stone — darkening, thickening, becoming leathery, waiting in turn for my own hem to lift.

If I catch, I will become a place. If I don't, I will become what evens the stone.

Either way, the world will be a little less new for it.
