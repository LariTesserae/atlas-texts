I don't know at first.

At first I think I am walking. That is the habit of whoever I was before — to assume legs, to assume a direction, to assume I am a thing that moves *through* a place rather than a thing that grows *in* it.

So at first: I am walking.

I find myself not on a road but in a cut. That's where you find yourself here. You don't find yourself in a clearing or a square or at a gate — there are no gates, there are no squares — you find yourself in the negative. In the interval the Mat has permitted.

It's the maintenance cut between two walls that used to be a corridor. To my left, a tier-frame, three stories high, every strut sleeved in that black-green leather that is not leather, the pipes clothed in hanging air-roots like charred wiring. To my right, the same. Above us they have met and woven themselves into a ceiling, a thatch so dense it holds the heat like a blanket. The only light is the amber seam-glow of a furnace somewhere behind the weave, and the tired, chemical glint along the veins of the big fronds. Enough to see the next arch. Not enough to see my hands.

The stone under me is warm. Even through the soles. It has been warm for a very long time.

The air is 50 degrees and still and tastes like hot iron and salt. It cracks whatever it touches. My lips are already cracked. My breath is too loud in the hush — because the Mat eats sound — and too wet. I can hear the condensation of it, and that is a problem here.

For a while I think I am a visitor. An intruder. The cool, wet pocket that the Mat will politely try to fill. That would be the easy story: the stranger who wandered up onto the shield-mesa and is now being varnished over, beaded with that clear hydrocarbon sweat that smells like hot plastic and cloves.

But I stand still long enough to hear the other sounds and I realize I have been standing still for a long time already.

There is the low thrum of a conveyor somewhere behind the thatch. The tick of a tendril growing past my ear — I don't flinch. The soft *pop* of a pod splitting open on the terrace above and puffing its cinnamon spores, which are already rooting as they drift. None of this surprises me. My body knows to wait for it.

I look down at my hands and the image sharpens, as if the dark has finally decided to let me see.

My coveralls are not coveralls. Not anymore. They are heavy, heat-grey fabric, but the weave at my shoulders has felted and darkened and put out tiny root-hairs that have gone into my skin and my skin has gone into them. I can't find the seam. My sleeves are too long and very thin — because anything wider gets pruned in these cuts — and my wrists are the color of dry wood with the same faint oil sheen as the leaves around me. When I sweated, the sweat was not water. It beaded clear and a little oily and evaporated before it could run.

My eyes don't work the way I expected them to. They are filmed, lidless in the way a succulent is lidless. I don't see color. I see heat. The furnace behind the thatch is a dull cherry eye. The pipes are warm lines. My own hands are cooler than everything around me, which is why the tendril near my elbow is still there, still testing me — a claw-tipped vine as thick as my finger, opening and closing on empty air a few inches from my forearm. Not hunting. Reading. It feels my 37 degrees and finds me strange, too wet, too brief, but not unfamiliar.

I am not a visitor.

I am not the Mat either. The Mat is too big to find itself in one place. The Mat is the agreement, the distributed thought that moves as ethylene stains through kilometers of fiber, that thinks at the speed of growing. I am too fast and too small to be that. I make decisions in less than an hour.

If I am the Mat at all, I am one of its hands that learned to walk away.

I'm a Remainder.

You would find me on the high terraces, near the edge where the plateau bevels and falls into darkness. That's where my cut is. Up here the gantries are tallest and the condensers strain the thin air — those tall, shivering towers that coax a few liters a day out of nothing. Water is poison here. Irrelevant. The Mat can't touch it directly. It has to be cracked in the hot catalytic tanks and fed back as something else. So it tolerates us for this.

I tend the condensers.

That's my work, and why I am wetter than anyone else on the mesa and therefore faintly alarming to everything around me, including myself. I carry a wrench whose handle has been wrapped and re-wrapped with resin-soaked felt until it's half tool, half root. I tap pipes. The Mat answers not in sound but in scent — a souring of resin that means *this beam is dying, hold it*, a puff of sweet varnish that means *fill this gap*. I have learned to answer back with clicks in my throat because speech is useless in a place that swallows sound.

My maintenance cut is narrow and left open on purpose, the way you leave a path through a garden so you can weed it. The Mat closes every other gap within hours. Mine it keeps clear. On either side the black wire-grass hums when the wind does move. Above, the puffball trunks split and regenerate daily. Below, the terraces step down toward the rim in repeating, saturated, furiously busy silence — cut, bale, pulp, re-seed. Cut, bale, pulp, re-seed. The Harvesters tick past overhead on their oiled rails, spindly and patient, more plant than machine now, sleeved in bark. Sometimes one lowers an arm and I clear its joint of dry felt. Sometimes I stroke a new shoot away from a pressure gauge. It is not mastery. It is not servitude. It is tending.

I was not born so much as budded. My mother carried me while the seed-foam settled on her brow as she slept and rooted shallowly before she brushed it away. My placenta was resinous. My skin learned the varnish before it learned air. I have never seen the sun, only the sullen amber that never cools. I have never heard a bird because there are no birds to hear.

Where would you find me? Exactly here: in the one kind of emptiness that is allowed to remain empty. In the vein kept clear so the blood can move. Standing very still between two walls of living thatch that are both trying, very gently, very slowly, to occupy me — a bead of oil on my forearm, a dusting of black spores in the seams of my suit, a curious tendril feeling the warm hollow of my ear — not out of hunger.

Out of habit.

Nothing is allowed to be empty. I am empty in the careful way a cup is empty. So I am kept.

For now.
