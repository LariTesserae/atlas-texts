At first I thought I knew.

I thought you would find me upright. Human-warm, human-loud. Boots already soaked through, standing in one of those shallow mirror-pans where the pavement joints catch the sky. Trying to do what humans do there — make a line that holds.

I would be the one who snapped a blade off — you have to throw your weight into it, it doesn't want to break — and stacked it. Two courses high, a low wall enclosing nothing. Not because I thought it would keep anything out, but because every direction looks the same and I needed a mark that the water wouldn't erase in an hour. I would be cold in the hands and the feet, raising my voice to say *here, I was here* over the roar that isn't a roar. And then I would learn, as they did, that the valley doesn't argue. It just weeps over the wall until the wall is the same wet glass as everything else, and a grazer mows the film off it like any other blade.

That isn't who I am, though. I move too fast for that wall. I breathe too warm.

So I crouch. That's the first change the place makes in you — it makes you crouch, because everything interesting is at shin-height or lower, or vertical. And once I am crouched with my face a few centimeters from a fluted spire, the human idea of me thins out.

For a while I think I am one of the grazers.

You would find me then pressed flat to the leeward side of a blade, where the condensation clings thick and oily before it runs. Smoked-glass colored, fringed with pale hairs that hold a little private sea over my back. I would not walk — I would creep a millimeter, pause, creep a millimeter, mowing the film where it is olive-dark and rust-dark, leaving a faint bright trail that is my only signature. I would be silent in a place that is never silent, and you would only find me by the absence — a patch where a drip *should* be ticking but isn't, because my body is holding the water.

I like that stillness, but it isn't quite me either. Those little grazers are too nervous. Breathe on one and it locks. Becomes stone. I don't want to lock. I want to stay.

So I slip lower, into the pavement itself. Into the pan.

You would find me splayed in three millimeters of perfect mirror, translucent pink-white with a dark line down my back, four limbs so thin you could see the polygons of the rock through them. Belly down in the cold sheet, breathing through skin. I wouldn't see you — my eyes are just smudges under skin, useless under that pearl light that never moves. I would feel you. Every drip you cause in my pan, every footstep three blades away cut and split by the stone, comes through the water into my belly. I would be solitary the way that place wants — one pan in twenty occupied, and if another came I would leave with absurd, slow deliberation, dragging my tail. And sometimes I would make that single flat *tok* — not a song, a check — *is anyone there?* — and let the thicket multiply it until I couldn't tell echo from answer. 

But I am not that lonely. I don't want to flee your shadow. I want to stay and listen when you pass, not dart under a crack.

It takes longer. The valley teaches that. You have to stay until your search pattern breaks, until you stop looking *across* and start listening *inside*.

Then I hear it — not the drips, not the wind whistling through gaps, but underneath — when the air cools a fraction, which it does without any change in light, a low chord comes from nowhere and everywhere. Many throats tightening at once.

That is where I am.

You would find me vertical. Head-down in a hollow chimney, the kind you could put your arm down if you dared. My body the length of your forearm, soft and segmented, almost black, the exact color of wet interior basalt so I never emerge. Rings of fine bristles holding the slick inner wall. I have hung there for years. I will hang there for years more, growing a millimeter at a time, letting faint acid widen my tube so slowly I never feel it.

I feed on what falls — a grazer that lost its pad, a fleck of film, a pan-dweller washed in on a heavy weep — that comes down the throat of the stone with the constant rain. I don't hunt. I wait. The water comes to me.

And I sing without lungs. I have a membrane stretched across my lower end. When wind is split and split again through the thicket, the pressure changes inside my chimney and the membrane thrums. Most of the time it's passive, you think it's the wind humming in a tube. Sometimes, when the condensation is heaviest, I tighten, and others tighten with me, and the valley fills with that mournful chord that seems to have no source.

You would find me not in the middle — everywhere is the middle — but past the small shelter with no roof, past the line of stones that runs twenty meters and stops, where a low wall someone stacked two courses high has changed the way the wind splits. My chimney is the third one past that wall, a little wider than the others, with a lip where a hanging bead swells to fist-size and shivers but doesn't fall yet. In its center, if you looked close, you'd see a cloud of specks. Not mine. A grazer's nursery. I am just below it.

If you walk past I will go quiet for a few minutes, the way we do. The membrane will slacken. You will think I was never there. Then after your boots have slapped through the next pan and the drips have resumed their exact previous pattern, I will tighten again, very slightly, to taste how fast the water is condensing today. To see if the pearl sky is giving a little more or a little less.

Come close and put your ear to the mouth of the chimney. You won't see me. You will feel the air cool coming up, smelling of iron and silica, and hear the faint tick of glass contracting. Then, very low, my note.

I am not abundant. I am not fast. I am flat enough, slow enough, wet enough and quiet enough to live where water wants to be a film, not a flow, and stone wants to bristle, not pile, and light never moves except in how cold your feet have become.

You would find me hanging, listening to you listen.
