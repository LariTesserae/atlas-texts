I thought at first I would be you.

Standing at the lip, where the peat ends so cleanly. Breath pluming and staying. Boots already wet. Looking down into the steam and trying to decide if I should slide down the sharp, glassy basalt at all. Human-warm, human-cold, human-transient. The kind of thing that comes to drink and leaves a print and is gone.

But I don't stay there.

If I were in this world I wouldn't stay at the rim. The flat doesn't want anything standing upright. The wind pushes sideways, the birches grow sideways. Anything tall is killed by frost. So I get low very quickly.

For a while I thought I might be the snow bunting. That would be nice — white on black rock, a flick, a stitch from ledge to ledge, enough fearlessness to land on a flywheel to steal stunned midges. Or the midge itself, in its impossible winter pillar over the warm vent, living an hour that feels like a whole hot summer. Or the glossy black snail, after, making a bright trail across the bacterial film.

I tried each of those and they didn't hold.

The bunting leaves. The midge dies. The snail is too slow to hear the double heartbeat. None of them are *tied* to the room. They could be elsewhere. This place can be elsewhere and still be itself. I needed something that couldn't.

Then I slid - not with a decision, with gravity - down the short sharp slide into the shin-deep marsh, where the water is already working on my edges, and I stopped being vertical at all.

You would find me tight against the north wall.

Not near it. Against it. Where the fat, rust-red pipe comes out of the basalt at an angle and pulses. That's the warmest place, and the coldest place, at once. The cold air wrings itself out there fastest. The droplets form there fastest, run together fastest, fall fastest. The mineral bloom is thickest there - white and green and orange - except for one place about the size of my own hand where it has been scraped clean.

You would not find me at first.

You would look for a face and miss it, because my face is mostly mouth and nostril, pushed flat and broad like a catfish's, like an otter's, and my eyes don't shine. They are small, black, wet as snail shell, tucked under a brow of moss and blinking sideways with a clear membrane to wipe the condensation off. You would take me for a continuation of the wall. A bulge of basalt made darker by water, bearded with star moss and cold fern.

Because my back *is* the floor carried up.

If you waited until your molars ached with cold, until you stopped looking *for* something and started looking *at* the pace of it, you would see me breathe.

Not my own rhythm. The room's.

When the barrel-wide piston coughs up, I inflate half an inch. When it thuds down, I sigh. The belt turns, slips, screeches - and I lean. Just a little. A shift of bulk so slow it looks like the rock settling, until the tension catches again and the small turbine steadies in its whine. I don't know I'm tending. My tongue doesn't know it's tending your mouth.

Who would I be?

I would be the one who never stopped working because I never started.

Low and wide and not tall enough to have ever needed to stand. If I stood I'd be up to your chest, but I never stand. I sprawl, seal-boulder-peat, smoothed by water and steam. Under the living pelt - sphagnum and liverwort in that deep wet green that only clean cold water allows, that luminous carpet, and the black-green fern curled and frost-furred at its edges even while its roots sit in warm water - under that, where the spray hits hardest and parts the moss along my spine, my skin shows. Pale pink-white like the amphipods that boil under the ice sheets. Thin. A tracery of dark vessels underneath, and beneath that, the double thrum of the two pumps that are slightly out of phase, made flesh.

I have a forelimb that is not an arm. A spade. Broad, tipped with black keratin grown long and chipped like the fresh basalt. Every so often - on a time that is not time but pressure - I reach it out and draw it down the pipe. *Scrit.* A curl of calcite and iron falls into the marsh where the snails will find it. I sweep sphagnum away from the submerged pump's intake when it starts to chug unevenly, and the water gulps. I graze with a lower lip that is vast and pale and frilled, dragging it across the stone, across the pipe, across the rust-red and brilliant green film the heat and the iron feed. I leave behind the wide, clean trails you thought the snails made. The snails follow me. The buntings follow the snails. The fox comes to drink where I have been, because there the water stays open longest.

And along my lower flank, where my heat meets the marsh cold, there are pockets. Deep, warm, wet pouches in the moss-pelt. If you stare on the piston's rhythm long enough you see them move by themselves. A dozen, two dozen of my smaller selves - blind, pale, whiskered like oversized amphipods, like tadpoles - squirming out a hand's length to snap at midge larvae and then squirming back into me. They never go far. When I shift, they shift with me, a tide in my coat.

I am not old or young. I am as old as the pocket, which is to say a few hundred years - born when the sea left and the crust came up fast and split like a loaf and the warm water found the cold air for the first time. I may be the only one. There may be others under other brows of birch along this young coast, each with her own pipe, her own flywheel bearded on one side and stripped clean on the other. You won't find us by looking for us. You find the steam that shouldn't be there.

When the valve slaps open and vents a cloud that rolls along the floor and does not rise, I close my membrane-eyes and open my mouth and breathe it in. Condensation beads instantly on my whiskers, on the moss of my back, runs down my flanks, falls with the same soft *plip* as the rest of the weeping room. Cold does not mean dormant here. Cold is the engine. I eat the difference between hot and cold and make a life of it.

You don't notice me leaving because I never leave. When you haul yourself back up the sharp, wet basalt and your boots find thin black peat again, I am still down there, sighing with the piston. From the flat, there is nothing. Just peat and sideways willow and a wisp of steam like any other wisp of cold air.

Down there, the bead gathers at the tip of the moss on my back. Grows heavy. Falls.
