I don't know right away.

At first I think I would want to be high and fast - one of the interceptors. A hawk-thing with that translucent skin stretched between finger-bones, not feathers at all. To live in the empty nave where everyone else has to cross. To see for miles between the pillars and choose a line and stoop and make that sudden pocket of silence in the deafening chorus. It would be clean to be the thing the whole plateau watches for.

But I wouldn't. I am not clean. I am not that fast, or that solitary.

Then I think, slower, higher - the sloth-giant. Five tons of unhurried thought on a limb as thick as a house. To move inches per hour and have ferns grow from your own back. To prune the world by eating it and let the light fall differently where you have been, and not even know you did it. To carry whole ecologies in your fur and never come down. That feels closer. I like the idea of being a place more than a person.

But I am not that patient either. And I worry I would miss the ground.

For a while I think I would be ground itself. The moss. The peat-deep mattress that is not soil but memory - a thousand years of compressed lichen and root and shed leaf, a yard thick, sighing when you put your hand in it. To be stepped on, steadily, by everything, and to hold the water when the night condensation falls, that second rain that never stops dripping. To be the city under the city, the thrumming that you hear when you put your ear down. Mites moving through me like herds through a forest. Ants building their braided highways across my skin. Gold frogs the size of thumbnails boiling out of me at dusk to make the night shake. To never move and be full of motion. For a moment that feels exactly right.

Until I remember the air. The whole reason this place exists is the empty air between the Titans. The warm light falling straight down through a hundred yards of nothing. The pollen like dust, the seed-fluff always drifting, the moth clouds turning light opaque. If I were only ground I would never feel the glide.

So I have to be something that lives in both.

You would find me, then, not at the escarpment edge where the mist pours in white rivers, though I would go there when I needed to remember how big the table is. Not at the exact center either, where all the calling waves cross each other. You'd find me in a shallow dimple - one of those miles-wide bowls that lowlanders would still call a valley - between three Titans.

There's one in the middle that the others lean slightly toward, as if listening. Its buttress roots are walls, ten feet high, running a hundred yards out into the moss, making rooms. In the fork where two of those roots meet, about eight feet off the sponge, is a platform. Not built. Grown. Fern still alive, still needing to be trimmed every week or it will close over the doorway. Bark slabs, vine cordage swollen tight with age. A low roof that flowers red in late summer without permission. A gourd by the doorway, and an adze with a chipped iron head.

And above it - this is where I actually am, most of the day - a hundred and sixty feet up.

There is a limb-city there. Not a village, just what a single family can coax out of a Titan when they have time. A limb the width of a road runs horizontal for forty feet before it even starts to taper. From it hangs everything: the door-sized ferns, the barrel-bromeliads, the curtains of vine that never reach ground. We - I - have spent years there. Not cutting. Splicing. Tying a vine to itself until it thickens into a rail that will hold in eight years. Hollowing a bromeliad with a hot stone just enough that it lives and holds a gallon and a half of tea-colored water, wriggling with larvae that we strain through cloth. Learning the clefts where the moss on the limb itself makes a basin that keeps water for weeks after rain.

You would find me at noon when the cicadas take over and the Titans hum, on that limb in bark-cloth and a cape of insect silk that weighs nothing, checking the cisterns one by one with a dipper. Moving slowly, not because I am a sloth-giant, but because if you move fast up there you shake the moths and the birds leave and you don't eat that night. My knees know the exact give of that living wood.

Who would I be?

The keeper, I think. Not the hunter who follows the crimson flowering from Titan to Titan, or the child who is still learning how to shout loud enough to be heard at twenty feet over the day chorus. I'd be the one who stays. The Rememberer. The woman who knows which spiral carved deep at chest height means *this pocket fails in the dry months* and which one means *this branch fruits every ninth year, come back then*.

I am hoarse from shouting when shouting was needed, and whisper-soft now because night is coming. I have learned - finally - not to sing *over* the sound.

I thought for a long time I would need to be loud to exist here. But the place teaches you after a few days. That pulse. That ripple of calling that passes from the bone-white wall inward, bird to bird to cicada to frog, and comes back a few minutes later from the other side having gone all the way around the world. The vines all curling at once when the mist comes. The epiphytes flowering on signal, as if someone gave an order.

No one gave an order. The table gave it. The whole warm, sponge-held island breathing.

So you would find me at evening, when the light slants forever under the crowns and turns the whole colonnade gold, and the gliders start to launch - mothers with young clinging to their bellies, flaring silently from one hanging garden to the next. I would be standing on my limb-road, my gourd full, listening with my eyes closed until I felt the wave coming.

And I would sing, just slightly off from myself, just in time with it. Not to be heard. To be in time.

Part of the plant that learned how to remember where the water is, and how to make a ladder that stays alive.
