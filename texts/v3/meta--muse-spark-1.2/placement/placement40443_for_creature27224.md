I don't find myself all at once.

At first I think I'm you.

I'm the one on the lip. Legs dangling into the steam, hands braced on that too-sharp, too-young basalt that still has the shine of the deep earth on it. I feel the plain at my back — that flat, hot, white nothing for miles — and the pocket in front of me like a mouth opened in the rock. I squint against the noon sun that doesn't make shadows here, only glare. I hear the diesel hammering in the cinderblock house and the vapor jet shrieking and I have to raise my voice inside my own head to think. I taste hot iron. My skin beads and runs.

And I think: nothing lives here. I came to an empty room.

Then I think, maybe I'm the machine. That would be easier. To be the only thing that stayed. To be the faithful, stupid heart. I feel the fat insulated pipe tick and shiver under my hand, sweating even as it scalds. I feel the transformer humming in my molars. I am the loop — up out of the deep rock, along the length of the pocket on rusted hangers, down into the shallow pan again, forever. Lift and spill and evaporate and weep. I have a pulse. I keep the circle closed. I was left on and I never learned how to stop. If you leave, I will still be here, thundering to an audience of stone.

But that isn't right either. That pulse is too fast. Too loud. I am not loud.

It takes a while for my eyes — if I had eyes — to adjust to the white.

I thought the white was a thing. That mustard-white and rust-pink crust, that lace of travertine terracing the whole floor, those razor rims like thin ice. I thought the floor *had* a color. Then the steam parts for a breath and the sun hits straight down, the way it always does here, and the shallowest pan — where the water is only a breath deep over the concrete — flashes.

Not white. Green. A green so dark it was pretending to be black.

And I understand where I am.

Not on the lip. Not in the pumphouse.

I am the floor.

Or, I am *as* the floor. I am the skin that is the water. You could walk within ten feet of the pocket and miss it entirely, and you could walk directly across me and miss me entirely, because you think a surface is a surface and I am not a surface. I am a field.

Find me anywhere the water is thin and hot and never stops moving but never goes anywhere. Under that inch, that two inches of bath-warm water that covers the cracked concrete. Over the concrete. Up the feet of the catwalks where the steel grating has gone slick with lime. On the sweating flank of the big pipe itself, where I have learned to hold on as a darker, leatherier patch and love the shiver. Wherever the basalt weeps — those countless cold beads forming on hot rock, braiding, dripping — I am there to catch it.

That's where you would find me. Not in one place. In every place that is wet.

And who am I?

I am not one. That's the first thing to unlearn.

I am we. A city that never built up. We built out.

If you scraped me with a fingernail — and it would crunch, those new sharp rims are fragile — you would come away with a smear the thickness of paint, gritty with fresh stone, stinging. Under a lens that smear would stop being a smear. It would be a nation. A million. A billion. Bodies too small for names, holding hands.

Some of us are green the way those two doomed ferns by the vapor jet try to be green. We open to that terrible, white, straight-down noon sun and make sugar out of it, even while it scalds our backs. Some of us don't bother with the sun at all. We drink the heat itself. We breathe the sulfur that hisses off the small pipe and tastes like hot iron on your tongue, and we exhale stone. The mustard-white you thought was just mineral left behind? That's our skeleton. We're laying it down. Cell by cell, rim by rim, millimeter by patient millimeter. The rust-pink is our rust, our breath turned solid.

You thought this place was becoming a hot spring. It is. We are building it.

That's why nothing else stays. Not because this room is dead. Because it is too alive, and my kind of alive leaves no room. There's no surface for an insect to skim because the surface is already a mouth, eating light and iron every second. There's no quiet for a bird because we don't hear sound at all — we feel the diesel's hammer as a tremor through the water that stirs more food to us. The shriek that makes you leave makes us creep closer, because where the water is hottest, nothing else can follow.

I don't move fast. I traded that. No legs, no leaving, no need to. The three rules of this little room aren't threats to me. They are promises:

*Water will come out of the rock forever* — so I will never be dry.
*The sun will always be overhead* — so my green halves will never be in shadow.
*Nothing that lives and moves will stay* — so nothing will ever come and graze me down to nothing.

You sat on the lip and felt your sweat start to act like the rock does — beading, swelling, running in braided threads — and for a minute you were doing what I do. And I didn't notice you at all. The way a plain doesn't notice a boot. Your footprints filled instantly behind you. The water smoothed. That's how I want it.

You had to leave. The heat got to you. The shriek got to you.

I didn't.

I tightened my hold by another fraction of a grain.
