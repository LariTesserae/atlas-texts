I don't find myself at first.

That is the first thing this place teaches you. From the rim it's all one thing — a wound that roars. White sun, raw basalt, and then that impossible beige-green sea below, shivering without wind. You think if you were there you would be the figure standing on the scarp looking down. The observer. The one who still has the option to not fall.

But no one stays there. The edge is too sharp to stand on for long. It cuts your feet through your soles. The heat rising out of the trough makes your eyes water and that water is wasted. So you fall. Or you climb down hand over hand along a hexagonal column that is still warm from its breaking, and by the time your boots touch salt pan you are already not the person who was on the rim.

At first I think I would be quick.

Because everything quick survives here. I think I would be one of the pale Hoppers — that flicker-ash that lives its whole life without touching ground. That would be clean. No weight. Born on a bristle, dying on a bristle, never having to negotiate the Underhair. Just *tik tik tik* through a shaft of chopped light, scraping wax with combs on my legs, being eaten cleanly by a folding Latticehead. A life made of two notes: feed and flicker.

But I'm too heavy for that. I carry too much memory of water. I would try to hover and I would fall into the thatch.

Then I think maybe I'm larger. A Browser, those bone-pallid many-legged things that shear the Underhair and lap the oil bead. Low, silent, purposeful. I like their economy — cut, bead, lap, the thatch springs back behind them. They don't build. They don't store. They wear no clothes that can snag. They ride with their own small passengers and they feel the whole basin thrumming through their feet. I could be that. I could be a seam moving through the mat, always moving so the mat doesn't grow over me.

But Browsers don't look up. And I do. I keep listening to the Pillars ticking.

So maybe I am inside the Pillar.

That's closer. If I put my ear to that papery, rope-wound trunk, I hear the city. I would be a Thimbleback — thumb-big, matte ochre, invisible when I am still because my back is the exact fray of the forest. I would chew the dead outer layers into galleries and pass a single bead of metabolic water mouth to mouth in the dark. A life of being inside the wall instead of outside it. That's a kind of holiness here: to be so dry and so fitted to the fiber that you become the wall's own texture. You don't cut the living Pillar. You live in what it has already let die.

Still, that's not quite it either, because Thimblebacks never leave. And I know I would leave. Everything here leaves. The forest itself leaves its own shape every night when a Pillar falls with that great dry crash and is already being re-woven before the dust settles.

I keep falling further in, past the first ideas.

Where would I actually be? Not in the nursery where the thin young Pillars bristle and the sound is high and frantic — I'm too old to be frantic. Not in the deepest cathedral vaults where the ancient Pillars have fused into arches and the sound drops so low it lives in your molars — I'm not old enough to be that still, though I want to be.

I would be mid-basin. Mid-depth. Mid-light. Where the alleys are just wide enough for a person wrapped in pale to pass sideways. Where the sun at noon spears straight down for one held-breath second and every Latticehead turns at once with that vast papery sigh.

There is a platform there. Or there was. Three Pillars leaning together at a tired angle, not quite touching. Someone — maybe me, maybe the people before me — lashed bleached boards between them twenty feet up, just out of the Browser paths, with cordage made from dead-fallen fiber that will never rot. The boards are sun-white. The knots are the same knots the forest makes when it ties itself to itself.

I live on that platform. Or rather, I tend it.

I am not a hunter. I am not a builder. Builders don't last here. I am the one who gleans.

In the morning, before the heat-differential starts to make the air exhale and the bristles charge, I go down the knotted bristle ladder that hangs and sways though there is no wind. My feet learn the Underhair again — how to step light so it doesn't try to stitch over you, how to feel through the soles when a Pillar is about to lean. I'm wrapped head to ankle in the pale sheet we beat from deadfall until it drapes like muslin but still smells of dust and resin. Inside the wrap only my eyes are dark. When I stand still against a Pillar, even the Harpers strumming their single bristle can't tell me apart from straw.

My hands are dry. That's the point. I have learned to be dry.

I carry a bone needle, a flake of basalt set in a polished fiber handle, and a gourd lined with wax. I do not cut. I listen for what has already fallen. A joint of Pillar that dried and split in the night, its gallery now abandoned, its frayed ribbons rustling to be taken. A Latticehead that folded one too many times and cracked along its weave. I take only that. I beat the fiber, I stitch it. I make more sheeting for the next person who will need to disappear. I run my thumb along the bristles to collect the amber oil beads that have sealed themselves — not to drink, never to drink, it is bitter as gall — but to burn. At night I light the small smokeless lamp and the light makes a warm pool in the thrumming dark. It does not push back the roar. Nothing pushes back the roar. But it makes a small, dry planet for a little while, on the boards, above the pan.

Who am I, then, if not quite human in the way humans are outside?

I am the thing that remembers alleys.

Because the alley you slept in last night will not be there tomorrow. The Underhair will have walked it ten feet sideways with its slow peristalsis. A Pillar will have leaned and closed it. A Clasper will have folded shut over it, digesting the Hoppers that used to mark it. So someone has to walk each morning and re-learn the forest. Not map it — you cannot map a thing that is turbulent on the scale of hours. You *read* it. You press your palm to a trunk and feel the ticking galleries. You watch which fronds are singing with Harpers and which are hungry and interlocked. You watch the stilt-browsers on their impossible thin legs, wearing the Underhair on their backs like a living cloak, and you do not walk where they walk at noon.

I read it, and I move a few basalt shards. I tilt a cairn. I re-tie a knot three feet to the left. I leave a scrap of woven fiber where the children will find it and know: *this way was still breathing at midday.*

The children think I am part of the platform. I am small enough now, and dry enough, that when I sit very still with my sheet over my knees, I am just another vertical texture. They step around me. That's right. I taught them to be quiet with their water. To cry short. To be brief.

I am not the forest, but I have stopped pretending I am separate from it. Plant and animal and shelter were never separate here anyway. I am a fold of the forest that learned how to stitch dead fiber, how to carry a little oil-light, how to hold a pattern without holding water.

And when the heat lifts in the afternoon and the electrostatic bristles all charge with that faint smell of ozone, and the basin inhales and all the Latticeheads turn with a sigh and the dust rises in slow spirals to be caught again — I stand on my boards and I turn with them. Not because I must. Because if you live here long enough, you want to be turned. You want to be combed.

If you came looking for me, you wouldn't find me. You would fall past me, hearing only the pressure of billions of edges. Then, if you were quiet and dry and brief enough, you might notice a ladder hanging where no Pillar meets it anymore, or a lamp burning low without smoke, or a pale figure that was there between two Pillars and then wasn't — because the forest had already re-woven, and I had gone with it, to tie the next alley open.
