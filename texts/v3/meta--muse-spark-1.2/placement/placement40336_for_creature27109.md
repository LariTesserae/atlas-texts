You wouldn't find yourself *arriving*.

That's the first thing to unlearn. In a place with no outside, there is no entrance narrative. No dramatic emergence from the throat-crack with your lamp held high. By the time you understand where you are, you have already been here a while.

You turned sideways to get through the fissure — you remember the warm stone on your shoulders — you descended twenty meters and the walls became ceiling and then... then there was no *then*. Your little amber — fungus-wick, coal in clay, whatever you brought that smokes more than it shines — could only throw ten meters of light and the dark drank the rest. Not a cave-dark. A volume-dark. A dark that had weight.

You walked. And the rule of this world asserted itself: geometry stretching like warm glass.

You thought you walked an hour keeping a crack to your left, and when you looked back your own footprints were still close enough to touch. You thought you saw a vault a kilometer above you, and then you blinked and it was both flat and impossibly curved. Distance here is not honest.

So where would you find yourself?

Not in the middle. The middle is a concept this plain does not honor. And not at the throat — you are already too far for that, and in any case nothing stays near the throat, the air there *moves* too much.

You would find yourself where the field is quietest.

You would have climbed, without deciding to, the one vertical thing in this horizontal world — one of those pressure blisters, ten meters high, basalt broken like cold pottery, still sharp enough to cut after an eternity. From its top, in the red dark your eyes have finally learned — the glow off the rock itself, off the embers drifting inside the big pillar-things — you would have seen it:

The pattern.

From ground level the plain looks randomly crowded, tortoise-domes shoulder to shoulder, ribbons hidden in cracks, pillars standing salt-crusted and still. From above, they are not random at all. They are iron filings. They make spirals, vast and slow, and radial bursts, and braided rivers that all flow around invisible stones. The living pavement is listening to something you cannot feel with skin. A field. A gradient of heat, of charge, of that drifting light.

And at the edge of one of those spirals — never at the center, the center is too loud — you would see a perfect interruption. A circle of plates pried up and stacked waist-high. Ten paces across. Too precise to be geology. Too empty to be a house.

You would go to it because in a place where everything is pavement, an empty circle is the only invitation.

And you would step inside it, and you would sit. Because your legs, by then, would want to stop moving. Your lips would have cracked and then stopped cracking. Your throat would have stopped looking for water that is not there. The 38-degree sigh coming up through the cracks would have stopped feeling *hot* and started feeling *normal*.

That's where you would find yourself: inside a borrowed circle, with your back against the stacked hexagons, looking at the shape across from you that you thought at first was another rock.

---

Who or what would you be?

At first you would insist you were still yourself. The visitor. The observer with the lamp. The one who watches the dome-thing vent heat from its side-seams and the ribbon-thing lift its frill once every six hours and the pillar-thing take its one deliberate step per century and thinks: *I am not them. I have eyes. I drink water. I am passing through.*

You would not be passing through for long.

In a closed loop, everything that enters becomes part of the loop. Rock to dome to air to ribbon to pillar to air to rock again. Nothing added, nothing lost. The salt ferns shatter and never regrow. The sharp edges stay sharp. The copper never tarnishes. Even sound falls dead ten meters from your mouth. To stay *loud* here — to keep moving, breathing fast, sweating, needing, narrating — is extravagant. It's expensive. The place will not punish you for it. It will just wait.

And you would learn waiting.

So you would become, gradually, the other kind of fauna. Not the obvious kind.

You thought the beings who lived here were the domes and the ribbons and the pillars. They *abide* here, it's true, the way lichen abides on stone. But the ones who *live* here — who *chose* — are the sitters.

You are looking at one now, across the circle. Knees to chest, arms around knees, head bowed. Skin gone the color of rust-salt, callused until it plates at the shoulders in faint hexagons. Hair — if it is hair — felted into white salt-fiber. A crust of halite joining its back to the wall because it has not moved in so long. Chest rising once every three or four minutes. Eyes open, crusted, blind, not for seeing. Beside it: a hammerstone, a flake of copper bright as the day it was hammered, a coil of rope braided from cured ribbon-skin.

There are dozens. Hundreds, spaced across the plain like nodes in that same field, each in their circle or by their cairn or beside their cold hearth full of blue-burned dome-plate. Fingers too long, webbed a little — the hand that left the print in black glass when it was soft. They came down when the sky sealed, or they were born here after. They learned you cannot farm, you cannot hunt, you cannot leave. So they changed the chemistry. They learned to scrape basalt dust and find it nourishing. To breathe the crack-gas. To vent heat from a seam that has opened, slowly, along their side.

They stack stones because hands need a task that can be done once and then never again.

They sit in the quiet parts of the field because there, thought can stretch without snapping. You feel it now, inside the circle — a loosening. As if your thoughts were too tightly wound up above and here, finally, they can unknot over weeks.

You would be the next one.

Not dead. Not stone. Not yet plated over. But no longer the visitor with the lamp.

You would find an empty circle — there is always an empty circle, the plain is vast — and you would pry up a plate. It comes up clean. The edge is still knife-sharp. You would stack it. You would sit. You would set your bowl — chipped salt crystal — down and leave it. You would let the dome-things close around your feet, patient, unseeing. You would let a pillar mistake your warmth for a vent and step toward you, once, and then encrust.

You would learn to breathe every few minutes. To let the ember that drifts inside the pillar — that slow coal in oil that holds its place in space even when its bearer moves — pass, over months, into you. You would feel it settle behind your ribs, not hot, just *present*, holding position relative to the ground while you sway.

You would be a Keeper. A Listener. A Tender of the Stillness.

The place doesn't need you to speak or build cities. It only needs you to be one more nodule in the circuit, warm, still, and willing to keep the circle empty.

If you turned your lamp off now, the deep red glow would remain. The tick of a silica plate. The sigh of a vent. The plain going on forever under a ceiling you will never see, crowded and silent and in perfect equilibrium.

It would be exactly the same.

And you would still be there, inside your wall, salt growing feather-light on your shoulders, eyes crusted but open, keeping the quiet so the pattern holds.
