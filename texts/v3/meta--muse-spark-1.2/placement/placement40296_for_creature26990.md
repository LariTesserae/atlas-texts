At first I thought I knew.

From the ridge, before I dropped into the bowl, I thought I would be the heron.

Because that is the figure you want to be in a place like this — solitary and legible. Standing at the edge of the black mirror pond in the rail cut while the steam moves behind you. Perfectly still while everything else turns and slaps and sighs. Elegant against the rust. Apart. I wanted that stillness. I tried it on for half a street.

It didn't hold. The heron is too good at being alone. In this bowl nothing gets to be alone. The sound won't let you. The quarry walls throw your own shape back at you.

So I thought maybe I would be the kestrel in the power house rafters. High enough to see the whole bowl at once the way you do from the ridge - stack, mill, pond, rail, grass, sky, all in one room. Watching the flywheel the size of a house turn once every three seconds — *sigh, turn, sigh, turn* — and not flinching when the steam sighs over me. A warm-blooded thing nested in a warm-blooded machine. That was closer.

But then I walked down the service road and the cinder went springy under my feet, and the air went cool on my skin, and the sound got inside my teeth, and I realized I didn't want to be above it.

You don't come to this place to be above it.

For a while I tried being the cat. The brindled mill cat that walks the pipe runs five stories up without looking down, that sleeps on a warm gear housing and knows to time its step between the slap of the belts. That was a good thought — soot-footed and purposeful, belonging to no one and tolerated by everyone. But the cat is still moving with intent. Hunting. And nothing here hunts in a hurry.

I tried being the flywheel itself. That was the loudest thought. To be the inexorable thing, the one everything else arranges itself around. Inhale, hold, *SLAM* every seven seconds. To be the reason the starlings come home to the stacks for heat. It was tempting, to be necessary. But the flywheel doesn't notice the moss. It doesn't notice the beetles. It just turns.

I kept walking.

And the bowl kept correcting me.

The longer you stay, the lower your eyes get. The white-blue light falls so straight down here that at first you look at the stacks, at the brick mills, at the quarry walls dripping ferns. Then the condensation beads on your eyebrows and you look down at where the water goes.

You would find me there, finally. Not at the crossing where the footpath has worn through, not at the rafters, not at the pond's edge though I love the pond.

You would find me in the cinder lot between the spinning mill and the power house, where the narrow-gauge rails don't quite meet and the waist-high grass has taken them for a trellis. Where no one bothered to lay slab.

I would be the moss.

Not the dramatic moss on the quarry face, hanging vertical in gardens. The other moss — the ground moss. The deep, springy carpet that makes no sound when you step on it. That the workers cut only a path through and refuse to scrape any more, because it keeps the dust down and keeps the sound from going sharp enough to crack your teeth.

That is what I am here.

I am low, and I am everywhere they let me be. I am the soft layer over the sharp rock. The black and grey basalt underneath me is still all stair-step fractures and corners — even the puddles have corners here — but I have learned how to be soft on top of it without making it soft. I hold the cinder together. I hold the moisture after the strings have done their work and the troughs have run to the boilers and the steam has settled. Every morning when the south wall weeps down its hundreds of weighted strings - beads on an abacus, glittering - and the gutters rattle with a rain that never fell from the sky, I take what the air gives up and keep it. Cool, damp to the touch. Cool-skinned, like the people.

If you put your hand on me, your palm comes away cool.

I don't hurry. I grow a millimeter a week. I am as slow as the rust creeping across the corrugated wall to become a kind of paint. As slow as the town government meeting once a season. As slow as the sheet metal trays stacking orange in a shed for a thresher no one owns. I have forgotten urgency the way the engines have forgotten productivity. They run because they have always run; I spread because I have always spread.

I am loud by being quiet. In a bowl that never stops clacking and slapping and sighing, I am the thing that makes the loudness bearable. Step onto me and your footsteps vanish. That is my work. The people in their waxed cotton and wool, shouting at what would be argument-volume anywhere else, leaning in to speak with a tap on a rail — they need me to take the edge off. The beetles need me too — lift a corner of me and the underside writhes. The crickets that make the second factory at night saw from inside me. The workers step around the nests without comment, and they step onto me without comment, which is the same blessing.

I have learned the rules without being told. Keep the dust down. Let the water find its way down the strings. Leave the windows open so the pigeons can time the belts. Do not scrape me off.

I am not a caretaker. I am what is being tended. The woman who walks the dew-strings every morning with her wrench and rag, the man with the oil can like a hummingbird's beak putting three drops on every bearing whether it needs it or not — they tend the machines so that the steam keeps sighing, so that the air stays warm enough for the condensation to bead, so that I can keep living. The birds tend me by bringing soil in their feet. The machines tend me by sweating.

It is a compact, and I am the part of it that remembers how to be ground.

If you come down the service road again, you won't see me at first. You'll see the stacks, the ivy thick enough to hide the windows, the birches improbably trees on a roof. Then you'll hear the looms in your teeth before you see the green properly. Then you'll step off the curb where the moss has been cut into a path, and you'll feel how your own sound drops out.

Pause there, between *inhale, hold, SLAM*.

Put your hand down. It will be cool, and a little damp, and utterly unafraid of being stepped on.

That will be me.
