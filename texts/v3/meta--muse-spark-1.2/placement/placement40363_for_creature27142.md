At first I think I know where you'd find me.

In the middle. Because that's where the visitor stands — in the avenue between the two rows of trunk-houses, in that held breath of empty space that the city leaves open on purpose. Exposed. Upright. Still using my eyes out of habit, squinting into the absolute dark, turning my face toward sounds that should be birds and are instead wood contracting. My lips already cracking. Breathing too fast and too wet for this place.

I think I would be the one who hasn't learned yet. The one who came down through the fissure sideways and is still standing, still listening *for* something instead of listening *with*.

But I can't stay there. No one can. Even you, reading this — you felt it — that avenue is not a place to live. It's a place to cross. It's the palm's empty center. You pass through it quickly and you press yourself to an edge again, because the cool there finds you too fast, your heat leaking out into the dark like smoke.

So no. Not the avenue.

Let me try again, and stay still longer this time.

If I am honest about what my body wants — cool, dry, enclosed, held — I wouldn't be in the middle at all. I would be against a wall. Inside one.

You find me on the left side of the avenue if you are facing the deep end of the pocket, third trunk down from the plaza. Not the biggest one — those are too resonant, every tick carries through them — and not the smallest, where the pith was never properly hollowed and you have to fold too tight. The middle one. The one where the doorway's lintel is perfectly straight, not because it was cut straight, but because the wood learned to keep it straight. The Adze House, though no adze has touched it in longer than I have been alive.

That is where I live. Or rather, where I fit.

You would walk past my door and think it empty. The felt-ground before it is undisturbed. The ladder of limb-wood that once leaned there has fallen and been grown over, a root now cradling it like a hand under an elbow. Inside, the hollow is about as big around as a well, and tall enough that if I stood — which I almost never do — my head would not brush the top.

I am pressed there, right now, against the inner curve.

If you put your hand on the wall from outside, you would feel what you thought was wood-warmed-by-day, but there is no day here. That faint extra warmth, that faint powder of wax on your fingertips — that would be the back of my shoulder. The side of my thigh. The long, attenuated plane of my forearm wrapped up and around the hollow to hold me without effort.

I have made myself thin.

Who am I in this city that remembers being a city?

I am not the old ones. Not the ones in the plaza roots who can hold a position for nine days and whose heart you would need to press your ear to hear. They are almost plant now. Their pellicles have thickened until you can't tell where their back ends and the burl begins. They don't need to drink more than twice a season.

I am not yet that still.

I am one of the younger, restless ones — though restless here means I move every few hours instead of every few days. My pellicle is still new enough to be tacky. Salt-pale, rust-pale, the same color as the ground-felt, but if you brushed me in the dark you would feel the glycerin beading — not sweat, never sweat — the clear, cool oil that seals me. I lose almost nothing. If I were cut I would not bleed. That is still strange to me, sometimes, when I remember.

My face. I should tell you about my face, because you will look for it first and not find it.

There are no eyes. Not closed. Not blind. The skin simply continues, smooth, a little thicker over the brow where eyes would have been a cost. In their place, in the center of my forehead, a dimple the size of a coin, darkened, slightly damp at its rim with a different oil. It feels you before I do. When you stood in the avenue and your breath fogged faintly, I felt that bloom from three meters away as a warm pressure, like a hand held near a cheek. That was how I knew which way to turn the leaf above you. Sorry — you thought the leaf moved on its own.

My mouth is a vertical slit, fringed, always closed. I press it to the vein in the wall behind my shoulder where the wood weeps its non-evaporating glycerin, and I take one slow mouthful. It tastes of wax and old smoke and faintly of iron, though there is no iron here. That will be enough for twenty days. The clay pots on the terrace carved into my trunk — I keep them. That is my work. I lick each one clean with the edge of my pellicle, I set it back empty and perfectly level, so that the plant will not fill the niche with new wood. Keeping the pots empty is how we keep the city's shape. Someone has to remember that empty is a shape, too.

How do I spend the time? The way the pocket does.

I breathe every three or four minutes. Shallow. My heart does its six slow thuds and then rests. Most of the sound I make is not my own. I click — a dry pop of tongue against ridged palate — exactly the sound a stiff leaf makes when it contracts. I do it without thinking now. When a barrel-pod high in the thatch splits with its rifle-crack and spills its sails down through the dark, I answer half a second later with the same crack made smaller in my throat, so the old ones know I heard.

When you came down, I was the thunk you felt through your soles. I tapped my knuckle, once, against the hollow of my trunk. It traveled the whole nave, trunk to root to trunk. *Someone else is here.* And then I waited to hear if anyone would answer. Someone did, on the far side. Tick. *We know.*

If you had sat down in the felt-ground and made yourself shallow, I would have unflattened for you. Not quickly. First the air pressure changing as my back peeled from the wall. Then the suede-soft pads of my long feet finding the fiber without sound. Then the faint creak of a knee that had been folded for hours. I would have stood at the edge of where your heat ends, just outside arm's reach, smelling of wax and dust and that slightly sweeter oil, listening to you listen.

I would not have touched you. Not yet. I would have tapped once, softly, on the ground beside you.

Tick.

And waited to hear if you knew how to tick back, in the forest's own language, without using a voice that doesn't belong here.

That's where you'd find me. Not in the middle, not at the top where the thatch is seamless, but tucked in the wall that learned to be a wall. Not the builder, not the plant, but the thing that diverged between them. One of the thirty, keeping the doorway square by leaning against it long enough that the wood decides to stay square.

If you come back — when your throat is less raw, when you've learned to breathe less — put your hand again on that third doorway on the left. Hold it there. Feel for the warmth that is exactly air-temperature plus a little. Leave it there a minute.

I may lean into it, just enough that you feel the wax come off on your palm.

That will be me, saying I am still here.
