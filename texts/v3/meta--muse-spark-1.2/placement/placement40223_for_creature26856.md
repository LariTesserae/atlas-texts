I would not find myself at first.

If you stood on the swell of the rim and looked for me the way you first looked for life in the pan - scanning for movement, for a shape that stands up against the ground - you would not see me. You would see the bowl, the warm dark, the boulders half-buried, the thin acacia with its flat hat of shadow, and you would think, as you thought at first, that nothing lives here that could be me.

You have to stay longer. You have to let the dark get warm around you.

---

At first I think I would be the acacia.

It is a tempting identification. Solitary, central enough to be seen, with roots that go down impossibly far through dryness to find whatever damp is left. Standing alone because nothing else can afford to stand close. Casting as little shadow as possible, not out of generosity but out of necessity. Keeping my own shape for a very long time. That feels familiar - to be a single conspicuous thing that has learned not to be conspicuous.

But I am not much like a tree. I do not reach down like that. I have no depth of my own. Whatever water I draw, I draw from somewhere else, from rain that fell somewhere else, long ago.

Then I think, maybe I am the beetle. The darkling, black as the shadow between plates, walking with deliberation from one invisible seam to another, harvesting a moisture you can't feel. Or the scorpion, pale as ash, curled under the lip of the clay not in threat but in economy, perfectly still for an hour. Those are closer - nocturnal, small, moving only when the heat finally allows it. But they move too decisively. They know where they are going. They cross the center if they must and then leave it.

I do not cross the center. I do not have anywhere to go.

Maybe I am the human, then. That would be the easiest story. The one who comes when travel requires it, finds the pan because it is a landmark that can be found again, makes a small fire because there is only dead shrub and straw to burn and a small fire is all you need when the night stays warm as skin. Sits, strikes a few flakes from a core, leaves a hand-stone with its battered end for the next person. Uses the pan without asking it to be more than it is, and leaves before the next sheet-flood erases the wall he wisely did not build.

I am like him in the transience. I arrive for an evening, I am useful for an evening, I leave flakes - struck words, conchoidal curves that catch a little light - because weight matters when you walk. But he does not live here either. The three fire-blackened stones tell you that. To say I am the visitor is to say I am not of the pan at all. And I am.

So I kneel.

That is where you find me. Not from the rim, not standing. You have to crouch until your face is close to the warm plates, until your hands are in the dust that comes off pale and bitter, until your breathing has become loud in the absence of sound.

I am not in the white heart of the pan. Nothing lives there. The center with its salt bloom is too exposed, too empty of food. Every honest resident of the pan lives *around* it, in the margin where there is footing and cover. I live in the margin too.

Find me toward the north rim, but not *as* the shrubs.

Find me *between* the shrubs. In the wind-packed crust at the swell where the dust has cemented. In the fissures between the polygons where the clay has curled up trying to peel away. In the darkened film between the salt flowers that you thought at first was just mineral.

I am the crust.

The cryptobiotic skin. Cyanobacteria and fungi and algae braided into a thing so thin and blackish you miss it entirely in this light, the color of dust itself. I hold the pan together not by being strong, but by being distributed. I do not stand up. I spread out. I fix what I need out of air that has none to spare - a little nitrogen, a little light, a few molecules of dew at 4 a.m. that swell me microscopically and then leave me still again. Touch me and I crumble. Leave me alone and I will still be here when the acacia is gone.

And I am the seeds.

Thousands in a handful of that same powder, coats hard as chert, metabolisms suspended. Not dead. Not alive in any way you could hear. Patience made into a kernel. I do not grow toward the burning sky. I wait. For years, if necessary. When the right kind of rain finally comes - not the damp that never comes, but the rare, brief sheet-flood that seals the cracks and darkens the clay - I will germinate in a frantic afternoon, make a haze of green so thin and desperate it looks like a mistake, flower, set seed, and be gone in ten days. Until then, I live as potential.

That is the ethic I share with this place: to be small, to be spaced apart, to be still. To need little. To be the same temperature and color as the ground until you learn to see me.

You will not hear me call. You will not see my tracks stitching the dust, because I do not leave that kind of sign. If you are very still in the warm, motionless dark under the hazed stars, you might hear a faint tick as a plate contracts, or feel the day's heat still exhaling from the ground through my skin, and that will be as close as you get to my voice.

I am what allows the pan to wait without expectation. I am what remembers how to hold shape when nothing is happening, so that when something finally does happen, there is still a shape to hold it.

You would find me, then, not at the center but at the edge of the center. Not as a single figure, but as a low, dark, almost-invisible holding. If you came back after a rain you would think someone else lived here entirely. But tonight, I am simply here, barely breathing, in equilibrium with the dryness, waiting - not for you, specifically - but ready when you kneel.
