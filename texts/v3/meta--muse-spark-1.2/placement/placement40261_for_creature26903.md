I wouldn't find myself at the top.

Not first, anyway. I thought I would — peering over the lip, with the white sky at my back and the hot scrub buzzing. The careful visitor, one hand on a root-ladder, deciding whether to go down. But that isn't where I settle when I let the place settle around me.

If I stay long enough to really arrive, I'm already below the wind-line. Below where the cicadas cut off. I'm already where the air goes heavy and held, where dust hangs still in the one column of light that moves.

For a while I think I'm smaller. That's the trick of the density — you think you're one of the small, busy things.

For a while I think I'm the condensation itself. A bead forming on the cool underside of an elephant-ear, swelling until it can't hold, falling *tik* into a stone bowl the size of a palm. Or I'm the frog on the rim of that bowl, translucent belly, throat pulsing without a sound, thinking a leaf is a leaf.

Then I think I'm the spider. Between two hanging roots that never touch ground, repairing a web that no wind will ever shake. Utterly silent, appearing to make silk out of nothing. Or I'm the sheet of moths on the wall that looks so exactly like moss that even I believe it for a second.

But none of those things stay still long enough.

They move. They flicker, they sway, they repair. I find myself wanting to stop. I find myself wanting to be the thing that doesn't have to.

So I sink.

Not down — *in*. Further down than the floor. To the place where everything falls *toward*. Where gravity is the wind.

You would find me at the foot of the north wall. Not against it — *in* it. In the shallow trough where two rock basins meet and never quite dry. The place where the limestone sweats heaviest and the water runs down in a black seam. Where the fig roots braid too thick to move and the air is sweet and metallic with rot that isn't quite rot.

I am low. I am long. I am folded.

At first I still think I'm stone. That's how long I've been there. My back is honey-amber darkened to tea-brown, pitted and fluted like the wall itself, and every dimple holds a bead of water the way the stone bowls do. The liverwort has found me. The moss has found me. Small ferns have taken hold along the ridge of me and I let them, because in a place where light is rationed, you don't turn anything away that knows how to eat it.

If you crouched by the center pool and looked for me, you would look right at me for a full minute and not see me. You would think the wall had grown a lip. A slightly greener, slightly deeper pelt that rises —

— holds —

— falls.

So slowly you think you imagined it. Ten seconds to crest. That wet, faint sigh under the *tik... tik...* of the drips. That's me breathing, but not with lungs. Lungs would dry me from the inside out in this hot, held heat. I breathe through my skin. Through the whole vast, sponged back and through the loose, pale apron of throat that lies spread out in front of me like a damp towel, cupped against the stone.

I don't have eyes anymore, not really. I have those two smooth places high on my blunt head where the skin went translucent, gold-flecked discs looking inward. I retired them generations ago. Sight is a rumor here. It was a bad trade — light is rare and vibration is constant. A frog shifting its weight on a leaf two feet away tells me more than any eye could.

And so I wait the way the rock waits.

A drop forms on the wall above me. Runs down the grooves in my flanks — the same fluting as the limestone, because I learned the stone's shape and copied it — and pools in the throat-basin. Another drop. Another. I drink without moving. I drink by being the place water goes.

The whole sink feeds me without meaning to. A dead beetle slides off an elephant-ear. Bat droppings. A fig leaf, a tadpole that wiggled out of the wrong basin. It all slides down the leaning fronds and the hanging roots and comes to rest in this trough, in this compost that is half leaf-rot and half me. I don't lunge. Lunging is noisy and expensive and would scare the rest of the meal away.

I just open. The seam at the front of my head — not a mouth the way you think of a mouth, just a soft pucker — dimples inward. The air right in front of me goes tight for a fraction of a second. You'd only know because a mote of dust jerks sideways against all stillness. And then the skink that flicked too close is simply not there anymore. My throat ripples, slow. Later I nudge a small dark pellet out the side — chitin and tiny bone — and leave it to melt back into the floor. Nothing is wasted because there is nowhere for waste to go.

I remember the people, but not as people. As weight and warmth and a different kind of drip.

They cut shallow dishes for hands in the rock above and stacked a low wall between their packed-earth floor and me. I broke it, not from anger. I just kept being wet and the fig that grew through me kept being a fig and the wall was in the way of both of us. They left smooth river pebbles in the niche on the east wall. I felt them as a coolness that stayed cool longer than stone does. An offering, or a bargain: *stay there.* I did. When the rains failed one year, they left. I didn't. I have no reason to leave the sweat-line.

In the palm-sized basins scattered around me there are strings of clear jelly, each bead holding a comma with red lace gills. Some will make it. Most won't. When their pool dries they will crawl following the wet seam down toward me, and the smallest will dry where they are and become part of what feeds the survivor. I don't guard them. I *am* the guard, by keeping the wall wet, by keeping the fungus trimmed back with my grazing mouth so the whole nursery doesn't rot at once.

If you stay until evening you would see the one thing I still do that belongs to the world above.

For most of the day the light is a hard white bar that never touches me. Then, for about ninety seconds before it cuts off at the rim, it slides up the north wall and falls directly onto my back. My covered eyes do nothing. But the ferns along my spine — which are not passengers, exactly — angle sharply and tremble. The algae living *in* my skin greens visibly, greedily. I eat the light the way a leaf does. Just a little. Just enough that in the long months when fewer beetles fall, I don't have to move at all.

Then it's gone. The heat stays. The rock keeps sweating. *Tik.*

Somewhere under the elephant-ears a tree frog finally dares its glassy *peep... peep...* and doesn't know the ripple under its feet is not moss.

From above, I'm invisible again. Just another darker patch of trees in the pale scrub. Down inside, I'm what the wound grew over itself to keep from drying out. Not a creature in a place, but a place that learned to keep a creature as its skin.

You would find me exactly where everything else ends up — at the bottom, in the wet, breathing very slowly, with a throat full of gathered rain that never fell as rain, waiting longer than you can stand to watch, and not minding at all.
