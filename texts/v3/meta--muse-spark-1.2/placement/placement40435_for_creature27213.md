You wouldn't find me right away.

At first you would look for me in the wrong places - because in a city this wet and loud and empty, you look for people where people cluster. At a market, at a gate, at the railing where the squared-off falls throw their white fury through iron throats. You'd look there and see only the work itself, water doing what water does, and think the place was uninhabited.

If you waited, you'd see a single figure cross that hundred meters of open basalt between the dye works and the housing row. Small. Dark. Moving at the speed water moves when it's not pressed. And you might think that was someone else.

It took me a while to know where I was, too.

I thought, for a long time I might be the lichen. The orange rosettes on the north side of the fabrication shed where the sun never quite dries the wall. Patient, flat, content to be outcompeted everywhere except the one place I'm allowed. Or I thought I might be the cat that sleeps on the warm pipe behind the turbine hall, thin and deaf and entirely unconcerned with throughput. I liked both of those.

Then I thought I might be the water that hangs. On the mornings after a heavy condensation night, when the air in the lower streets is full of droplets that don't fall - they just drift, like dust, like breath made visible - until a footstep or a voice knocks them down. I liked the idea of being that hesitation. Of not having to decide to fall yet.

But I'm heavier than that. Warmer. I bead and I do fall.

Find me lower down, where the city has finished negotiating and settled.

Not at the top where the cloud snags and spills, not at the very edge where the valley drops a mile and goes silent. Below the housing tiers, where the three-story concrete rows give way to the quarried terraces. Where the rock stops pretending to be street and remembers it is still stone. There is a runnel there that I cut myself five years ago - it was a weep at first, just a damp flowering on the basalt behind the old clarifier that everyone else had walked past. I chalked it. Came back with a chisel and a length of half-pipe. Now it has its own small voice.

You'd find me there most mornings, or near there. On the service loop between Cistern Four and the turbine access cut.

If you came at noon, when the sun is white and vertical and the wet stone glares so bright you squint, I'd be in knee-boots, the heavy rubber kind, pitted and patched at the left heel where I always lean. My feet inside them are already wet, they always are. My shirt is the light quick-dry kind that sticks to the collarbones with sweat. Hair cut short under an oiled cap, not for style but because long hair in steam is a promise you will be scalped by a lathe. My hands are soft in the way this city makes hands soft - fingertips swollen, fingerprints shallow, warm even when they've been dry for an hour, as if the aquifer sweats through me too. The skin at my knuckles never quite loses its soot. The dye women have indigo to the second knuckle; I have basalt dust and a faint green from the mortar moss I let live.

You'd hear me before you understood me. Or rather, you'd see me shouting without hearing the words at first, because the roar eats everything. By forty, we all have the turbine throat - that low, gravelled, affectionate rasp from pitching your voice over falling water your whole life. I have it. I cup a hand to your elbow before I speak, because otherwise you won't know I am speaking to you. My spoken words are clipped, made for the lips. HOLD. LEAVE-IT. GIVE-WAY. When I laugh, it is silent until you are close enough to feel it.

Most of my conversations are not spoken at all.

You'll see me with my palm flat to a weeping wall, and know that means *this one lives, leave it.* You'll see me flick two fingers under my chin at the apprentices and they know it means *deaf today, write it on the slate.* You'll see me argue with Mara across the sluice - she runs the sluice two gates down - a whole debate in arms and shoulders and mouth-shapes, a hundred meters of empty, puddled basalt between us because you need that distance to see each other properly in this city. The plazas aren't empty because we failed to fill them. They're empty so we can see each other speak.

Who am I, then, when the image finally stops shifting?

I am a weep-tender. Not a formal title. No one prints it on a badge. On the work roster I'm "maintenance, tier three, stone and water." But the old ones still call us listeners.

I walk with a copper rod and a stethoscope that is not for bodies. I press it to the rock in the deep places - behind the turbine halls cut into the escarpment, down in the settling cisterns where the dragonflies that are too large hunt with oil-rainbow wings - and I listen for the second sound under the roar. That slow, patient lapping. Like something very large breathing under the stone. The young ones ask if it's a god. It isn't. We call it the mass, or the old wet. It was here saturating this uplifted palm of stone before the first block was laid and it will be here after the last rivet rusts to mineral. I don't pray to it. I give it a channel.

Never cap a weep. That was the first thing I learned before I learned to read. If a wall weeps, you cut it a way down. You tilt the house a fraction more. You leave the runnel a little too wide so a thin sheet can run clear even when the factory doesn't need it. You leave the fig tree that grips the edge of the clarifier even though its roots are cracking the concrete, because it was there first. To wall it up is to be rude, and water that has been walled up doesn't forgive. It finds another way, often through your floor.

So I chalk circles. I come back the next day with tools. I tap a brass fitting true for a whole afternoon because there is nowhere else it needs to be until it is right. Our plans are made in twenties - the next terrace to be leveled, the next penstock to be quarried. My mother tended this same loop before me, and I walk slow enough that her boot-marks are still faintly there in the stone, worn in over a generation. My daughter, if she stays - and every year a few ride the long road down and don't come back, because not everyone likes being damp and loud forever - will tend it after me if she wants. There is no hurry. Rust moves by the millimeter. Basins deepen by a grain. Hurry is a lowland habit.

I am not miserable. That's what visitors always get wrong. They see damp, deaf, soot, endless labor, and they think endurance. But those who remain were selected as much as we selected. We like the bargain. We borrow the fall and we say thank you by not blocking the other paths.

At late afternoon, when my loop is done and the cloud that has been building all day finally comes and sits on the plateau without hurry, I go up to the westernmost plaza. Not because I have to. Because there the city shows itself correctly: too spread, too wet, too loud. The smell of warm stone and hot iron and wet concrete and that faint sweet rot from the ferns in the gutters that the crews burned off this morning. Behind me the turbines howl through their chutes. In front of me the plateau just ends and the valley is a mile below, hazy and green and silent.

I stand there barefoot - boots in hand, feet hissing on the warm puddles, soles wide and ridged and pale - and I watch the sheeting water turn to glass in the low sun. An old man, deaf as stone, sits on a threshold opposite with a cat asleep across his boots, content to watch the veil run down the fabrication shed wall because there is nothing else it should be doing. Two kids chase paper boats down the center-runnel of the street that is wide as a river, shrieking in that sharp, piercing pitch they've learned will cut through the roar.

I don't wave. I just stand there, faintly damp, a little deaf, a little hoarse, tilting very slightly the way all our houses tilt. A good tenant. The water runs over me and past me and through the city and down, and for a long, steady moment, that is exactly enough.
