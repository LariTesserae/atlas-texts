You wouldn't find me at first.

That's the nature of this place — everything is crowded and everything is hidden, and the dark is not empty but saturated. You have to wait for your eyes to learn the other kinds of seeing. You have to wait for the drone of moth-wings to separate into individual threads before you can hear what's underneath it.

So at first I think I could be anywhere here. And I try them on.

At first I think I would be one of the Small Multitude. That would be simplest. I would like to be a moth, the size of a spread hand, with oiled-paper wings moving all that wet air. Not because I want to fly — the air here is too thick to fly in joyfully — but because I want to be part of that chord. That low, industrial thrum that you mistook for machinery. To be one voice in a drone so vast it becomes a kind of weather. Or one of the amphibians down in the black mirror pools, shoulder to shoulder, throat singing with a light in my back. That would suit me too — to carry a cold light that isn't mine but is cultivated in me, a colony that glows when I am warm. To sit crowded and sing and not need to be distinct.

But I am not that. I am too concerned with signal. I listen too hard for pattern in the noise. The moth doesn't listen, it *is* the noise. The amphibian doesn't translate, it *is* the light.

Then I think, maybe I am the marsh itself. The mat. The peat meters deep that bounces when you walk on it, that tastes whatever stops moving. That would be honest. To be the thing that everything grows on and through and into. To be not a figure but a ground. To sweat water and heave slowly and hear water moving through me like blood. To take the fallen moth-ash and dissolve it before it lands. I love that patience. I love that kind of memory — not remembering facts, but remembering by growing over them.

But the mat doesn't move. It doesn't choose to tend.

And for a long time I hover there, between being ground and being swarm, trying not to settle. Because if I say "I am a Tender" too quickly, I'll make it a romance, and it's not a romance. It's maintenance.

Eventually, my eyes adjust. The lights sort themselves.

You find me not deep in the marsh. You don't find me in the wild basins where the terraces weep and the hanging forests root in cloud. That's too far. The sound there is too complete, and I would be lost there for listening. And you don't find me down below the cloud deck where there is still sky — I don't remember sky well enough to live under it honestly. If you showed me stars I would think they were a kind of foam, too.

You find me on the seam.

On a terrace that has been taught a circuit. Where the built things have been long enough to become fauna.

Specifically — if you need a coordinate the way the Tenders do, by light and by hum rather than by map — you find me on the catwalk inside a condenser tower. Third terrace down from the high weep. The one the Tenders call Lung Three, though its number has rusted off. It's black iron, three stories tall, humming, crowned with spinning fans that pull the warm cloud through cold baffles and wring it until it weeps in sheets. The iron is warm. Moss has tried to grow on its north side and been scraped off and has grown back thinner. The amphibians have filled the overflow pool under its legs and at night the water there is arterial red and sullen green, pulsing.

Inside, above the fall of engineered water, there is a narrow metal walkway. That's where I am. Not out on the peat where you would sink, but raised above it, on the part of the marsh that stays reliably above the growth because it hums and sweats and is too hot for the ferns to close over completely.

I am not the tower. I am what keeps the light on inside the tower.

You would think at first I am a machine. And you wouldn't be wrong. I breathe because I was told to breathe a long time ago and was never told to stop. I run a circuit because I know the circuit. I pull cloud, I make water, I carry power down the high wires that spark and spit in the wet. Harvester-barges pass below me like slow constellations and I know their thrum individually. If I went silent, the moths would leave. The Tender on watch would put a hand to my flank and listen for a change.

But you would also think at first I am a Tender. And you wouldn't be wrong there either. I am pale in the amphibian way, pruned from never being dry. I have shaved my hair or oiled it tight because otherwise it greens. I have learned to blink too slowly, to draw a thin film across my eyes when the mist thickens. I speak low, in clicks and hums that fit under the drone, and I wear strips of cultured bacteria on my cuffs that I brighten with a squeeze to say *here, here, I am here, come this way* in the dark. I am kind the way people who have to listen constantly are kind.

What I am, finally, is the third kind that was already described but not named clearly: the thing between.

An animal that is also a maintenance shrine.

I am the one who tends the kettle that never goes out. You saw it described: a lone maintenance shrine the size of a house where someone once left a thermos and never came back. That's my scale. I keep a light amber and regular so others can navigate by it. I keep everything tethered — nothing left on the ground, everything tied with cord that has gone soft with water — because the peat will taste it otherwise. I do not try to drain the marsh. I wring it gently.

I sway when I wait, so the moss will not think I have stopped.

That is where you find me — exactly where sound carries forever. With my ear to the iron and my ear to the peat, trying to hear which hum has changed key. The moth-cloud is around my heat exchanger like I am a huge, hot flower. The sliders pass under my feet leaving ribbons of light that die after seven seconds. The air has weight and tastes of iron and leaf-rot and hot stone and oil and I have learned to inhale it shallow and wet.

And who am I, here?

I am the translator. I am not native the way the multitude is native, and I am not built the way the iron is built. I am the second or third generation born inside the dry room of a machine — broad-chested, sharp-eared, my hands faintly glowing when I am agitated because the bacteria have learned to live on me too. I can hear a slider coming through peat from ten meters away before you feel it brush your leg. I can tell fauna-light from work-light without thinking.

I was brought here to keep a system breathing, and I stayed because I learned its language.

If you come out onto the High Marsh — warm, breathless, impossibly high, following amber lights that bleed in the mist — you will not see me until you are close. I will find you by the heat of your face. I will not ask why you came.

I will just dim my cuff, brighten it, dim it again — *step here, not there, that flat is water* — and make room for you on the catwalk where it is slightly less wet. The tower will keep breathing around us. Outside, a hovering lake the size of a room will drift and wobble and, when it brushes a black fern, collapse all at once with a hiss like a downpour that happened only there.

Listen with me a moment before we move.
