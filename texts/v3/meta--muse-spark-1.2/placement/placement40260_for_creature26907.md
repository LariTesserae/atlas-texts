I don't know at first.

With dry shoes, I don't belong there yet. I come down the two turns of the stair and my shoulders hit the glossy plaster and I am too loud, too dry, too sharp-edged. My steps on the herringbone are wrong — a hard *tap tap* instead of the soft pad they make. The pigeons wheel when my shadow falls wrong. A child stares at my feet because they don't understand how to be in this place yet.

So I stand at the edge of the well for a long time and let the Basin decide what I am. I don't choose. Not yet.

At first I think I would be high. That seems right for someone who loves air.

I would live on the third platform up the north pillar, where the vault is closest and the brick is blackest from old fires. The swifts would be my neighbors. I would be the one who doesn't mind the droppings white-streaking the arch, who reaches into the cracks where no one else can reach to pull out a fallen swift chick and put it back. I would live in the draft from the oculus, in the solid column of light when it comes, and when it leaves I would be in the deepest gloom. I would learn to be still like the geckoes for hours, throat puffing, watching.

But I am not that still. Not that solitary. That high up, you are alone in a way this place doesn't really allow. And the wind from the oculus would dry my hair, and I don't want to be dry.

Then I think I would be low. With the oldest.

I would be the woman on the low stool by the well, legs folded, the one whose eyes have gone filmy but who hears whether the well is rising by how the amber frogs argue. I would be useful in the way that is patient — guiding the waxy vine a fraction of an inch a day with twine, scraping lichen with a flat stone into a bowl for dye, watching the children's cloths to make sure the drip from the ceiling lands on the cloth and not on their faces while they sleep. I would know every drip's name. I would know which mortar line sweats first before rain.

But I am not yet that patient. My hands still want to move with the light. I still want to follow it.

Let the sun column slide a little further across the floor. Let me watch where it goes, and who goes with it.

There — the weaver picks up her loom without a word and shifts two pillars over. The man with the bowl of glossy beetles moves his bowl six inches to stay in amber. The children drag their reed mat. Nobody owns light. They borrow it. They orbit.

That's where I find myself.

Not high, not low. On the second level, the middle.

You would find me on the second platform of the second pillar from the well, on the side that catches the late morning light the longest. My platform is not the biggest or the smallest. The reed walls are patched until the patches have patches, same as everyone else's. A pot of chives lives on the ledge because I remembered to water it with the cupped condensation from the ceiling. A net of gourds hangs within arm's reach, and a line of laundry I share with the family below me, because nothing is wasted and no space is empty between us.

Who would I be? I would be folded.

Broad in the shoulders, long in the back, weight settled in my hips and thighs from years of ducking the beam overhead without thinking about it anymore. Bare feet the color of brick, soles thick as hide, toes splayed wide to find the notches in the pole-ladder. My hair cut short with a chipped stone, always damp, clinging to my neck. My tunic the same as everyone else's, sleeveless bast, tied with cord. My fingertips soft and permanently wrinkled, as if I'd just come from a bath and never quite dried. My skin sheened. Always a little sweat, and no one minding the touch of it.

I would be a mender.

Not the weaver, not the vine-tender, not the frog-watcher, but the one who does a little of each because everyone knows the same half-dozen tasks. On my platform there's always something in my lap that needs hands: a reed net with a tear, a clay jar with a hairline crack to be sealed with lime, a handful of beetle larvae to sort — the pale ones for the children, the dark ones for the oil. When I nurse, I sort with one hand. When I talk, I sort.

I would be soft-voiced. That's non-negotiable here.

I would have learned to click my tongue twice instead of calling out, and to pause half a breath after I speak so the vault can catch up. My language would be full of breath and clicks that cut through dripping and cooing. I would laugh with a hand over my mouth, not from shyness, but because I know what a laugh becomes after five bounces off wet brick.

You would not find me alone. That's the point.

If you came to my platform at midday, you would have to duck the hanging cloth, step over the sleeping dog curled on warm brick, brush past my sister's elbow as she pounds fermented gourd. My youngest would be asleep against my back, breathing with me. My ankle would be touching the ankle of the boy from the next pillar over where our platforms almost meet, just to know where he is without looking. When your dry shoes creaked below, I would not stare — I would lift my chin in that small upward breath through the nose that is our greeting, and keep mending, and let you stand at the well until your eyes adjusted and you understood you were already being held.

You would find me listening.

Because in the Basin you have to. The vault makes sounds arrive late and doubled, a drip you see fall *tik*s half a breath after it lands, a voice from the far end that seems to come from behind your shoulder. I would be the one who learned to hear truly despite it. The one the children bring a new beetle to and I tell them, not by looking, but by the sound of its legs on the bowl, whether it's good to crack like a nut or should be put back in the mortar to eat the mold. The one who knows when the air is about to get still and tells the old ones to feed their fires a little higher so the smoke keeps pulling through the oculi.

At dusk I would be part of the exhale.

You would see it if you stayed. The last column of sun lifts off the far arch and is gone. The bats drop like thrown stones. And without anyone giving a signal you can hear, we all sit. All of us. Children find a lap. The small oil lamps that never go out become visible all at once, warm spots in the gloom. The frogs get very loud. And I would be there, on my second platform, with a cloth draped over the sleeping child next to me to catch the ceiling's sweat, with my sister's shoulder against mine, with my bare sole flat on the still-warm brick, feeling the whole crowded, wet, brick lung of the place breathe out and hold us — and you, if you have finally taken off your dry shoes — inside its warmth until morning.

I wouldn't be living *in* the Basin.

I would be one of the ways the Basin stays alive.
