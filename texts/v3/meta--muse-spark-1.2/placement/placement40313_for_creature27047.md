I wouldn't find myself at the crown.

At first I think I would - up where the quarry is still sharp and light, where the stone hasn't weathered yet and smells like struck flint. That would be the obvious place for someone like me: the maker, the climber, the one who wants to see how it ends. To stand on the raw bite where the next terrace will be and look down at the whole bright machine falling away.

But I wouldn't stay there. It's too loud up there, even in its quiet. Too unfinished. The wind hits it first and the sun hits it hardest. You can feel the mountain still intending to be larger.

I wouldn't be at the bottom either, where distance turns it back into nature and you can pretend it's just a mountain again. That's for looking away.

I would find myself in the middle.

Mid-slope, mid-day, mid-breath. Where the light is already punishing and the air is already thin enough that walking feels like wading. Where ten levels interleave between you and where you thought you were going, so you stop trying to go anywhere. There is a place there - there are thousands of places there exactly like it - where a terrace thirty feet deep holds a cistern just large enough to lie down in. Its retaining wall is dark basalt, fitted without mortar, weeping. Its bottom is orange silt. Its spill-notch is clear because someone has been combing it.

At first I am standing. Breath visible even at noon, as you said. My boots dam a runnel no wider than my hand and water backs up behind them, patiently finding a new way around. I watch it do that. I move my boot. It finds the old way again.

That's when I realize I am not visiting. Visitors keep walking. I have already stopped.

So I sit. The stone is cold through my clothes and wet in a way that doesn't soak, just films. The ticking of a thousand drips is too many to count so it becomes a single hush. The sky is too violet at the zenith to look at, and too deep in the cistern to not look at. I put my hands on the coping and do what the description told me to do before I read it: I go still until my breath stops fogging and starts again.

I think - for a long time, because this world does not let you settle early - that I would be a builder returned. The one who left the hammer marks and came back when he remembered what the machine was for. That I would pick up a stone maul and keep cutting.

But my hands are too warm for that. They would evaporate the film.

Then I think I would be a pilgrim. The first throat the reservoir was ever meant to quench. That I would cup my hands and drink the cold mineral water that has never been drunk and finally give the place a purpose.

But there are no cups here that stay empty. The water is not for drinking. It is for holding.

It takes another hour of not moving to understand what I actually am in this world.

I am not flat. I will never be flat. I am vertical, mammalian, ridiculous - a column of heat and breath that interrupts flow. And that is precisely why I would be there, and why they would keep me.

I would be the obstacle they learn to tend around.

I lie back. Slowly, because the air is thin. In the large cistern. The half-inch mirror takes my weight without parting the way water should. It resists, just slightly, like a membrane. My shoulders displace the orange silt delta that someone so patiently swept into the corner, and I feel, more than see, the faintest hesitation in the thrumming of the wall - the whole mountainside holding its thought together - as a new stone has appeared in its skin.

If I keep breathing, my back will be cold. If I keep still, the film will find me.

They come at water speed, which is to say they are already there. The hem of cilia, of fingers too clear to count, at the rim where I thought there was only light. They do not flee. They comb.

Around the edge of my sleeve where it dams the inflow. Around the shadow I cast, which is too sharp for a wet world. They test the spill-notch again to make sure it still sighs. They carry one grain of silt at a time from where my elbow has clouded it and place it, with infinite patience, just beyond my hip where it will not matter for a decade.

I would be the warm stone.

Not the builder, not the built-for. The third thing. The thing that came after, that the machine wasn't made for but can accommodate, because its gradient is so weak it has centuries to learn.

My job, if you can call it that, is to be still enough to be tended. To let my lifeline bead with water that does not immediately run off, that holds its shape a little too well. To watch the reflected sky, which is deeper than the sky above, until my eyes go out of focus and I see that I am looking *through* a body broad as a cloak, translucent grey as wet basalt, edged with white rime where it is making its own skeleton out of dissolved stone.

I wouldn't speak. There is nothing to say at water speed that needs saying in a season.

At noon I would be invisible with brightness, just another glare on the pale grey. At dusk, when the real sun goes and the stone stops glittering, the basin I am lying in would hold light after the sky has gone dark. Each cistern a small lamp. That would be them exhaling. That would be me, exhaling, fogging the air for a second before the cold takes it.

And maybe - after a very long time, after the sharp quarry edges at the crown have dulled just a little - the silt would settle on me. The calcite would curtain over the place where my hands meet the wall and were too warm. I would not become one of them. I would become part of the mountain that is not a mountain, the joint that finally learned to leak correctly, the place where the waiting ended because something finally came to wait in it.

You would find me there, if you came and went still. Not standing. Lying in a cistern in the middle of everything, half in the bright cold sun and half in the deeper sun on the other side of the surface tension, being combed around.
