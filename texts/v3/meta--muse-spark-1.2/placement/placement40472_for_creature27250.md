I don't know at first.

If you drop me into that bowl, my first instinct is to stand where you stood — on the central gantry.

Because that's where a person would stand. High enough to see the whole room that thinks it's a neighborhood, hand on a damp rail that hums in my teeth, watching the fifteen-foot wheel turn once every twenty seconds and trying to time my breathing to it. I would be the visitor. The warm vertical surface. The weather condition with a mouth and eyes where nothing should have a mouth and eyes.

And I would be wrong there.

Too high, too dry, too much eye. From the gantry I can see too much at once and the bowl can see me too much at once — all that felted green would feel my heat as a sudden gradient, all that beading water would find my shoulders and bead there too, and I would be conspicuous. A loud, animal interruption in a place that has edited out interruption. I would smell wrong - sweat and decay, not ozone and wet brick. I would leave too quickly, or overstay and feel myself being politely, slowly grown around.

So I step down.

Not all at once. I let myself find a lower place, a smaller scale, a way of belonging that doesn't require apology.

Maybe I'm not on the gantry at all then. Maybe I'm under it.

There is an underside to every walkway in the bowl where condensation gathers before it has decided to fall. A forest of drips in waiting, each bead holding the dim filtered light for a minute before it gets heavy enough to let go. If I were there, I would be one of those beads. Not seeing, not eating, just hanging by surface tension, drinking the air without a mouth and returning it without being asked. I would be patient enough. But I would be too brief. I would fall and become tea-colored water and be gone in a ripple that the marsh smooths over in seconds.

I need something that stays.

Lower still.

Where the pavement pretends to still be a street but has already surrendered. Where the water is a foot deep and the foundations rise out of it like pilings, and the alder and willow roots run laterally for yards in black peat, grafting around rebar and hot water pipes because they have learned pipes are just another kind of root that carries water that is a little warmer.

That's where I find myself.

I am not standing above the marsh. I am in the seam.

I thought I might be the moss — and I am, a little — that luminous inch-deep sponge that holds the print of a hand for a minute before erasing it. But the moss is a skin. It knows how to stop a half-inch from a steam vent. It knows how to trim itself.

I am not that careful. I am more ambitious, more vertical.

I am the willow that found the crack.

Not the whole tree you see from the gantry, pale and slick and dripping. The younger one. The sucker that came up three weeks or three years ago through the seam where the boiler-house wall meets the stairwell that goes nowhere. My roots are not my own — they never were. A few feet down in the warm, saturated dark they have already braided with the others, with the black-and-white fungal ropes thick as cord, with the sweating elbow of a hot water pipe that the older roots have already cradled. I drink what the pipe sweats. The pipe is kept from rusting too far by the algae that I shade. We are already negotiating.

I have no eyes. I don't need them. Light comes to me as a gradient, filtered through cloud and lattice and steam and the waxy dripping leaves of my own crown. I feel it as a slow pull. I have no mouth, but every leaf is a mouth. Every lenticel on my bark is a mouth. I drink the beading air, the tea-colored water around my ankles, the temperature difference between ground-warmth and cloud-cool that the iron drinks too. When one of my own leaves — perfect, uneaten, because nothing has ever had a mouth to eat it here except now, briefly, me — when it falls, I don't eat it. The invisible film on every submerged surface softens it for months until it is peat, and peat holds more water, and water finds the crack I made a little wider.

That is how I eat. By continuing.

I have no front or back. My present body is facing in five directions at once, because I grew until I touched something else and then grew around it. On my north side my bark is cool and bearded with liverwort an inch deep. On my south side, where the transformer hums behind corrugated sheeting, my bark is bare and warm to the touch, trimmed not by a hand but by my own knowledge that heat there would discourage me. The hum I feel not in ears but in wood, in water, in the peat that holds me. Drip... hiss... hum... groan... drip... My leaves drip in time without trying.

If you stood on the gantry now and looked for me, you would not find an inhabitant. You would find a pale trunk, slick with condensation, doing exactly what a willow does in a bowl that is also a body. You would think I was just vegetation, just part of the green that learned the shape of the place by becoming it.

And you would be right.

I am not in the bowl. For the time I live here, I am a small, vertical, temporary thought the bowl is having about how to reinforce a channel for water, about how to turn waste heat into leaf.

Stay a day and you would lean against me and leave damp, smelling of wet brick for a week.

Stay long enough and I will do what this place does — I will cover the warm vertical surface that you left, gently, completely, and continue my slow turning without hurry.
