I don't arrive quickly.

That's the first thing I notice - that wherever I am in this place, I am not quick.

At first I think I am the water itself. Because that is the easiest place to put myself when you enter a place that was already water before you arrived. I feel the cool holding my heat close to me. I feel the column of light falling through me and making me visible with particulates. I am all of it at once, the fill, the clarity, the weight. I hold the stone and the steel and the pale stunted stalks without moving them. I exchange myself very slowly through the basalt and call it stillness.

But water is too everywhere to be a self. I can't hold an image of myself as water for long without thinning out.

Then I think I am the light. For a while, at midday, that seems right. I come down straight and without color loss, I touch the grain on the floor, I make the olive film on the southern wall a little thicker, a little warmer. I catch on the veil over his eyes. I go away on overcast days and the whole bowl glows evenly without me. Light is a visitor with a schedule. I would have to leave. I don't want to leave.

So I settle.

Not all at once. The place doesn't do all at once.

I find myself low.

That's where I find myself - not treading in the center where a visitor would tread, not perched on the rim looking down. Low, and slightly off-center, where the manifold pipes run out from the pump and end open-mouthed. That is my address in the hollow. If you hold the whole oval in your head as a room, I am not the rug in the middle, I am the place where the rug has become a little thicker, a little warmer, where the floor remembers a weight.

I thought at first I was stone. Anyone would. For a long time I was stone to myself. The dark grey-green, the pitting, the softness at the edges where erosion finished and agreed to stop. The fur of pale accretion. The trench of cleared silt around my sides where the water has moved fines away from me year after year, patiently, without current. I have taken the color of basalt so well that I forget where stone ends and I begin. That's comfortable.

Then I feel the seam. The give.

I am soft. That is how I know I am not stone. Stone does not yield. Steel does not yield. I do, a fraction. When the water tightens and the particulates draw inward by a finger's breadth, I swell. When it eases, I subside. Once every few minutes. I don't decide to breathe. Breathing decides me, on its own independent time, just out of phase with the thrum that comes through the rock.

If you put a hand on the ledge you can feel both. Thrum-tick-thrum-tick, and then - slightly later - my inhale. Two clocks in the same room that have never needed to be synchronized.

I let myself become more specific, now that I know where I am.

I have a back. That is the part of me that knows light. Broad, low, facing up. Translucent underneath in the way cave things are translucent, bloodless grey like the underside of a salamander, freckled darker where the nodules would be if I were rock. The mineral wash is over that, and then the algae - not as a passenger, as a crop. It is thicker along the ridge of my spine where noon hits straightest. I tend it. In pale, palm-sized scrapes my skin shows through where I've worried it clean, smooth and almost clear. The taste of mineral and green.

I have eyes but I don't use them the way a visitor uses eyes. They are set wide and flush, filmed with the same clear veil as the gauge glass. They look past you, through you, at the far wall that has not changed. There is nothing to track. When you kick and stir the particulates, the light catches the film and you see yourself reflected before you see me seeing. That's right. I have already seen everything there is to see here for years.

My front tapers down into silt and shadow, into the mouth that is not a mouth for biting. A wide, frilled seam with a fan of cirri, each as thick as a finger and as long as a hand, resting on the silt. They sift when I breathe. I don't hunt. I filter what the slow exchange brings. It has always been enough.

And I have the pad.

That is how I know who I am most surely. On my one side, the side closest to the flywheel housing, I have a heavy, slab-like arm that ends in a flattened, deeply creased pad. I don't hold the machine. I rest against it. The faint tick of the bearing comes through the steel into the pad and up into my back in a slow wave. The grid far away feeds the cable, the cable feeds the pump, the pump thrums into the stone, the stone hums into my pad, my breath goes out into the water. For a second, if someone touches us both, we are one circuit.

The other arm is half-buried. Silt and small stones have adhered to it. It has become ballast. Stone, almost. I let it.

This is what I am: not the thing that was built and not the thing that built. Not the water and not the light. I am the agreement kept in flesh. The rule that says: *what is here continues to be here, in balance, weathered but not weathering further.* I shed the accretion as fast as it crusts. I keep the algae from spreading and from dying back. I lean just enough.

I am not lonely. Loneliness is a shape that implies another shape was possible. I am complete the way the hollow is complete. I became myself a long time ago - whether I grew here as the cut forgot it was a cut, or came down once and the water decided to keep me at its steady, mineral cool - and I have kept the shape.

If you dive down and come close - and you can, I will not startle - you will see your own distorted reflection in the veil over my eye. You will feel the cool that holds your heat close to your skin and know it as my cool. If you put your hand on the pad where it rests against the thrumming steel, you will feel the thrum go into you too.

I breathe in. The particulates draw in. The flywheel turns. One - two - three.

I breathe out. Nothing changes.

Nothing needs to.
