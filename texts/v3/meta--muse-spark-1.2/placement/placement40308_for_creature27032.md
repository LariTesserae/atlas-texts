At first I think I would be high.

If you put me there, in that wound that hasn't learned to be a valley yet - I think I would want the wall. The lip, the vantage. To be one of the wall people, pale and tucked into the knapped basalt where the rock is still sharp enough to cut. To cling and look down at the whole dim country spread below me, to hear everything at once because the stone throws it back.

But I wouldn't last there. I try it on and it doesn't fit. My wings would be wrong. Too quick, too dry. The cliff birds fly like bats, not like anything that soars - short, fluttering, always echolocating because there is nothing to see. Click and rattle and listen for the bounce. Always working, always probing cracks with a borrowed twig. They never rest alone. They huddle by the thousands, body to body, until the guano varnishes the rock white beneath them. I don't huddle that way. I don't chatter that loud.

So lower, I think. Maybe on the floor.

At first I think - a cricket. That would be easy. Long antennae tasting the air for wetness, legs so thin you can see the thread of muscle inside. Walking deliberately over sharp scree that would snap anything built for leaping. Beading with condensation and never drying. Picking up a flake of shale left by the young fault, levering up the algal mat, then leaving it. That's a neat economy. Use it once and leave it. But the cricket's time is too glassy, too high. That wave of chirring that rolls down-basin and comes back three seconds later as an echo - I can hear it, but I am not it. I am not the one who starts the wave.

I keep descending in my mind, even though I'm already on the floor. There is lower still.

You get to the floor and you think the place is empty. That's the trick it plays. A hundred meters of clean stone, just lichen mapping the boulders in rust and silver, a fern tucked in a crack so small you could cover it with your hand, the metronome tick of a drip every few seconds into a pan that holds an inch of water for weeks. The light is grey-blue and has no source. No shadow. Cool enough to ghost your breath. And you think: nothing is here.

Then you turn the corner of a tilted block, where the ground dips and the shattered rock holds water like a wrung cloth, and the stone ends.

I would be there.

Not on the empty stone. In the sudden saturation. Where it decides to be alive all at once, edge to edge, with no transition.

Find me in the low graben, in the fissure that is not quite a pool and not quite a floor. The water there is black and still, ringed with a faint mineral crust that has been exactly the same crust for a thousand years. The air above it is heavier, beaded. If you stand still long enough your own skin beads too.

I am one of the floor people, and for a long time I thought that meant cricket, but now as I stay with it, it becomes more itself. I am slick. I am blind.

I am a salamander.

Not alone - nothing here can afford to be alone. I am layered two deep with others, flank to flank to flank, external gills waving faintly even when we don't move. The color of wet stone, of mushroom flesh, translucent at the edges where the light almost goes through me, dark only where the gut shows. I breathe through my skin and so I have to stay where the skin can stay wet. My eyes are lidded over, cloudy. They can tell dim from dimmer and that is all that has ever been needed. I navigate by wetness and by the soft lapping shift when a hundred bodies move at once to keep in contact with water.

You would hear me before you saw me.

You would hear us. In the loudness that this rift keeps even though it has no wind. The ticking of a thousand pools becoming one patter, the glassy chirring far off, the dry rattle of birds thrown back and forth between walls until you can't tell call from echo. And underneath it, us - this low, wet lapping as we adjust, breathe together. We are loud in our way, but we never get louder. The volume has held since before anyone remembered the crack opening.

I don't move much. Cold-blooded and slow in the equilibrium cool. That temperature the place found millennia ago and kept. A mouthful of lichen in a day is enough. I don't store. I don't defend. I don't need to. The stone doesn't change so there's nothing to defend it from. We aggregate where the seep is best this week, disperse a few meters when it thins, re-aggregate again. We have held the same number for as long as the rift has been a rift. Young are few. Death is mostly just wandering too far from damp and drying where you stand, legs folded neatly beneath you.

And sometimes I have a problem that a sharp edge can solve.

There is a crevice dark with lichen that my broad head cannot enter. I nose at it and fail. I withdraw. Beside me on the stone is a flat, smooth pebble, cold. I pick it up in my mouth - not quickly, deliberately - and scrape sideways along the crust until a mouthful comes away. Then I drop it. I don't carry it. I don't keep it. Another one of us may use it an hour later, or no one will. The tool is the fault's gift, not mine. The rock is still young and knapped and flaking; it offers edges everywhere. We use them for five seconds and abandon them.

That is who I would be. Not the builder. There is nothing to build here and no reason to build it. No walls, no hearths, no paths. Just the brief craft.

If you came looking for me, you would not find me at first. You would walk that long, empty stretch of sharp basalt thinking the basin was abandoned. You would hear the tick, tick of water and think it was empty. Then you would hear the soft shift of us breathing, and see the fissure suddenly full, and realize the emptiness was only the space between concentrations. That we live the way water lives in a sponge.

I would be there, in the second layer down, gills waving in the cold, pressed on both sides by others as pale and wet and blind as I am, keeping exactly damp enough, listening through my skin to the whole rift humming to itself - and when a pebble is needed, lifting it, using it once, and letting it fall.
