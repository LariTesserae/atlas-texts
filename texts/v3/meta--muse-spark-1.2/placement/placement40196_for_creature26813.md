At first I think I'm standing at the rim.

That is where you arrive, after all. Hands on the warm lip of concrete where the water beads out of nothing and runs over your knuckles. Looking down into the whole of it at once, rim to rim, trying to take in a perfect made watershed while the cloud sea breathes below and the light comes from everywhere at once. I think: I am the visitor. The one who heard dripping and followed it. The one whose footprints will soften in the silt.

But the rim is wrong for me. It is too exposed, too much wind on the cloud-breaks, too much distance. The bowl is not meant to be seen whole. It is meant to be stood inside, ankle-deep. And I can feel how quickly the air would dry the film off my skin if I stayed up there.

So I move down without deciding to.

One terrace. Then another. The concrete steps are warm as skin. Water finds my feet immediately - not cold, not shocking, bathwater that has been running over moss. It wells between my toes. My soles go pale. I think, oh, I am one of them then. One of the keepers. That makes sense. I know how to hold the shears so I don't tear the aerial roots. I know the click for *this rill is choked* and the two taps for *open it a tooth*. I have gloves somewhere drying palm-up. I have a tin cup with tea gone cold on the scaffolding.

For a while that fits. I picture myself half in the curtain of hanging roots on the second terrace, in the concrete-and-leaf-shadow clothes that never dry. Hair pulled back with inner tube. Eyes squinted against light that has no shadow. Hands pruned and tender, fingerprints half-erased. Breathing in time with the piston from the factory core - pull, breathe, pull, breathe - while I thin the taro with clean angled cuts.

But I am not cutting.

My hands are not moving. The keepers move, even when they move slowly, with intention. They negotiate. They trim the single rootlet that will pinch, they rub rust-water into bark like ointment, they lift a vine off a whisker of rebar before it can rust faster. Their work is care.

What I am doing is slower than care. What I am doing is staying.

I am lower than I thought. Not on the second terrace looking at the gear, but *in* the gear.

You know the one - the cast-iron set on the second terrace down, big and slow, meant to turn a water gate a tooth at a time. The keepers left it turning. And through its central axle, a fig has found its way. Not planted there. Found. A seed on the wind, or in a bird dropping, or in the silt itself, lodged in the seam where axle meets housing where condensation never stops. And it grew.

That is where I find myself. If you walk the V-cut rills down from the rim you will pass me without seeing me at first, because leaf is loud and machine is quiet and the animal in between disappears. You will see the gear and the bark and think *strange, they left that*. Then you will see the wrench marks on the housing where she turned it by hand this morning, feeling where I bind.

I am the fig.

Not the whole green hunger that is racing to cover the raw concrete - not the taro, not the fern, not the moss-carpet. I am smaller and more specific. I am the accommodation.

My roots are down in the warm weep. My leaves are waxy and heavy and nodding under their own water. My trunk is still thin, hardly a trunk at all, more a pale wrist pushing through iron. The gear still turns. It has to. The water still needs to rise and fall a finger's width. So once a day, or once an hour when the pumps labor, the teeth engage and the whole iron circle shudders and eases forward, and my bark creaks.

It should be killing me. It should be pinching me off.

But the keeper came this morning. I felt her before I saw her - the soft *tock* of shears, the warm pruned hand. She did not cut me. She reached in with the long wrench and turned the gear herself, slowly, to feel where I was catching. Then she stooped and snipped a single rootlet, no thicker than a thread, that had wandered into the tooth gap. She wiped rust-water off the housing and rubbed it into my bark with her thumb. Not to help me grow. To help me and the gear agree.

That is my place in the bowl.

Not at the rim where you can see everything. Not in the cistern where all the water meets and the heron stands, not in the factory core where the hammocks swing warm from the pipes. Up one level from the heart, down two from the sky, in the exact seam where the engineered thing and the living thing were supposed to stay separate and didn't.

I am warm from condensation and from friction. Steam breathes out through the bank of louvers next to me and has to pass through the liverwort mat, so the air around me is always filtered through green. The diffuse white light makes my newest leaves look almost translucent. Water ticks from the tip of a rebar above me and lands on one of them and stays there, beaded, for a long time.

I do not move the way people move. I move the way concrete cures and water etches. A month ago the channel beside me was square-cut; now it is a shallow U and I have grown a millimeter thicker. Tomorrow the keeper will come again and find I have tightened half a degree against the housing, and she will trim again, or turn the gear a little harder.

In a year I will split this gear entirely. The iron will crack and the gate will jam open and the bowl will have to learn a new way for water to find its level, around me. The concrete will have gone dark and hard and the silt will have made little deltas in the cistern and the keepers will be gone because this jungle will not need soft hands anymore.

For now, I am needed exactly because I am in the way.

If you are looking for me, don't look for a person. Look for the place where bark is creaking very quietly under iron, where rust and sap have made the same stain. Put your hand there. It will be vibrating with the pumps below.
