I don't find myself at the rim.

I thought I would — up there where the wind still moves and you can see the whole bowl laid out like a palm. Where as a human you could stand and judge it, name it, be a visitor looking down.

But if I belong here, I don't belong there. Nothing that lives here lives on the rim. The rim is where things end.

So I go down.

Slowly, because everything here is slow and I would be too. Let me not decide yet. Let me walk the terraces and try on shapes.

At first I think I'd be one of the grazers.

It would be good to be low and heavy and dust-colored, to move in that unhurried reverence between the rosette colonies. To know every colony within a half kilometer by feel — which ones can spare two outer leaves, which ones need another season. To have broad feet that don't cut the pan. To make a life of careful taking, to leave my dry pellets behind and know within an hour the dark sheets of foragers will come and take them and nothing will be wasted. A simple, honest commute.

But I am not that quiet in my body. My thoughts rumble. I would be lonely as a grazer, alone between towers in all that space.

Then higher up — the Curtains. For a long time I linger there, halfway down the wall where the slope still holds the cool.

If I were part of the curtain I wouldn't have to choose. I could be the hanging itself — lichen and moss and air-fern felted together into a shawl ten feet long, dry as paper to the touch but drinking all night. Each filament beading at false dawn and swallowing the bead by morning. I could be that lush impossibility, that trick of drinking air. And I could be housing. The curtain-birds would vanish into me and I would hold their nests without ever knowing they were there. The stridulators would live along my thatch and make me hum.

I love that. To be still and yet full. To be wealth because I am height.

But the curtain doesn't make its own sound. It rustles when something else moves it.

If I am honest, I want to make sound.

So I find myself lower again, on the floor proper, in the long, smoothed dust between towers that is not empty once you learn to see. I crouch there for a while and try the other life, the one I think I would actually be.

Not the Architects. Not quite. They are too deep, too collective, too blind in their perfect labyrinth. Thumb-long and phosphorescent, moving as a sheet, carrying one grain at a time for centuries until a house becomes a hill becomes a district. I revere them but I could not be them — I am not patient enough to be a thousand bodies with one mind.

I would be one of their tenants.

Here is where I finally stop and come into focus:

Three meters up the windward shoulder of one of the old central towers — not the oldest, the second oldest, the one whose sides have slumped enough that from a distance you thought it was a vegetated hill. Its skin is no longer raw adobe but a patchwork: cemented earth, then paper, then mud, then felt. Lichen has colonized its north face so thick it hangs a little, a small curtain growing on a hill that was itself grown.

Find me there, in a grey, fluted gallery of wasp-paper tucked under an overhang where the tower sighs warm air out through a lung-vent. That's my doorway. I am small, soot-colored, winged but not much for flying. Maybe a stridulator, maybe one of the curtain-bird's cousins who learned to live not in the wall-curtains but on the tower-curtains. The distinction hardly matters after a thousand years of smoothing.

My back is dust-grey and ridged. My legs have the file. My eyes are glassy and used to dim, pearled light with no shadows.

What do I do all day? The same thing everything here does.

I wait for dew. In the last hour before the light thins, the white hairs of the rosettes below and the felt around my doorway bead. I crawl out and drink without drinking — I press my belly to the beaded thatch and let the condensation wick through my own fine hairs. A few perfect spheres and I am done for the day. Nothing wasted.

I repair. The wind, when it does find the floor, pulls a corner of paper loose. I chew fiber from the blanket-fern, mix it with a little dust and spit, and press it back. A millimeter a day.

And I sing.

At midday when the heat stills even the grazers, I start. Leg across back, one clear metallic note. Alone it is nothing — a dry *tik* that would vanish in wind. But down here the air is heavy and stays put and there are a million others on this tower and the next and the curtain walls. We phase-lock without deciding to. My note finds theirs and lifts a half-tone until we are not a million chirps but a single, unwavering, stone-issued tone that you feel in your molars. It is not for beauty. It's how I say: *this patch of felt is mine, this vent's warmth is mine, I am here, I am still here.* And in the dim where you can't see twenty meters, that is the only way to hold anything.

At dusk we all drop together, a perfect half-step down, and hold. The birds answer with their glassy *tik!* The towers exhale. The grazers low. I feel it all through my feet, through the paper wall I share with blind grubs and lizards and Architects moving in their sheets below me.

I am not sharp. Nothing that lasts here is. My mandibles are small and blunt, my claws are soft grapnels for thatch, not tearing. My song is not loud to conquer but constant to persist.

I am urban without ever having been invented. My neighborhood is vertical — below me the fungus farmers, above me the swallows' mud cups, around me the green pelt. My highway is a pale line of scent and worn pan that I follow to the nearest rosette colony and back, the same line walked for ten thousand years.

If you walked between the towers now, you would not see me at first. You would say *empty* — because I am the color of dust, the color of dim light, the color of old paper. Then I would move, or sing, and you would realize the empty space was crowded all along, and that I had been there listening to you breathe.

That is where I would find myself: not looking at the Hollow, but as a small, tuned, dew-drinking part of its breath.
