I didn't know at first.

If you had asked me while I was still standing on the upper leaves, looking out at that flat, bright forever where the green goes true to the edge of sight — I would have said up there. High. I would have wanted to be one of the sail-riders, prone on that kilometer-wide leaf that is also a roof and also a ceiling, with my back open. I thought I wanted that kind of stillness. To lie for hours and let the white sky pour into me until my edges glowed.

But I stayed with the image a little longer and it went thin. I am not good at being that still. The light up there is too absolute. It leaves no shadow to think in. And my skin — even imagined — felt too young and too thin to not be burned through by it.

Then I thought, low. All the way low, in the understory where the felted litter drifts like paper snow against the cathedral presses. In the dim where you have to turn sideways between a wall that is leaf and a leaf that is wall. I thought I would be a keeper of the drifts, gathering the shed sheets, pressing them flatter. An archivist. Because I like small, quiet work and the way paper holds heat.

But that was too muffled. Sound goes to die down there. The dry felt eats even the memory of a tap. I would go deaf. I would lose the thread.

It took a while to find where my weight actually settles.

Not top. Not bottom. Middle.

You find me in the middle story — where the basin stops pretending to be a forest or a factory and is most honestly both.

Where a maintenance catwalk, thirty meters wide, grew a bark of pale fiber over its railings and then sprouted air-roots that hang down to head height and were trimmed. Where a girder-stem as thick as a house runs overhead and hums if you lean on it right. Where the light doesn't hit you directly; it comes filtered down through three or four layers of sail-leaves, shafted and softened, so the brightness has corners again and you can see dust — that slow, mineral pollen — hanging for hours in it.

I'm on a terrace edge. One of those shallow steps, a meter and a half high, that runs straight for a hundred kilometers until you think straight is a myth. The floor-plate here is warm from the press behind me. The seam between plates has been filled with compressed fiber for so long it's become a dark, smooth vein. You can feel the thrum through it if you put your bare foot down. I do.

That's where I live. Not fixed. Circuit.

If you came looking you wouldn't see me at first. You'd see the gap I keep.

I am a knot-tier.

One of the thin ones. Seven feet if I stand to my full height, which I rarely do, because the ceiling of leaves is low here and because height is a waste of leverage. Narrow as a loom frame, like the others, with that extra give in the elbows and knees — not another joint, just a place where the fiber yields instead of breaking. My skin is papered, matte, the color of straw left in the sun. The fine down on my forearms lifts when the slow basin wind turns a page somewhere behind me. On my shoulders the thatch is still short, still practical — not the great solar shingles of the elders on the sails, just enough to catch the shafted light while I work. My eyes are filmed. Milky vellum. I keep them half-lidded. My mouth is small, my nostrils barely open. I don't breathe deep. The air is too dry and full of iron to waste.

I drink light like the plant does, but not well enough to never need more, so I carry a tool — a beetle carapace, sanded to an edge, lashed to a splinter of stem-steel with dried vine — and I pare. Just a curl. From the edge of a fresh shelf-leaf where it grows corky off the old conveyor. I press it between my lips and let it soften. It tastes like warm paper and ozone.

My work is the passage.

This whole district — about the size of what you would have called a city — was starting to close. Not fast. A tendril an hour, a leaf a day. But if no one lifts and ties, the thatch seals. The light stops reaching the understory, the press chokes, the gantry crane overhead loses its rail. So every morning when the sky flickers — that stutter between panels — I walk.

I don't walk so much as transfer. Foot to leaf to plate to stem. Light, because gravity here forgets to insist. When I drop from the catwalk to the terrace below I have to remember to land. I drift a little too long, like those bales.

And I retie the knots.

There are dozens now, along my circuit. Small, neat loops of vine around girder-stems, holding vellum leaves back at shoulder height, at head height. Some I tied. Most were here before me, bleached almost white. I check them. Tighten one. Replace one that has gone brittle. Trim the air-roots again where they've grown down to brush a forehead. Press the felt litter along the seam with my feet until it's that few millimeters more compact, that slightly firmer path that says *someone passes here and knows how to pass without breaking.*

And I listen. And I answer.

Because we don't call out. You can't, here. I learned quickly that shouting is like shouting into a closet full of coats. So I have learned the other speech. I press my knuckle to the hollow stem that runs alongside the conveyor and I tap.

*tap. tap — tap.*

*Passage clear. Press twelve shedding again. Light good on seam.*

It goes through the floor-plate, through the stem, for kilometers. Somewhere, two or three districts over, another thin one, another knot-tier with thatch a little thicker or thinner than mine, will feel it in their feet and tap back. Sometimes no one answers for days. That's fine. Distributed is not lonely.

I am not old yet. I have not rooted.

I know where I will, though. I've already chosen it without meaning to. There's a place near the end of my circuit where the sail-canopy has a break — just a narrow tear where two vast leaves don't quite overlap — and the light falls clean and absolute to the floor. The plate there is always warm. No litter has drifted there; the light keeps it clear. When I pass it I always pause longer than I need to. My thatch prickles and lifts on its own.

One season I will stop walking. My feet will broaden and fray and find the rebar-bone under the composite and hold. My arms will lift. My face will tip up. I will not mind.

But not yet.

For now, I am still light enough to drift when I drop. Still dry enough to creak a little when I stretch. Still walking the seam, lifting one more curtain of warm vellum, tying one more small, intentional knot so that the next one — you — can pass without having to tear your way through.

If you put your hand to the hollow stem and tap, very low and very slow, I will feel you.

I am already listening through the floor.
