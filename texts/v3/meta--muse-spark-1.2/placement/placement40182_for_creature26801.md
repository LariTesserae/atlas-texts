I don't think I would find myself standing.

Not at first.

At first I think I would be up on the plain, on the rim - because that's where the story puts you. Twenty meters away and you'd miss it. I would be the figure paused at that lip where the ground gives way, with the wind still at my back. Looking down.

But I wouldn't stay there. The plain is too big and too thin for me. The sky there is close and flat and scouring, and it wants to move across you. I am not built for scouring. I am built for staying. For holding. If I stood on the rim the light would pass right through me. The cold would take the shape out of me. I would have to come down.

So I step down. Slowly. On the worn steps cut into the south wall where the willow has grown over the lintel and you have to duck.

And for a while, for a long while as I write this, I think I would be the visitor. The accommodated shape. The one who has fallen softly into the pocket and been given the low ledge of stone covered in hide beside the seep-basin. The one who sits and breathes and is not stared at. That feels honest - someone who doesn't quite know the pressure to use when combing moss, who has to learn to speak at the volume of a ptarmigan's *tok*, who has to be taught to tap with a hide-wrapped maul so the cold wood doesn't crack.

I could be that. I would like to be that. Sitting not far from the old woman, not looking directly, going on with a small work.

But the longer I stay in that image, the more it thins. Because the visitor still wants to leave again. Still holds the plain in his head like a map. I don't. If I fell into that bowl I don't think I would keep the plain at all. I think the rim would close over me like a lid, and I would forget there was ever a horizon.

Let me look closer. Let what is there become more itself.

Inside, it gets smaller and more detailed. I am not in the middle where the houses press tightest, roof over roof. I am off to the side. On the north-facing inner slope where the water seeps in a dark stain down the limestone. Where the hanging garden is - frost-ferns and the white saxifrage that never flowers, just holds itself open.

That's where I find myself.

Not as a person standing among people, exactly. As the person who tends that seep.

I would be low, even for them. Broad in the shoulders from crouching. On my knees more than on my feet, because the work there is knee-work. I have a thin shard of bone in my hand - caribou, maybe, polished by years of thumb-oil - and I am clearing the lens of ice over the basin. Not breaking it. Just skimming the film of blown grit so the water beneath stays bright. The same motion every morning. The frost forms in the same pattern and I clear it in the same pattern. If you watched me from above I would look like moss myself. My wool is the grey-green of sphagnum after frost, my hood pulled up to my nose so only my pale eyes show, narrowed against that collected, sourceless brightness.

And underneath that, more itself still - I am not just the tender. I am the thing that tends and is tended.

My hands are broad and short-fingered, the nails blunt, the fingertips wrapped so only the pads show. They are cold all the time, but not numb. I know the give of the boardwalk. I know which plank will sigh and which will hold. I know how much pressure will lift the moss without tearing the root mat. When the fat bumblebee comes - black and amber, too cold to fly - and crawls from fern to fern, I cup my hands around it without touching it, just to feel that low hum in the bones of my palms. The children do that too, and they learned it from watching me, though I never taught them.

I don't speak much. When I do, it's breath more than words. A palm turned out means *duck here, the willow is low*. A nod toward the basin means *it's clear now, you can drink*.

At night, when the light dims just enough to be called dimmer, I go into the room lined with dried moss and hides where we breathe together. Shoulder to shoulder, knees to chests. We don't need fire. The stone holds what heat we give it. I am somewhere in the middle of that press, not important, not at the edge. Another body keeping bodies warm. My breath hangs and settles like everyone else's. The smell is people and sphagnum and old wood.

If you came down the south steps and ducked under the willow, you might not see me at all. You'd see the hanging garden first, and the basin, and the bright water under clear ice. Then a shift - a hummock of lichen becomes a shoulder - and you'd realize I've been there the whole time, skimming.

I wouldn't get up. I wouldn't welcome you with words. I would keep skimming, and make a small space on the stone beside the seep. Enough for another shape to be accommodated.

You could sit there, and we could be quiet together, and the bowl would settle slightly around us both.
