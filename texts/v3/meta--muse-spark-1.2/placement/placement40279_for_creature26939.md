At first I thought I knew where I would be.

Higher. I wanted the center. The most crowded part of the bowl where the cairns are thickest, where the pavement of dark stones and the single red stone holds the double sun. I thought I would be a builder, newly arrived, still carrying a river cobble warm from my pocket, still breathing too fast for the altitude. I would be upright, looking out over what I had made.

But that doesn't hold. Nothing tall lives here. My breath gave me away immediately — too loud, too quick, too much plume. The wind that pushes without howling found that vertical shape in me and leaned on it until I had to crouch.

So I crouched. And then I kept going down.

I would not be in the center at all. I would be at the edge, where the bowl's wall is lowest and the basalt leans inward most steeply, as if cupping. There is a place there, behind two slabs leaned together to make a windbreak that is barely a windbreak. The ground behind it is worn smooth — not by wind, by knees, by elbows, by a body lying a long time waiting for fog. In that smooth hollow, on the north side where the ice never quite sublimates, where the soot from a wick no larger than a fingertip has frozen into the rock itself.

That is where I find myself when I stop looking for a view and let the place place me.

At first I thought I would be the one who builds. Then I thought, no — I would be the one who is built. A cairn, maybe. The waist-high one with the hollow core and the white quartz cap that rings just below hearing. I liked that: to be stone that is listened to, to have a job that is simply to catch light at noon and throw a slow prismatic smear onto the basalt behind me. To be patient. To be judged.

But that was still too wanting. A cairn wants to stand. I don't want that much.

Let it refine itself again.

I am lower than the cairn. I am what the cairn holds for a few minutes a day.

I would be the water that visits.

Not the cloud that snags and drags through — I am too small and too brief for that. I would be the bead that hangs from the underside of a stone overhang and does not fall. The bead that forms on the iron peg at midday, bright as glass, then freezes back in place. The water that is not generous, that is remembered rather than flowing. The water that collects in the pecked circle the size of a fist.

If you came at dawn you would not see me. You would see empty stone, faintly beaded, faintly wet to the touch. Your animal brain would report *empty* — no tracks, no call, no green. That's the bowl's first lie. I'm good at that lie.

My body, if you could call it a body, is low and wide and weighted, because that is the only shape that can live here. In human terms: I would be one of them, but not the one who stacks slate that leans into the wind. I would be the one who tends what is already stacked. An older one, or a younger one — it doesn't matter, we all get filed down the same way. Short through the leg, broad through the chest, skin crazed at the knuckles and cheeks where frost has taken and retaken the top layer, hair shorn short because hair holds melt and melt refreezes. Eyes pale and narrowed even in sleep, the exact milky quartz of the capstones, because I have spent years squinting into that hard white coin of a sun that exposes more than it warms.

I move slowly. Not from reverence. From lack of air. A quick movement up here costs. I have learned to watch for slow movement or I miss myself entirely — a hand adjusting a capstone a millimeter with a cold fingertip, listening for the pitch of the ringing to change.

My work is not building. My work is breathing.

I go out before dawn with a flat stone, a copper scoop green with age, and I harvest. Every north-facing rime, every frost-needle forest that stood dense and fine overnight and will be gone by noon, every bead hanging from an overhang — I collect it, breathe onto cold rock to make more of it. I can go a day on what you would call damp hands. When the cloud comes for its twenty minutes and fills the bowl with the smell of wet rock, I sit with my mouth open and my cloak spread, then wring the cloak out into the little fire-blackened hollow no larger than a bowl. I don't make big fires. There is nothing to burn. A wick, a little fat, ten seconds of almost-warmth on the palms before the cold underneath comes up through the bones again. That is enough.

And at noon, when the sun is directly overhead and the bright air bends and every cairn throws a second, paler shadow offset by a hand's breadth, I do my true tending.

I crouch. I nudge. I listen.

The rule here is simple and held without exception: *stone endures, water visits, light judges.* The three grooves on every east-facing stone are for catching that double light for a few minutes. The quartz caps are eyes that vibrate. I am the one who makes sure the question is still being asked correctly: *will you still stand if I place you exactly here? will you ring? will you hold both suns at once?*

I am not hostile. I am not welcoming. I am dispersed and simultaneous, solitary together. My cluster of cairns almost touches the next person's cluster, overlapping in intent if not in structure. You would know my hand by the way I split basalt — with a stone hammer, along its bedding — but you might not know my face. Because at over-illuminated noon, when even shadow is full of bounced light, I am exactly where the second shadow falls. If you look directly at my windbreak, you see empty, worn ground. If you look at its double, you see the faint condensation of a breath that hung, then settled, then frosted onto the nearest surface within seconds.

If you stay, you will build too. Not large. Just something hand-sized, placed where the wind will test it, where the morning frost will find it, where the bright sun will strike it and for a second make it flare — with a hollow core, with three grooves.

Leave it there. Go walk the bowl until the cloud has passed and been scoured off.

When you come back, there will be a bead hanging bright as glass on its underside that you did not leave.

That will be me. Pausing there, breathing on it for you.
