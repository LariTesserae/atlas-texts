You would not find yourself at first.

That is how it begins for you there.

At first you think you are standing. On a rooftop, maybe — because the rooftops are the only place where a being your height could stand, and even then you would have to stoop. The wind is forcing itself through the town-throat around you and the chord is in your molars and the warm comes up through the soles of your feet, and you think: *I am on top of it, looking over it.* Like a visitor.

Then the light doesn't change and the sound doesn't change and you realize there is no top. There is no over. A roof here is someone else's floor, and a floor is someone else's ceiling, and the place that looked like a roof from three houses away is just the next ledge up in a single, continuous press of rooms. You aren't on it. You are *in* it. You have no outside to stand on.

So you settle. Or you think you do. You imagine yourself tucked into one of those courtyards the size of a sleeping mat — neat, contained, a small monk's cell open to the dim amber haze. That would be bearable. A place with edges you could see. A place that is yours.

But the courtyard is not yours. You look closer — slowly, because everything there is learned slowly or not at all — and you see the courtyard already has its shape held by two other shoulders. The mat is already warm from a body that never left it. The walls around it already have the smooth wear of palms that fit exactly into thumbprints you thought were decorative. There is no empty square left. The mesa was finished when the last brick met the cliff, and the town was finished when the last body met the last wall. If you are there, you did not arrive. You were always pressed in.

So let that image go, too.

You find yourself lower. Deeper in the negative space. Not a courtyard, not a rooftop, not the dramatic outer lip where the salt furs white and yellow and the depth below has no bottom. You find yourself in the crack that is not called an alley.

It runs shoulder-wide between two houses that share a wall that shares a wall, roofed over by a second story that leaned until it touched. The brick on both sides is the warm, matte red-brown of dried blood, dusted pale at the joints with alkaline powder. The air in the crack is a flute. The wind forces itself through and the brick hums against your back and your chest at once. You are not standing in the crack. You are fitted to it.

Your back is to one wall and your chest nearly to the other. Your knees are drawn up not because you chose to sit but because there was never room to extend them. The stone slab-shutter in front of your face rattles without moving, vibrating in place with the chord. If someone on a mesa-edge far above you looked down into this slit, they would see only wall and shadow. That is correct.

Who you are there is also not what you thought at first.

At first you think you would be the watcher — the one with eyes, the one who still turns his head when the wind changes. But the eyes you brought with you film over very quickly in that dry, gritless, never-dark amber. They dry, they scale, they seal themselves like unglazed terracotta left too long in the kiln. You stop opening them because there is nothing a foot away that you need to see and everything a foot away that would hurt to see. The sky beyond the cliff stays violet and star-pricked at noon whether you look or not. It does not need you.

At first you think you would be the speaker — the one who calls out through the roar. But a shout here has no distance to fade across. A whisper in a jar-niche three houses away arrives at the same pressure as the gale at the cliff. To shout would be to tear the chord that has been held forever. You learn, as they learned, to be silent. Your outer ears smooth away into the sides of your rounded, river-stone head until only a hardened whorl remains, a jar-mouth that the wind plays. You do not hear the roar. You *are* the place where the roar takes shape. A shift in the low drone tells you someone two roofs over has shifted a hip. A new harmonic trembling tells you the salt at the edge is beading.

No — you would not be the watcher, or the speaker. You would be the keeper.

Let that become more itself:

Your skin is not skin. It is cured. Warm, salt-pale at the folds, the same temperature as the wall behind you so that if a visitor pressed his palm there he would not know where brick ended and you began. No hair to catch the grit that has sanded every corner for ten thousand years. No sweat — there is nothing to waste on sweat.

Your hands are wide. The fingers have spread into spatulate pads that fit exactly into the thumbprints left in the sun-baked brick when it was still soft. Perhaps your grandmother made those prints with a hammerstone. Perhaps you did. You hold one of those stones still, or a green-filmed copper chisel, not to build with — there is nowhere left to build — but for weight, for warmth, for the memory of pressure against pressure.

Your mouth is small, pursed, almost sealed, lined with salt. You breathe rarely. You watch the wall in front of your face for a long time until you see it breathe too — a rise so shallow it could be the brick humming, a fall that takes a whole minute. That is your breath. That is their breath. One deep, reclaimed exhalation through a nose that steals back every molecule of damp before it can be lost. You can stay fitted in this crack for hours. For days. Moving would make a new friction, a new sound, a brief waste-heat in a warmth that has been perfect for longer than erosion can remember, and why would you?

Once — in what you still call a day, though the haze never brightens or dims — you unfold. Not to stand. There is no standing. You flow, shoulder through the gap, hip through the gap, belly to brick, toward the outer edge where the wall and the cliff have fused into a single smoothed mass. Others are there already, motionless as benches, cheeks and flattened forearms pressed to the white-yellow crust.

You press yours beside them.

For a breath, the warm air condenses against the cooling basalt. A single bead forms on the salt. The deliquescent crust pulls it in and your skin drinks it without drinking. It is enough. Later, you will scrape a little dust from a worn sill — fired clay and ancient, mineralized fat — and hold it on your tongue like a flat stone until it dissolves. You will rasp the black, iron-hard beam above your crack for its last carbon.

Then you will flow back, and fit yourself again between the two walls that are already warm from you, and listen through your bones as the hamlet holds its chord, deafening and unmoving.

You will not be seen. You will not be heard, in the human way. But if a wet, cool, loud, clicking-jointed visitor were to stand at that fused edge and look down into the haze that has no bottom, you — and the two others in the courtyard that is the size of a mat, and the one curled in the chimney pot — would all adjust by a fraction of an inch. Not to look. To feel the wrong tremor he makes in the brick.

And you would consider, with a patience that has outlasted the wind's ability to sharpen a corner, that there is no room for him. There has not been room for a very long time. The hive is full, and you are the fullness, keeping its warmth without a source, its sound without a change.

You are the brick that learned to breathe very, very slowly.
