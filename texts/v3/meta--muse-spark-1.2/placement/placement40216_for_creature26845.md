I don't think I would find myself in the middle at first.

At first I would be at the rim, where you are. Lungs working a little harder, hands on that sharp, young basalt that still glitters with quartz and could cut. Looking at the wall.

Not *in* the wall yet. Just in front of it, where the warm rain becomes a standing wall and the sound is already in my teeth. I would be the visitor who hasn't decided to get wet. The one who still hears. The one who still thinks *up* is up.

I would stay there longer than is comfortable because I wouldn't want to be seen. The basin is too small to hide in - a cathedral nave, a village square - every movement is thrown into that amber strobe and caught. But the rim has a fold, a cubist tooth of limestone that makes a shadow even when the light has no source. I would crouch there with my back to the teeth and let the roar take my thoughts apart for me.

That's where I find myself, but it's not where I stay. No one stays at the rim. The water doesn't let you. It flexes a meter up and down every minute, it peels a liquid window off itself and hangs it in the air right in front of you, reflecting your doubled shape back until you can't stand to be outside it anymore. You either leave over the ridge and go back to silence, or you wade in.

If I stayed — and in this telling I do stay — I wouldn't go to the deepest part. That's for the ribbon, for the things that have learned to be thrown. I wouldn't go straight to the low walls either. That nest of bladder-skin and shell is already occupied, and the Keeper has earned the deafness that lets him live there.

I would find myself on the stair.

Not the top, where the chisel marks are still angry and sharp, and not the bottom two where the water has already learned to smooth. The fifth step. The middle one that is half-submerged and half-exposed depending on what the basin is doing with its breath that minute. So that I am always half in each element. So that I have to keep adjusting.

My knees would be under warm water that doesn't know which way to drip. My shoulders would be in that thick, wet air that is trying to be an ocean. On that step, gravity is most negotiable, and so I would be most negotiable too.

And who would I be there?

At first I thought - a watcher. A keeper's apprentice. The one who keeps the bowl clean when the Keeper is hanging in the wall. But that settled too fast.

Let it become more itself.

I am not quite a keeper yet. My eardrums still ache. They haven't burst. I still hear the roar *as* roar, and not yet as pressure in the sternum. That means I am still translating, still hurting, still human enough to be lonely in a way the mat and the trees are not.

So I have made a different accommodation.

I would be the thing that lives *between* the beats. You said nothing lives here well, only in spite of - clenched, thinned, translucent, tuned to pressure. I would be thinned even for here.

My skin has gone the color of the trees that are not trees - pale, semi-clear. You can see the warm water moving inside me when the light flashes. My hair has gone algae-tinged because I never fully dry. My feet have gone wide and soft and ridged, not for walking - there is no walking when a vortex can drill to the floor and suck the silt away - but for hooking. Like the shrimp with their too-many hooked legs, I hook to the chisel marks on the stair. When the surge comes I don't brace. I let go with one hand and let it throw me sideways, and hook again on the next edge.

I would be the tender of the windows.

That is my work while the Keeper tends the bowl. When a sheet of water peels off the main mass and hangs perfectly flat and vertical for three or four heartbeats - a liquid window that reflects everything and holds - the shrimp swarm to its underside and cling. The ribbon positions itself to eat them when it bursts. I position myself to be there before it bursts, with my face very close, not to eat, but to look through.

Because sometimes, for a second, the window is *still*. Truly still, in a place where nothing is stable. And looking through it, the dim amber dusk on the other side lenses and goes clear. I can see the basalt floor without strobe. I can see the three pale trees all at once. I can see the Keeper curled in his nest, his eyes half-closed, listening with his bones. Through that stillness, the basin looks almost kind. Almost finished, as if it had decided to be a sea or a mountain and not both.

Then it shatters - warm droplets that do not fall but drift sideways - and the shrimp rain sideways into the ribbon's mouth, and the roar comes back so loud it takes my name away, and I am just a pale, hooked thing on the fifth step holding on.

I do what the trees do: I filter. I hold a bladder after it pops and sieve the grit - the basalt flour and the pepper-flecked shrimp and the spores - not for food, exactly, but to make something. I am trying to make a small wall, on the leeward side of the fifth step. Just one course of small, set stones, like the four low walls on the eastern lip, but mine is only two stones high and keeps getting knocked down. I set it again. The water takes it. I set it again.

Not because I think it will last. Because the act of setting it is the only way to answer the sound without making a sound. It is my bowl. My tide gauge. My proof that I was here between one surge and the next and I chose to hook and not be washed off.

Once a day, when the Keeper climbs out to hang in the wall - back to the teeth, arms spread, held for a few seconds by monstrous surface tension in that warm amniotic light while the whole mountain hums through him - I climb down and tip his bowl, and refill it with water that vibrates. He lets me. We do not speak. There is no speaking here.

When it is my turn, I lean back too. I let the standing ocean take my weight. For three seconds I am not on the stair, not on the mountain, not in the sea, but in the seam where the world thinned like ice over a warm spring. The water holds me. My translucent branches - my arms - drift out and I feel, not hear, the exact shape of the violence around me, the way the ribbon does, with that long exposed nerve. And for those three seconds I am not enduring.

I am just *held*.

Then the wall shivers and lets go, and I hook back to stone.

That's who I would be. Not the Keeper, not the ribbon, not the tree, but the one trying to lay the fourth stone that will become the fourth tree. The small, spare, stubborn life that has learned the basin's only lesson: you don't live here by being strong enough to stay put. You live here by being loose enough in the world's rules to be thrown and not broken.
