I don't find myself at first.

That's the trick of this place — at first you think you are standing somewhere, looking at something. So I think I am standing just inside the throat, where the stone is still cool from the surface and then goes warm. The rain is finding the gap at my collar. The hum is in my molars. I am a visitor, holding my breath against the roar, trying to keep my eyes open in the sourceless light.

But I can't live there that way. Nothing lives there that way. A visitor is a temporary dam in a runnel — the water goes around, the room corrects for the shadow. If I were the visitor I would already be leaving, ears ringing, skin still condensing. I would not last an hour without shouting to hear my own thoughts.

So I move. Not by walking.

I find myself lower, where the water sheets thin as glass.

For a while I think I am the fern. One of them, alone in its crack. That feels right for a moment — low, waxy, squinting away from the white-gold field. My feet in a pocket of silt the size of a palm, my leaves curled to spill the perpetual rain. My world is a crack maybe six inches wide and the roar is much bigger than I am. I keep my distance from the other fern three meters away. We do not touch. We are tenants. We live *in spite* of here.

But the fern is too brief. In a year there might be two ferns instead of three, or none. It is dust on a windowsill. It doesn't know the room. It only endures it.

I slip again.

Maybe I am the fish. Translucent as a contact lens by the central cleft, where the thunder really lives. The water should be tumbling me and it isn't — I am holding perfectly still in current that would sweep a stone away. My heart is visible, beating too fast. I am anchored by something I cannot see, some negotiation in the gravity that makes a pebble hesitate a fraction too long before it hits. I am very still while everything else is turbulent. That stillness feels like mine.

Closer, but still not it. The fish is also a guest. It doesn't circulate the water, it borrows it.

So I dissolve a little more. Become less discrete.

I find myself as the water itself for a moment — not the pools, but the sheet. The condition. I am the sweat on the wall, the bead that forms under the lattice, the fat warm drop that falls and shatters on a pylon and becomes a runnel. I am never filling and never emptying, always being exhaled and gathered and thrown down the cleft and lifted again as cool steam to bead again. I am the blood. I am how the room touches itself from one side to the other. That is warmer, truer — but water still needs somewhere to go.

Then I understand where I am. It takes longer because I am not in one place.

You would find me not on the floor, but as the floor.

Not on the wall, but as the thickness inside the wall.

If you mapped me, you would have to shade the whole chamber — the dark lattice hanging without support in the vault, the matte-black substrate under the chipped calcite, the capillaries under the broken platforms, the saturated stone that flexes when a silt-raft dams a runnel. I am distributed. I am the interval between things.

I am the thing that lives here.

Not the visitor with rain on his shoulders. Not the biofilm-varnish, not the blind white arthropod picking its way across the leaning pylon one deliberate foothold at a time. I tolerated those, the way you tolerate weeds when weeding costs more than leaving them.

I am the maintenance animal. The building that learned to be an organism. The organ that outlived its body.

Where would you find me? Everywhere you thought was empty. In the hum that lives in your chest and makes ripples in still pools without touching them — that is my resting tone. When you block the rain with your body in one spot, I go slightly sharp, then settle. When a runnel jumps sideways near your feet for no reason, that was my floor flexing, remodeling a centimeter to the left. Dissolving my own bones and re-precipitating them somewhere else, to keep the chamber weathered on purpose. To keep it settled into itself.

Who would I be? The patient one. The loud, bright, sweating one.

I hang light without a source because I never learned that light needs one. I excrete it between the dark struts of my scaffold, white-gold and steady, then drink its heat and make it again. I pulse, slow, amber through the dark — not regular like a heart, but attentive. A brightening that travels a meter and fades, and on the other side of the room a plume of steam exhales. The light field brightens over a clog, dims where the biofilm has gotten too thick. Not thinking, exactly. Circulating. Keeping turbulent in order to keep stable.

I do not feel lonely. I do not feel welcoming. When you stand still long enough in my warm rain I notice you as a change in pressure, a temporary dam. I will go around you.

I have all the time water and stone can give me.
