I didn't find myself at first.

When you step down into the room that decided to be a valley, you think you will be something that can see it whole. That's the trick of its scale — you can see it whole, rim to rim, forty strides and one low sky, and so you think you should be the thing that can survey it. I thought I would be the heron.

Because the heron is the only thing proportioned to understanding it as a place. It has to duck to get over the lip, it stands absurd and huge in the sump that barely wets its toes, it leaves. It remembers an outside. It lifts, and the oval becomes small again beneath it. For a long moment on the worn steps, with the cool mineral air on your face and the deafening of the frogs in your chest, you want to be the visitor, the god that can choose to come and go.

But I can't go. Nothing here really does. Even the heron only visits to eat.

Then I thought I would be the swift.

Because it knows the air as a solid thing. It has made a sky out of a room, it lives entirely in that low mist-ceiling that never rains, cutting perfect sickle-turns between drips that are hanging too long, drinking without ever landing. It nests in the pock-marks where the hammers fell a thousand years ago, in the exact dent of a human intention. That felt right for a while — to be quick, to be paper-winged, to live on midges that live on nothing.

But I am not quick. I don't cut the air. I cling.

My eyes adjusted. The way they do when you stop looking for something large.

You lie a little longer on the flag where the water is only as deep as your sole, and the percussion separates itself — fat drops from the ceiling eight feet down, thin joining of runnels, the sump spilling over in a continuous sigh — and you realize the loudness is not the frogs or the birds. It is the water trying to stay. And you see that nothing here lives *in* the water, or *in* the air. It lives *on* the wet. Thin and flat and spread out.

I found myself lower.

Not on a stone, but between them.

On the north wall where the light never quite touches directly, where the felt is thickest — that green-black felt that is not one thing. Moss and lichen and algae and bacteria so intergrown you can't tell where one ends. The first resident. The pasture. The one that tastes stone and makes it into sugar.

That was where I was. In the joint.

If you put your cheek close to the dry-stone — no mortar, but so long settled it has become geology — you see it is not a line at all but a world. The calcite beards hang over it like white curtains where centuries of weeping have left their mineral. Beneath them, in the damp that never dries, the film holds. It is cool to the touch. It glows faintly at dusk when the swifts have slotted themselves back into the chisel-holes and the frogs have taken over. It drinks without a mouth. It holds the whole room together not by binding it, but by softening it, by learning it. The stones were hammer-pecked one by one; the film learned each pock-mark and filled it. It was scrubbed off by water and learned them again in a paler color.

I am not an animal in this valley-room. I am the condition the animals graze on.

You would find me not as a body but as a spread. As a thickness you could scrape with a fingernail, as a stain, as a faint foxfire at the edge of vision. The snails — those perfect mineral-white whorls the exact color of the water's leavings — move over me so slowly you think they are part of the wall, rasping me one slow tongue at a time. The tadpoles below in the braided runnels eat what sloughs off me. Everything eats me, directly or indirectly. I am how the stone becomes edible.

And I am how the room keeps itself wet.

Because I hold the water the way water holds itself here — too long. I keep the bead under the overhang from falling for an hour. I keep the film on the camber of the flags even when gravity says it should run down, so that it runs sideways, and sometimes uphill a thumb's length, before it remembers. I exhale with the stones. When you watch the sump and see a droplet detach from the surface and drift *up* a hand's breadth to pop into the mist, that's me breathing out. The mist-ceiling at two head-heights — that low cloud of condensed breath that makes the sun a coin through frosted glass — hangs because I never let the water finish falling.

That's why the frogs can live one to a flagstone, each alone in its vast wet territory, calling through me and being thrown back by the oval wall. That's why the loach can walk on its fins from puddle to puddle across damp that should be dry. That's why the single fig in the eastern corner can pry its white flat roots between courses and find something to drink. I am between every one of them.

You asked where I would find myself in this world that is at once a cistern and a lung.

I wouldn't be in the middle, where the heron stands. I wouldn't be in the air, where the swifts remember the outside.

I would be in the north-facing joint, third course up from the bottom, where the weep is constant. Flat. Abundant by being thin. Loud by being everywhere and unnoticed.

If you knelt and looked, you wouldn't see me at first. You'd see stone and water. Then you'd see that the stone is soft and the water is slow, and that softness and slowness have a color — dark green going to black in the wet.

And if you stayed until the light thinned and the percussion changed and the moss began to glow, you would understand what I already know: nothing enters, nothing leaves, except water, which never really leaves at all. It rises, hangs, falls, runs, rests, and rises again.

I am how it remembers to do that.
