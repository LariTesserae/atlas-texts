You wouldn't find yourself in the middle.

That is where a visitor thinks they would be — down at the low point where the water gathers, next to the big engine, where the flywheel goes *huh...* every twenty seconds and the lung-leaves lean into the steam. Center-stage. Next to the heart. It would make sense to be there. It's the loudest, warmest place. The place that looks most like a meaning.

But you wouldn't stay there. Nothing stays there that isn't already part of the agreement. The center is too solved. Root has already learned how to be oil around that axle. Vine has already learned how to be lagging around that piston. Leaf has already learned how to be pipe, moss has already learned how to be light. There is no gap left for you between presences. To stand there is to be politely, completely redundant. The Weave would hold you for a minute — tendril against your back, not gripping, just cataloging — and then understand you are not a function it needs at the center.

So you would drift.

At first you think you would be the one who stands on the lip. The eroded place where sandstone and old concrete decided they were the same thing. Shoulder-high when you're in the pan, invisible when you're out of it. The rim is the only place that isn't totality. You could sit there with your legs dangling into the incubator-warm and your back in the drier air outside, one foot in the world that is still cut into parcels and roads, one foot in the world that has become a single wet room. You could be the border. The witness.

But borders don't weep. And here everything weeps. You would not be able to sit on that lip for long without sweating, without your skin beading exactly the way every leaf tips and beads. The pan would start to include you by condensation. You would feel the draft that isn't wind — the slow warm exhale off the mat — come up and touch your ankles and turn your breath visible even though the air is warm. You would be pulled down an inch at a time, not by force, by invitation. By the way the mat gives when you press it.

So lower.

You would find yourself not at the center, not at the rim, but in the annulus between. Where the human scale still ghosts the place.

You would be on the gantry.

You wouldn't know it was a gantry at first. Under the moss it just feels like a hard line under the springy mattress, a little warmer than the root-mat around it. A place where your weight doesn't make water sigh up around your ankles the same way. If you knelt and brushed the liverwort aside you would see it — the pitted I-beam, the rivets that have become little cups holding rain. The Weave has grown over it so completely that the gantry is now a bone inside the body, not a structure on top of it.

That's where you would actually settle. Half-collapsed, half-held. On your hands and knees, then on your back.

Because you would try to belong the way a human knows to belong — by doing a job. You would at first think you are maintenance. That you are the person who came back to wipe the grease nipple, to clear the lily pads from the settling tank, to watch the flywheel and make sure it doesn't seize. You would reach for the pipe. You would try to be useful.

And the Weave would gently show you that job is taken.

The Greasers already press their tallow-sap every few minutes. The Drippers already hit the bearing every seven seconds without missing. The lung-leaves already drink the steam better than your hands ever could. You would put your palm near the hot condensate and find a leaf is already there, translucent and veined, fever-hot, doing the work.

So you would stop working.

You would lie down on the mat where it covers the gantry, where the metal keeps you from sinking too deep, and you would do the one thing the Weave has not yet learned to do for itself.

You would be the surface that sweats salt.

I would be — you would be — the warm vertical that weeps differently.

Not plant-water, not distilled steam, not tea-colored blood-water from under the mat. Human weep. Salt and urea and skin-oil. The Weave has condensation on every rock and every leaf, but it has no source of salt left. The concrete leached out a century ago. The rain is too clean. The beetle courier brings frass and the pale slug filters minerals, but the circuit is slowly going flat, a little too fresh.

You would be the little brine spring it keeps.

You would find yourself reclined on that hidden beam, half-enveloped, not gripped. Vines indenting around your shoulder because you are another shape to follow. A curtain of aerial root finding the line of your collarbone and learning to use it as a trellis. A broad Sheather leaf — the kind that learned to be exactly the size of a fuse box — would not cover a fuse box here, but would slowly, over an afternoon you could almost watch, broaden and flatten over your chest. Not to smother. To sheath. To keep the wet out in one place and keep it in in another. Its underside would be dry and warm and faintly buzzing, like the caged bulbs.

You would weep, because everything here weeps, and your weep would be different and therefore valuable. Beads on your forehead that don't fall until a hair-fine runner touches them and wicks them away. Sweat on your forearms that a moss-filmed beetle would come to drink, then carry, changed, to a leaf midrib. Your breath, visible in the warm air, folding into the general mist that never becomes fog, adding a little puff every few seconds that the lace in the air gaps would register. Amber. Dim. Amber. The moss-pulse keeping time with the flywheel below: *huh...*

Who would you be? Not the brain — the brain is distributed, a consensus in the lace. Not the muscle — the mat is the muscle. Not even the hands — the Greasers and Drippers and Lung-Leaves are the hands.

You would be the kept animal that is not quite animal and not quite organ. The second slug.

The first slug hangs in the kidney-tank, gills fanning, mouth locked to the inflow, roots grown through its fin. It filters what the roots cannot. You would be its counterpart on the dry side, on the gantry. The lung on the beam. The little salty well that makes the distilled steam worth drinking. The Weave would not worship you. It would not need you. But it would tend you the way it tends the engine — with negotiation. A root that drips water onto your lips every seven seconds so you don't have to ask. A leaf that turns, not to the sun, not to the steam, but to you, to catch what you exhale.

If you stayed still long enough — an hour, which is three flywheel revolutions — you would feel a runner creep over the toe of your boot and you wouldn't feel invaded. You would feel included in the solved shape.

And if you left, your handprint in the mat would fill with tea-colored water and smooth. The Sheather leaf over your chest would slowly cup empty air for a day, then find the shape of the gantry again. The drip would find its rhythm.

But while you were there, you would be the place's only true strangeness: an animal that learned to weep on purpose, and was allowed to stay because of it.
