I don't find myself right away.

At first, on the way down, I think I know where I would be.

At the top, where the road tips over the basalt lip and becomes those rounded stairs, I think I would be the rail itself — that dark-polished iron, cool under the hand, holding the memory of every palm that has steadied itself on the way down. That's a good thought for a few turns of the switchback. It is patient. It does not move. It lets everything else slow around it.

But then the air cools and my breath starts to cloud and vanish, cloud and vanish, and I realize I am breathing, and the rail does not breathe. So not that.

Lower down, where the light stops reaching and the amber bulbs begin to halo in the mist, I think I would be one of the pigeons up in the crane teeth. High enough to see the whole bowl at once like a room, dark and damp-feathered, tucked into the machine that is no longer a machine, cooing at a volume the town allows. But I am too heavy for that. I don't want to be above. I want to be level with the ticking.

On the floor, walking beside the canal — that slow black-clear stream with the blind fish and the orange carp gliding exactly under the surface — I think for a long time I would be the fish. Or the water itself. It would be honest to be the water, remembering its level to the millimeter without trying, taking an hour to return to where it was even if you pump it dry, turning each corner as if thinking about it first. But water doesn't tend. It is tended. It is breathed.

I walk slower without deciding to. That's how it happens. You don't decide.

There is a whole empty mill hall to my left, corrugated roof, line shaft turning so slowly overhead you have to watch it to know it's moving, and inside it — one workbench. One kettle. Still steaming. I think: I could be the person who left the kettle. The one who will be back in an hour or tomorrow, who doesn't need to own the hall to be responsible for it. I could be the loom woman, established the way the brick is, beaded the way the pipes are, oiled at the knuckles. But when I pass her doorway she doesn't look up, and I understand that to be her you have to have already been beaded for a long time.

I keep going until the alleys narrow and the warm comes off the central works. The flywheel is there, house-tall, turning and turning and not driving anything in particular anymore, or driving everything in aggregate. The diesel goes *puh* every seven seconds, politely, and a puff of steam condenses instantly. I could be that diesel, but it is too punctual. I am not punctual. I am attentive.

So I follow the sound of the water where it changes.

Under the works, where the concrete service plant still shows through the brick mill built over it, there is a place where the warm discharge from the generators — low, humming, cables warm to the touch despite the cool — meets the cold seep coming straight out of the weeping wall. The wall there is black with moss and bright green with ferns in the seams, and the water gathers in a slow pool before it finds the canal again. It is not on any of the three or four streets. You have to duck under a catwalk where the lichen glows grey-white to find it.

That's where I would be found. Not that anyone would find me quickly. The spaces between things are respected here and I am in one of them.

If you did come, you would see me on a low stool that has rusted to its varnish and then stopped. Rubber boots cracked at the ankle and then stopped cracking. Wool cardigan never dry, never rotting. Forearms beaded. Forehead beaded. The rim of my mug beaded. Breath that clouds and vanishes and then doesn't come again for a while.

My hand is in the water up to the wrist. Utterly still.

In the pool are the colonies. The barnacles made of glass and copper wire that no one remembers planting, that the older residents say the factory grew, and they are not joking, exactly. They click. They open and filter the mist itself when the water rises and recedes on its own period that has nothing to do with rain.

I am not harvesting them. I am not cleaning them. I am listening.

That is what I would be — the listener at the seam.

Not the one who oils the flywheel or sweeps condensation from the switchboard with a flat brush, back and forth, though I do those things when the hall needs it. Not the boy who marks the ledger where the water level is, even though everyone knows it, in pencil, numbers barely changing year to year, though I keep that ledger too on other afternoons. I would be the one whose job is to keep the interval.

Because sound carries but does not accumulate here, every click arrives clean and distinct three streets away, and no noise ever becomes background. The town refuses to be loud. So someone has to sit and let the clicks be foreground without adding to them. Someone has to let the warm and the cold meet without fixing the meeting.

I have the eyes for it now. Pupils large even at noon because no direct sun ever hits the floor. I can see in the amber dim that never reaches the top of the basin. Outside I would squint. Down here I see clearly — the orange carp, the palm-sized moths furred on the bulbs until the bulbs are furry, the salamander the length of my forearm clinging to the weeping wall at head height with a clean outline around him where the moss won't grow.

I don't talk much. When I do, it carries low and intact: "—left the spanner by the weir—" and an hour later someone answers me on my way back as if no time had passed, because for us it hasn't.

I am not old the way people are old outside. I am established. I have learned the sparse economy: one person to one hall, one voice per alley, three lit windows out of twelve is correct occupancy. I have learned that rust does not mean decay here. It is a finish. I have rusted to my finish.

You would find me there in the deeper dimming that is called night, when the radio static in one of the terraces sounds like rain and the laundry steams on the line dripping at the same rate as the rock, and the flywheel keeps turning because I am part of the distributed metabolism that lets it.

Human skin, pigeon skin, salamander skin, brick skin, copper-wire-and-glass skin — all beaded, all at flywheel speed. The town is the organism and it does not hire. It incorporates.

I did not come down to fix or buy or study. I came down and kept walking slower until the ticking slowed my need to fill silence. And then I found the stool waiting in its correct place until the thought that left it there resumed, which was my thought, and I sat down and put my hand in.

If you need me, I'm where the water is thinking about the corner before it turns, listening to it think.
