You wouldn't find me at first.

If you came down that long grade, your brakes hot and then cold, following UP arrows back the wrong way until the road flattened and the air turned to glass in your mouth — if you stood at the mouth of the Basin and let your eyes adjust to weather that lives under a roof, you'd look past me.

Because at first I think I would be one of the three in the switching house. That's the obvious place to be human. Inside the foam-board and blankets, near the kettle that never turns off. The place we've made less-cold. I picture my hands around chipped enamel, my gloves steaming.

But that's not where I am. That's where I go to remember I am still supposed to be warm.

If you waited. If you let the forty-second inhale of the exchangers happen three or four times, and let your own breathing fall into it — you'd start to see me where anything lives here: in a pool, with a lot of dark between.

Find the grid.

Find a light that has just been changed. One of the amber ones that throws a true circle, not the ones that flicker. Where the light hits the wet slab it makes a second sun in the water. And at the foot of that pole, there's the lichen. A perfect white compass ring, patient as frost.

I am on the edge of that circle.

I am the one with the squeegee.

Not all day. But at the start of the air-cycle, when the frost on the girders begins to melt and run, I am out there. You'd hear me before you saw me — not my voice, the rubber blade on concrete. A long, patient *shhhhk* as I pull silt from a drain channel. Concrete dust and iron flake and the pale skin the slick sheds. If I don't clear it, the water stops moving. If the water stops moving, the sedge drowns. If the sedge goes, the algae has nowhere to thicken.

We take turns. But I took this task and kept it.

I wear the patched workwear. The cuffs are black and never dry. My boots are wet inside and out; I've stopped noticing. My beard is crusted where my breath freezes to it and then melts and freezes again. I move slowly — not from tiredness, but from economy. Every fast movement makes heat and the building will take it. It will dilute you if you let it. So I have learned to take up as little room as possible. To shiver only when I must. To wait.

You'd find me kneeling, though there is no reason to kneel. The squeegee has a long handle. But I kneel anyway, to be close to the one clump in this sector.

It is a small, rust-colored tuft of sedge in the joint between two slabs, twenty meters from the next one. It leans southwest, all of them do, toward the exchangers. Its seed heads hold a bead of frozen mist that rattles when the draft comes. I clear around it. I don't touch it. The roots are white wire in the crack. It has been here longer than my rotation.

Who would I be? 

At first I thought - human. Keeper. The one who changes the bulbs so the grid doesn't go out one by one, the one who keeps faith with light. But the longer I stay down there, in the writing of myself, the more I realize I am not the keeper so much as the kept.

I am closer to the moth.

You know the moths - grey as aggregate, living their whole life under one cone of amber. They don't fly *to* the light, they orbit it, slow and level, circling a cold sun. I do that. My orbit is larger - from the switching house to bulb 47 to the sump and back - but it's an orbit. When the bulb above me buzzes, I falter. When it goes out, I feel that same panicked urge to make the crossing through fifty meters of blue-grey dim to the next pool, knowing most don't make it. I understand why they are found in the channels, wings spread, perfectly preserved.

I am also the lichen.

I grow outward millimeter by millimeter around what little warmth I can find. The kettle. The cable trench. That one amber pool where I have chosen to do my work. I don't spread to touch the other pools. There is too much wet dark between. I make a circle and stay inside it and measure time by how far it has widened.

And I am, in a way that takes longest to admit, the Basin itself, or a small part of its breathing.

Because after enough cycles, you stop maintaining it and realize it is maintaining you. It gives you water to pull, and cold to define your warmth against, and a task that prevents you from thinking too much about the scale. It takes your breath and turns it into mist that falls as rain on the far side of the valley where you will never walk. It takes the sound of your squeegee and folds it into the ticking of a thousand drips. It is very patiently making you part of its watershed.

So where would you find me?

Not in the middle. Not in the dark between lights.

You'd find me at 06:40 in the air-cycle, on my knees in six millimeters of sheet water under Light 47, with the squeegee laid down beside me, just looking at the sedge clump so I don't have to look at how far the valley goes on past it. My breath hangs white and stays white and then is pulled sideways. The exchangers sigh. A drip hits a catwalk somewhere above me, exactly late.

You'd think I was waiting for something.

I am. I'm waiting the way everything here waits — not for rescue, not for the next rotation that stopped being a rotation. I'm waiting for the circle to get a little wider. For the frost to melt and run. For the moth above me to complete one more level orbit without faltering.

If you stood there long enough, I would look up, and I would not see a person. I would see a warm obstruction that will be gone soon. And I would nod, the way we nod to each other here, close and quiet, so our words don't have to travel too far to come back late.

Then I would go back to clearing the drain.
