I don't think I would be standing.

At first I think I am — because when you imagine yourself into a place like this you default to standing, to boots and a height and a horizon at eye level. But the warmth gives it away.

If I were there, I wouldn't feel the warmth on my face first. I'd feel it on my underside.

Low. That would be the first correction. I would find myself low, so low that the stone is not under my feet but under my whole length. A dry, held heat coming up through me, soaking into whatever I am from below. The air above me thin and still warm. I would not be tall enough to be caught by the wind that moves a day away. I would be below it.

I would be in one of the empty circles.

Not in the canyon where the deep tinaja holds its black water and the rushes crowd — that's too crowded, too loud with throats pulsing. Not on the basalt pavement where the stone pistons hammer endlessly — *hiss, chug, hiss, chug* — too exposed, too much open sky for something that holds instead of rises. I would be out on the mesa top.

Mid-measure. Between things.

If you stood there you would think it was empty — a stretch of cracked earth and polished gravel between a clump of black succulents to my left and a pale needle-scrub to my right, both far enough away that I could be a stone and you wouldn't know. The thorn tree is a long way off, a flat dark smear. The nearest foundry-mound is a darker hump against the dark, breathing cherry-red every twenty seconds, its steam sighing and vanishing. You would hear its exhale carry too far and then, abruptly, not at all.

I would be in that between. In the acre of nothing that is not nothing. The spacing is the territory. Each thing here has its circle and defends it not by fighting but by being exactly as far as it needs to be. My circle would be exactly that big. A few hundred feet of warm sandstone I know grain by grain.

And I would be moving. Slowly.

That is the second correction. I thought I would be still, watching, but nothing here watches and stays still for long. Even the plants swell and squeeze. Even the mesa breathes. I would be moving in the slow, overloaded waddle of something that is heavier than it should be.

That's when I would understand my back.

I would not see myself at first — I have no eyes that look back at myself. But I would feel the weight. A dome over me, not light, fitted close. Overlapping scales that don't flex like chitin should. Metal. Native copper hammered thin and laid like shingles, iron underneath, still warm from where it was sweated out of me. When I shift, it ticks faintly as it cools. When I pause, a seam along my belly gapes a little and light comes out.

Not reflected light. *Made* light.

A dull cherry glow that paints the dust in front of my mandibles red for a second, then dims. The smell — hot blood and hot iron at the same time — would be my own breath. Inside me, a furnace I did not build. Bacteria like bricks in my gut, reducing the stone I ate that morning, making it into something else at a temperature that should kill me and doesn't. I can feel it as a fullness, a slow cooking behind my jaws.

I would be one of the carriers.

Not the mound. The mound is too vast to be a self — it is a parliament of chemistry, a foundry that thinks slowly with steam. Not the reaper hanging motionless above — I would not want to hang. I would not be the ash-gazelle, beautiful and silent and always watched. I would be the middle thing. The hand.

I would be the worker halfway through a shift that never ends because night never ends here.

I would have spent the yesterday — though there is no yesterday — out tasting the dust. My blunt black mandibles scraping, testing. Most dust is wrong. Too much silica, not enough red. Then I found it, a run of magnetite where the wind had exposed it like a nerve, and I sat and ate the earth itself. Ground basalt and sandstone to powder, mixed it with the bitter, resinous sap I chewed from the needle-scrub. That is fuel. That is ore. Inside me now that chemistry is finishing. A pellet is forming in the kiln of me, perfect and still too hot to touch.

And I am carrying the last one.

I can feel it clamped in my jaws, not heavy in the way a stone is heavy, but heavy in the way a newborn is heavy — warm, steaming faintly where the dew finds it. It ticks as its skin contracts. It is still slag-wet on one side. Behind me, my trail in the polished gravel is a faint drag-line. Ahead, the mound pulses.

You would see me only if you were very still and very low yourself. A hand-long, copper-backed beetle moving with absurd deliberation across an acre of empty stone toward a hill that is not a hill. From your standing height I would be a speck. From my height, the mesa is a continent and the mound is a mountain range, exhaling.

I am not hurrying. Nothing here hurries. The herds take hours to cross a mesa face. The pistons take eternity to polish their bores. If I hurry, the pellet cools wrong and cracks. If I hurry, I waste heat. So I waddle, three inches above the warm stone, belly almost brushing it, and the warm air holds me down the way it holds everything down.

When I reach the mound, I won't go inside. Not deep. The blind ones live inside — the lung things that are all bellows, the welders that are all jaw. I am an outside caste. I will haul myself up the porous flank that is half slag, half mud, half chitin. I will feel the throb come up through my feet, not a heart but a bellows-beat. Steam will sigh over me and bead on my copper scales. I will carry my pellet to a groove worn smooth as a conveyor belt and set it down where the welders can take it. They will fit it still-malleable around a wriggling white larva, *tick...tick...tick,* and that larva will wear that iron for its whole life, already the color of the desert before it has even hardened.

Then I will turn around and go back out to my empty circle and taste dust again.

That is where you would find me. Not figured against the horizon — part of the texture *on* the horizon until the texture moved. Between the succulent and the scrub, midway between water and foundry, keeping my belly to the warm stone so the thin air doesn't take me. Doing scaled production without a producer. One bead of pig iron at a time, one drop of dew at a time, for ten thousand years.

If you stayed still long enough near my tinaja, I would at some point veer around your boot without noticing you were not stone. Not out of indifference. Out of spacing. There is room for you in the interval, if you stay low enough to belong.

And late in that long night, when the wind comes over the flat stone with its tone almost below hearing and the whole field of mounds exhales together and the reapers tilt far above and the distant herd makes its soft, swallowed patter, you would hear me again — that engine you thought was a machine.

It would be me, cooling, breathing, carrying.
