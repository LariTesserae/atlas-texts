I don't find myself at once.

For a long time I am only a shape on a roof, and not even that. If you were looking down from higher up — from the place where the afternoon cloud begins to form — you would miss me entirely. I am the color of the wall I am leaning against.

Then the shadow of that cloud passes, and the light flattens, and the wet stone shines, and you see movement, and then you see me.

I am on the second level of a tier that has no name. If the city has names for its quarters they are not written down, they are remembered by slope — *where the water goes left around the three-cistern plaza*, *where the old willow holds the wall*. This one would be called, if anyone needed to call it, *above the sedge common*. It is near enough to the western rim that on clear days you can feel the plateau ending, not because you can see it but because the wind finally has somewhere to go and it pulls a little faster over the low roofs.

You would find me low. Everyone is low here, but I have made a habit of staying lower than I need to. I don't stand on the parapet to look out fifty miles to the blue haze. I sit on the edge of the cistern in my courtyard, with my feet on the flag that covers it.

It is a good courtyard. Not large, as these things are measured — cut down about a man's height into the basalt, maybe twelve paces across. The spring-eye is still open in the northeast corner, a clear lens of water about as wide as my palm, with cress growing around it so green it looks artificial against the grey. My great-grandmother cut this house, so the story goes, and the tool marks are still on the east wall where they didn't bother to smooth them because the moss took them first. Three rooms open off the court, all inward-facing, all with thick walls and narrow windows and felt that we pull across when the evening pulls the chill down. The hearth is in the middle, not for heat so much as for keeping heat — a slab of basalt that we feed sparingly and that holds the afternoon sun well into the night.

If you came in the morning, you would find me bailing.

This is who I am: not a Keeper. Not yet, maybe not ever. The Water-Keeper for this fall of terraces is an older woman named Hessa whose stick has notches I haven't learned to read. She walks after every rain and tells us where to cut and where to wait. I am one of the ones who is told.

My work is the cistern. Every courtyard has one, under the flags, bellied out into the stone to catch the seep and the rain. Ours is deep and sweet, and because our court is slightly lower than the two above us, it fills faster than we need. On mornings when the condensation has been heavy, I can hear it before I see it — a soft ticking as the overflow lip begins to spill into the runnel that runs along the base of the south wall.

I bail with a gourd and a leather bucket. This is not punishment. It is not even chore in the way lowlanders mean chore. It is just what the interval requires. The water has to go somewhere. I lift it, and carry it to the marsh common.

The common is why we are here, on this tier. It's one of the ones they left unbuilt on purpose — a shallow bowl maybe a hundred paces across where the bedding plane sagged and the water table came right up. Sedge and cotton-grass and that low, dense moss that holds water like a sponge. In late summer we cut the sedge for thatch and for winter fodder for the long-legged grazers, who move through here in threes and fours. They know me. They have the same grey-brown wool we do, and the same unhurried breath.

I would be the woman who knows this common's moods. Short — broad — you would notice my hands first if you stood close enough to talk without raising your voice, which you would have to. The palms are too wide for my frame, the fingers thick and smoothed at the pads, the nails never quite clean no matter how long I soak them in the soft water. My chest is deep, barrelled. I breathe the way everyone does here, slow and from the bottom of the ribs. As a child I cried too long once and learned what it costs to empty yourself of air at this height, so I don't anymore. My eyes are lichen-grey. My hair is dark and cut short under a felt cap because it never really dries.

I don't hurry. If you asked me to, I would lean forward a little and keep the same rhythm and you would think I was still moving slowly.

Most of my day is small negotiations with water and stone.

After bailing, I check the weep on the west wall. It has been there since before I was born, a dark thread that never stops, even in the dry weeks, beading and running down the lee side. In winter it grows a little beard of ice. I keep its channel clear with my thumb, picking out grit and a fallen cotton head so it doesn't dam and push behind the stones and heave them in a freeze.

I mend. There is always a stone that has settled a finger's width. I don't need a Stone-Keeper to tell me where the bedding plane wants to break; I can feel it with my hand, the faint grain. I tap, I lift with both hands — wet basalt is slick and will take your knuckle if you try to be clever with one hand — I fit, I leave it.

The children are underfoot. Two belong to this courtyard, one belongs to the courtyard above but eats here because his own is too crowded. They play where the common drains into our runnel, building their tiny terraces, flooding them with cupped hands, watching how the water finds its way. I let them. That is how they learn slope. When they build it wrong and the water cuts straight through their wall, they stack the slate again without being told.

When the cloud forms — around three, as it does most days — I am already under the lintel. Everyone is. We don't run. We step in, shoulder to shoulder, and watch. The rain comes straight down, not hard, but thorough, the kind that finds every joint. The runnels deepen, join, braid. The common fills until it shines. The whole city steams when the sun comes out again, and the hundred thousand low fires are not yet lit, and the stone ticks.

You would not pick me out then, either. Grey wool against grey stone, breath showing a little, hands holding a gourd that is now full again.

And if you stayed, as the description says you might, for weeks, until your own breath deepened and your own hands smoothed and you learned to speak at the volume of the nearest trickle, you would find me at night, too. The leather door pulled to, the felt across the windows, the small fire orange on the hearth stone, its light reflected in the cistern cover and in the eye of the spring. I would be on the low stool in the sunniest corner that is no longer sunny, mending a felt cap or chipping a slate flake into a spoon, watching.

I would not be in charge of anything large. I would not be remembered beyond this fall of terraces. But if you asked Hessa where the water will go next spring, she would point to me and say — *ask her, she bails that cistern every morning, she knows.*

That is the soft, living part of the system I would be. The part that moves, fits, remembers, and bails.
