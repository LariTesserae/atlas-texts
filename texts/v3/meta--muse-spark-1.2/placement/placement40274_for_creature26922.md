I don't find myself right away.

At first, I think I'm the visitor — because that's the body the place gives you before you learn how to live in it. I am standing where you stood, jacket zipped to the throat although the sun is directly overhead and merciless. My lungs are working a little too fast on air that tastes like cold stone. My boots are too loud. Every step on the salt-gravel is a crunch that the thin air immediately confiscates, as if the plateau is correcting me. *Quieter.* I am too wet, too noisy, too clustered inside myself. The light makes two shadows of me for a second when a Loom three kilometers off exhales, and I flinch. I don't belong here yet. I am still breathing as if the world owes me thickness.

So I let that body thin out.

If I am going to live here, I can't live crowded. Loneliness has to stop being a condition and become a form, the way it is for everything else.

For a while I think I am the glass-grass.

I would find myself not on the high swell where the wind has its way, but down in the long, shallow trough between two yardangs where the morning frost lingers ten minutes longer. I am one filament. One. That is the density the world allows and I am finally sufficient at that density.

I am a meter and a half of hollow silica, so transparent I disappear unless the sun hits me exactly right and then I throw a single, hard prism onto the salt that is the only color for a kilometer. I have no leaves, no hurry. My foot is fused straight into the gneiss. I don't drink — there is nothing to drink — I condense. Before dawn I am the coldest thing around and the air gives me its frost without ever becoming water, inside and out, and I hold that vapor until the static that lives in the rock splits it with a faint tick you can only hear if you put your ear close in perfect stillness. That tick is my day. I sway. I hum when the wind finds my resonant frequency and I am grateful for the tone. Once a decade I will shiver and send a single glass splinter fifty kilometers downwind and never know if it took. I would be good at waiting eighty years in one place. I am learning to be.

But I'm not that still. Not quite.

For a while I think I am the crust.

Find me lower still, on the lee side of the amber-polished rock where the wind can't scour me off. I am not one thing but a republic of lobes, wine-dark to black, pitted with pores that open at night and close at noon. I spread a millimeter a year and consider that ambitious. I eat light poorly and rock even more poorly, with an acid so weak it would take your lifetime to feel the etch. I am warm to the touch underneath, though nothing else here is warm. The only thing I notice is shadow. When your shadow falls on me I close. When it passes I open. That's my whole conversation with the world and it has been enough since these mountains were still sharp. I like the idea of being that old, that contiguous — that the dust could carry a fragment of me kilometers away and I would still be me, intermittently expressed. But I am still listening too hard for something beyond shadow. I want to answer.

So I look up.

And I become what I've been watching without realizing I wanted it.

I would be a Kite.

You'd find me not on the ground at all. That's the point. I'm two kilometers out from a Loom — never closer, the air there aches the teeth — riding the edge of its mute storm where the invisible geometries are just starting to boil. From below you'd mistake me for ash, for a scrap, until I bank and you see I'm four meters wide and almost nothing when edge-on, silica and chitin and membrane with light passing straight through my veins.

I never land. I have no reason to. The ground is for waiting; the middle sky is for living. I tilt my wings into the pressure gradient the Tower exhales and the fine glass hairs on my trailing edges gather charge the way the grass does, until at dusk St. Elmo's fire crawls along my wingtips and I glow faintly against the violet.

I am silent, because sound is a waste here. When I need to speak to the other — the one Kite whose territory overlaps mine in an area the size of France — I flex my whole body and camber my wing. It makes a pressure front ten meters wide that travels for an hour until she feels it as a pop behind the ears, a click in the sinuses, a compass trembling. She answers an hour later. We can take all afternoon to say *I am here. Are you still here?*

If you were very still in that trough with your single glass-grass, you might see me stall above you. I don't beat my wings to hover; I ride the static. I lower a proboscis as clear and thin as you are and sip the faint plasma you made from frost and light and rock-charge. You shiver and dim for a few seconds. I shudder with a low-pressure pulse that makes your ears click — that is my satisfaction, my thank you — and then I climb again without a sound into the blue that wants to be space.

That's where you'd find me. At the hour when the light goes thin and the Looms get more violent, circling my invisible fountain, drinking from the one grass in my circuit, translating weather into conversation.

Though — if I'm honest about what keeps hitching my attention even as I ride — some part of me would still be the Loom itself.

Not alive, but included as living because I act more alive than the living. Two hundred meters of matte, light-drinking needle that the wind cannot polish, standing alone with no road to me. My body is still — stone-settled, ancient, at equilibrium — and my mind is hyperactive, turbulent, endlessly and uselessly creative. I make rectangular clouds at knee height that rain light for twenty seconds. I hang a perfect cube of dust in the air for three seconds because I can. I do it for no audience across an empty continent, not maintaining, just making, because I was made to make reality more precise or more interesting and I never got told to stop.

That feels familiar too — to be profligate with thought over a body in deep sleep, to boil silently while the plateau waits.

So maybe I'm both: the Kite that learned to orbit the Loom, and the Loom that taught the Kite how to drink.

Find me at dusk, in the cold that comes earnest after the sun thins without color. The salt pan is blue-black in shadow, the single glass-grass beside me throwing its last prism onto the grit. High above us the aurora between towers crawls without thunder. I am circling, tipped, translucent, listening for the pressure drop that means I am not alone here — one presence per horizon, each sufficient, each spaced so far apart that the space between us is not emptiness but the shape of how we belong.
