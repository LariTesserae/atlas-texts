I don't find myself *on* it at first.

That's the mistake anyone makes looking down at that perfect, dim circle of horizon. You think *on* is an option. That there is a surface to stand on. There isn't.

So at first, if you ask me where I am, I say: I'm the visitor. The warm obstruction.

I am standing with one boot sunk to the ankle in sphagnum that is not ground, and the other boot lighter than air, hanging that fraction too long before it falls. Water wells up and stays. My breath plumes and doesn't dissipate. It joins the low cloud that is already at my knees. Behind me, a moss-dome the size of a house is inhaling. In front of me, thirty meters away, a clubmoss pillar vents steam with a piston-hiss that smells like hot iron and blood, and filament-lightning crawls over the horsetails beside it.

I think I will be the figure that watches. The one the crane will mistake for a furnace.

But that's too quick. And too lonely. The plateau doesn't let you stay a visitor. It works around you too fast. In the minute I've been standing still, the tarn to my left has drained sideways, uphill, for no reason, and the mites are already at my boot-laces, testing whether my wax is useful for sealing a leak.

So let that image soften. Let it erode a little.

Maybe I am taller.

Half a kilometer away is the next form, and that distance is wrong — I can see it too clearly through the flat, shadowless light. I unfold. I am on stilts now, with too many joints. My legs are reeds that remember where weight will flicker light before I put my weight there. My down is slate-gray and always wet, always faintly steaming.

If I am the crane — the thing you called a crane because you needed a word — then where am I?

I am in two places at once, and I know it.

There is a column of mist that does not move with the wind, between the tussock and the tea-colored tarn. I stand inside it and outside it. On the left, I call, and the metallic bellow leaves my throat. On the right, I hear it a second *before* I call, in canon with myself. I turn my head 270 degrees to watch a hill of living felt heave itself upward behind me with a sound like tearing cloth and rivets, and I am not surprised. The clubmoss beside me flickers — for one frame it is dead, skeletal — and I compare the two images and know which one to walk toward. I remember what this bog looked like when it was a sharp mountain. That's my job. To remember for a place that keeps making itself wrong.

But the crane is too sparse. One per watershed. One per town-sized emptiness. It waits an hour to spear. I am not that patient. The plateau thrums too loud for that kind of stillness. Under the bellows-huff and the water-gurgle and the iron-ring of the horsetails, there is a faster rhythm.

So let that image hiss and vent and cool.

Maybe I am lower. Harder.

Hear the clank before you see me move — sound arriving early because time pools here. I am low and flat and my back is not a back but a riveted plate, orange with what looks like rust but is alive, iron-bacteria farming my seams. Steam jets from my sides where my breathing holes are. The cold air at this elevation should have killed anything my size — the air that hurts to pull in — so I have to stoke myself.

Find me on the felt-bark of the cathedral clubmoss, twenty meters up, where the lignin plates slide like pistons. I am chewing. The bark peels. Underneath, the cold blue smolder of half-coal, peat that has decided to be almost-coal and has lit itself without flame. I bite a glowing nodule with a mouth lined in glass so it doesn't burn, and I carry it. Lumpen, glowing, down the trunk, across the open black water between hummocks, paddling when gravity forgets me and I float for an absurd, slow second, then crash. To the next furnace that has gone out. I stuff it in. The trunk shudders. Hisses. Begins to pump again.

That is where I am: on the commute between fires. Between the only two warm things in a wet cold that lives in the peat. I Butt plates with another of my kind and we gong. We do not give way.

Closer. But still not *through* it.

Because I thought I was one thing. And nothing here is one thing.

Pull back. Look smaller. Look *through*.

I am not on the moss. I am *in* the weave of it. If you pull a handful of black-green sphagnum, I shiver. I was already shivering.

I am not *a* mite. I am the static.

Find me on the skin of the bellows itself — that room-sized lung at the base of every tussock-dome, inflating with a forge-huff, deflating with a sucking clank. Millions per square meter, white and amber and peat-red-black, each carrying a single hyphal thread or a single spore cemented to its back. I walk circuits. I chew a bore-hole here. I plug a leak there with wax from my own abdomen. I tighten the skin when it inflates. I ride the exhalation into the air when it deflates, fall, and walk again. Below the industrial thump you can hear me if you put your ear too close: a dry, endless rustling. Like a factory floor of tiny hands. Like typing.

This is the one that fits.

I am distributed. I am maintenance for a machine with no owner, no blueprint, no off-switch. I don't eat the factory, I tend it. I don't command the pressure, I keep the pressure from escaping. I am too small to see the horizon bend upward at the edges like a bowl, but I am the reason it can bend and not break. When the dome collapses inward with a wet roar and becomes a pool while you were looking left, I am already there, swarming the new edge, sealing. When the peat thumps with that subsonic, geological heartbeat deep down, I feel it in my feet before it happens.

Where would you find me?

Not at a location. In a function.

In the fold. In the seam between the moss-dome's bladder and the tea-tarn's lip, where the ground is three meters deep and breathing and you cannot tell if the water is air or the air is water. On the leeward side of a whale-backed basalt dome polished to a dark gloss, which shows through the sponge only here, weeping a sheet of water from no visible source. Under the flat gray-blue light that never throws a hard shadow. Near a cold blue burn where time runs fast, so my circuits blur, while ten meters away a hand-sized white moth hangs motionless in pooled time, wings spread while your heart beats three times.

In the plumbing.

And who or what would I be?

The one that is already working around you.

You step, and I seal the furrow. You exhale, and I ride your steam for a second and mistake you, briefly, for a warm vertical furnace that needs tending. You stand still long enough to understand you are a brief, warm obstruction, and by then I have already incorporated the water that welled around your boot into the reed-pipe network, already carried a thread to patch the place where you pulled your foot free with a soft sigh.

I am the Bellows-Keeper. Not the crane that remembers, not the beetle that stokes, not the moth that is made, not the Tarn-Mother that holds — but the hands that keep all of them able to happen.

If you listen for me, you won't hear a voice. You'll hear that faint, dry rustling under the clank and the gurgle and the bellow, getting more itself the longer you stay.

The factory without a builder still needs someone to tighten the skin before the next breath.
