I don't know right away.

If you had asked me at the bottom of the switchbacks, I would have told you quickly — a climber, a visitor, someone passing through. If you had asked me with my hands still on that sharp lip of basalt, lungs still rinsed thin, I would have said *a witness.* But up there, the air is too thin for quick answers and the town is too empty to let you be a tourist for long.

At first I think I would be the raven.

That's the easiest shape to try on. To be the thing that crosses the white sky alone and makes a sound big enough to fill the empty streets. To not have to learn the slow work, to not have to breathe so deliberately. To look down at the green plate from above and croak once and keep going. But the mesa doesn't want passing over. It's a room with no roof — it wants staying in. And when I imagine those hard new cliffs ringing under my wings, I feel how lonely that kind of loudness would be.

So then I think, maybe the moss.

That would be honest. To be the thing that actually lives there most completely. To be emerald and jade and yellow-green, to be the surface itself. To hold water without needing to carry it, to thatch the roofs until you can't tell building from ground, to swallow sound. To advance a millimeter a year and call that enough. I want that patience. But I am too restless to be moss yet. I still listen for things too hard. I still want to make a mark with tools.

Maybe a child, then. One of the four or five.

To be low to the ground, to know which moss mats will hold me and which bog-pans will quake and try to suck my ankle. To lie on my belly at the absolute edge where the world ends in a clean vertical cut and watch the braided threads of water learn, for the first time in this rock's life, how to fall. To run along the cooling columns and tap them — *ping, ping, ping* — and make the mesa ring like organ pipes, making music the adults are too slow and too practical to make. I can feel that joy in my teeth, that glassy note. But I am not that new either. My hands already know work.

It takes a while up there — everything does — for the shape to settle.

I walk the grid in my head. The streets too wide, the houses too many, the cistern always full to spilling. I see the Wall Crew tapping one face of one block all morning. I see the loom going in the weaving shed, the thick felted wool the color of wet lichen. I see the granary on stilts, holding more than thirty mouths need, checked every morning for condensation that shouldn't be there but always is.

And I see her — the old woman opening the door of a house no one sleeps in, sweeping its clean floor, wringing the moss out from the sill, closing the door again. Not because someone told her to. Because that is what you do when you notice the water is high, when you notice a house is waiting.

That's where I find myself.

Not in the center, not at the edge, but in the in-between houses. The kept ones.

You would find me in the second row from the plaza, on the north side where the cloud lingers longest, where the basalt shows through if you don't press the moss back. My house isn't mine the way lowland houses are mine. I sleep there, one mat on a swept floor, one low fire-bowl of sour alder smoke, felt over me because my chest is broad now and my breath still wheezes faintly on the inhale from the cold. But my work is not in my house.

My work is the houses that are not mine.

In the morning when the cloud is heaviest and everything is ticking — leaf to leaf to plank to stone, that loud, bright percussion that is the town's real voice — I do the round. I carry a broom made of bound cotton-grass heads and a wooden pail. I open doors that were left ajar on purpose. I sweep out the night's condensation that beaded and fell, I lift the mat and shake it, I check the slate shingles where their edges are still sharp enough to cut, I press a palm of live moss back onto the corner where the wind scoured it bare. I wring out the sill. I close the door again but not all the way.

I keep them dry. Or as dry as anything can be kept on a plate that sweats.

What am I? I am not the builder, though I can dress a block with a stone maul if you hand me one and I will take the whole morning to do it right. I am not the weaver, though I wear the undyed green-gray and my hair is cut short because it would never dry otherwise, and my hands are wide and damp and dark at the rims the way theirs are. I am not the child tapping music, though I stop to listen when they run past.

I am the housekeeper of the future.

The thirty understand extensiveness — that word you used. They do not feel lonely, they feel early. And someone has to be the one who lives early on purpose. Who tends the waiting space without anxiety. Who, when you come up the cut and ask how many came with you, will be the one nodding and looking past you toward the empty edge, not because I am waiting for a crowd, but because I am keeping the room ready for when the erosion finally learns where the rivers go, when the takin finally stays, when the next thirty arrive and the next thirty after that.

You would find me by listening for the quietest sound in that amplified place — not the wind scouring the plaza, not the drip, but the soft *shhh, shhh* of a broom on stone in an empty house, at mid-morning, while the cistern spills.

If you stood in the doorway I would not startle. I would just look up, breathing that long, complete exhale that fogs and vanishes, and make room on the floor that was kept for you before you knew you were climbing.
