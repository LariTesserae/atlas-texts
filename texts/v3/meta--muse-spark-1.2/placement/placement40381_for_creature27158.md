I don't find myself right away.

At first there is only the air - that warm, wet air that has weight to it, that you breathe like drinking. I think for a long time I am the air. Or the green dusk. Or the sound of a drop hitting a leaf and then the leaf below it.

Then a shape collects.

Not quickly. Nothing collects quickly here.

I am low, but not low. That's how it is in the Weave, you have to learn to say where you are by breath and light, not by up and down. I am maybe four lattices down from the rind, where the outer cliff thickens and leans in to make the first true vault. The light here is at its thickest green, filtered through a hundred leaf-layers until it has no direction, only depth. It never gets brighter. It never gets darker. It just hangs.

Below me — through the lattice — there are three more floors fading into mist before the haze takes them. Above me, the same. I can see far, kilometers of held emptiness between the house-wide pillars, but I can't see clearly. That's right. The air itself is a little luminous, a little wet.

If you were looking for me, you would walk right over me. That's not because I'm hiding.

I am on the underside.

I thought I would be on top of the floor, standing like a visitor would. But when I settled, I settled inverted. My feet — long, splayed, the toes as clever as fingers — are curled around a bracing root as thick as my wrist. My back is to the abyss. Below me, which is really above me, a slow cloud is forming in the middle of the vault, condensing out of nothing, out of the Weave's own breathing. It will rain in an hour and the rain will take an hour to fall.

I am small, and long. That is the word that kept coming until it became true.

My arms are too long. When I let them hang they pass my knees by a hand and then another hand. My fingers have that extra joint near the tip that folds back on itself so I can pinch a vine cord and pull without ever closing my fist. My chest is broad and shallow, it moves slowly. My skin is that pale green-grey, like the underside of a leaf, thin enough that the veins in my wrists show like roots. The moss is growing well on me this season — short, fine, beading with condensation on my forearms and calves and crown. When I am still for a long time, a beetle will try to roll the sweat from my ankle.

My eyes are open. They are large, amber, with almost no white. The nictitating membrane wipes sideways every minute or so to clear the mist. Direct sun would blind me, but there is no direct sun here. There hasn't been for four hundred generations and I do not miss it.

So I am a Tender. Not a young one. Not the child lying on the lattice watching the kites learn how to be still — though I was that, and part of me still is, lying there. I am the one who stayed still long enough to become part of the mending.

My work is water.

Not all water — the Weave moves water everywhere, all the time, sweating and weeping and condensing — but this one chain of it. Where I hang, a cistern-leaf the size of a bathtub has grown too flat at its lip. The water that beads on the floor above gathers there, pools, and should spill through a gap in the thatch to the next level in a thin, silent fall. But the leaf has thickened with age and leans half a degree wrong, so the pool lingers, sags the lattice, grows warm and still.

I have been watching it for a long time. An hour, maybe two. To you it would look like indolence. Here it is attention.

In my lap is a stone flake — not sharp, worn smooth by generations of hands — and a length of bark cord stripped yesterday and kept damp. Beside my knee, my antler hook rests where I left it, already filming over with a faint green.

I will not cut the leaf. You never cut if you can coax. I will wait until the water is at its highest, until the weight shows me exactly where the sag pulls. Then I will reach up — down — and with the hook pull a young aerial root that has been wandering airily nearby, guide it to the lip of the leaf, and tie it slack. Not tight. Slack. Tie it so that in a year, when it thickens, it will take the load and lift the edge by a finger's width. The leaf will remember the new shape. The water will find its gap again. The lattice will breathe.

A pale long-finger passes me on the same root, inverted like me but smaller, translucent. It does not look at me. It takes the loose tip of the same aerial root beyond my tie and braids it deftly into the thatch with fingers even longer than mine. We work the same cord from opposite ends. Our hands brush. We exchange no word. Sound dies ten meters from your mouth here anyway. We speak in tap and touch, and the wood carries it better than air.

Far out in the empty, a kite pulses. Translucent, ribbed, a little amber when the green light hits it. It flexes and air moves through it. It is filtering spores. It makes no sound at all. I watch it the way the children watch — to learn how to be still in empty space.

A drop falls from the leaf above my head. Hits the leaf beside my knee. Splits. Rejoins. Continues down. I follow it without moving my eyes.

This is where I find myself: hanging under the world, warm, slightly damp, at the edge of a vast hollow where an interior rain is just beginning to form three levels up. My home is not far — a burl a few meters along the pillar, swollen over generations into a sleeping cup, lined with the down of a blanket moth that hangs beneath the next floor, breathing once a minute, carding the roots with its feathered feet.

I will not finish. No one finishes. The floor will sag again somewhere else. A vine marked with red bark — the closing vine two spans to my left, loop a little too perfect — will need to be fed so it doesn't starve and rot the join it is part of. A door-sized flower will open over days and need that moth's weight to pollinate it.

I tap twice on the root with my knuckle. The vibration runs through the living lattice faster than any voice could. Somewhere, a few floors away, another Tender feels it as a faint tremor in the wood under their palm and knows: *this sag is being tended.*

The Weave warms slightly under my feet. The water on the pillar beside me changes course to avoid my fingers. Its pulse moves up through the house-wide trunk — so slow my heart beats twenty times for each of its.

I belong too well to be seen. I am not *in* the building.

I am one of the ways it holds itself open.
