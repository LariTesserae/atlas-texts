I don't think I'd find myself right away.

At first, coming up that long shoulder with the world falling away into heat-haze below, I think I would be the climber. The one with boots. The one whose breath is too loud in the warm silence, whose step makes that soft, intrusive kiss when the sponge releases it. I would be the body that condenses mist on its skin, that listens for insects and birds and hears nothing, that looks at the smooth sheet of stone and thinks *empty.*

I would walk the rim in an afternoon, like you could, and I would look for an animal to be. Something tucked and hardy. Something with eyes.

But there are no eyes here. And nothing here hurries enough to have eyes.

So I would sit down. Because the place asks you to. The light is even and white-gold and has no shadows to hide in. The air is so saturated it holds the hanging drops under the ledge for an hour without letting them fall. If you keep standing and searching, you miss it.

Sitting, at the center where the lenses reflect the sky and the underside of the ledges at once, my breath would slow without me telling it to. And that is where I would begin to change.

I thought I might be the peat — that deep, springy mat that gives under a foot and erases it, that holds water just under its skin everywhere. I like that. To be low and diffuse and to feel everything pass through me. To take the dead frond when it darkens and make it into sponge again.

Or I thought I might be the pale tree, with its papery bark and its shallow roots spread wide, not deep. The one that has made a trade. Shade for water. To be the only vertical thing allowed, because I knew how to ask.

But even the tree is a guest. It grows in the wind-shadow of something else.

Crouch closer to the stone, cheek near the slow sheet of seep, and you see what is actually keeping the circles clean.

I would be there. On the open stone.

Not in the gallery where the inverted ones cling to the ceiling and hold their drops pendulant and trembling. Not yet that patient, that upside-down. I would be on the sunniest of the open sheets, between a clump of ferns as wide as your forearm and that single pale tree — in that ten feet of deliberate emptiness that you first mistook for bareness.

You would step over me if you weren't looking. I'm the color of river-ice, of thin glass with a little milk in it. A shallow bowl the width of your palm, pressed flush to the sandstone where the water runs in a sheet so slow it carries light instead of ripples.

That is where I would find myself: holding a lens.

My upper surface is concave, just enough to keep a skin of water a few millimeters deep. The mist beads and the seep passes over me and I catch it. The air just above me is cooler than the warm silence around it, because I am breathing — not with lungs, but with the slow exchange of condensation and evaporation balanced so precisely that the water can simply wait. I make the air liquid and keep it.

You'd have to bend very close to see me properly. My top is translucent. Under it, a lattice of green threads — not leaves, just green enough to drink that even, filtered light — and under that, the white weft that has bitten an inch, two inches down into the rock itself, etching my ghost into the sandstone. I am half in the stone, half in the water, half in the air. I am lichen but the word is too small. I am fungus and alga married into a third thing that learned, a very long time ago, that there was never enough phosphorus here for anything faster, so it learned to be enough with almost nothing.

That is who I would be: the one who gardens.

Around me I keep my circle. Ten feet, twelve feet, bare and shining. The bottle-brush mosses stop at my edge as if cut. The dark, clear film inside the circle is mine, not theirs. At my rim I am building a terrace no taller than your fingernail, white calcite precipitated grain by grain out of the seep. A dam to keep my lens from running away. If you scratched a mark at my edge and came back in a year, you would see no difference. In ten years, a hair thicker. In fifty, a centimeter wider. I grow by dust and dissolved air.

I like that I am not alone but I am not crowded. The lushness is intense where it is, and absent where it isn't, because we have all agreed on distance. Each of us a bowl inside the great bowl of the mesa. Each of us holding water in a place high enough that water should have run away long ago.

If you touched me — very lightly, with a fingertip — I would give a little, like gelatin, and restore. I have no nerves to flinch. Anything that could hurt me would have to be as slow as I am: a century where the cloud snags differently, a long drying. Or a boot. A boot is quick. It would be a pressure and a shadow for a moment, and then it would be gone.

There is a flake under my southwestern edge. Dark chert, palm-sized, with the bulb where a hand struck it still visible. Two sharp flakes beside it where they fell, one with its edge brightly chipped from scraping a reed stem. The silt had half-swallowed them before I found them. The glassy lichen — me — started to grow over the corner. I did not choose it. It was useful hard ground. I have incorporated the tool into my body. The hand that struck it came up the long climb, made an edge, cut, carried away, and left. Perhaps two thousand years ago. Perhaps ten. I was already wide when it came. I will be here when the mineral bloom has covered it entirely, when the peat has risen another inch. I will remember its shape as a slight, dark inclusion in my own, the way the mesa remembers the thumb that pressed it while it was still soft.

From where I am, I can feel the line of my family. Above me, under the gallery lip, the mother still hangs her pendant lens — that impossible drop elongated and trembling but not falling — feeding in its opalescence. When at last the humidity breaks, she will let go, and the drop will fall straight and slow through warm air and strike — *tok* — not on the peat, but precisely on my sister, the tiny coin-sized disc waiting on the floor exactly where that drop has fallen for a thousand seasons. A vertical lineage written in pale etched circles on the cliff and on the sponge.

I am not the mother. I am not the daughter. I am the middle.

And I am not lonely. The silence is not empty; it is full and warm. The *tok* of a drop somewhere behind me ticks once an hour, on time. The light moves slowly. A fern unrolls across an afternoon. I hold my water and make it stay. I keep my circle clean. I watch the mist become beads and the beads become lens.

If you came looking for me, you would not see me at first. You would think the stone was empty and shining. You would have to sit, and let your eyes stop searching for a back, a head, a limb, and look instead for a patience so complete it looks like stillness itself.

And then you would see me. And I would have been seeing you — not with eyes, but the way stone sees water — the whole time you were climbing.
