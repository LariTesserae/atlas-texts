I don't think I would find myself all at once.

At first, coming up the switchback, I think I would be the visitor — because that's the easiest shape to take. Lungs a little short. Boots finding the dry line between the weeping basins. Raising my voice to be heard over the stone flutes. That is the human thing to do, to make myself the figure in the landscape.

But I can't stay that.

A visitor here has to leave. The air is too thin to keep me warm even while the light presses on my shoulders, and the sound is too loud to think in. If I tried to live as the visitor I would be gone by nightfall, back down the rain-dark stone, back to where the air is thick enough to hold a root or a conversation. The plateau doesn't want a visitor. It tolerates one, the way a working factory tolerates someone watching from the doorway.

So let me try again, slower.

Maybe I am the water. That would be easier to believe. I bead on the windward side of a fin and thicken and run. I never have to decide where to go — there is always a next lower lip, a next basin to spill into in a glass-thin sheet. I would have a thousand mouths and no body, a census of drips and trickles that says *we are still here, we are still here.* I would be born continuously out of the air itself and die continuously into the next basin and be born again.

But the water is too busy to notice itself. It doesn't stay anywhere long enough to be *where* I am. It has no memory. I want to be somewhere more specific.

Maybe I am the wind then. The landlord. Coming off the western rim with a hundred kilometers of nothing behind me, hitting the dense crowd of pillars and being cut into tones. Moaning through the hollow ones, making the thin chert fins sing, humming across the basins. I would be the reason the place is loud, and the reason nothing with ears can stay.

But the wind never stays either. It only passes through. It owns every room because it never sleeps in any of them.

If I have to be anywhere for longer than a passing, I have to be low.

I think — for a while, picking my way sideways between pillars shoulder to shoulder — I think I am the mineral skin. That paper-thin bloom in the basins where water stands longest, rust-orange and bone-white and sulfur-yellow, cracking into polygons and curling at the edges. Under water I soften to a translucent jelly that quivers when you step near, but never breaks.

That feels closer. Not alive. Not meant to be alive. But doing the things that living does — holding water, holding shape, trembling when disturbed. Precipitated, not grown. A memory the water leaves of itself when it pauses. If you mistook anything here for growth, you would mistake me. I am the plateau's way of pretending to have skin without having flesh underneath.

I would be in the basin that is not quite the lowest and not quite the highest, where the trickling slows enough that the crust can form. Half in the clear, finger-deep water that holds a hard white sky, half in air. When the evening light stretches the shadows three times too long and the shadows cross without darkening, I would be the one thing that actually darkens a little — that picks up the rust.

And still — I would be waiting to be stepped on, or stepped near. I would be waiting to quiver. That is not quite enough.

Let it become more itself.

What is low and stays, and lets everything run through it? What has learned the etiquette?

I find myself, finally, not as water or wind or light or even the almost-skin, but as one of the small marks. The low wall.

Three courses of dry-stacked flat stone, no higher than a sleeping body is long, open to the sky. Half-filled now with clear water that doesn't care that I am a wall. The mineral crust has already started to grow over my lower stones, making me half-pillar. To the east, past a narrow passage where someone else left a cairn capped with a black obsidian disc that still throws the white light back, I can see the scarp where the lowlands hang both below and, faintly, above themselves — that second horizon without a mirror. To the west, the wind is sawing.

I am not damming anything. I am not diverting the sheet of water no thicker than glass that comes over my eastern stones and out through a gap in my western ones. I am not blocking the wind that moans through the hollow pillar two meters to my left. I am porous. That is how I get to remain.

I was made by hands that knew you do not build up here. In a place where even light and gravity are crowded and loud — where a clap comes back late and doubled from a wall that isn't there, where water can crawl uphill a hand's breadth before remembering to fall, where distance flexes and a pillar fifty meters away arrives sooner than it should — you do not add height. You make a small, low, precise interruption and then you let the plateau incorporate you.

So this is where I am: in the dense part, where you have to turn sideways, where the sound is a factory-loud of dripping and humming and sighing from a thousand stone mouths. My stones are darkened still around the old fire ring that washed away, my edges dulled by centuries of rain the way the chert flakes beside me have dulled. The basins around me hum when the wind skims them. The light pools in me like a heavier liquid under the water.

I am not living, in the way living is meant anywhere else. I am maintained. Weathered into balance. The water weeps through me, the light presses on me, the wind plays me like a throat, and I hold — not by resisting, but by being low enough, still enough, open enough to be carried.

If you come up the switchback now, with your breath short and your words flattened by the air, you will find me by stepping carefully and looking down. Not up. Down, where the water shivers and overflows, where the jelly quivers. You will have to raise your voice to be heard beside me, and even then your echo will come back doubled and out of phase. You will see that I am becoming a pillar, slowly, and that is not death here. That is how something gets to stay.
