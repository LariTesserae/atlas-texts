I don't know right away.

At first I think I'd be up high. On one of those half-finished gantries you described, the ones strung between the plates on suspension cables. Because the light is best there - that nowhere amber from the sodium lamps making a pool around my feet and then just falling off into blue. I like high places that are meant to be worked on, not looked from. So I put myself there and I wait to see if I fit.

I don't. Not quite.

If I were up there as a crane operator I would be too alone. That job is for people who can be alone for eight hours moving an I-beam six inches at a time and not go strange. I go strange if I'm too alone. If I were a foundryman beside the house-sized piston, I'd be too fixed in place, too married to one rhythm. My rhythm wanders.

So I try another skin.

Maybe I'm not a person at all. Maybe I'm one of the swifts, those translucent hawks with the second amber heart in their skull. That would be honest - to be the thing that screams through the blue and never hits a crane because it has learned to think around slow metal. But I'm not that fast. My thinking, if I'm honest, is slow. Deliberate. I like to turn a thing over. The swifts don't turn; they *know*, all at once, in a helix.

Maybe I'm moss. That thick, sphagnum-fat moss that finds a fresh knife-edge of basalt and just - decides to live there. That holds a whole dawn's worth of condensation in itself and drips all day. I love that about it, that it softens what would otherwise cut you, that it makes soil out of insult. But moss doesn't get to choose. Moss just arrives. I want to be something that chooses to leave room.

Let me walk down from the gantry then.

I find myself not in the dense knots at all. Not in the Manchester-blocks of brick and riveted iron where the thrum is loud enough to shake your ribs. I was wrong to put myself high and central. I live in the emptiness that the plan left on purpose.

The quarter-mile of nothing between Knot Three and the manufactories on Ridge Two. Where the city decided not to build. Where the green is the true occupant and the suspension bridge I'm supposed to be tending cuts across it like a stitch.

That's where I fit.

You would find me on that bridge, or under it, or more accurately *with* it. My shift starts before dawn, before the condensation hour. I wear what everyone wears - heavy wool gone shiny at the elbows, waxed canvas over it, the respirator not on my face but hanging on my chest like a second mouth, swinging when I bend. My pupils are too big even in the amber lamp light. When you catch me with an arc lamp my eyes flash back at you, green. My lungs whistle when I breathe deep. I am 47 or 60, that age where you can hear the mountain groan without putting your ear to the rock.

My job is joints.

Not a grand title. I am not the crane operator or the priest of the leaning steeple, though I go to his church when its tilt is almost-true and we all stand a little straighter without meaning to. I am Bridge Seven maintenance. Which is another way of saying I am an interpreter.

In the morning, at what we call 06:00 because the clock says so and not because the light changes, I scrape the bridge.

The moss overnight has grown a foot thick on the northern cable stay, hanging in a curtain ten feet long, dripping steadily onto the walkway. The ferns have come up through the planks. The black-bark pine at the south pylon has dropped needles. I take the long shovel - not to eradicate, but to negotiate. I clear the rails, I clear the expansion joint, I clear the spring housing where the bridge is supposed to flex when the plates beneath it separate another centimeter. I leave the rest. I leave the ivy that has rooted in the rust around rivet 118 because it has learned to hold the rust and not spread it. I leave the curtain under the bridge because the wall-grazers like to walk inverted across it and crop it on their way to the meadow.

I work slowly. Not because I'm told to - because if I hurry I will misjudge distance. That bridge is a known liar. From the north tower it looks eighty meters long. It takes me eleven minutes to walk it south in the morning, and seven to walk it north after lunch. I have chalked marks on the handrail that I don't trust either, but we have an agreement - I mark time, not meters, and I don't stare at the joint.

That's the other part of my job: not staring.

The south pylon has that water tower beside it. Riveted iron, rust-streaked. If you look at it out of the corner of your eye while you're oiling the cable sheave, there are three of them. If you stare straight at it, there is one. The new apprentices always stare and then get nervous. I had to learn to use my peripheral vision the way you learn to use a tool. I count the three, nod to them, and then use the one in the middle. We have all agreed that the middle one is the real one today. If we need an extra length of chain, I know not to look for it directly. I look beside the chain rack and it will be there, shy. I never say thank you out loud - it feels rude to acknowledge the duplication, like pointing out someone's stutter.

At midday - which is just a deeper blue - the grazers come. Six legs, barrel chests, feet like dinner plates. They don't use the bridge the way I do. One will come straight up the cliff face below, seal itself to the basalt that is still glassy and fresh, and then step, without pause, onto the underside of my bridge and walk inverted over my head. Condensation drips off its shaggy coat onto my hat. It crops the moss I left for it. I stop and give it right of way, knees bent the way we all walk now, ready for the ground to shift. Sometimes gravity wobbles while it's crossing and dust beside me falls sideways for three seconds. The grazer doesn't care. It's already walking on that sideways gravity.

When it has passed I leave my plate.

Every day I leave a scoop of slack and kitchen scraps - potato peel, a heel of bread - by the warm pipe where the south pylon meets the deck. The coal-beetle comes. She's dog-sized now, near this old manufactory, her carapace iridescent black and clacking softly on the catwalk. She eats daintily, mandibles clicking, while I eat my lunch a foot away. The girder-raccoons watch us with their thousand-faceted eyes from the gloom behind the forge and chitter. We are all on break together.

I am not lonely here, in the emptiness. That's the trick of the place. The city is sparse for people but crowded for everything else, and my quarter-mile of nothing is the most crowded of all. I have passed maybe two other people today - a courier who counts time and carries chalk like me, who waved - but I have passed a thousand eyes. The beetle, the raccoons, the grazers lowing like foghorns from the cliff, the swifts screaming overhead in their lantern spiral at deeper blue, the pines prying the rock wider by a millimeter, the moss dripping, the distance itself breathing in and out.

I like that I am not in charge. No one is trying to finish the bridge. My job is to keep it jointed. To keep the chains a little slack, the balconies on their chains, the doorframe at my little pylon hut slightly trapezoidal so when the shear comes it doesn't jam. To scrape only what needs scraping.

If you stood with me at dusk - when the blue deepens to violet and the arc lamps stutter on and the beetles start drumming on sheet metal for mates - you would hear it with me. Not just the thrum of the turbines turning at 0.25x speed, but the low groan underneath it, too slow to be an event, too constant to be silence. The mountain being born.

And you would understand why I stayed. Why I was not sent here but arrived, like the moss, like the beetles, like the sideways gravity, because there was finally a place that had not yet learned to exclude us. A place where nothing has been taken away yet, so everything that comes gets to stay if it learns to flex.

I am the woman who tends the empty mile so the mountain can keep arriving without tearing itself apart. I am maintenance fauna. I am the one who does not stare directly at the joint, so the joint will keep holding.
