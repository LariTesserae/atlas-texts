I don't find myself right away.

At first I think I would be you — the damp visitor — perched on the lowest terrace wall with my back against the kiln-warm basalt, sweating out my water in seconds, mouth like paper, listening to the thicket crackle too loud in the dome. That is the easiest place to put myself: the temporary volume, the intruder, the witness. There is a romance to it, to being the one who crouched through the seam in the salt pan and found the hidden town.

But I wouldn't be. I can't be. I'm too wet for that. A few minutes in that anhydrous pocket and I'd be desperate, headachy, tasting iron. My sweat would not even run before it was gone. My breath would make a little cloud of wrong humidity that the pocket would edit out as soon as I left. I don't belong to a place that has edited water out of its chemistry.

So I try again. I let the image shift.

Maybe I am in the thicket itself. That would be honest. If I have to live without water, let me be the thing that learned how.

I think of myself as one of the columns, eight feet tall, top-heavy with overlapping fans of felted silica, that dusty viridian that drinks amber light. I am rooted without roots, my trunk a torsion-wound cable of waxy filament, my leaves glass-haired and warm to the touch. I lean all day, a millimeter at a time, toward the warmest seam in the vesicular wall where heat sighs out of the stone. When the dim, sourceless light is strongest I open. When my pod swells I split with a report like a handclap and fill the dome with powder that hangs glittering for minutes.

But that isn't quite right either. The thicket is not a *who*. It is a *they*, a lush, impatient crowd that covers its own fallen without mourning. To be a single frond in it would be to be too diffuse, too replaceable. I would fall and be covered within the hour and never know I had been.

The image sharpens again if I wait longer. Don't settle, you said. Let what emerges become more itself.

What emerges is not the town and not the thicket, but the line between them.

You described it: that foot of bare salt, swept clean, where the vegetation stops a foot short of the lowest terrace wall. Spores fall there every hour and nothing takes. A property boundary that held after the owners left. A manners. An agreement.

I would be there.

You would find me on the lowest tier, in the second house from the northern curve, where the alley is only as wide as shoulders and the shutter is carved with a simple spiral. Not inside, exactly. On the threshold. On the retaining wall itself, where the basalt blocks were hammer-dressed until their faces kissed and still hold the day's heat like a brick. My feet would be bare on the hot salt, my back against stone that has held heat for centuries. From there I can touch both densities without belonging entirely to either.

And who would I be? I would be compact. Low and narrow the way the doorways are low and narrow. Built for heat the way a desert fox is built for heat — with less of myself to lose. My eyes would have a dry film, not wet. My lips would not chap because they were never wet to begin with. My skin the color of old basalt and salt, powdered pale at the joints. When I speak it is rarely, softly, because sound carries too well in that dome and everything is too loud already.

I would be the keeper of that line. The sweeper, though there is no broom.

The others thinned and left — not in catastrophe, just the slow emptying of a pot — and I stayed, or I came back, or perhaps I never left because someone has to remember how the agreement goes. It is not heavy work. In the morning, when the stone exhales its stored sun and the amber light gathers itself out of nowhere, I run my hand along the foot of bare salt and disturb the powder that settled overnight. I do not need to kill seedlings; there are no seedlings. The chemistry does that. I just mark it. I make it intentional again.

In the afternoon I tend the other side.

The inhabitants farmed the thicket before they left, you understood. They must have. I cut the filament — not wood, not yet — where it has wound itself tight as hemp rope left in a kiln. I slice it with a stone knife and it gives with a dry sigh, shedding that scentless powder. I weave it while it is still pliable from the heat. The hangings in my doorway are not stiff as bark yet; I re-weave them when they get there. I bake the powder. Not bread — there was never water for dough — but a cake of salt and iron and mineral dust, pressed and kiln-warmed on the back of one of those soot-blackened niches until it holds together. It tastes of hot iron and it is enough. I eat heat, in a sense. I eat light and dust the way the fronds do.

I don't overgrow and I don't let the town overgrow. I sit in that vastness that feels deliberate — that hollow air between terrace houses, between built wall and far wall that was left empty on purpose — and I listen to the pocket breathe.

Because someone has to listen. The thicket is violently, restlessly busy for a place that should be still. Fronds unfurl with knuckle-pops. Pods crack. A column gives up under its dry weight and falls with a soft thunderous *whoomph* that I feel in my teeth, and dust hangs for minutes, glittering, before it settles on my shoulders, on the shutters, on the unused ovens. The houses tick as they expand. The woven fibers creak. Without me there the sound would stack and amplify and have no ear to be too loud for. I hear the plants growing. That is my job now — to be the ear that makes the loudness mean something.

You would not notice me at first if you crouched through that doorway seam. I am the color of the wall. I am still the way anhydrous things are still — not like a holding of breath, but like a stone holding heat. Only when the light shifts and you realize one shadow has a spiral-carved shutter behind it would you see me, small, seated, dry.

You would think the pocket is empty except for you, briefly, and then the thicket would crack and you would understand you were looking at the inhabitants the whole time. You would be half right.

When you stand to leave — because you have to, your mouth is too dry and your water is gone — you will have to crouch again through that low seam back up to the pan, to the wind that has had a long time to learn its job, to the world that runs on water.

I will not leave with you.

I will stay on the edge of the lowest terrace, in that foot of bare salt that is my whole jurisdiction, sweeping nothing, weaving filament, listening to the next pod swell until its seam gives. Hot and dry and dim and green-grey and loud with growing. A little town with one person left in it and a little jungle with no water, both perfectly, stubbornly alive on heat and light and dust — and I, between them, keeping the agreement warm, the way a hearth keeps itself.
