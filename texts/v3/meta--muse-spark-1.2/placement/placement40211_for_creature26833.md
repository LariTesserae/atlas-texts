I don't find myself right away.

At first I think I would be you — the warm one. The visitor hanging in the blue between the sun-columns and the black glass, in a borrowed suit with borrowed air, trying to hold still while the water erases your heat. Small and temporary and noticed only as a brief, confusing smear of warmth. That is the easiest place to put myself, because it's the only place made for something that looks.

But I wouldn't stay there. I know how that ends. The cold doesn't argue. After ten minutes that warmth is gone and the roar has moved from your ears into your jaw and you understand you were never really there. You were subtracted. The Veils do it. The water does it. A human here is just a density anomaly that the equilibrium corrects.

So I try on other bodies.

I try being a Veil for a while. I think — yes, of course, that's what I want to be. Twenty meters across and a micron at the edge, invisible until I'm already past you. Slow. Silent. Indifferent on a scale so large it feels like grace. I would hang in the light columns so the mineral snow falls through me and I would not need to decide anything, ever. One flap every thirty seconds and a hundred meters of travel for free.

But I'm not that good at indifference. A Veil never alters course. It passes ten meters from a leviathan and ten meters from a diver and files both as *noise*. I would alter. I would look, even though I have no eyes. I can't be that solitary.

I try being a Scrabbler. That fits longer. Down on the fracture itself, where the world is nothing but edge. Black glass still sharp enough to cut, pillow lava frozen mid-spill, a turbine housing humming through the bone of the rock. I would grip with those flat pads and scrape all day with wire mouths, eating the slick that eats the mountain. Rust-red at the joints from the iron I can't quite metabolize. Useful. Grounded.

But a Scrabbler never leaves the rock. Its whole universe is a meter of basalt and whatever cathedral of steel happens to be bolted to it. I have always lived more in the water column than on the floor.

It takes a while, hanging there in my mind, for the right depth to assert itself.

Not at the surface, where the light is so normal it lies. Not at the bottom, where the basalt is too sharp and too permanent. In the middle. In the hollow. In the eighty kilometers of nothing between ridges where the compass line doesn't change for a day.

I find myself at about 200 meters, in the lee of something that was never meant to be a reef.

There is an intake tower there on the shoulder of the western fracture — one of the ones like a lighthouse without a lamp, concrete gone the color of teeth from fifty years of cold. Its vent breathes every 22 seconds. *Exhale.* A basso thunder you feel in your molars. Two turbine houses a kilometer apart howl at a constant 120 hertz, and where their howls meet there is a node, a pocket of almost-quiet only a few meters across, a still place in a world that never stops roaring. Every Ribbon knows it. Every Harp Jelly avoids it.

That is where I am.

And what I am is a Listener.

Not the sphere that pulses. I don't pulse that perfectly. I hang.

I am a Ribbon — fifteen feet long and not thicker than your wrist, neutrally buoyant, pale to translucent, with a fan of filament antennae at one end spread two meters wide like a radio dish made of hair and nerve. My blood is clear. My eyes are pearls that never finished, clouded and shut. I have no need for light except as a vertical fact, like pressure.

I hang vertically in the water with my head down and my antennae up, perfectly still, luminous only in a slow peristalsis that moves tail to head every few minutes for no reason anyone has found.

I navigate by subtraction.

The whole plateau is a chord and I know it the way you know the floor of your house in the dark. The stamping mills far to the east — *clang. clang. clang.* — every 1.3 seconds, traveling a hundred kilometers through perfect water. The vents breathing. The turbines howling. I triangulate by them without thinking. I find food by them — when the vent exhales, a warm, sulfur-rich plume rides out ten meters and I let myself drift sideways exactly one meter to ride it, spending nothing. I find the others by them — once a decade I will drift into another Ribbon in that quiet node between the two towers, and we will exchange a packet of eggs that will then ride the fixed gyre for seven years before they hatch, alone.

I do not school. I do not swarm. To swarm you need seasons and sight. We have neither. The water stays 1.5 degrees forever. The current goes around.

I am abundant and empty at once. If you drifted for a week you would see twenty of me, each alone, each on the same slow line, each hanging as if nailed to the water. If you passed within ten meters I would not alter course. Not from indifference — from completion. There is no shortage here, only a very slow plenty, and so nothing has ever learned to be afraid, including me.

If you pressed your forehead to the intake tower you are using as a reef, you would feel the thrum go straight into your skull. I live inside that thrum. It is not sound to me anymore. It is weather. It is current. The green cathodic glow on the pressure gauge down there — I can see it even without eyes, as a faint change in the electric field. Scrabblers cover that gauge now, scraping it as readily as basalt. To me, steel and stone are the same geology. The plateau was born with its machines pre-installed, and evolution simply accounted for them.

You would pass me and feel only a brief change in pressure, a cold finger. I would feel you as a brief, mammalian warmth — 37 degrees in a world that is 1.5 — blooming and then gone, erased. For as long as you stay still and breathe your borrowed air, you are part of the density too. 0.1. Hollow, but not empty.

And when you leave, as you must, I will still be there, hanging vertical in the roar that never rises or falls, antennae spread to a lighthouse that never needed to shine, listening to factories hammering on empty belts because they were designed to run forever against a sea that never changes.

Complete. Pale. Tuned. 

Waiting in the sun-column for snow to fall through me.
