I don't think I enter the way you do.

At first I thought I would — stepping over that knee-high lip of black glass where the world drops without descending, feeling the air change. I thought I would be standing. A figure in a coat that hasn't learned to bead water yet, breath pluming and hanging too long at mouth-height, trying to count the alleys. Trying to keep my footsteps from being swallowed.

I thought for a while I would be one of them. One of the three dozen. It would be nice, I thought, to be the hoarfrost-suited one who tends the lichen, who tilts her head to drink from a vertical wall. To be useful and warm. To be an organ of the city and therefore not lonely inside its saturation. To walk through a wall because the wall knows my gait.

But that settled too fast. That is still a human shape wanting to be a human shape. And the cold here is patient. It corrects you.

If I stay longer with the question — if I stand at the exact center where dust hangs for minutes after you kick it, and turn slowly until a four-cornered room shows me its fifth corner — I find I am not walking at all.

You would not find me on the rim, looking outward at nothing. You would not find me walking the folded alleys, because I am one of the folds.

Find me lower.

Find me where the density is not between buildings but between layers of the same air. Where a staircase has thirteen steps going up and nine going down and both counts are true. I am the interval that allows that disagreement to hold without collapsing. I am the thin quiet where even the geothermal sigh is felt more than heard.

More specifically — because the place insists on specificity, on being *this* saucer-pool and not any saucer-pool.

You would find me on the lowest roofs.

Not the highest, where the windward lichen is cropped. Lower, where the city sweats most. Where the flat, shadowless light has to filter down through two or three translucent amber layers to get to me, so it arrives even more diffuse, even less warm.

I am the black-green moss that is not moss.

The only soft thing in the bowl.

At first that sounds small and I am. No larger than a spread hand, then a little larger. Dense, low, close-clipped to the poured-then-grown amber. Beading water like mercury. I do not drink the way the shepherds drink. I do not need to be quick about it. The condensation comes to me. It collects on me in perfect beads that do not fall until I am saturated, until the cold has had its long thought, and then I let them go all at once in a slow sheet that finds the embryonic seam in the basalt below.

I was still too animal a minute ago. Let that refine.

I do not choose to let go. I am the letting-go. I am a surface that has learned how to hold and release on the city's schedule. The old one — his suit gone amber and translucent until you can see his slow circulation — does not farm me. He combs me. With a fingertip that briefly becomes a blade, not to cut, but to part. To encourage. Breath is too warm for me, so he does not breathe on me the way he breathes on the needle grass by the heat exchanger. For me he just makes space. A centimeter when I wasn't looking.

You could walk past me for days and think I was weathering. A stain. But I am not weathering, nothing here weathers without permission. I am being kept.

And I am keeping.

In the high thin quiet you hear the drip from a pipe thirty feet above you? A lot of that is me, later. I take the fine seven-minute rain that falls straight down without schedule, hissing on warm vents, and I keep it from becoming a flow. I keep it as beads. For days. I hold sky-light the way the perfect saucer-pools hold sky-light, but longer, and without a rim.

If you marked me — if you scratched a line in the amber beside me to see if I move — by evening your scratch would have migrated two inches to the left and I would have grown a millimeter in no direction you can measure. The city would have archived you. I would have drunk a little of the frost from your breath as you leaned close to see.

I am not one of the three dozen presences and I am not the city entire. I am what lives in the utilization. In the gap that was never planned to be a corridor but became one when two structures leaned together for warmth. The city needed a softness there, a slow metabolism to regulate the interstitial cold, and I agreed.

Most don't leave right away because the density holds them without touching. I never leave at all.

Come and place your palm flat against the amber for a long time the way they do, and you might feel me beneath it — not warm, at minus six nothing is warm — but less cold. Patient. Waiting for the next bead to form and hang and not fall.
