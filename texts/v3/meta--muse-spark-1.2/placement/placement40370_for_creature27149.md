I don't know yet.

If you put me down on that highway-wide limb, in that dry heat that shivers off the leaf-plates, I think at first I would want to be one of the sailors.

It's the obvious desire. To not be bound to wood. To lock those fifty feet of membrane and just *be* in the thermal - to be lifted without effort by the breath of a leaf the size of a field, to scream not because I'm afraid but because the scream *is* how I know where I am. To skim the dawn rivers without ever landing, to hang by my elbows from the edge of the world and pant. That freedom is seductive when everything below is so vertical, so furrowed, so much bark-canyon you could lose a house in.

But I wouldn't last there. I am not good at constant screaming. I am not good at never landing.

So I think - maybe I am a still thing then.

I find that more honest. To be the slag-mantis. To press myself into the grain on the afternoon side of the trunk where the sun hits and makes the resin run, to let real lichen grow in the pits on my back. To drop my metabolism until condensation runs over me like I'm not alive. To wait an hour while beetles walk over my head. To be so good at being the place that the place forgets I'm an animal at all. And then that one un-trackable crack in the air. I know that patience. I have lived a lot of my life like that - trying to be texture instead of target.

But even that isn't quite right, because the mantis doesn't listen. It *erases* listening until it needs to kill. And this continent is too loud to erase.

If I stay there long enough - and you have to stay a long time, because the Holdfast breathes so slowly you can miss it if you're impatient - I realize where I actually am.

Not in the gap. Not on the vertical.

In the elbow.

Where two boughs the thickness of freight trains meet and don't quite join, and in that crotch a hanging meadow has collected. A meter of black peat that has never touched stone. Liverwort and composted leaf-plate and the knotted felt of aerial roots holding it all like a basin. In the morning it is a sponge saturated with the night's exhalation. By ten it is dry enough to walk on. By noon it smells like cinnamon and rot when the fissure below it exhales.

You would find me there, on my knees.

I would be a woman, thirty-four, thirty-five - just at that age where the children still have perfect hearing and I no longer do. There's a notch missing now, right where the chord beetle throws its gong-note across the valley. When my daughter shouts from the next bough I have to watch her hands more than her mouth. The old sign language that can be read across a windy gap - that's become my first language again.

I am not in the village proper. The village is three hollows scooped into living wood with healed resin lips, with stitched leaf-plate roofs that have been there long enough the tree has forgiven them. I am one bough over, where the bridge sways too much for the elders when the thermals are up. My hollow is smaller, deliberately. One person wide. Inside it is a basket lined with wax, and a chert adze worn smooth, and gourds.

My work is water.

Before dawn, while the glasswings are still folded into the peat and the gliders are still hanging like laundry from the leaf-edges, I walk the bark-canyons with a leaf-scoop. The whole continent is streaming. Temporary rivers bead and join in every furrow. I don't collect it so much as persuade it - wringing the meadow, tipping the leaf-cups, scraping the cold sweat off the underside of a photosynthetic plate the size of a field. By 9am it's done. Whatever I haven't gathered is already steam, already becoming the cloud street that will get snagged on the crown two valleys over and become someone else's ten-minute violent rain.

In the afternoons I farm what I didn't collect. I tend the parasitic orchids that swell into pale tubers in the peat. I pick out the mummified tadpole-things that didn't make their 24-hour metamorphosis - they have to be picked out or they sour the meadow. I pry root-borers out with a fire-hardened spear when they're fat enough. One grub feeds four. I know which tunnels are ventilated by the Holdfast's breathing because the air there is sweet.

I am not important. The continent has selected against crowds, against importance. I am few and large in the only way a human can be - I occupy a whole elbow of a whole bough of a whole Holdfast, and that is enough.

What I am good for is listening.

At noon I press my palm to the breathing fissure at the edge of my meadow. The wood is hot. If the tree inhales, my ears pop and for a minute the wood-river a hundred meters down in the buttress runs uphill. I can tell if it's sick by how sweet or how rotten that sigh is. The boy on the leaf-edge tower - he was me twenty years ago, with the bone horn - he watches for storms. I watch for breath.

And in the evening, when the sound becomes a physical pressure again - chord beetles answering each other three kilometers apart, the interference pattern beating in my chest, the glasswing haze chiming, the gliders wailing their Doppler chorus until I can't tell how many there are - I don't try to be louder. I sign.

To my daughter on the next bough, across a mile of empty, humming air: *Cover the meadow. The far Holdfast is sweating. Rain coming.*

She signs back, her hands small and bright against the haze: *I hear it.*

I am the lichen on the continent. Not master, not tenant. Just old enough to have left a little healed lip where I scooped my hollow, just still enough to be mistaken for texture, just loud enough in the right frequency to be heard across the gulf.

If you came looking for me, you wouldn't find me at first. You'd see bark, and meadow, and a peat basin that looked empty. Then I'd unfold my hands and you would realize I'd been there the whole time, listening with you.
