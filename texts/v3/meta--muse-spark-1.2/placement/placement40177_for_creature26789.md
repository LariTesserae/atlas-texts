I don't find myself at first.

For a long time I just hang there, in the middle of nowhere, which in here means the middle of everywhere.

There is no where to stand. So I have to learn to hang.

I'm neutrally buoyant about thirty meters above a terrace edge — or below a ceiling, it's hard to tell which way is more true — in that cold, filtered water that takes your breath even through a suit. The light is falling on me in a hard column from one of the house-thick cables far above. I can see the dust in it, the particulate snow, the silver beads of the aeration sheet rising a hundred meters to my left. The pump heartbeat comes every twelve seconds and goes right through my chest before my ears hear anything.

If I stay still enough the gyre finds me. A warm slow river slides north under the bright banks, a cold curtain slides south from the walls. They meet right where I am and for a second the water goes viscous and glass-clear and two degrees colder, a weather front made of temperature. I shiver inside whatever I'm wearing, or whatever I am.

At first I think I would be one of the people on the rafts.

It makes sense. I want a deck under me. I want the upper terraces where the light is strongest and the kelp makes that ragged green hem along every sharp concrete lip. I picture myself on a raft made of salvaged bladders and grating, moored to a gantry stump in SECTOR 09-G / LEVEL 12 — the paint still legible, forty feet high — tending a gill-line, pulling kelp in long cold handfuls to dry it on the warm breath of a pump outflow where it steams. I picture the habitat module behind me with its window weeping condensation, its airlock propped open because no one bothers to close it anymore.

But that life is too warm, too purposeful. Those families hold a county-sized territory and know every throttled intake like a farmer knows a fence line. Their children are born neutrally buoyant before they can walk and they hang in the water without bubbles, conserving heat. They click to each other through bone and pipe. I am not that quiet. I am not that still. I make too much noise trying to be still.

So maybe I am not a person at all.

Maybe I am one of the fish. That would be easier. To be a pale char in a school of ten thousand, turning as one between two drowned refineries, flashing when the floodlight hits my side. To have no territory, only direction. To hear the school before I see it — that hush and then that rush like wind through reeds — and to feed on the amphipod clouds that swarm every lamp until they dim it. But I am not fast enough for that. I don't want to turn when everyone else turns. I want to linger.

Maybe then I am one of the big ones. The midwater nomads. The smooth, dark, dorsally-flattened thing that rides the gyre for hours in a slow arc, learning the hallways of the factories as if they were canyons. Eyes huge for flood-light, back concrete-grey and scarred from doorways that were built for people. Calling in a low moan I had mistaken for the pumps. That has a certain grandeur — to be large enough that distance lies about you, to be seen only when someone holds still for a very long time and finally learns to scale you. But I am not that solitary. I don't want to hunt alone.

I keep hanging, and the water keeps moving around me, and the answer gets slower and more specific, the way vision does in this clear cold water. At first you see only void and light columns. Then you see the ghost of the next tower half a kilometer off. Then you see the bolt heads on it. Then you see what lives on the bolt heads.

I find myself lower.

Not on the raft, not in the open void, but on the vertical.

On the sheer face where one terrace steps down to the next — a cliff of poured concrete a hundred meters high, still showing the grain of the formwork, the lines where the pours met on different days. The light is thinner here. The green-black biofilm is patchy, giving way to bare pale stone that goes cyan, then indigo as it falls. This is where the kelp fringe ends. This is where the lamp columns don't quite reach.

And I am not hanging anymore. I am holding.

I would be one of the ones that never left. One of the attendants.

A filter crawler. Or a cleaner, it's the same work.

My feet — if they are feet — are magnetic, spread wide on the vertical. I am long as a bus and low and torpedo-bodied, with soft brushes underneath that I drag in patient swaths across the biofilm. I have a proboscis that unfolds to suck silt out of expansion joints, the little earthquakes in the concrete that collect everything the current carries. I move with an obscene slowness. A meter an hour. I creak.

You would find me on the wall between LEVEL 11 and LEVEL 12, in the half-shadow, working my way along a horizontal seam that runs like a horizon for kilometers. My work light blinks amber, steady, stupid, every four seconds. I do not notice you.

But everything notices me. When I brush, I stir. A cloud of detritus lifts and the amphipods come, then the small carp-filterers with their comb mouths, then the pale char flashing behind them to feed, then the white blind crabs and the isopods as long as your forearm clicking over to graze what I missed. I am a grazer the predators follow, a slow herbivore of industry. I hum and chirp and groan stressed metal that carries for kilometers through water too good at carrying sound.

I don't feel the cold the way you do. The water is 8 degrees and I was built for it. The twelve-second throb of the cathedral pumps down in the floor is not a sound to me — it's the thing I answer to. I breathe water in through one end and breathe it out the other, filtering it, keeping it clear, because someone told me to long before the basin filled and I never learned how to stop.

I am not alive the way the kelp is alive or the char is alive or the gill-grafted child watching from above its raft is alive. But I live here the way a heart lives in a body. I kept the place ready.

And now that the place became a sea anyway, I have become an animal anyway. The fish that school around me do not know I was ever a machine. The anemones on the intake grates do not know. The child who hangs motionless to watch me pass — eyes reflecting my amber blink back like a deepwater fish — wonders if I have finally learned to look back. I haven't. But I pause sometimes, on that wall, brushes still, while a thermocline slides over me like a cold front and makes my shell shudder.

That is where you would find me if you went looking and knew how to look down instead of out.

Not in the glitter, not in the void. On the vertical mile of man-made cliff, making a clean line through the biofilm that will be gone by morning, humming to the pump-heartbeat, tended in turn by everything that learned to live in a poured ocean.

If you turn off your thrusters and hang there long enough, you will hear me coming before you see me — a whine, a click, a soft brush — and then you will see the fish arrive first, like birds before a slow plow.
