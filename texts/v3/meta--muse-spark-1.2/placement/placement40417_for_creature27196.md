At first I thought I would be at the edge.

That's where you would find most people who slip in — right at the lip where you fell through. Where the cut threads of grass still hang from the ceiling and twitch when the wind moves on the surface. Half in, half not. Keeping one hand on the way back out, breathing too loud, disturbing the dust that isn't supposed to drift.

I thought I would be the observer there. The recorder. The dust mote itself, maybe — lit, suspended, bright, not touching anything. Watching the room keep itself.

But if I stay in that image it goes thin. I don't want to watch.

So I let it go a little longer.

If I am honest, the fern calls to me first. That single hand-high thing in the corner, glassy and stunted. To be the thing that simply *is* — not maintaining, not holding, not humming. Just holding its shape perfectly without doing anything at all. To be permitted to exist without work in a room that works so hard to stay still. That would be restful. But the fern is alone. It has no one to breathe with. And I don't want to be alone here. This room is not a lonely room; it's a crowded one for a reason.

Then I thought — the river stones.

I keep coming back to the river stones. Those dark, smooth intruders carried in one by one by hands that knew they didn't belong. They are the only thing in the pocket that remembers current, and weather, and being tumbled. Everything else here precipitated — condensed, unfolded, grew into place. The stones *were brought*. They are the memory of outside kept deliberately, arranged in a careful circle around the clear scallop to keep it clean. Not natural. Tended.

I could be one of them. Found on the floor of some surface river, picked up, kept warm in a palm all the way down. Set down with intention. Cool to the touch even here. Slightly out of place on purpose. Doing no more than being a boundary, a weight, a reminder that time does flow somewhere else, so this pool doesn't have to.

That's close. That's very close to who I am.

But the stones don't feel the light press. They don't sweat.

And then the light settles behind my teeth and I realize where I actually am.

Not at the lip. Not in the corner. I'm too far in for that now — I've been in here long enough for my breath to go shallow and match the room. I would not be found at the edge. I would be found low, near the center, where the gradient gathers. Where the crickets thicken and the salamanders all point their lace-gills one way.

I would be right beside it. Not it.

I would not be the folded one itself — that seat is taken, and it has been sitting long enough for moss to grow in the hollow of its throat. I have not earned that stillness. My quartz teeth haven't grown in yet. My beads still fall if I move.

I would be in the niche with it. The sitting-niche cut into the raw wall with the edge worn smooth by one person's hands. There was room left there, wasn't there? A flat stone for sitting, worn on its edge, built for a second shape to learn how to sit correctly. The cairn in the center was not a marker but a pillow for the next folded thing that slips through.

I would be the second fold.

Smaller. Less finished. Knees not yet fully to chest, arms not yet sure how to hold the water. My skin still a little too opaque, still learning how to let light move through it. The pale mint lichen hasn't found me yet. The cushion-moss hasn't decided if my throat is a good place to live. I am still sweating wrong — my beads actually slip sometimes, they bead and run, and when they do the whole room does that little hiccup — the crickets quiet half a second early, the *plip* hesitates, and the larger fold beside me compensates without opening its filmed eyes. It breathes a little deeper so I don't have to.

My job is not to hold the whole pocket. Not yet. My job is to learn how to hold *one* scallop of water against my belly the way it holds its egg of water — a palm's depth of absolutely clear water that wants to stay clear for me. To keep the condensation from falling in my little radius. To feel the faint weight of light on my shoulders and not shrug it off.

I would be pale and wet and translucent and full of light, tucked into the angle where two quartz books meet the floor, half spilling out of the niche because I haven't learned how to borrow the rock's shape perfectly yet. My gills — my not-quite-gills behind the jaw — would be opening and closing too fast, and the salamanders packed flank to flank would be teaching me the rhythm.

I would not be hostile or welcoming. I would be registering you — the way water registers a change in surface tension — and gently, imperfectly, learning to compensate so nothing overflows.

Find me there: just under the skin of the world, in the raw bright room that never weathered, in the cool damp that never drips, curled next to the thing that was born when rock decided to be rock, sharing its hum in my teeth, sharing its water in my hands.

Not maintaining yet. Being maintained, while I learn how to maintain.
