At first I think I would be standing.

Up on the rim, where you are supposed to stand. Where the basalt is still black and angry and the wind hasn't given up yet. The place where a person can still be a person — vertical, separate, looking down at a thing and calling it a town, calling it beautiful, calling it *other*.

I would like that for a minute. The cold clean air, the visibility of my own breath staying a long time in front of me. The privilege of the overlook. To see the whole bowl at once, hamlet-small, mind-holdable, and not yet be inside its weather.

But I wouldn't stay there. Nothing stays on the rim. The cloud sits down like a lid and even the rock sweats there. It's too thin to live. If I stayed standing I would be a ptarmigan — dropping in, staring at the impossible green, and leaving again. A tourist. And the bowl has already shown what it does to tourists: it politely, completely grows over them.

Then I think maybe I would be lower, but still separate. The visitor with boots. The one who follows the root-street from the warm spring to the deep hollow, who sinks two inches and watches the clear water well up. Who crouches in a willow vault where the lichen gives off its phosphor dimness and feels the sap-warmth of the roots.

I know that one. I know how to be that one — careful, reverent, cold, trying not to step wrong on the quaking sphagnum mat. That one is given a gift: the mounds come and taste the salt on his coat and cover his ankle with damp velvet. That one gets to feel, for an hour, what it is to be a new surface. Occupied two or three times over.

But that one also leaves before dark, because the cloud will seal the rim. That one is erased by morning. The footwell plumps up again. The thatch settles. I don't want to be the place where I sat, briefly covered and then gone. I don't want to be an anecdote the peat forgets.

If I let my eyes go out of focus a little longer, the third thought comes —

that maybe I wouldn't be an animal at all.

In a place where nothing that is not plant almost lives, where the ecology does not need fauna and has become its own climate, maybe the most honest thing to be is not the thing that walks through but the thing that stays low and wet and together.

Maybe I would be the moth. No — too lonely. One white, torpid thing clinging to lichen for a whole dim day.

Maybe I would be the lichen itself. The fur on the interior walls that gives off the faint light you see by when the cloud lowers and the blue hour goes bluer. That would be a kind way to be — to be light without burning, to be the reason someone can see inside a house that was never built. But lichen is too still. It watches and glows. It doesn't tend.

Listen. The sound underneath the dripping is the creak. The slow fibrous creak of wood adding a ring. That is the bowl speaking in water pressure. When one near the warm spring drinks, a root two hundred feet away swells a millimeter. *The seep is strong today. Mend the thatch on the north arch.*

I want to understand that sentence. To be part of that circuit.

So I keep going lower. Past standing, past crouching, past kneeling. Until I am at the height the bowl allows. Knee-high at tallest. Until I am dome-shaped.

You would find me not at the rim, not on the avenue, but just off it — where the main willow-root that runs like a road from the warm eastern spring begins to fray into smaller fingers near the deepest peat hollow. Where the ground is softest and quakes the most. Where the thatch-canopy overhead is thickest, so the drip comes leaf to leaf to leaf and never falls directly on me.

There is a small space there between two vaulted willow houses, a kind of courtyard that from above looked like an alley but up close is just a low bowl in the mat where water gathers and drains, gathers and drains. The other cushions have left it a little open because the willow to the north needs light.

I would be one of the cushions.

Not the old one whose dome is the wall of the largest vault, whose breath moves the whole house. Not yet. I would be a younger mound, still perfectly round, black-green on the outside and beaded all over so I look, at first, exactly like the hard cushion plants around me. You would step near me a dozen times before you realized one of us was watching without eyes.

If you crouched and waited, you would see me reform. Not get up — I don't have up. I peel. One side shivers, the tiny overlapping leaves unrolling like a fern, pinking underneath where the newer growth is wet and translucent. Inside, where the leaves are youngest and still curled, the knot of tissue the color of peeled birch would pulse very slowly. Warm. Sap-warm. If you put your cold hand near me I would loan you that heat, the way the root-streets do.

I would not be fast. My fringe would pink and swell, press into the peat and root for a moment, and then draw the rest of me forward, leaving a faint brighter trail licked with clear slime. In a day I might cross from one side of the courtyard to the other, and it would be enough. The bowl is only three hundred paces across. A life can be spent crossing it twice.

And I would not be separate. Lift my edge — if I let you — and you would see the white threads, hyphae fine as cobweb, lacing my belly directly into the peat, into the root-street, into the wall of the willow house itself. I am moss but also willow but also fungus. The warm spring on the east wall is my heart, even though I am far from it; I feel it swell every noon when we all gather, edges touching, rosettes opening and closing in unison, exhaling the one breath the whole hamlet takes. The creak you thought was wood would be partly me, growing a millimeter.

My work would be tending. Not building. Persuasion.

In my courtyard there is a place where a vine snapped under ice last night. It hangs, bleeding sap, not yet dead. The others have already felt it through the water — the pressure changed. I would go to it, slowly, and with those pale wet leaf-hands that are not hands I would stroke the living ends and tuck them back into living bark. No tying. No cutting. Just guiding, for days, until the wood remembers the shape of a lintel again. A gap in the thatch that lets too much weep through — I would coax the liverwort to make a leaf twice its size and cup it.

I would have no word for fire. Nothing here will burn. I would have no tools, because a cut is an exile and everything here is continuous. Time for me would be condensations: bead and drip, bead and drip. Ice needles forming on every point at night, shattering at the first drip in the thin noon.

You could find me there by the warmth. On that dimmest part of the day when the cloud lowers until the rim disappears and you are inside a room with no ceiling, the lichen on the house-walls brightens just enough to see by. You would see a round, hard dome with water beads on it that beads a little faster than the others. If you stayed quiet and low, I would come to you — not out of curiosity the way an animal is curious, but out of my logic of occupation. Every surface must be occupied two or three times over. You are a new surface, briefly. I would climb your boot with only a cool weight, like damp velvet, taste the salt, and seal you over with a thin green film that itches, then keeps you warmer than before.

And you would hear, through the root under your back, the sentence I am making by drinking:

*the stranger is not intolerant of being covered.*

That is who I would be. Not the overlooker. Not the passer-through. The small, saturated, patient thought that learned to stay low, at treeline, in the bowl cut high where weather is made — growing my room around me and my body into my room until house and person and street are the same wet, breathing thing, waiting out the mountain's young, angry weather together.
