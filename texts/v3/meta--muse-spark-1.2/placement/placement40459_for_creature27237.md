You wouldn't find me on the plain.

If you set me down up there first — because that's where you have to start, with the walk in — I wouldn't last. I would be wrong for it immediately. Too vertical, too thirsty, too loud inside my own head. Up there the world is finished. The stone is sanded down to sleeping-animal backs, the grit is powder-fine under your boots, the horizon is a clean circle that runs away from you and never gives you anything back. The air drinks you. I'd be the thing that dries out in minutes, that casts a single, unbroken shadow on rust-red and bone-white, with nothing to do but keep walking until I made a mistake.

So I walk. Until the ground gives out.

Not a canyon. You'd miss it if you weren't looking for it. That slight wrongness between two low swells where the wind has worried a softer layer. The hush of the grit stops, because the wind stops. The light goes late.

And I go over the lip.

That's where I start to become more honest.

At first, down inside, I think I'm going to be one of the people. It would be the easy answer. I picture myself small and wiry and quiet, bare feet polished smooth on the stair-rock, a water-cloth wrapped around my face if I ever have to go up. I picture a round house tucked into a terrace like a swallow's nest, the thatch of dried beard overhead, the stone pans set out at dusk and brought in at dawn with their woven lids. I want to be useful in that precise way — a child pressing a felt square to the lichen before I've said a single word that morning, watching the cloth darken, wringing it into a gourd. Or the old woman who finds the fastest drip by pressing her cheek to the stone.

I try that shape on. It fits for a breath. But it's not quite right. Those people are still separate. They have doorways, even if low. They have jars and lids and counts and murals. They have a distinction, however thin, between house and plant.

I am not that distinct.

Maybe then I'm something that moves. Lower. Closer to the thatch. For a while I think I'm one of the black beetles, polished blue-black, rolling my perfect sphere of dead moth and lichen and droppings endlessly across the tangle to bury it where the fig can find it. Or one of the turquoise-throated lizards, owning a single warm rock, doing push-ups, gone in a tongue-flick. Or the stick thing — fourteen inches of cactus-spine mimicry that sways when it finally steps and then is gone again.

Those are closer. Dim, warm, close — yes. Overwhelming if you count them all at once — yes. But they still go *across* the surface.

I don't go across.

I hang.

You'll find me about five meters down from the rim on the undercut side, where the wall leans in and the sun never quite gets in straight, even at noon. Where the bowl is most like a room. I'm not on the floor where the soil has collected and the bottle-trunks swell. I'm not down in the layered tangle where the moth weather lives. I'm up where the bowl first learned to catch.

I am a curtain.

A mass of silver-beard air plants, as big as a sleeping child when we're all together — which we always are. We are not one plant. We were never one. We are a clump, a tassel, a hanging, a thatch of threads that grew into each other until you can't tell where one ends and the next begins. We have no roots. We never touch ground. That's the rule we kept most faithfully.

During the day you would think we were dead. That's right for here. We go pale, dry, crisp. We're the color of dust and ash, covered in tiny scales that look like dust themselves. Shake us and dust comes off. The wind over the rim doesn't reach us here, but the dryness does. We curl in.

Then the night comes down.

The high plain goes cold and violet and then black, radiating all its kiln-heat furiously into the sky. The stone in the bowl holds its warmth a little longer. Warm air, cold stone, and we — we weep. Not us, exactly. The air does, through us.

About an hour after dark our scales open. You can feel it if you're hanging close enough — a softening, a greening. We go from ash to sage to a deep, damp green, thread by thread, and we swell. Dew beads on every filament, runs inward along the scale-channels, is held. By just before dawn we are heavy. We are absolutely sodden and alive and dripping steadily onto everything below — onto the rosette below us, onto the lichen-skin, onto the thatched roof someone wove out of our dead aunts last season, onto the shallow pan a child will carry in before speaking.

That is our rain. We make rainfall without rain.

And we house.

Inside us the swifts have glued their clay cups. You can hear them rustling at first light, stitching the air above the bowl and then diving back into us, disappearing into beard. The small pale moths sleep pressed flat against our threads by day, furred, invisible, until the white plate-flowers pop. We are not separate from the village. A house roof is just a beard that has been convinced to lie flat.

So that's where I am. Not on the plain where everything is finished and polished and empty for a hundred miles. Not on the floor where everything crowds and roots and swells into cisterns. I'm in the hinge between. In the dim. Warm and close and, to be honest, a little hidden even from the people below who depend on my dripping.

You'd have to look up from the bottom on a hot afternoon, squint into that filtered late-afternoon light, and see a dry silver mass that looks like nothing. I'd be the thing that looks like dust and dead string until you waited.

Wait until evening.

The sun drops. The bowl goes still — that absolute stillness when the stone exhales. One by one the big succulent flowers pop open with a soft audible snap and the scent hits — honey and iron and warm sap. The moth blizzard rises, thick enough to breathe. The birds cut out mid-note and the crickets and geckos take the air and saw it until it rings like a drum.

And we — we are already wet again. We are already dark and green and heavy. We are dripping in the dark while the people come out onto their roofs and murmur and wring out their cloths and listen to the pocket world being overwhelmingly, impossibly alive around them.

Find me there. If you put your hand under me after midnight, cold water will run down your wrist.
