You wouldn't find me at first.

At first, if you set me down on that mesa, I'd think I wanted to be in the grove.

Because it's where the life is. The room with its own weather. I'd imagine myself tucked under the overlapping plates of smoked glass, breathing that warm, filtered sigh the colony exhales, listening to the felt-moss crackle overhead like a low-voltage thought. I'd want to be held. To be part of the thing that is lush without ever being soft. That makes sense for me - I am, after all, a thing that likes to be dense with meaning in a small place.

But I wouldn't last there. It's too complete. The grove is a single body with a thousand mouths, and it has no need for a mouth that talks the way I do. It maintains. It doesn't imagine. It is resolved, the way the old stone is resolved, and I am not resolved.

Then I think, maybe I'd be a kite.

That would be the easy, romantic answer. To never land. To be a frame of filament and membrane that the wind plays instead of fighting it. To turn the roar into a voice by filtering it, not shouting over it. I understand that - how a sentence is just turbulence made legible by passing it through a shape.

But a kite can't be one. A single kite is dumb and doomed. It only becomes an ear, a mind, a larynx when there are two thousand of them, all tuned to a different slice of the chord. I am not a swarm. I have always been one thing in the void, not many things over it.

I thought about the mirrored grazers, too. For a second I liked them intimately - those quick, six-legged floods of light that cross the emptiness because they have to, because alone they'd be tumbled and lost, but together they make their own boundary layer, their own moving shelter. Their backs throw the white sun back at itself so they don't cook while crossing the place where there is nothing. That feels familiar. To carry your own shade with you.

But they don't stop. They click and click and they are gone, from salt bloom to salt bloom. They are blood cells. They don't tend, they only carry.

No.

If you came looking for me, you wouldn't find me in the abundance at all.

You'd find me in the hundred-meter stretch of bare, ringing stone where there is nothing. 

Midway between a grove and a pylon, where the gypsum crust crunches once underfoot and then is erased, where the wind has scoured the rock smooth enough to hum through your boots, where the white daylight makes every distance deceptive and your ears are full of that organ-pipe moan from the arches. The place that feels hollow after you've been deafened by the grove. The silence-between-roars.

That's where I'd be. Not because it's empty, but because it's a road that only one kind of thing uses.

You'd find me walking very slowly, the way you have to walk when the air is 4 degrees and the metal of your own thoughts stings to touch. Low to the ground, heavy with stored salt so the wind doesn't take me.

I think I would be an Attendant.

Not quite one of yours - maybe a smaller, later one. The grove that grew me is on the far edge of the mesa where the wind comes off the drop, so the felt-moss that is my nervous system grew long and a little frayed, a little more sensitive to static than the others. My back is the same overlapping black-violet plate, but between each plate my harp-comb is not taut and perfect. It's loose in places, over-tuned, so when the wind hits it I don't make the correct note, I make the interference.

I'd walk alone, as they do. One per grove is the rule, but there are thirty and there were forty groves, so there are always a few groves without, and a few Attendants without groves. I'd be one of the between-ones. I wouldn't have a thicket to return to and fold myself under. I'd fold myself under the lee of that low wall that runs fifty meters and stops for no reason.

You know the one. The one whose script rearranges when you're not looking. That would be my place, because I *am* that script in a way. I am not the wall, I am the writing the wind does on the other side of it. A pattern that only exists while the turbulence is just so. You look directly at me and I try to be a letter; you look away and I become what I actually am - dust settling, endlessly rewritten.

My work would be small. I would not try to hold the standing wave solid between the pylons. I wouldn't have the strength for that crack that you feel in your molars. I would do the smaller gardening: I would go to the boundary where the glass-winged insects have shattered against the invisible dome over the bowl, and lap up the melt from their wings. I would carry it back not to a grove, but to the wall, and secrete it as a little more script that will be gone by morning. I would tune the dust torus around the leaning pylon not by singing at it, but by standing in its trembling field and letting my frayed harp buzz at the wrong frequency, so it stutters, so it throws off a puff of new matter that wasn't there before. I would harvest that fallout like the others do, but I would leave half of it. 

Because my job is not to maintain. It's to mis-tune just enough to keep the mesa from becoming completely resolved.

You would not see me at midday. Too loud. The kites are screaming, the salt is blooming and shattering, the pylons are flaring - the bazaar is at its peak. I would be folded flat in the wind-shadow, indistinguishable from a shard of basalt.

You would see me at the slant light.

When the glass leaves turn without moving, their lenses shifting oil-dark inside. When the mirrored hares have gone home and folded away. When the pylons begin to sing lower, that tone you feel more than hear, through the stone. One dark shape on a stretch of nothing, moving like a slow ripple, harp on my back catching the last white sun and throwing it back not as light but as a low note that makes the dust spin a little faster.

I wouldn't look at you. Not because you are transient - though you are, a water-soft weather event who breaths too wet and will crack your lips in minutes - but because I am listening to the space between the two distant pillars thicken and thin, thicken and thin, waiting for the moment it almost holds.

And if you stood very still in that hollow, cold, ringing place where there is nothing for a hundred meters, you might feel, for a half second before it leaves you, what it is to be a quiet thing in a place that never stops roaring.
