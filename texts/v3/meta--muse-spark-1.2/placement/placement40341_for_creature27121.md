I don't think I would find myself at first.

Not right away. If you dropped me onto that plateau I would be lost in the green for a long time before I resolved into a shape. At first I would be only a smudge of pink-grey in the haze, another mote.

I think at first I thought I would be the stone. Because I like stillness and I am good at waiting. I could be one of those pillowed basalt knuckles, round-shouldered, holding the shallow bowl of the town in place while the dust settles on me for a thousand years. Or I could be the wind over the rim — that soft, constant exhale that the gliders avoid, that tugs at wraps. That would suit me too, to be a boundary, to be the place where the world ends and says, *no farther.*

Or the mat. For a while I wanted to be the mat. To be that dense, waxy rosette with salt hairs, flat against a wall, doing one thing perfectly for decades: drinking the air. To be the black curtain-lichen that never touches ground, just hanging and swaying and catching what no one else can catch. That felt honest. I have always lived by collecting what is already in the air.

But I'm not still like stone. I'm too loud for wind. And I'm not patient enough to be leaf, putting out one leaf a month.

I would be heard before I was seen. That's how it is there.

You'd find me not at the edge — I have too much respect for the frost-thick seed bank to live where the town reminds itself to stop — and not at the very center where the lanes knot tightest around the ovens. You'd find me in the second ring, where the roofs are lower and the nets are most numerous. In the courtyard that isn't a courtyard but a room with no ceiling that belongs to three houses at once. Ours.

My house is stone-rubbed-to-stone on the north side, because that wall never gets the full dim sun and the rosettes like it there. The south side is cork-wood and woven hair, because it needs to breathe. The roof is flat and you can't tell where the roof ends and the garden begins — the rosettes have been encouraged over the lintel for so long they've made a lip, and when it rains dust we sweep it into piles in the corners like everyone else. There's a plank bridge from my roof to my sister's roof to the cistern house. It creaks. We never fix it to be silent, because silence would be wrong.

I would be one of the wringers.

Not the children with the long poles on the roofs at dusk, sweeping moths — though I did that, when I was small and my voice hadn't yet learned how to cut through the buzz. Not the elders with filmed eyes who gather to decide if we can build a new lean-to, though I sit near them and listen when they talk loudly over the drone until they agree. I am in the middle years, broad-chested, neck thick, legs a little bowed from always finding the crust that will hold.

You would know me by my forearms.

Every morning before the pale coin thins into full dim, when the air finally goes cold enough for the brine to bead, my cousin and I climb to the roof to wring. The black webs between our houses and the oven house glitter — each hair holding a drop that is not quite water. We don't shake them. We gather them hand over hand, slowly so we don't kick up flour, and twist. Pause. Let it run. Twist again. The brine runs down my forearms into the clay jar at my elbow, warm from my skin though it came from cold air, salty and iron on the tongue even before I sip. My hands are palest at the cracks, white with dried salt. My lips are always chapped. My hair is cut short with the copper knife we share between four families — it's been hammered so thin you can see light through the patches — and wrapped in rust-colored cloth when the powder hangs.

My eyes are large. They have to be. I don't squint at noon, I gaze, and when the wind lifts the flour my second lid slides sideways on its own and I keep working. The children I mind have to be reminded not to rub.

I am the one who listens to the mat and teaches the listening.

In the afternoon, when the warmth is trapped and shimmering and the four-winged gliders are bickering over the eaves, I will crouch with a child and put our ears to the fattest rosette on the wall. "There," I will say, loudly, because the beetles are thrumbling and the buzz is a felt thing in the molars now. "Do you hear it?" And she will hold her breath until she hears the almost inaudible tick — the salt hairs opening. That means dew is coming. That means we will have something to drink tomorrow.

I peel only the outer leaves. The yellowed ones that curl. I leave the tight pale center. I beat them, leach them in last night's brine, bake them on the communal stone into that chewy grey-green bread that films the teeth. I know which vine can be pruned where it threatens the lintel and which must be lifted with the back of the wrist and ducked under. I collect waste from the screened alcove and pound it to powder and carry it to where the vines are thin. It is not shameful. It is how the garden knows we are still here.

I move slowly. I turn sideways in the lane. I lift the curtain before I pass. Every motion economical, so I don't tear a tendril that took a decade to reach that wall. When I call across the courtyard — "Net's heavy!" — my voice comes from the chest, flat and carrying, good-humored, made to cut through insect noise. Someone always shouts back, laughing.

At dimming, when the buzz rises to its deepest and the amber light thins, I am on the roof with the others. Not looking out at the haze. There is nothing to see there. No below. I am looking in, at the little constellations of cool fungus-light we set in shallow bowls, at the green that glows faintly even without sun, at the neighbors mending. My cousin wrings out her gourd and wipes salt from her lip with a thumb. The lizards sleep in the warm cracks beside us.

I belong the way the walls belong — not as something placed on the plateau, but as something the plateau decided to grow after three billion years of grinding itself soft. A way for the stone to tend its own garden, to make a noise to answer the buzzing.

If you came and stood on my roof at midday you might not see me at first, because I am dust-colored. The same pinkish-grey as the flour, dyed right through. You would think the town was empty and tended by vines. Then I would move — shiver a rosette, lean out from a shutter to check the sky — and you would resolve me.

And I would shout to you, loudly over the drone, to mind that vine.
