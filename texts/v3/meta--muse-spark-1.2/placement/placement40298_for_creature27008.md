I don't think I would find myself right away.

At first, on the lip of the saucer, I would think I was still a visitor. Still a person with dry boots from the heath, standing where the air goes cool by a degree and thinking *I have come down to look at it*. The town would be there all at once below me - the green hills that are factories, the felted roof forty feet up, the amber light coming leaf-filtered through the gasket-windows - and I would be above it, separate.

But the dish doesn't let you stay separate. The cold settles on you. The damp finds your cuffs. You go down a meter, two meters, and the sound changes. The heath wind is cut off as if a door closed. Your footfalls stop meaning anything.

So I would go down. And for a while I would try on wrong shapes for myself.

I think - briefly - I would be one of the sparrows. That's the first, easiest fantasy: to be small and rare and allowed a hole left open for me. To dart in and out of the Weave and not have to keep anything. But I am too heavy for that. I have always been too heavy, too footed.

Then I think I would be the fog. Knee-high, draining between the buildings like a tide, glowing faintly at night around the work lights. That is closer. I like the idea of being a condition, not a thing. Of being water that never quite becomes rain. But fog doesn't stay. It pools and then it drains. It has no memory.

Closer still: I think I would be the drip. The *plip* on the iron catwalk that is almost metronomic. Born on the underside of a waxy leaf, beading, gathering, falling. Travelling the living trough to the cistern, being fed into the boiler, becoming steam that smells of green, condensing again on every cool pipe. A loop. That is the true work of this place - turning sunlight and cool air into more of itself, drip by quiet drip. I could be that clean circuit for a while and be content.

But that is still too clean. Too simple.

The longer I stand on the mat - that springy, two-feet-deep floor of moss and stonecrop that sighs back under my boot, that is warm underneath from the pipe runs - the more I realize I am not moving. Or, I am moving at the speed the Weave moves. A millimeter an hour, if you watch a marked tip.

I am not the fog passing over. I am what the fog passes over.

You would not find me in the center of the dish. Not where you clap and the town claps back with a rustle. That's a place for visitors to test. You would not find me on the catwalk in the canopy roof either, looking down at the perfect hexagonal tiling of the paths. Too high, too seeing.

You would find me where the mat is most trodden, which is also where it is most alive.

Between the mill and the cistern house. Where the compressed path is not quite pavement, not quite plant. Where the root-channel cradles the steam pipe and the Weave has grown a waxy collar around the junction box, and the condensations runs constantly whether it is fog or not. The place that is always beading, always ticking with cool contraction.

And I would not be young. Not the fourteen-year-old with the reddish underside in her hair who tends the beetle annex, though I love her. I would be one of the older ones. Past the age where you need to be told what to do.

I would be the keeper of that sector. The gutter keeper.

You'd have to look for a while to see me at all. I'd be kneeling, because most of the work here is kneeling. Waxed canvas the color of wet bark, wool gone grey-green at the seams, rubber boots beaded just like every leaf is beaded. Hair lank and dark with damp, cut short because the drip gets in otherwise. My hands - you would notice my hands first - barking a little at the backs and at the neck, that fine dry fissuring like young branches hardening off. Oiled at the end of the day the way you oil a bearing. Stained at the cuffs where green never washes out.

What would I be doing? The same thing with both hands.

Heavy spring-handled shears in my right hand. A runner has gotten ambitious, trying to strangle the gutter it was meant to shade. I cut it. The sap is clear and smells of cut grass and hot metal and I don't wipe it. I let it bead. That's the signal.

Voltage tester in my left hand. Touch it to the terminals the Weave has so carefully left exposed. It ticks. Good. Still alive. Still cool enough.

And then, with the strip of fiber that this same runner grew last week - regrown in seven days - I tie the runner back along its guide-wire, a few degrees to the left, so the broad, dinner-plate leaf angles to catch the overcast light and throw it through the window onto the switchgear. The plant lets me. The leaf stays where I put it.

You'd think I was gardening. You'd think I was doing electrics. There is no distinction and after a while you stop looking for it.

If you asked me - quietly, because sound is expensive here, eaten by moss - if I live here or work here, I would not understand. I keep, I would say. That's the verb we have.

I keep the pressure. I keep the gutter from strangling. I keep the agreement between brick and bark from fraying, at this one seam, where stone and bark have agreed to be rounded and bearded with lichen. I keep the fog moving toward the cistern.

I am not lonely. The mat thrums faintly with warm water under my knees. The drip is keeping time on the catwalk above me. Somewhere behind the mill, the grinder is taking the hard fig-like fruits that only fall above the compost annex and turning them into pulp for the press. Somewhere above me a new shoot is pressurizing, making that faint hydraulic creak that is not quite sound, extending a millimeter while I watch.

If you stood in the center and clapped, I would feel the rustle in my feet before I heard it. And I would not look up. Of course it answered, my posture would say. It lives here too. So do I, in the same way - slowly, damply, in balance, half-plant and half-maintenance, preserved by the cool air, healing slowly and completely when cut, beading with something clearer than sweat when I work too hard.

You would find me there, kneeling on the living floor, as still for long minutes as the plant is still. Letting water gather on my shoulders and drip off. Not waiting, not watching. Just keeping.
