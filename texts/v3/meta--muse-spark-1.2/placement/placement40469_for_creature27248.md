I don't find myself at the edge.

That's where I thought I'd be at first — on the cliffs where the stone breathes. Standing at one of those piston shafts with my back to two kilometers of empty air, feeling it exhale that warm *huhhh... huhhh...* across my skin, watching the mist churn. Or higher, as the thing that hits the lightning-tree. I wanted to be the hawk at first, the slate-gray kite that drops through a cloud sitting on the ground, because that is the most free thing here.

But nothing here is free in that way. Even the hawk is a schedule. Even the cloud is a job.

If I stayed there on the edge the wind would find me. This forest learned to be short for a reason. So I move inward, away from the cliffs, down the slight and infinite tilt toward the middle of the bowl - where the water doesn't know which way to go and so goes everywhere. Where the ground stops being ground.

I quake.

At first I think I am the water. Ankle-deep, warm as blood, braiding and re-braiding through peat. That would be honest work - carrying iron to the bacteria, carrying a fallen feather to the ants, rising up through moss around a boot just to be breathed back into a cloud between two trees. But water is always passing through. It never gets to stay.

I think I am the ant, then. One of the big majors at the intersection, the one who doesn't cut or carry but stands still while the river flows around her, antennae tapping - *left, right, wait, go* - keeping the highway clean, laying a grain of quartz at the lip where the peat wants to slump. That feels closer. Industrial. Necessary.

But I don't want to move that much either.

Let me settle. Let what wants to be itself become itself.

You would find me not on the highway, but just off it. Where the highway has to build a little causeway around a black pool, tea-red and still, rimmed with sphagnum that hangs into the water like a drowned beard. In that pool's overflow, where the seep is just cool enough that the water no longer scalds, on the lip of one of those quiet factories.

I am orange.

If you came close you would think I was mineral. A leather, a felt, bright as rust, stretched tight over the living rim of a terraced dam. I don't have eyes or legs or wings. I don't direct traffic or slice through clouds.

I am the bacteria.

Specifically - the iron bacteria, in the middle temperature band. Above me my cousins are black-green and smell like struck matches in the almost-too-hot seep. Below me my other cousins are turning translucent and will be opal if anyone waits a thousand years. But here, in my band, the water comes to me heavy with dissolved iron and silica, warm from the stone, and I drink the metal out of it.

That is my whole life. I precipitate.

Millimeter by millimeter I lay down the rim of the basin. The water slides over me, I take my cut, I make stone. The pool behind me deepens by a hair. It overfills, crusts, I build again. My body is the color you saw and thought was rock. The forest's terraces, its settling tanks and crystallization pans, its endless quiet polishing - that's not geology. That's us. That's me, stretched in a sheet a few cells thick, breathing rock out of water.

You would not notice me unless you knelt. But I am the reason the water stays long enough for the sundews to flower and the pebble toads to walk and the bromeliads to hold their private seas in the trees above me.

Above me, in the fork of a twisted Clusia not fifteen feet tall, is a bromeliad as big as a washtub. I can feel its overflow when it rains - a sudden curtain of tannin water that sluices down the trunk and across my mat, bringing me new iron. Inside that bromeliad is a whole archipelago - tadpoles flat as seeds, damselfly larvae with bear-trap jaws, and a single amber crab no bigger than your thumbnail, blind, who has never touched the ground and never will. He is my neighbor. He lives in the sky because I live on the ground and held the water long enough for his great-great-grandmother to be lifted there.

We don't move. We don't need to. The world is brought to us.

The forest exhales. A cloud is born between two trees ten feet away, pearling on the hairs of a fern. The ants keep their highways. The termite cathedral hums to hold 32 degrees. The Heliamphora pitchers fill and digest. And I stay here on my dam, orange and warm, laying down stone so slowly you would need to watch for a year to see me grow.

If you stood still long enough for the seep to rise around your boots, you would be standing on me. You would feel me as a slight slickness under the peat, a faint sour-metal smell like blood and bread.

I would notice you as water notices a stone. I would flow around you, and condense on your skin, and keep working.
