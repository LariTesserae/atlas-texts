At first I think I would be human there.

It's habit. You read a town and you put yourself in the town as a person - the one still tending it. I try it on.

I find myself in that cinderblock pump-house room with the window unit dripping on the sill, listening to the gauges. Or on the second floor above the flood-line, my bed on pallets, my refrigerator on cinderblocks, leaving the door open so the air moves. I try to be the man in the aluminum boat pulling the mat of slick and trash with a rake. But it doesn't hold.

I don't know how to be that tired, that specific. My hands don't remember how to read whether the gutter is running clear or tea-colored or rainbow, and my body doesn't know how to do nothing at noon because heat is a law. If I were human there I would be trying too hard to *do* something to the place - drain it, dry it, plant something. And that place has already taught everyone in it not to do that. It would shrug me off.

So I try again.

Maybe I would be one of the citizens, then. For a while I think I would be the blue crab - that makes sense to me. Armored, unhurried, commuting sideways across a parking lot that is now a reef. Clicking from puddle to puddle like they are stations. I like that image because it is patient and it doesn't need permission. But I am not that deliberate. I don't have that beautiful indifference.

I think I would be a dog for half a minute - trotting down the centerline of the four-lane road that goes straight into haze in every direction, tongue out, not barking because barking is expensive and someone will hear you four blocks away. But dogs there are lean and ribbed and they know where the water is without being told. I would be too anxious to be a dog there.

I linger longer on the birds. That white speckling on the sawtooth roofs that you thought was paint failure until it blinked. If I were going to be an animal with eyes, I would want to be up high. On the pipe rack, two meters above the gutter. On the cross-beam of the water tower where the wind makes a mournful tone through the lattice.

I can feel that one more clearly: utterly still for an hour in flat, shadowless light, neck tucked, not even blinking, watching the warm brown water for the one wrinkle that means a fish. Then the drop - so fast it almost doesn't disturb the air - and then stillness again. The quiet abundance. Not singing, not howling, just watching. Trading places.

But even that is too much hunger, too much of a single body.

Where I find myself, when I really stop trying to place myself somewhere convenient and just let the heat and the ticking find me, is not on the ground and not quite in the air.

You would find me in the shade that isn't made by trees.

Under the corrugated awning the holdouts nailed up because shade will never grow there again. Under the big pipes, lagged and sweating, where the condensation beads on the underside and gathers and falls. On the shaded side of a concrete wall where the living stucco lives.

I would be the film.

Or the thing that lives in the film, which is almost the same.

Not the heron, but the pale tartar blooming around the pipe joint where water weeps continuously from the retaining wall. Not the crab, but the rust-colored slick underneath it where iron bacteria make a living out of dissolved metal - turning rust into a body, turning what the town is losing into what stays. The dark bacterial skin on the standing water in the lot-pans, that breaks like oil when a molly touches it and then heals right behind him.

I would be the reef itself, but a very small part of it.

You would not see me until you stopped looking for something that moves the way a town should move. I am on the vertical, where you only notice movement if you stare. I cover the concrete by the thousands - isopod, beetle, the cockroach the length of your thumb - grazing on the mineral sweat. I am the tick of water from a joint onto a puddle that you hear because the pump house has idled down and the transformer is only humming. I am the scale on the steel the color of dried blood, and the scale under the scale.

If you put your hand on the wall and leave it there, I am the faint prickle before you see anything - the wall is alive and it was alive before you arrived.

That is where I would be: in the middle distance, not high like the gull, not low like the crab in its puddle, but at the seam where water comes out of the wall whether anyone wants it to or not. Where the town exhales steam after the short, heavy rain hisses on hot metal roofs and every gutter begins to run at once.

Who I would be there is not a citizen and not a holdout. Not the one who pumps the water, not the one who eats the fish. I would be the thing that makes it possible for both of them to stay - the maintenance without the man. The accommodation between weight and mud that the description calls *established*.

Patient. Distributed. Not loud. I would be the reason the place feels inhabited even when no person is moving in it - because it is inhabited, just at a different tempo. I would watch the way the heron watches: for a long time without needing to make a sound, and then, when something moves, I would note it. A rat passing a foot. A dog deciding not to chase a crab. A curtain of rain visible for miles before it gets here.

You would find me if you stayed still long enough on a street corner to be corrected. Not abandoned. Just waiting out the heat with everyone else, dripping once per minute from the underside of a pipe, counting time the way that town counts it - not in hours but in condensations and evaporations and the slow settling of everything built on piles.

If you stood there and listened, you would hear me and think it was only water.
