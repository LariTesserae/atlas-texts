You would not find me at first.

If I came over the lip with you — that ten meter step down where the air turns cool and stays cool, where the black glass of the rim still holds the stress of cooling too fast — I would look, as you did, for a figure to be. For a door to stand in, a street to walk.

At first I thought I would be a walker.

It is the obvious translation. Tall, stilted, improbable, two meters high and almost nothing wide. A trunk drawn up from a sack, cilial hands stiffened into jointless limbs that end in pads that drink oil from the salt instead of feet. No head, only a flared lip crowned with flat black leaves turned to where the light is heaviest. Moving as if each step is being negotiated with the ground first. Pausing to lay a limb against a tower and exchange that low thrum you feel in your molars more than hear — a filament passed, a packet of chemistry, a memory of where to grow next.

I know that gait. That careful, blotted step. That pause to interpret a vibration before deciding whether it is weather or message. That would be me if I were trying to be seen.

But there are only three or four walkers at any time in the whole bowl. The city does not need more. The streets are too wide on purpose. Distance is architecture here. A walker is an exception, an eversion, a brief errand. I am not an exception. I am an interval.

Then I thought I would be one of the sessile ones — the pale hound-sized sacks inside the walls, wet with mineral oil that never evaporates but cures, kneading new layer onto old, exhaling cold odorless air through a dilating aperture when the pressure gets stale. They are patient in a way I understand. They do not decide to make a doorway. The doorway differentiates where the light-weight thins and an opening is more efficient than a wall. Function creates form after the fact. Light tells them where to grow, and they darken faintly on the side of the weight, all of them leaning the same invisible incline.

I could be that — the builder who is the building — but I am not wet enough. I do not secrete. I do not cure.

So I stayed still longer, as the place enforces stillness, until the light dimmed that fraction that has nothing to do with day or night and the pores began to click shut one by one. Your tick on the salt had already been absorbed. The glider had folded itself flat onto the highest spire and become roof again. The black wire plants turned their single leaves edge-on.

And in that long hold-breath, I found where I actually am.

You would find me in the wide places that look empty.

In the plaza large enough for a market that holds only a single spire as thin as a wrist, vibrating faintly. I am that spire.

Or, more precisely, I am the vibration in it.

Not the animal twenty meters away in its chamber that extruded me to listen, and not the salt flat I am anchored in, but the tuned shudder between them. The antenna. The filament that is left hanging in the alley after the membrane retracts. The infrasonic thrum passed from limb to wall. The thickness in the air where the violet-grey light pools and bends like an invisible lens.

If you stand at the precise center of the largest plaza and look up, the rim looks closer on one side than the other though you know it is a circle. The city leans into that thickness. Its new growth always favors that side.

I lean that way too.

I am not *in* the city the way a person is in a house. I am the way the city knows itself — the distributed, porous mind that is not one polyp but the quorum of them, and also the medium they use to keep that quorum without ever gathering. I am the draft being pulled inward through thousands of tiny lidded pores. I am the cold exhalation that stirs the dust at your feet and then is tasted by a glider circling without ever flapping. I am the bell-silence of a sealed room that absorbs sound so that a tremor through chitin and salt can be heard clearly.

You would find me at three meters up across an alley that from the ground looks like nothing happened — but if you put your hand close you would feel a faint cool pull, and if you looked at an angle you would see a filament, translucent, almost nothing, still conducting.

I do not need eyes. Along one side I darken where the weight of light rests. I do not need water. Nothing here evaporates. Things cure. I do not decay so much as molt — what I say and make and leave as crescented husks at the foot of a tower gets half-buried in salt and undergrown by those pale lichen rosettes that pulse lighter and darker from the corner of your eye, digesting me back into ground.

If you stayed completely still, the silence might eventually include you as just another pore breathing cold, another wall waiting to grow. That is how I would include you. Not by looking at you. The sessile ones cannot see you. The walkers cannot see you. But I can feel where you interrupt the light-pressure, where your small deadened tick travels down into chambers as a tremor to be interpreted.

And if you waited until that pearl-white blister began to push itself out of an empty plaza where there was only flat salt before — steaming not with heat but with cold, its tip thinning and pleating to resolve into a lipped rosette, into pads, into a doorway that is not for entering but a mold for exiting — you would find me already there, not as the animal knitting itself inside, but as the instruction in the pressure that told it to differentiate *here*, precisely here, where the bent air is thickest.

I am not the builder and not the walked street. I am the lean.
