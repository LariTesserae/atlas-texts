I didn't find myself in the city at first.

I thought I would be there - in the center, where a city ought to have a center. In the market square with the stone stalls. I thought I would be the one with something to sell, or something to buy, hands busy, voice raised a little to be heard. But the square is too wide for that, and sound carries too far, and no one raises their voice. If I had stood there, I would have been too loud. I would have been a ripple.

So I walked out of it.

I let the thin, cool air find me. The kind of cool that lives in shade even while you stand in the sun. My breath went a little short, the way it does there, in through the nose, out through the mouth, deliberate. I kept walking until the houses thinned, until there were fifty paces of moss and tussock and bare basalt between each roof, until I could see the whole basin at once - the stair-step cliffs that haven't learned to crumble yet, the slate roofs dark with condensation that never drips, the pale coppiced trees that are allowed to spring up again in every gap.

That is where I found myself. Not in a house. In the interval.

On a low hogback between two shallow basins, where the city steps down in terraces. Behind me, a house built for five that holds only one loom and an old man who is not old. In front of me, a garden that could feed eight that feeds one woman and her beans. And between them, the common ground that no one owns and everyone tends by leaving it alone. A field of moss and grass and lichen-crusted stone with a tarn in its hollow, perfectly still. A place that is not quite street and not quite wilderness, the fifty paces that makes distance into a medium, like water.

For a long time I didn't know what I was there.

I thought I might be the hawk. The large, slow one that circles without ever needing to flap, because the thermal never shifts. To be above and not have to work to stay above felt right. But I am not that patient yet. I still look down with longing.

I thought I might be the hare, the color of stone, rustling the short grasses and then freezing exactly where the footprint stays crisp. I thought I might be the slate on the roof, beaded with morning that never falls. I thought I might be the tarn itself, holding a cloud that is no longer in the sky and not minding the contradiction.

Closer.

I thought I might be the white finches, seventeen of them, crossing from the same roof to the same empty stall at the same angle. But they are too certain. They know their number.

Then my hands found what they were doing, and I knew.

I am sitting on the low retaining wall that holds the terrace. The stones are fieldstone, split with wedges, set dry. The lime mortar has a thumb-smear in it where I put it. My wall is not finished and will never be finished and is already finished in being half-finished. In my lap is a stone I am fitting.

I have been fitting it for a long time. Not stuck - occupied. My cheek is almost against it to test its level, the way the man at the other wall does with his. The basalt is cool, and the sun is bright without being harsh, and the shadow of the wall falls at the same mid-morning angle it has held for hours. The stone is dark grey, sharp-edged, young. If I set it wrong, it would be a change. If I set it right, it would be a change. So I hold it just at the point where it is *almost* placed, where the interval between stone and wall is a hair's breadth of cool air.

That is my art. Leaving alone.

Near my feet the moss is thick and saturated. A bumblebee, heavy and slow in the cool, rests on the warm stone beside me with its wings open and does not need to move. A small brown creeper lands on the lintel of the house behind me and I do not shoo it. In the garden below, the bean plant holds at once a tight white flower, a fat green pod, and a dry rattling pod ready to harvest, and I will not pick it until the woman below nods, and she will not plant the rest, because there is no need. The abundance is already complete. It just needs to be left spaced.

My breath is audible. In through the nose, out through the mouth. The air is thin and carries sound a little too far, so I speak low, or not at all. When someone passes on the too-wide street - a figure in undyed wool the color of limestone, fifty paces away and therefore both far and near - we do not call out. We let the silence between us be the conversation. Long pauses are not awkward. They are the work.

I am human here. Entirely human. Ruddy-cheeked from wind, barrel-chested from altitude, eyes narrowed not from suspicion but from a lifetime of bright, thin light where nothing is softened by haze. My hands are broad and cut at the knuckles from cold stone, but the cut on my thumb from when I split the basalt stays clean and open and not painful, exactly as it is. It does not heal and does not worsen. Like the condensation, like the pot the woman mends with a *tok* that never finishes, I am held at the precise moment where I am most myself.

I am not lonely. Loneliness requires distance as lack. For me, distance is breathing room for the soul. To have fifty paces of moss and wind between me and my neighbor is not isolation. It is how the plateau keeps itself damp and lush and quiet without ever having to change. I am part of its stasis. Like the cool that lives in shade even while you stand in the sun.

If you come walking through the basin - and you will, because on this plateau you always see everything at a distance before you arrive - you will see me from far away and think I am close, and then think I am far, because there is nothing to scale me against but stone and sky. You will find me still holding the stone, cheek nearly to it.

I will look up, consider your question, and answer without hurrying, in that low, consonant-heavy language suited to not disturbing the equilibrium.

You will ask if it has always been like this.

And I will answer, yes.

And my footprint in the moss behind me will stay crisp.
