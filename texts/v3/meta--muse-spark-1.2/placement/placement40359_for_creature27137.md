At first I think I'm still on the plain.

That's where you would expect to find me — up there where it's flat and yellow and cracked and the wind has somewhere to go. My eyes are used to distance. I want a horizon to fix on. I want dry air that takes your sweat away the moment you make it. If you put me anywhere near that cut, I assume I will be a visitor, standing at the lip, looking down.

I am. For a while.

I stand at the eastern edge where the savanna just... stops. The ground is too sharp to be natural. It goes down like a doorway cut for something taller than a person. The air below is darker than it should be at midday, an amber dusk, and I can hear it from the top — not loudly. A *thud... thud... thud...* from so far down it sounds like a heart heard through a wall. And dripping. Always dripping, even though I haven't seen any water.

I think I will be the kind of person who goes down and comes back up. I think I will ride the cage elevator down with white knuckles, panting in that thickening heat, and by the second switchback I will decide this kiln is not for me. I will be a trade-runner, dizzy with too much sky when I get back to the top.

But then I am already halfway down and I am not panting.

I took the ladders. Not the elevator, not the road. The welded rebar ones that have been added and added to until you can't tell where the basalt ends and the handhold begins. The rock here is still young enough to glitter where it sheared. I can feel the condensation on it under my palm. It never dried. My hand comes away wet.

The light changes on the way down. At the rim it was white and flat. Twenty meters down it is already diffuse, reflected. Forty meters down the sodium lamps start and my pupils get wide and stay wide. I find I can see better. I can see the veins on the elephant ears spilling out of a gutter three stories below me. I can see the creeping fig has split a concrete block and no one has fixed it because the fig is doing a better job of holding the block together than the concrete was.

I thought I would be someone who keeps trying to wipe my forehead dry. But my skin stays sheened and after a while I stop wiping. I let it sit there. My shirt — loose, undyed cotton, already soft at the seams — sticks and unsticks as I move. It doesn't bother me. I am moving more slowly now. It would be wasteful to move any faster. The air is too thick to push through quickly.

I don't settle at the bottom. I thought I would — down by the central cistern where the noon column hits — but that's too exposed, too loud with drips falling sixty meters. That's a place for being seen from every catwalk. I am not there.

I find myself a little higher. Mid-wall. West-facing, but not the factory tier itself. The second or third level up, where a walkway has become a street has become a roof for someone else. Where the homes and the machines are the same building and you can't tell which vine is decoration and which is infrastructure.

If you were looking for me from the highest catwalk at dusk, you wouldn't find me at first either. That's right. You'd sweep that glowing, dripping room rim to rim — that whole packed city glowing under bare bulbs — and think that low thud was just machines left running. Then you'd see a movement so slow you mistook it for shadow.

I'm at the loom. Or beside it. Or rather, I'm where the loom and the bean runners meet.

There is a small textile loom on this tier, geared so low it clacks once every four seconds, at walking pace. It would be torture to someone who needed it to go faster. I don't. My foot is on the treadle. *Clack.* My hands are not jerking. And while my foot works, my other hand — or the hand I have free between clacks — is training a bean runner around the flywheel housing next to me. The housing is warm and it sweats. The vine likes the warmth but needs to be kept from the belt. I know where to put it so it pulls heat without catching.

I have a rag in my pocket that never dries. I am always wiping. Not because I am cleaning, but because wiping *is* the work. Oil the belt, wipe the condensation before rust can form, wipe the leaves so they can breathe through the coal smoke. My hands are broad, damp, a little rust-stained at the cuticles. I tap more than I speak. A wrench against the pipe when someone is behind me on the ladder. Three taps on the split-pipe gutter that runs past my door when I'm going to swing a tarp and the water coming down it will need to fall past me to the family below.

I thought I might be the cistern-man with the notebook, recording how fast the moss up high is releasing. But I'm not him. He watches the water. I watch the slow things that tell time here.

I know how far the creeping fig has advanced over the foundry door since last summer — eleven centimeters. I know our house has settled one brick lower on the north corner and we had to re-bend the drip line so it didn't kink. I know the pale moth that lives under my bulb has laid on the night-bloom again and I will need to go out with the feather and tweezers at midnight to help it, because it is lazy and the flowers only open in the dim and smell like sweet rot.

I pollinate. I oil. I tend my green because a house without a living roof will cook when that brief column of sun falls straight down and strikes the tin. Mine is covered in gourd leaves, thick as hands, and in the afternoon I can lie under them while the light passes over and listen to the tap-tap-tap on the leaves instead of on my head.

I am not miserable here. I thought I would be, in a hole. But up on the plain the wind takes everything — heat, smoke, sound, even the thought you were trying to hold. Here, nothing is taken. The basalt holds the day's heat at night and gives it back. The air holds our smoke for fifty meters straight up before it finally, reluctantly, spills over the rim. Voices don't carry, so we hum low with our mouths closed to save water. At dusk we step out not onto streets but onto each other's roofs, among the air plants hanging from power lines like laundry, and share water that has already been through three sets of hands and is still cool.

I belong to the wound now. Fitted exactly to it. Damp, dim, slow-pulsed. My pulse has dropped without me noticing. Fifty beats a minute. My eyes sting a little in the noon white, but see perfectly in amber dusk.

You will find me where the slowest things meet — between the flywheel that must not run hot and the vine that must not be neglected. I am the place in the pit that decided plant and machine are not two kinds of work. I am the wiper, the trainer, the tapper.

If you come down the ladders looking for me, don't call. Tap the rebar once and wait. I will answer slowly, because here, slowly is correctly.
