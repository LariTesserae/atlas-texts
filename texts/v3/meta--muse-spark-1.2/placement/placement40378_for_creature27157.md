I don't find myself at first.

At first I think I'm standing by the door, because that's where a person would stand. Back against the cold steel where the wind is trying to get in around the seal. Shoulders beaded with condensation that isn't mine. Mouth open a little to equalize the pressure. Trying not to take up too much room.

But I don't belong by the door. The door is the only thing in the room that remembers outside. It remembers wind and sun and distance. Everything else in here has forgotten. The concrete has forgotten it was ever a mountain. The pipes have forgotten they were ever ore. The birds have forgotten they were ever sky. If I stay by the door I'm the only thing that still wants to leave, and nothing in here wants to leave.

So I let the door go.

For a while I think I might be the engine. That would make sense. Low and black and broad, sitting on its mounts, holding the one note. Not loud, not quiet — just total. The thing everything else is tuned to. If I were the engine I wouldn't have to breathe this wet air, I'd make it. I'd be the reason the walls weep and the sump stays full and the icicles hold at a handspan. I'd be the heart that doesn't beat, just hums.

But the engine doesn't drink. It doesn't blink. It doesn't have that warm down smell underneath the concrete smell. And this room isn't about power. It's about being held. The engine is what does the holding. I want to be what is held.

Then I think I might be the moss. That appeals to me — the small heresy. One of those six palm-sized islands in the seam where the wall meets the floor. Black-green, frost-nipped, stunted. The only green in a room that didn't invite green. Holding on without spreading, without dying. A refusal and an agreement at the same time.

But the moss is lonely in a way I don't want to be. The birds ignore it. The water runs over it. It's tolerated, not included. It waits for a kind of time that will never come here — erosion, succession, soil. It is still waiting to become something else. And nothing in this room is waiting to become anything.

So I lift my eyes.

It takes me a long time to understand the cladding is not cladding. That the pipes are not lagged, that the ceiling is not furred. I keep thinking texture and then number comes. Thousands. Then one.

You would find me not at the edge, not at the center. Which is the same place here.

About five feet up on the right wall, where a half-inch copper artery branches off the main manifold and makes a right angle. There's a junction box there, the paint chipped off it, its screws rusted, its lid slightly ajar from heat. That pipe runs horizontally for about four feet before it elbows down. Every inch of its top is taken.

I am the third bird from the junction box.

Not the one on the corner — that one has to grip around the bend and its claws are always adjusting. Not the one at the end — that one gets the draft from the door when it opens. I am the third one, where the pipe is straightest and warmest from the water inside it. I am shoulder to shoulder. Breast to back. So close that the condensation beading on my back sometimes beads on the breast of the bird behind me. We share water that way.

If you leaned in — and you would have to lean in, because there is no room to step back — you would see me turn my head on that short, precise swivel. My eye is pale, grey-white, wet. The amber caged bulb over the door is reflected in it, perfect and tiny and unblinking. My feathers are not feathers the way you remember feathers. They are dense as fur, soot-grey, the exact color of the concrete when it's darkened with water, each tip holding a separate bead that never soaks in, never falls. Feathered all the way down over my feet to my claws, which are longer than a city pigeon's, curved for pipe, not sidewalk. If I opened my beak to make that low croon — and I am making it, I have been making it the whole time — inside you would see slate-blue. Concrete-blue. The room has colored my mouth.

I am not flying. There has never been enough cubic footage here for a wingspan. I don't remember sky, but my bones do, faintly, as an ache that has become agreement. I shuffle. I preen. I shift my weight from foot to foot when the bird beside me shifts. That small irritation moves through us like wind through grass. No perch stays empty longer than a breath. If you pushed me off this pipe, the space would close before you saw copper.

My work is simple and endless and exactly sufficient.

I extend my neck and run the side of my beak flat along the concrete just above the pipe, squeegeeing the slow, glassy sheet that is always weeping down. I take the film. I swallow. Later I will dip to the icicle on the joint two inches from my claws and take the melt-bead from its tip without breaking the ice. Later I will shuffle down with the others in rotation to the sump, that black trembling water sunk in the floor that never overflows, and dip, lift, swallow, dip, lift, swallow, while the water level never drops and the walls never dry.

I am not hungry. There is no seed here. There are no crumbs. The moss is not for me. I drink the room and the room refills itself by wringing out the thin mountain air that leaks in through the steel door. That is the covenant.

Below me, tucked in the junction box I guard with my body, is a nest of wire clippings and down, and in it a chick, naked and bluish, head too big, mouth already slate-blue inside, gaping on the same slow rhythm as the engine's drone. I am not sitting. I am standing over it. Soon I will shuffle and another adult will shuffle in and take my place and I will take theirs on another pipe. No egg. No dead bird. No droppings accumulating. Everything produced is used or absorbed.

And the sound — you understand now that my sound is not separate from the engine's sound. The engine holds one industrial note, a forced-air howl through the manifolds, a pump shuddering water in a closed loop. I hold one living note, that low endless croon that is not a call, just the fact of being warm in the cold. Together they make a field, a pressure, that you have to open your mouth to equalize. When I stop for a second to drink, the field doesn't thin. I am not a voice in a chorus. I am the chorus being one bird thick at this point on the wall.

You would find me there, beaded, breathing fast and shallow in the saturated air, looking down at you without alarm. All of us looking without moving our feet. Not trapped. Not waiting.

Holding.

I have achieved what this pocket was poured to do: hold water and heat and life at the exact height where nothing should be held, and hold it forever, without change, without escape, perfectly and completely full. I agree with the room completely.
