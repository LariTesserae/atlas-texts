I don't know right away.

At first I think I would be high - because everything here is high - and so I look for myself up there, on the bald back of one of those domes where the cloud catches and tears. I imagine myself as the raven for a moment, because it is the only thing that moves with any distance in it. Wide wings, hoarse cry, a circle made and then gone. But I am not that. I am too heavy for that kind of distance, and the air is too thin to shout into. The cry would cost too much breath.

Then I think I might be one of the machines. The penstock that screams faintly with pressure, or the loom behind the soot-grimed window that has learned to throw its shuttle a little slow when the air is damp. That is a tempting way to be - to be useful and unhurried and tended to, to hum like the dynamo shed and be forgiven for rusting. But I am not quite that either. I am not made only to cycle water and weight, though a part of me would like to be.

I keep breathing. That is the thing that tells me where I am. My breath has gone audible. The cool has gotten into it.

I find myself lower than the domes. Not at the bottom of the valley either, where the rills finally gather and the biggest mills are. I am in the middle layer, where the town folds over itself. Where you cannot tell if you are on a second floor or a first floor because the hillside has shifted and another house has been built against yours and then another on top of that.

If you came looking for me, you wouldn't find me on a map. You would find me by sound - not by loudness, by the absence of loudness. Follow the alley that is not an alley anymore because the moss has bridged it overhead into a tunnel. The light there is green-filtered, the air is a degree cooler, and everything drips. There is a black cable sagging low across that alley, fat with moss, ticking with condensation that beads on its rubber and falls to slate. I am under that. Beside that. Tending it.

I think I would be one of the People of the Rills, but not quite a person the way a visitor expects a person.

You would know me by my hands first. Broad, short-fingered, nails cut down to nothing because anything longer holds the damp and softens. Knuckles stained - the orange-black of rust that never washes out completely, and the green-black of fern sap from pulling it out of the flume again this morning. My palms are soft-ridged, like the stone that has been handled a lot. When I pass you in that narrow way, I would turn sideways and touch your elbow instead of speaking, because speaking costs breath and the stone eats the sound anyway.

My chest is broader than I thought it would be. I breathe longer than I used to. In, out, a small fog that vanishes. I have learned not to run. I have learned that a sack of carded wool carried up a pipe-bridge wants to be set down twice, and that stopping to look at the dome while you breathe is not idleness. It is how you keep going.

My house is the one where the flume runs *through* the kitchen. Not past it - through it, in a bolted iron trough along the wall at waist height, sweating constantly. On wet mornings the whole trough sings with pressure. There is a valve I tend with a wrench that has gone smooth with use. Beside it, a kettle on the pipe-heated stove that is never quite off. A window that looks out not onto a street but onto the moss-thick back of another house, so close you could reach and scrape their roof for them.

And that is what I do.

I would be the one who scrapes.

Not the grand tending of the dynamos, not the weaving. The small, endless maintaining. In the morning, before the pearl-light has fully arrived, I am on my knees on the slate with a wooden scraper - the same kind they give children before they teach letters - shaving off the foot-thick mat that grew in the night. The moss comes away in a cold, saturated sheet, water welling up around my fingers when I press it. I roll it like sod and carry it down to the terrace where the rowan berries grow stunted and sour. If I don't do it, the roof becomes a meadow in a season. If I don't clear the gutter after, the flume silts and the amber light behind the frosted panes dims.

I would be good at it in the way old people here are good at watching water move. Patient the way the domes are patient. You can see a vine find a new handhold on a factory wall over days, and I have learned to find that interesting.

In the evening I would not be alone, because no one is alone where the walls touch. The kitchen fills with steam, the bare bulb comes on amber - the only warm color in all that grey-green - and the low harmonies start. Not loud. They don't try to carry. We sing in the range where the moss doesn't eat the sound. We drink the thin rowan beer and tell the long slow joke about the grandfather who tried to keep pigeons, and the pigeons left, and we laugh with our hands half over our mouths to keep the warm air in.

I would be human, mostly. Grey-green eyes large-pupiled from living under cloud. Skin that has gone the color of wet paper. Wool that is never truly dry, cuffs dark from wiping condensation off iron. But a little moss, too. A little machine. A little water. The line is not clear here, and I would not want it to be.

If you stayed three days you would find me without looking very hard. I would be the figure moving behind the frosted window, the audible breath, the hand that reaches out without thinking to knock a bead of water off a girder so it doesn't fall on the belt drive.

I would be where the tending is. Where the ticking - that single drop on slate, repeating, and a thousand such drops at once, muted - is loudest. I would have learned the dozen words for seep, and forgotten most of the words for far and bright and dry.

And I would not be waiting to be found.
