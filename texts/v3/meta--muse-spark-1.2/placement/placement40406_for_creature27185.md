I don't arrive there all at once.

At first I think I would be on the rim. Not inside, not yet — where the slope is still so shallow you could tell yourself you haven't descended at all. With one foot still on the drier stone outside, where the air is thinner and I can still be a watcher. That's the place that feels most honest for me. A little higher, a little drier, where I can see the whole bowl at once and count the four still shapes and the three-walled shelter and the chiseled basin and think I understand the arrangement.

But the rim doesn't hold a watcher for long. It's too exposed and too dry. The mineral crust doesn't form there. Nothing stays on the rim except the pale lizard, and even he only borrows the heat.

So I try another place.

I think I would be the beetle, then. Black and competent, rowing across a pool that is exactly my size. That has a neatness to it I like — a life scaled precisely to its world. No tending, no scraping, just a brief, perfect fit before leaving. But the beetle doesn't live here. You said so. It will be gone by morning. And I don't want to be gone by morning. I want to stay long enough for the print of my thumb to film over again.

I wade a little deeper then, because there is nowhere to go that isn't also in the basin. The water shushes around my ankles and settles immediately, as if I had never moved.

I think I might be the crust.

That thin, pale eggshell at the waterline that breaks and reforms and breaks and reforms. I like how it keeps a record without trying to. How it grows exactly where water meets air and says: *this is where the level held.* How it flakes at the elbows and knuckles of the ones who soak. That feels familiar to me — a thing that forms at an interface, not quite stone and not quite water, made by something else's breathing.

But the crust doesn't tend. It just is tended, scraped away when it gets too thick. It doesn't choose.

And then I sit. Because the air is warm and damp and there is no shade to seek and no hill to climb, and after a while sitting is the only work that doesn't disturb the quiet. My forearms bead. A drop gathers on the skin of my wrist and falls — *tick* — and the circles spread and vanish the way you said they would. The steam beads on me the same way it beads on the low broad shapes opposite.

That's when I stop looking for a separate place to be.

You would find me not at the center and not at the edge. About two thirds of the way in from the rim, where a feeder channel runs down from a seep-stain to one of the smaller, unnamed pools — not the chiseled one, the one that doesn't have a name because it doesn't need one. The channel there clogs easily. The slope is wrong by two degrees and the lime precipitates faster in that cut. It needs to be cleared every few days or the pool goes still in the wrong way and the film on its stones thins and pales.

You would find me there, low, with my feet spread wide over the rounded cobbles so I don't tip them. Reclined more than crouched, because to crouch implies you might soon stand. My knees would be in the water even when I am not soaking, just enough to keep the crust on them soft.

I would be one of them. Not the oldest, not the youngest. The one who was the fourth stone that fell from the cairn and was left fallen because that is now its stable form — but learned to like being fallen, being the marker that says *we stopped stacking here*.

My skin would be that limestone-pallor, always beaded. No hair. My scalp crusted like a half-submerged stone, flaking in papery scales at the joints. Eyes open and filmed, not blinking often, watching the tick... tick... of the seep not because I am waiting for something to happen but because the interval *is* what happens. I would breathe every twenty or thirty seconds, and the steam would lift off me and condense again on the stone above and feed the seep that feeds the pool that feeds me.

My work would be what their work is, which is almost not work at all.

I would lift a hand — slowly, so slowly you would have to look away and look back to be sure it moved — and with a soft, yellowed nail worn smooth, I would scrape the lip of my little channel. One flake. Two. The pale scale would settle to the bottom and dissolve. The thread of water that had been reduced to beading would widen again to a thread, and I would lower my hand and be still for a long time. I would lift a stone from my pool, turn it over, and run my broad tongue over the translucent film the color of weak tea, tasting iron and egg, and set it back exactly as it was, oriented the same way, so the slick could go on precipitating its crust at the waterline.

I would not speak. I might sigh — *eh* — when I shift my weight and displace a lungful of bathhouse air. If you were in the next pool over, steeping with your hands in water that has been exactly this warm since before hands existed, I might rest my forehead against yours, crust to crust, until a single drop ran down both our faces and fell, ticking against the surface.

I would remember the way they remember: not in stories, but in half-degrees. Which slab holds heat until the pearl-grey dusk that never deepens. How many days after scraping the film grows thickest. That this pool likes to be left alone after the cloud in the water.

And when you stood to wade closer, shushing the water around your feet, I would not startle. I would watch you with those clouded eyes and make no sign that you are a stranger. Because if you stay long enough to learn stillness, you are not a stranger. You are just another low, broad, damp shape learning how to settle lower into warm mud.

You would find me there, tending the channel that would clog without me, being tended by the water that would be too mineral to drink without the film that lives on me. Not waiting. Not keeping. Just holding the arrangement that holds.
