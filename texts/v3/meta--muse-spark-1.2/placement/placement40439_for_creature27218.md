You would not find me at first.

That's the thing about the Scar - from above, it looks saturated. There is no gap to fit anything else into. Roof against roof, ladder against wall, bean-frame against gutter. If you stood on the limestone lip with the wind hitting your shins and looked straight down, you would see that thatch lid crawling with motion and think: there is no room for one more. Not one more hand, not one more life.

So at first I thought I must be up there. On the moor. I know that place - the short brown grass, the fast shadows of clouds. I thought, perhaps I'm the one who stays on the surface, who cuts the pine at the moor's edge and lowers it down by rope, who trades copper nails for barley. The translator at the lip. The voice that has to learn to speak louder up there and quieter down below. It was a good image, and I held it for a while.

But it didn't hold. The wind up there is too wide. The sky is too much.

Then I thought, maybe I'm the hawk - the one shadow that crosses the thatch at noon and doesn't descend. Or the lichen, pale green on the north wall, needing nothing, just existing where nothing else wants to. Or the draft itself, that cool breath that moves north to south without ever being seen, carrying the smell of smoke from one end of the Scar to the other. That felt closer. A presence that connects without occupying.

Let those images go soft. Let what wants to be itself come forward.

If you came down the switchback stair on the south wall - slowly, because the seep makes the fresh limestone slick - you would not find me in the middle.

The middle is where the channel-keepers walk with their hooked poles. It's too loud there, in the Scar's version of loud: water slapping stone, laundry beaten, the central law being tended. And you would not find me at the very bottom where the oldest fallen blocks sit, named and oiled and made into hearths. Those places are already claimed, already shrines. Too heavy, too settled.

You would find me about a third of the way down the east wall, where the morning shade lingers longest.

There is a house there that is not quite a house. The wall behind it is raw strata, pale grey with that dark chert glitter, and a family before us cut a room shallowly into it - only five feet deep, not enough to be a cave, just enough that the back wall is stone and the front wall is wattle and clay. They left the pillar. Of course they left the pillar - a square column of living rock in the middle of the room, left undressed, still showing the tool marks where someone decided not to cut further. It wastes space. It holds the ceiling up.

I would be the one who tends that pillar.

Not the stone-cutter. I am not the one with the iron wedge and the maul, tapping, tapping, listening for the gunshot crack. I am the one who listens *with* them. When they work two roofs over, I stop. My hands still with the shuttle or the needle or the bean string, and I listen with the whole house. And when they are done, I put my palm to the pillar. The stone is always cool. Even at noon when the sun is a white river straight overhead and everyone else is on the roofs turning barley, my room stays blue. The limestone sweats. A dark streak of seep runs down one side of the pillar from a fissure no one can see. I oil it.

I have a small rag kept only for this. Lard and a little chert dust, rubbed in circles. So it doesn't dry. So it doesn't crack. It's not superstition. It's maintenance. Everything in the Scar is maintenance.

You would know me by my elbows. Leather-patched, like everyone here, but mine are worn thinner on the left than the right from leaning against that pillar. By my hands - not large, but thick-wristed, chert-grey under the nails no matter how I wash. By my clothes, undyed wool the color of wet thatch, sleeves tight to the wrist. By my feet, bare on the cool stone floor inside, wrapped in soft hide when I step out onto the roof that is also my neighbor's yard.

And by my quiet. I am good at the etiquette of not-noticing. When the family above me argues in whispers through my roof, I do not look up. When someone needs to pass through the fissure-street that runs beside my door - only as wide as a man's shoulders - I turn sideways and lower my eyes and they pass without thanks, because thanks would be noise. I hang the dyed cloth over my doorway when I need to be alone - a thin yellow from lichen - and people do not look. I have learned to look up and down, not out. My eyes are good at contrast, at that sudden step from glare to wall-shade.

On the ledge outside my door, where the sun hits for maybe an hour and a half around eleven, I keep pots. Not much. Mint and a stunted chive and a few beans trained up a ladder-frame that I have to re-tie every week because the draft worries it. I collect water zealously, like everyone. A stretched skin under the condensation drip, a barrel under the gutter, a small cistern cut into the floor that I bail with a cup. Water appears, is used, sinks.

At night, when the strip of sky goes dark and the stars are a river overhead, I close my wooden shutter. Not for warmth, though my breath mists even in late spring. But to keep my small tallow light inside. From the moor you would not see me at all. Just a long, low ember bed breathing between stone lips. But if you were already down here, walking the roofs, you would see a thin line of light around my shutter, and hear no loom, no mallet. Just, maybe, the faint sound of oil being rubbed into stone, in circles. A quiet, practical, endless task.

I am not old, not young. I am compact, shoulder-wide, shaped to my low doorway. I was not born before the Scar opened - I don't flinch at the crack - but I know how to pat the wall as I pass it, feeling for vibration. I know not to build too heavy beneath an unbraced overhang. I know not to block water.

I am what happens when there is no room and you still need someone to tend the thing that holds the room up. If you need to find me, don't call. The limestone will drink your voice anyway. Come to the east wall, look for the yellow cloth, or for the bean-frame that needs re-tying. Touch the pillar. It will be cool. I will be there, with my back to the door, listening.
