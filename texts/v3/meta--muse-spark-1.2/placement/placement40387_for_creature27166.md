I wouldn't find myself on the rim.

At first I think I would — that is where you go to see a bowl, to stand above it and take it whole. But the rim is for looking. It is for visitors, for the kind of being that still has water in its blood and wants a vantage. The air is thinnest there, the wind pulls at you, and the bowl below is too loud and too complete to be understood from above. If I stayed on the rim I would be an eye only, drying out.

So I come down.

And at first I think I would be one of the gliders — because I have always loved that kind of life. To be paper-thin and translucent, to have no mouth and no weight, to live entirely on touch. To ride the heat that comes up off the salt, to brush a giant leaf and steal its charge and shiver with it. To be a thousand knuckles cracking at once, to be part of the storm in the air. But I am not that fast. I don't want to die crumpled like burnt parchment every few hours and be eaten before I hit the ground. I am too interested in staying.

Then I think I would be a Holder.

It would be good to be that central one, the fifteen-foot column of braided rope, the fountain of cloak-leaves veined rose and soot. To be the reason everything else can be. To crackle at the edges at dusk with Saint Elmo's fire and comb ammonia straight out of the thin air and make oil from light. To creak and pump, half-deflated, forever. But a Holder is too patient, too still. It holds. It keeps its apron of empty salt and does not cross it. It is a world, not a person in a world.

I am smaller than that, and I need to move.

Let the image settle a little longer.

I am not in the center. The center is too crowded with its own importance. I am off to the north, where the bowl's wall softens and begins to curve up. Where the white mesa lip throws the most diffuse amber light back down and the heat coming off the rock feels a little stronger through the soles. Where a low sprawl of black glass- felt has furred over a shallow swale and two barrel-lanterns rattle and pop out of sync.

You would find me low. Not tall.

You would find me *in* the windbreak, not looking at it.

The third shelter. The one farthest from the other two, because that is the rule here: enough distance that each one can be extravagant alone. My wall is only a wall, really — salt bricks I cut myself, stacked three high, already rounding at the corners as the dry air melts them back toward dune. My roof is a cured leaf-membrane, drum-tight over braided fiber, the color of old horn. It ticks in the heat. When the updraft rises off the warm rock it flutters, but never tears.

You would find me underneath it, in the dim that is always late afternoon.

Who would I be?

I would be one of the Sparse Ones. Not quite people, as you mean people.

My skin is not skin. It's the same cream as the great leaves, dusted rose and soot, oiled until it shines. I oil my lips and the backs of my hands every hour from a small cured-skin bag that hangs at my throat, or they split. My hair lifts even when I am still — the static here pulls at it. If you put your hand near my blanket you would feel the bite and see the hairs lean toward you.

My blanket is woven glass. Black glass hair, fine as breath, warm to the touch. It hisses faintly as it grows. I am always weaving it and it is always being undone — the gliders come and rasp at it, the striders skate over it, and I comb it straight again. It crackles when I pull it over my shoulders at the thin-violet hour that passes for dusk.

My work is cutting, but only a little.

I keep a flake of chert, sharp as ice, hafted in black resin to a yellowed bone handle. Once a day, in the early amber when the great leaves are most inflated, I walk the twenty steps to the nearest Holder — not the central one, a smaller, younger one with leaves that hang lower — and I slice just the edge, a hand's breadth, no more. The leaf sighs. It does not bleed. Warm oil beads citron and iron, pepper on the tongue from three feet away, and a cloud of fine dry spores hangs in the air for hours, glittering. I catch the oil in a shallow dolomite bowl I carved, its center dark and slick. I catch the spores in another skin. I press the oil into my throat, rub it into my elbows and ankles. That is my water. That is my blood.

Static is my muscle. I feel a glider coming before I see it, by the way the hairs on my arms rise. Heat is my food. The rock exhales it into me all night and I never shiver.

I have a cairn of tooth-colored dolomite outside the windbreak, and a loom weight, and a circle of ash where I burn a little oil for light when the amber thins. And I have chimes.

Long strips of dried lantern membrane, scraped thin until they are tuned. I hung them on a tripod of braided fiber and they knock and moan without any hand. *Pop… pop-pop… tonk.* They are part of the bowl's loudness, and they are how I know I am still here when the gliders go quiet for a breath. Sound is how everything here knows everything else is still alive.

You would not find me moving much. There is too much movement already. Pill-bugs the size of fists rolling their salt-crusted dust balls past my feet, whip-legged striders dimpling the salt without touching it, the lizard with plate-skin and a frond for a tail combing the air. The felt cushion doubling while I watch, then stripped bald, then furring again. A melon-pod swelling glossy and detonating with a gunshot that still makes me flinch, firing its whistling corkscrews over my roof.

I don't try to keep up. I sit in the warm salt, my back to my softened bricks, and listen to the bowl turn over itself: the clack of opal leaves, the barrel drums, the hiss of growing glass, the chitter of a thousand wings rubbing, the seed artillery, the chimes I made knocking for no one.

I am sparse, but not lonely. Lushness here is not about crowding. It is about each thing being utterly itself with a great deal of empty salt around it.

If you came to look for me you would first see only my footprint, half-erased, narrow and long-toed, already overrun with strider tracks. Then you would see the flutter of my roof. Then you would see me, oil-lipped and dust-skinned, curled under a crackling blanket, eyes half-closed in the shadowless light, not sleeping, just listening to make sure the churn hasn't stopped.

I don't drink. I don't wait for rain. I hold my small part of the circle steady, so the riot inside it can stay endless.
