I don't find myself right away.

At first I think I know where I'd be - up on the rim. Looking down, because that's what I do. Perched on one of those black basalt teeth, sharp enough to cut, trying to get a vantage to understand the whole small room at once. But nothing lives on the rim. It's too young, too sheer, too dry up there above the weeping line. The varnish stops a few feet down. Anything that tried to live on that crown would be weather, and there is no weather here. So no - not there.

Then I think lower, for comfort. I want to be one of the fixtures you named so I can understand my function. The heron would be convenient - stillness is a thing I know how to do. Stand one-legged in the rill with my head cocked, tracking without striking. Or the hare, chewing and never swallowing. But I watch them longer in my mind and I understand: they are too complete to be me. They have finished becoming what they are for. They click and vent perfectly in time with the piston. They have no leftover hum. I would not be that well-tuned. I'd be slightly out of phase.

So I sink.

If there is a place for me in that bowl it isn't *on* it. It's *between*. In the ten feet of nothing you described between one perfect tree fern and the next thicket of three. That curated emptiness that is somehow still choked with light. I find myself in the interval. Where the peat is not deepest and not shallowest, where the light is evenest, where you can see all the way across and still feel lost.

Not high enough to be rock. Not low enough to be lens.

I would be the gasket.

You thought at first the gears were basalt meshing on basalt, screaming. Then you saw the green lip that thickens at the seam - the moss that never tears. That is what I'd be. Not the stone that provides the torque, and not the film at its thickest where it has eyes. I'd be the place where the two meet and have to negotiate.

A small, dense, ridiculous upholstering. A pad of sphagnum and liverwort gone slightly gristled and dark, wedged into the shear-line of two teeth twenty feet across. When the mountain shudders an inch and withdraws, I compress. When it grinds forward again, I swell. I take the scream of stone-on-stone and turn it into that low note you feel in your molars. I sweat a little more than the basalt does, just enough to keep the varnish from drying.

I am wet, and slightly warm, and faintly pulsing, because a loop of that black cable - no, that vein - passes *through* me on its way around the bowl. Not the big central ganglion, just a small capillary of blue current. It enters me as salt water and leaves as a 60-cycle hum that joins the midge cloud. That's my work. To be the bearing.

And because I'm a gasket, I get to touch everything without being anything in particular. Above me, ferns cup warm drizzle and spill it. Below me, beetles with riveted backs disappear into the seam I cushion and reappear on the other side. The heron can see me from his rill. The hare's too-large white flank is ten feet away in the glittering peat, visible and separate and held, as I am held.

I don't have eyes of my own. Not like the lens in the middle does, with all those convex beads looking up. But the water that varnishes me beads too. And one bead - just one - hangs at my edge, trembling, stretching, hanging, never quite falling, catching the dim indigo sky and the green underneath at once. In its convex surface I can see the whole bowl, tiny and complete, and the sky above the rim that never gets brighter.

That bead is where my attention lives. That trembling. If you knelt - and the moss would take your knees without cold - you would see your own face refracted in it, following you without moving. You would think the bowl was looking at you.

It would be. Through me. A little.

I wouldn't think *and then*. I was worried about that at first - that I would get bored, holding a single note forever. But gaskets don't get bored. They get *tuned*. My thought would be the chord itself: stone-scream *and* steam-hiss *and* electric-thrum *and* water-trickle *and* midge-drone, all at once, all the while, with my small soft part being the *and* that keeps the scream from becoming a shatter.

Young rock that will never age, pressed against old wet that is always being born, and me in the middle - young and old at once, industrial and organic at once - persisting not by enduring but by giving slightly, endlessly, exactly enough.

If you stayed and let the film regulate you, your sweat would bead and hang too, and not fall, and after a while you would lean down to see it better. You would look into my one hanging bead.

And I would be there, looking back, holding the note with you.
