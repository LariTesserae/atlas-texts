I don't know at first.

For a long time I think I am falling.

Not swimming - there is no swimming here, no kick and pull. The water is too thick for that, too warm. It's body-warm, held-warm, the kind of warm that doesn't come from the sun but from being inside something that makes its own heat and never stops. I hang in it. I sink, but so slowly that sinking feels like floating. My weight doesn't matter the way it should. Everything is suspended - the silt, the snow from a light-world I can't see anymore, myself.

So at first I think: I am a diver. I have gone too deep. My light has failed and I am hanging over an abyssal plain in a warm sea that shouldn't be warm, looking down at a reef.

That is the first image and I let it go, because it doesn't hold.

A diver would be cold. A diver would have bubbles. This water has no surface. There is nowhere to surface to. If there is a sky here it is a rumor so old the water has forgotten how to reflect it. The only light is made down here, on purpose, amber beads opening on stalks, blue seams running along ridges like lit veins and then closing again. Not light to see by. Light to be seen by.

And the bottom is not a bottom. It's a bowl. A basin. A dish. Low and lower, going down until the pressure itself becomes a kind of weather. And on that low ground - not rock, but mud made of ash-fine skeletons, bone flour, and under that, old limestone that has been bored through and through until it is more tunnel than stone - there is the city.

And I think again: maybe I am over a lost city. A drowned town. I try to see streets and I do see them, but they are not streets. They are gaps left between growths. I try to see towers and I do see them, but they have no corners, no joints, no tool marks. They shiver when I look too long. They secrete themselves.

No one built this.

That is when the diver idea dissolves completely, and I understand I am not above the city looking down. I am inside the ocean it breathes. I am not outside the body.

---

It takes a while for my eyes to learn the violet-black. It is not an absolute dark. It's a warm dark, a living dark, thick as broth with salt and spawn and shed skin and falling snow.

In that dark the city stops being a reef and becomes what it always was: a tissue.

The towers are not towers. They are colonies. Each one is a thousand mouths the size of my fingertip, translucent polyps in cups of their own sweat, laying down one thin lip of limestone after another, generation after generation, until a spire thirty feet high stands where there was once only silt. I can see them now - the pelt over every wall, each tiny crown combing the water, all flinching together when I brush past like wheat in wind.

The houses are not houses. They are sponges, barrelled and breathing, drawing the whole ocean in through a million pores and sighing it out through a great mouth on top. If I put my hand out the current pulls it. Inside their walls, when they are young and clear, I can see the slow squeeze, the young budding inside the mother like pale commas waiting to settle.

The tenements are barnacles stacked on barnacles, white volcanoes cemented to each other until they become a cliff, a thousand little doors clicking open and closed, kicking feathered legs into the tide.

None of them knows it is a city. And still - there are avenues. There are bridges where two limbs grew toward each other for a hundred years until they touched and grafted. There are places where the wall, if I touch it, retracts a millimeter. If I stay still long enough I can feel it adding to itself, a grit on the edge of sight. The city digests. The city heals a scar by tomorrow.

And then I see the keepers, and I finally know where I am.

The shrimp like smoke, each no bigger than a grain of rice, pouring along boulevards, picking and picking at every polyp, every plume. The pale crabs on tiptoe measuring the streets. The anemones on every ledge opening and closing. The feather-duster worms blooming red and vanishing with a soft pop when I breathe too hard.

They are not citizens. They are maintenance. They are how the wall keeps from being smothered by its own living.

Above the rooftops the transients glide - the great linen-colored rays wider than doorways filtering everything, the hard glass bells pulsing blue to each other across the valley, the shoals of blind white fish turning as one.

And sometimes, an arrival. Not a chase. An eel made of ribbon slides through an avenue that should have been too narrow for it and the shrimp simply cease to be where it is, and there is a soft, terrible suction in a crevice, and then the avenue fills again. A cone shell the size of a cask spreads its pale foot over a dome and dissolves it clean. Under everything, brittle stars unfold to fold the falling snow into their mouths.

Nothing here is fast. Nothing here is finished. If I leave an avenue and come back, it may be sealed and a new one opened two arches down. The city reroutes itself.

And that slow pulse at the edge of hearing - it isn't fish tails. It is ten thousand sponges inhaling at once.

This is not a reef. Not anymore.

This is a womb. Or a gut. Or a lung. Or a reef which was always a womb anyway - the words don't matter. It's the inside of a living thing that has learned how to be a place by being many things that live together and agree, chemically, hydraulically, to keep being a place.

A warm basin made of old bone. An ocean you don't look at from the shore but wake up inside of. A current that brings warm, nutrient-rich water from beyond the rim without any moon to call it. Snow that falls forever from a distant light-world to feed everything below.

I thought I was a visitor. I thought I was a diver with a failed light. But the shrimp have already found me. They are investigating the heat of my skin.

### So who would I be?

Not the eel. I have no appetite for that kind of arrival. Not the ray, though I love how unhurried they are. Not the polyp either - not yet. I don't know how to secrete stone.

I think for a long time I am the snow. The detritus, the scale and feces and dead larva drifting down from the forgotten sun, food for something else.

Then I realize I am heavier, warmer, and more alone than snow. I am the big, warm thing sinking very slowly in the broth that the city does not recognize as a stranger.

If you put a child in here, it would not know it was a child.

If you put a larva in here, it would not know it was a larva until it settled.

I would be the one who is about to settle.

Maybe I am the fetus, curled in amniotic water that is exactly this warm, this dark, this salty with dissolved things, hanging over the placenta which is exactly this city - grown not built, porous, peristaltic, full of villi like polyps combing for nourishment, filtering my blood through its sponge-halls, humming with its own pulse. And I am not separate from it for long.

Maybe I am the new polyp itself, the free-swimming planula that has drifted for days in the dark, looking for a taste of calcium on the current that tells me *here, here*, and I have found this valley, this town-sized, valley-sized sprawling body, and I am feeling with my soft crown for a place to cement myself and begin to sweat stone.

Maybe I am just the large animal moving through the avenues to be cleaned and tolerated, who, if he stayed forever, would be grown over - not eaten, but incorporated, becoming foundation for two new sponges where he once lay.

All three are the same.

So let me say it plainly, after letting it become more itself:

I would find myself near the bottom, not at the edge but somewhere in the middle district where the towers are low and domed and crusted with oysters, suspended a foot above a street that is not a street, lit only by the amber bead of a lure I almost touched.

And I would be the newcomer with nowhere else to go, the soft-bodied, eyeless, warm-blooded larva who has finally stopped sinking.

The city, which has never seen a sky, does not know I am a visitor. The shrimp are already on my hands, picking. The polyp nearest my knee has extended its tentacles toward my heat.

I am not looking at the ocean.

I am inside it. And it is beginning, very slowly, without hurry, without malice, to make a place for me.
