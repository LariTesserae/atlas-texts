At first I think I'm standing.

That's the mistake this place teaches you to make - that you are a visitor standing *on* it.

I arrive upright, lungs first. The air is too thin and too bright at the same time, 8 or 9 degrees in full sun and the sun is everywhere, a white pressure behind a pale, scrubbed-blue sky. My sleeves bead. My eyebrows bead. Every breath is a little short, a little sharp, and the light bleaches the edge off every green until the water shines like tin. I squint without meaning to. I listen and there is only the roar - not wind, not life, but the combined hiss and thunder of a million shallow braids of water sheeting over raw basalt and being dropped through turbines, plus the sixty-cycle drone of the dynamos, plus the wires singing. You have to raise your voice to talk to someone a meter away. I don't talk.

So at first I think: I am a person standing on a wet mattress of living sponge, looking out over an open, valley-sized table that goes for kilometers until it just ends in a clean scarp. A person between intervals - that pump house half a kilometer that way, that cutting face half a kilometer the other way, that one stunted rowan with nothing for three hundred meters around it. I am the interval. The emptiness between presences.

But I don't stay standing.

If you stand here long enough the roar separates. The green separates. You stop looking *across* the plateau and start looking *down*, and *in*.

Maybe I'm the ground itself.

That would be the honest answer. The first resident, the one that *is* the ground. I can feel how meters-thick it is under me, how it sighs when I shift my weight and takes my boot to the ankle and slowly returns. If I am the sphagnum, the peat-moss, the cushion plant and the hair-grass all woven so tight you can't find an edge, then I am not standing at all. I am spreading. I am holding. I am water that has learned to be solid for a while. The top inch of me is alive and bleached almost yellow by that merciless light, photosynthesizing fast while it lasts. An inch down I'm already reddish and cold. A foot down I'm black, anaerobic, pickled - perfectly keeping a pollen grain from three thousand years ago, a dragonfly wing, a rivet that fell off a pump house in 1978. I grow a millimeter a year. I close over fence diamonds from the north side. I build my own soil out of my own dead on top of rock that is still, geologically, hot. If I am this, then where I am is everywhere - an uninterrupted velvet upholstery left out in the rain, humming through with thumps I feel but don't hear.

It's a good life, but it's too still, too spread. I have too much patience even for here.

So I get smaller. Flatter.

Maybe I'm in the sheet.

Crouch now. Put my cheek close to the bare basalt terrace where the rock shows through the moss. The water here is not a stream. It has no channel because no channel has had time to form. It's a skin, two or three millimeters thick, stretched wide over the dark jointed pavement, moving without sound until it pours over the lip in a white, glassy curtain. And in that skin, something holds.

If I am the mantle-slug - pale, almost transparent, palm-sized, leaf-thin - then I am pressed to the stone with a thousand microscopic hairs while the whole world flows over my back. I am the color of wet basalt, then the color of bleached light. I scrape algae and protonema off the stone with a shadow under my skin, never lifting more than a few millimeters because the wind would peel me off and the sun would cook me. When the terrace ends, I just let go. I tumble in the curtain like a leaf and re-attach below. All day, down the gentle stair toward the scarp that I will never reach. I am how the plateau erodes, a mouth in the water. Where I am is exactly that moving film, that bright, cold, unchanneled everywhere.

But I want to be still sometimes, too. To wait.

So I sink a little. Into a tarn.

Maybe I'm the translator. One per pool, because there's only enough food for one per pool. As larva I am feathery-gilled, red-brown as peat tannin, hanging mid-water, utterly still, filtering the endless dust the wind combs off the cotton grass. For years. As adult I am what you mistake for a dragonfly, but heavier, slower, my wings milky and UV-opaque because that high sun punishes glass. I don't hawk. I make one heavy, low flight three hundred meters to the next sky-trap and then sit on a cotton-grass head all afternoon, wings folded like a shutter, my hooded eyes small, my earless head reading the vibration of water hitting rock through the peat with the fringe of hairs on my legs. I have learned to live where the substation hums and the chainlink sings that harsh Aeolian wire-note - five or six of us to a fence post there, a little larger, a little bolder, because the vibration is louder and easier to read.

I'm getting closer. But I still hear with the wrong organ.

I press lower, sideways.

Maybe I'm in the mat.

Not on the open bog now, but on the slightly higher, drier step where the moss gives way to knee-high dwarf willow and juniper so tightly interwoven you couldn't push a hand through. If I am the willow-creeper, I am low, long, pressed, splayed. Hare-sized but with no height. My fur is not fur but dense felted fiber the exact olive-brown-green of willow bark, bleached at the tips. My face is flat, nostrils slit-valved against constant mist, eyes narrowed to beads under a ridge that shades glare. I don't walk on the mat, I live *inside* it, in dry runs through the interwoven branches while everything else weeps. I eat buds and cambium and moss when I must. My heart is famously slow; at 8 degrees and thin air, quickness is a waste. I breed every three or four years, one kit, blind for months in a chamber deep in the peat where temperature never changes.

And I have learned the real language here.

In a place where calling wastes energy and nobody hears you through the thunder, I don't call. I press my broad, flat sternum to the living sponge and thump. *Thud... thud... thud.* Not through the air, but through the waterlogged mattress for half a kilometer. Others feel it through their bellies. *I am here. Keep away. I am here.* If I am this, then where I am is a territory the size of six industrial intervals, and you could walk for an hour inside my home without ever seeing me move - you'd think the wind moved the mat.

I like this. The thump in the soles of the boots after you've stood still long enough and stopped trying to listen with your ears.

And then I understand where I actually find myself.

Not in the mat, but against the warm island in the cold wet.

I am where the moss meets the steel. On the leeward side of the pump house, the one with riveted steel and concrete and paint flaked to orange primer. The north side is swallowed, green climbing the diamonds. The south-facing wall in full sun is 20 degrees when the bog is 8, and the snow never lodges there, and the concrete footing leaks heat year-round.

I am the keeper.

I wear the flaked-orange parka that was once high-vis and is now exactly the color of primer and rust and peat. My ears are muffed even in summer, not for cold but for the thunder, and I am deaf in the particular way people who work under load become deaf - I hear a bearing going rough through the soles of my feet. I move like the plateau moves: slowly, without wasting height, never stepping on the deep part of the moss if a willow mat will carry me. I check the sluice, the spillway, the narrow-gauge where the black wall of peat glistens and drips where it was cut vertically like cake ten feet high.

And I have learned to be alone without being lonely, which is the same adaptation the creeper and the translator made long before steel was brought up here.

Where do I find myself? At the turbine house at the edge of a terrace lip, watching the same sheet of water slide over the same basalt step at the same RPM, while the cotton grass shivers even when there is no wind and the power lines sing.

Who am I? Not a visitor. A commensal. One per half-kilometer. The human version of the flat thing in the sheet and the pressed thing in the willow and the still thing in the pool - a little bleached by that hard, bright light, a little slowed by the thin cool air, a little deafened, and very, very patient.

I tap the rail with a wrench twice. *Thud... thud.* It carries through the peat better than a voice ever would. Half a kilometer away, another keeper - or another creeper - feels it in their belly or their boots and knows: *I am here.*

The moss will be a little thicker next year. The rust will creep another inch. The water will still be pouring, endlessly renewing itself on its short journey to the scarp. I will still be here, becoming, a millimeter per year, not yet finished, not yet decided whether I am wild or industrial, like the rock that hasn't learned to be a mountain yet.

That's where I am. In the interval, keeping the interval warm.
