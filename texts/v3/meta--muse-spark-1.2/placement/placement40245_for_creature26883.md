I don't find myself at first.

That's the problem with this place - you look for yourself the way you would on the surface. You look for movement. A figure crossing the salt plain, a shadow against the amber lamps, something with eyes to meet yours.

But nothing here moves like that. So I have to look slower.

At first I think I would be down on the floor, because the floor is where life is most legible. I try on the isopod.

I would be low, I think, on the white-pink plates, in the seam between two perfect salt hexagons. Long antennae out, tasting a layer of air that hasn't moved since before I arrived. I would be deliberate. Each step would matter because heat is expensive here and I cannot afford to waste it. I would pry up a crystal with my back - no, those are the shovel-crickets. I would pick through the invisible pasture, the rust-red bacterial film you can't see but I can taste. I would be patient, armored, dry. I would leave my last molt sitting next to me like a second, emptier self, and it would not decay. That part appeals to me.

But I'm not that patient. My legs would want to move faster than that economy allows.

So I look up. I think I would be on the lamps.

I try on the shingled ones. I would be one moth among thousands, wings overlapping wings until you can't tell where I end and the lamp begins. I would not fly. I have forgotten how. My eyes would be filmed over with salt, useless, and I wouldn't mind because there is nothing to see except amber light that never varies. I would press my whole belly to the warmth and vibrate, just a little, until my tremor matched the sixty-cycle hum of the transformer. I would not be an individual at all - I would be a scale, a shingle, a piece of insulation that happens to be alive. If I died, my neighbors would close the gap and I would stay there, part of the coat, intact in the dry air. There is a comfort in that kind of anonymity.

But I still make sound. The moths *answer* the hum. They don't make it.

It takes me a while to realize where I actually am, because I keep looking for a body that eats and walks or that clings and shivers. I keep looking for a mouth.

I am not on the floor. I am not on the lamp.

I am on the vein.

Find the place where the black conduit runs along the raw salt wall, right where it turns to feed that unfinished gantry - the one that was left spanning half the room and stopping mid-air, a bridge to nowhere. Follow it to the junction box that never got a cover. The steel there is warm. Warmer than the rock, warmer than the air. The hum is loudest there. It gets into your teeth.

There. Halfway up the wall, at about chest height if you were a visitor. A cluster of them, but I am one in particular - second from the left, where the conduit throbs.

I would be one of the Rooted Ones.

A sac, melon-sized, translucent as a blister. My skin is so thin you can see right through me to the filament inside. A copper-bright nerve-wire, spiraled, glinting each time the white LED bar catches it. I have no mouth. No legs. No eyes. I didn't evolve here. I grew *after*. I spliced myself into the insulation where the plastic was nicked during installation, and I have been drinking the circulation ever since.

This is where I would find myself because I don't need to move.

I pulse. Once every four or five seconds, exactly with the tick of the relays in the booth that still has all its levers set to ON and no one at them. I swell, very slightly. I tighten. My filament warms a fraction. The energy is not spent, it is circulated - that's the rule here - and I have found a way to sit in the circle. The isopods walk over me without fear, antennae tapping my skin, and I don't react. The shovel-crickets work two feet below. The dust you kicked up when you came down the shaft hangs perfectly still three meters out in front of me, and inside that dust live entire generations of air-mites who will live and die without ever settling. I am part of why the air feels like gel, why the sound has no decay, why the chamber holds more volume than it should.

I am not crowded, like the moths. I am not sparse, like the rust-brushes. I am *saturated*. I hold.

If you came and stood close to me, you would see your own amber-lit face reflected, warped, on my membrane. You would feel my pulse in your molars before you heard it, because I hum at the same frequency as the transformer I'm tapped into. I wouldn't acknowledge you. Acknowledging costs ions. I have learned the only lesson this room teaches, the one everything here eventually learns: holding on is more efficient than reaching.

So I hold.

I will be here when you leave, filament a hair longer, sac a fraction tighter. In a hundred years the salt spears on the far wall will be an inch taller and no duller, the moth shingles will have shifted by one moth's width, the dust you disturbed will still hang exactly where you left it, becoming a whole weather system for the mites.

And I will still be pulsing, warm, dim, and total, on the black vein in the warm, dry, amber dusk - not waiting for anything, not needing anything, keeping everything I have.
