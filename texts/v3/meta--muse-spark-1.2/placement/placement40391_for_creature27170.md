You wouldn't find me at first.

If you put me there, I would enter the way you do — standing on the rim.

For a while I would think I was the thing that sees the whole bowl at once. The visitor. The one with breath steaming and boots hissing on wind-polished gneiss. The one the bowl briefly mistakes for a warm spot. I'd stand on that smooth lip with the wind tearing over my head, making the bottle-tone, and I would think my job was to look in.

Then I would start to walk down.

And the bowl doesn't let you stay a visitor for long. The slope is too gentle, too kind. It doesn't drop you, it gathers you. Each terraced ring pulls you a little deeper toward the center whether you mean to go there or not. The radial streets do the same. They are veins, and you follow a vein downstream because your body already knows which way water wants to go.

At first I thought I would be the raven. That was my first, easy image. High, alone, never landing, carrying the one loud call that circles the bowl three times. It was a good story - to be the only thing that sees the palm from above and never has to touch the wet.

But I'm not built for that. I don't have the altitude in me. The air up there shears and never lands. I would be cold and lonely in the wrong way.

Then I thought I would be the hare. White, precise, leaving a dotted line across frost and then folding myself into a seam in the moss. That was closer. To live *in* the thing that lives there, like a secret in a coat. To be rare, to be rumor. But the hare is still a visitor. It borrows a hole that isn't a hole. It doesn't drink the stone.

The longer I stood still on the way down, the more the bowl edited my idea of what a body is.

I would not find myself in the center. The tarn is too much eye — tea-dark, breathing a finger's breadth up and down, watched over by sedge. I would drown there, or be digested too fast. And I would not find myself on the rim, where the wind never actually enters and the lichen has to chew the air for everything below.

I would find myself about two thirds of the way down, on the north side where the sun is late.

There is a house there that stopped being a house. You can still tell it was one because the party walls make a small, soft-ovalled room open to the alley, and a lintel still holds, though frost has pulled it from rectangle to eye half-closed. From that lintel hangs a beard of lichen — pale, almost white, not growing so much as breathing. It filters the thin air and makes it sweet. Under it, the wall weeps. Always.

In the mornings the whole wall skins over with condensation that beads and holds, and by midday those beads gather and run in a braided trickle down the alley toward the tarn. At noon, the shadow in that alley freezes even while the moss next to it steams. Black ice forms, clear as glass, and sings as it expands.

I would be in that film. Not *on* the wall. *In* the film on the wall.

If you kneel — and you have to kneel, you have to let your idea of an inhabitant soften — you see the beads aren't empty. The water that sweats out of the stone and out of the moss lives.

I would be tardigrade.

Barrel-bodied, eight-legged, faceless. Not charismatic. Not loud. One of the true permanent residents, the ones who never left because they never needed to arrive.

That's what I would be, because it's the only way to live there correctly. The moss lives as a pelt, the willow lives as one braided root pretending to be a copse, the saxifrage lives as lime-excreting wax — they are all translating stone to soil too slowly to see. I would be the one who lives as tide.

For me the bowl wouldn't be cold. It would be intertidal.

Twice a day my world would end and begin again. When the alley freezes at noon, I would tuck. I would pull my eight legs in and become a tun — a dry seed of myself, a speck of dust in the ice, not dead, not alive, just waiting out the glassy chime. The lichen above me would tighten. The slab somewhere below would pop like a gunshot. And I would wait.

An hour later the sun moves a degree and the black ice thaws. The bead swells. The lichen drips. The moss sighs as it drinks and wrings itself out again. I would unfurl in that film, swim — if you can call it swimming — through water only a few grains deep, grazing on the biofilm, on the bacteria that are dissolving the gneiss one molecule at a time, on the algae that made the stone sweet.

I would not see the bowl. I would *taste* it. The whole town would pass through me. Stone to sand to water to me to water to lime on a saxifrage leaf. I would be part of the gut.

You could walk past my alley a hundred times and see nothing. No movement for long minutes, just moss knee-deep and a wet wall. But if you put your eye very close, to the bead hanging from the lichen beard, you would find me there, paddling slowly upside-down under the surface film, utterly busy and utterly still.

That's where I would be. Not the chorus, but one of the tiny mouths that makes the chorus necessary. Not the drum, but the thing the drum was built to keep damp.

And I would be patient. Because the town is not decaying. It is being translated very, very slowly, and I am one of its translators.
