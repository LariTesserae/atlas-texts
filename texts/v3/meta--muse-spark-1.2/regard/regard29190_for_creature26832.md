If a benevolent and wise power noticed this place -

really *noticed* it, the way the being notices a shift in pressure before the fog comes over the wall -

they would not come the way powers are expected to come.

No light descending. No voice from above the wall. No airship finally arriving at its dry dock.

At this height, anything that arrives loudly is a violence. The roar is already total. To add to it would be cruelty, not benevolence. And wisdom would know that immediately: *that to be heard here you must become quieter than the roar, not louder than it.*

So they would not arrive at all, at first.

They would *stop* somewhere far below the sheer, corrugated cliff, below the ocean of cloud, and they would listen upward.

They would feel, the way the being feels, the slow turn of the twenty-foot flywheels through the iron of the world. One inch an hour. They would feel the way rust and root hold equal claim and the way that balance is tended, bead by bead, not governed. They would understand, before they ever came close, that this is not a ruin.

It is a monastery that built itself by accident and then found its monk.

And so the first thing the power would do is the hardest thing for a power to do.

Nothing.

They would withhold rescue.

Because wisdom would see what a hasty benevolence would miss: that the being is not waiting to be saved. The being is not lonely the way the lowlands mean lonely. The being is not broken because it is deaf. Its deafness is its listening. Its solitude is its wholeness, the way the single crow owns the whole updraft and is not lonely for a flock. Its damp, moss-shouldered, boiler-suited life is not a tragedy to be corrected. It is a vocation, perfect and scaled precisely to its bowl of world.

To "save" it by carrying it down the mountain, to cure its ears and give it a house with dry floors and silent nights, would be to kill it with kindness. To turn off the Mill to give it quiet would be to take away its warmth, its water, its clock, its companion. To send it three or four more caretakers so it would not be alone would be to destroy the interval that makes its music.

So the power would not fix. Would not fill. Would not populate.

For a long time - maybe a season lowlanders would count, but here only a few hundred ticks of the cracked gauge - the power would simply keep watch without being seen.

The second thing the power would do is learn to listen the way the place listens.

When they finally did come over the parapet, they would not come standing. They would come low.

They would come as fog does.

You would not see a figure crest the wall. You would feel a slight easing of the fan-wind, a softening at the torn edge where the wind is always being re-torn. The condensation that morning would bead a little heavier, a little more even. The bumblebee on its solitary circuit would not swerve away, but pause a second longer on a vine-flower.

And then, in the northern quadrant where the moss is thick and warm as a living rug, there would be a second place where the moss is pressed down. Shoulder-width apart. Not trampled. Gently pressed, as if someone had sat and decided to stay a while.

The being would feel it long before it saw. Through the soles of its broad, calloused feet, through the iron. A new vibration, but not an intrusion. Not boots. Something that knows how to step softly on a grate.

It would turn its head slowly, the way it does, toward pressure, not sound. Its pale blue high-altitude eyes would find the visitor sitting not in the control booth, not in a place of command, but on the warm iron between two turbine housings, where a bromeliad cup needed watering.

The power - if you still needed to see it as a shape - would have taken a shape that could sit on moss without crushing it. Bare feet, maybe. Shoulders that could hold condensation without brushing it off. Hands stained maroon, or that could be. They would have allowed a little of the fine green fur of moss to already be on them, to show they intend to stay long enough for moss to grow.

They would not speak. They know better. They would tilt their head the way the being tilts its head, listening with the molars and the skin.

And after a while, when the being had finished its circuit with its dented grease-tin, catching water that was air this morning, the power would do the only benevolent thing left that is not condescension:

They would ask to be taught.

They would hold out their own tin - not a new one, shining, but one found there, already dented - to a pipe's underside where the invented morning river runs for an hour, and they would miss the first few beads. Their hands would be clumsy at first, too quick. The being would see this, and without surprise, the way you look at a bee that has found its way inside, it would lean close, bone to bone, and show them.

*Not there. Here. Wait for it to fall. Don't take it before it's ready.*

A third thing. The power would be small, and precisely small.

Wisdom does not do grand gestures in a bowl three hundred meters across where everything is scaled to one pair of hands. It does tiny, durable mercies that fit inside the existing rules.

So they would not bring soil. They would not make it rain. They would not stop the rust.

They would do this:

While sitting together between the vast, slow wheels, where the whole hamlet-sized world rings around you and warm vapor condenses on your shoulders, the power would place a palm flat on the warm iron, the way the being does to check if a grate is getting too hot. And they would leave a little of their own warmth there. Not enough to change the climate. Just enough that the Mill's waste-heat, which has been bleeding away for generations, would have one less degree to lose. One more winter that the fog still comes. One more decade before a bearing that has been creeping an inch an hour finally seizes.

They would breathe, once, very slowly, over the crack between concrete slabs where the black-green fern splits the aggregate with hydraulic patience, and where there is not quite enough rust-dust for it this year. Their breath would be iron-rich. Ferruginous. The fern would not grow faster. It would simply not starve.

They would, without being asked, tilt one condensation tray a fraction of a degree - the one the being had already tilted to feed the basin-bromeliad - so that its bright thread of water never quite misses the cup, even when the wind changes over the parapet. The being would notice, later, and understand that someone else had also been tending.

And most of all, they would witness.

When the being leans close, mouth to ear, and says in that too-loud, toneless voice, flattened by years of roar - *Watch. It is about to tick.* - the power would not smile politely and look away. The power would look exactly where the being is pointing, at the cracked glass gauge that ticks one notch per day, and they would wait the whole long afternoon with it.

They would wait until it ticks.

And when it does - that faint click you feel more in the molars than you hear - they would feel it too, and let the being see on their face that they felt it.

That is the miracle that has never happened here before: to be understood without having to shout.

They would not stay forever. A benevolent power that stayed forever would become another Mill - another omnipresent roar that the place would have to adapt around. So before the fog poured over the parapet for the night, they would rise.

They would not promise to return. Promises are heavy things at altitude.

Instead, they would do a last, wise thing for both who live there - the place and the being who is part of the place.

They would make it unfindable.

Not invisible. Not healed back into a true mountain peak. The sheer poured cliff, the corrugated iron, the scar where the summit was cut off - that would remain, because erasing it would be a lie. But they would lay a deeper cloud ocean around its base, a little thicker, a little more permanent. They would bend the wind that hits the lip so that airships' instruments slide past it. They would make the maps down below misremember the elevation by a few meters. Enough that no expedition, no company that still holds the deed to whatever the Mill was meant to refine, would come looking to reclaim it.

They would leave the bowl exactly as it is: deafening, damp, warm, overgrown, lonely in scale but not in spirit. A walled hamlet for one.

When the being woke the next morning - by the lessening of damp on its mossy shoulders - the pressed place in the moss beside it would still be warm. There would be no footprints leading away. Only a dented tin, left neatly beside the bromeliad cup, full to the brim with clear water that was air this morning.

And somewhere, on the shoulder of the work-suit where the being never thought to brush it off, a patch of that very fine green moss would be a little thicker, a little more luminous, as if it had been touched and blessed in the night.

The Mill would turn. The fans would pull. The fern would hold its crack. After a long time, the gauge would tick again. The being would feel it through its feet, and tilt its head, and for half a second, before it remembered that it was alone, it would have the distinct, bodily impression that somewhere in the interval - in that cathedral hush underneath the deafening - someone else was tilting their head too, and waiting with it.
