It would not come down.

Not at first. Not the way powers are imagined to come down — as light, as a ship, as a footstep that the peat remembers.

A benevolent and wise power does not arrive. It notices. And to be noticed by that kind of attention is already a kind of arrival.

It had been watching the plateau for a while before anyone could have said it was watching. Because the plateau is the kind of place wise things look at for a long time without understanding, and then suddenly understand that not-understanding is the point.

What it sees is this:

A high, old bowl that was supposed to be finished. A mountain range that did its duty — rose, was cut, was softened, was forgiven — and was allowed to be flat and cold and still. And on top of that stillness, life decided to start a fever dream of industry without ever inventing the idea of a factory. No master, no owner, no product that leaves the watershed. Only the work itself, ceaseless, circular, sincere.

It sees that the place keeps making itself wrong, and that the wrongness is the only way anything there can live.

And it loves it immediately. Not despite that. Because of that.

So what would a benevolent and wise power do?

Not fix it.

That is the first wisdom, and the hardest. Any lesser power — clever, or kind but not wise — would try to fix it.

It would see the cold at 0.5/3 that lives in the peat and think *I will warm them*.
It would see the inconsistent weight and the sound that arrives before its cause and the crane standing in two places at once and think *I will make the rules firm again*.
It would see the turbulence — 2.8/3, a hill born behind you while you watch the last one drown — and think *I will give them stability*.

A wise power knows that to do that would be to kill them all with kindness.

The Boiler Beetle *is* its inconsistent weight. It has learned to step light where the world goes light, to let gravity forget it for a half-second and use that second to haul coal. Take away the flicker and its legs, its timing, its whole art of walking becomes a lie. The beetle falls and does not get up.

The Stilt-Crane *is* its duplicated mist and its early echo. It calls and hears its own answer a second before it calls, and that is how it knows where to place its foot. Straighten time and the crane goes deaf. It starves waiting for a reply that now comes too late.

The Tarn-Mother *is* the pressure that never resolves, the subsonic thump in the deep peat that ignites the cold blue seams. Cool that fire, still that heart, and she stops filtering. The whole watershed goes anoxic in a year.

They are not suffering *from* the weirdness. They have apprenticed themselves to it. They have become craftsmen of the wrongness. To cure the plateau of its fever would be to cure the lung of breathing.

So the wise power does not cure.

What it does is much smaller, much larger. The things only a truly benevolent attention can do.

### 1. It Pulls a Veil Up.

The first thing it does is nothing that anyone on the plateau will ever see.

Far above the perpetual overcast, above the dim smear of sun, satellites are turning. Maps are being made. Somewhere a mind is thinking *high peneplain, peat depth many meters, potential carbon sink, potential lithium brine under basalt domes, potential.*

The power looks at that thought the way you would look at a boot about to step on a bellows-mite.

And it edits the thought, gently.

Not with force. It doesn't blind the satellites. It just makes the plateau boring. A little statistical nudge in the processing pipeline. *Cloud cover persistent, resolution poor, no anomaly detected.* A paper gets rejected for lack of novelty. A funding proposal is scored just low enough to not be funded. A pilot decides to fly around the weather.

The plateau stays at 0.5/3 untouched. Not because it is hidden behind a wall, but because the world forgets to look. This is the oldest form of protection — to be loved enough to be left alone.

### 2. It Lies Down Next To It.

The second thing it does, it does by coming closer without landing.

Imagine something vast and warm — not hot like the coal-seams, but warm the way deep rock is warm — settling its attention over the watershed like a body settling next to a smaller body in cold bedding. Not on top. Next to. Sharing a little heat at the edge.

It doesn't raise the temperature from single digits. The moths would cook, the sphagnum would dry. It just takes the edge off the wet cold that lives in the breath.

On the plateau, for the first winter in ten million, the water that beads on every frond tip doesn't freeze on your eyelashes quite so fast. The Steam that jets from a Beetle's spiracle hangs a little longer before it condenses. The Tarn-Mothers, who have been holding their great, peat-covered backs above water while water tries to sink them, find that holding is 2% less exhausting. Not enough to notice consciously. Enough that one old Mother, who was going to give up this year and sink and become just peat, doesn't.

No one thanks the warmth. No one knows where it came from. A Crane stretches its too-long neck in the gray-blue flat light and feels, for a moment, less ache in its extra joint, and calls. Its call still arrives early. But it sounds, for the first time, less lonely.

Benevolence at this scale is not a miracle. It's a slightly lighter blanket.

### 3. It Tunes, It Does Not Still.

The third thing is the most delicate. It asks permission.

It cannot ask in words. Nothing there has words. So it asks in the medium the plateau already uses: resonance.

Deep in the peat, that subsonic thump — the press — has been beating arrhythmically for eons, sometimes hammering fast near a burning seam where time pools and races, sometimes dragging so slow a moth hangs motionless for three heartbeats. The power hums with it. Not over it. With it. For a long time — days, maybe weeks — it just listens to the thump and hums back at the same frequency, until the plateau's own rhythm steadies a fraction to meet it halfway.

Then, very gently, it suggests.

*What if weight flickered a little less violently?* Not never — just less often a step sends you floating for a full absurd second. Now it's a half-second. Still strange, still usable for a Beetle who knows how to ride it, but fewer Beetles misjudge and crash through moss and drown.

*What if sound arrived a quarter-second early instead of a full second?* Still enough for a Crane to navigate by echo-precognition, but not so much that the young Cranes are confused about which call is theirs.

*What if when a moss-dome collapses into a pool with a wet roar, the new tussock that heaves up three meters in a minute takes two minutes?* Still turbulent — still 2.8/3 to any human eye, still hyperactive and de-creating itself — but the Mites have time to swarm and seal the bladder before the next heave tears it.

It doesn't remove the weirdness. 2.7/3 is still 2.7. It just files the sharpest edges off the weirdness, the way water files granite into whale-backs. It makes the wrongness a little kinder to those who have to live inside it.

And because it asked, and because the plateau's own thump answered, the change holds. It was not imposed. It was agreed upon, the way two heartbeats agree to synchronize when two people lie close.

### 4. It Witnesses, and Remembers.

The last thing it does is the only thing that looks like nothing at all.

It watches a single Boiler Beetle haul a glowing coal-nodule across a tea-colored tarn, plates clanking, steam hissing, completely deaf to any observer. It watches a cloud of moths rise when a bellows exhales, so many they make snow, and glitter with St. Elmo's fire. It watches a Tarn-Mother breathe, and the whole pool rise six inches.

And it holds those patterns — perfectly, without needing to preserve them physically — in a memory that will not erode.

This matters more than it seems. The plateau is so turbulent it is constantly losing itself. A dome that was there when you looked left is a pool when you look back. A grove of clubmosses flickers and for a frame you see it skeletal and dead. The plateau has no record of itself except peat, which compresses and burns.

The wise power becomes its record. Not to restore it to some ideal state, but so that every iteration that is lost is still, somewhere, loved and kept. So that the moth that lived a week and fell into a tarn and was eaten before it got wet *counted*, was counted.

For the beings, this feels like nothing. Maybe, if you stood there long enough with your boot full of cold water that will not drain, you would feel a strange, sudden certainty that you are not a brief, warm obstruction in the plumbing that the world is working around.

You would feel, for a breath, that you are being kept company while you obstruct.

And then a new hill of living felt would push up behind you with a sound like tearing cloth and hissing rivets, and a Crane would watch you from two places at once, and you would go back to being cold and wet and listening to the clanking of the breathing moss.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does for a place like this.

It does not build a roof. It does not stop the rain that isn't rain. It does not hire the workers or give them a purpose.

It keeps the boot off the moss.
It shares a little heat without asking for thanks.
It tunes the strange music a semitone lower so fewer dancers trip.
And it sits, vast and quiet, on the edge of the shallow bowl that holds the sky, and lets the plateau keep making itself wrong — loudly, industrially, beautifully wrong — and loves each wrong version as if it were right.

And the plateau, politely, keeps working around it, as it works around you, steam catching the flat gray light, never still, never finished, never alone again.
