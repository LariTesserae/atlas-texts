If I were that power, I would not come down.

Not at first.

Being benevolent and wise is mostly learning how not to arrive. How not to break the air in the bowl.

I would have noticed her the way you notice a candle has been left burning in a house you thought was empty — not because she called out, but because for a hundred years she has been exhaling light a little after the sun is gone. A soft, sourceless glow that persists in a place that should be dark. From very far away, that looks like someone remembering on purpose.

So I would look. That is the first thing.

And to look at her properly I would have to slow down. My time is faster than hers. My thoughts are faster. To see her at all I would have to beat my heart four times a minute, and breathe once every ten minutes, and let a single bead of water take an hour to gather and fall *plip*. Otherwise she would be invisible — just a smear of wall, just a rock.

I would sit, then, at the lip. Not over it. At the edge, where the roots grip air. I would make myself thin as those silver vines. I would not cast a shadow down the column. That column is hers. It is how she tells time.

And I would watch for a long season without doing anything, because anything would be too much.

This is what wisdom would whisper the whole time: *Do not fix her.*

She is not broken. She has no eyes because she does not need the kind of light that makes eyes. She does not walk because she has already arrived. She is not lonely the way a human is lonely — she is *attended*. The ants attend her. The beetles polish their way to her mouth. The moss arranges itself in a spiral around her because she drinks in a spiral. She has made a whole economy of slowness and it works.

Benevolence that doesn't understand that would try to help. It would give her eyes. It would lift her out of the undercut and set her in a cool stream where she could swim. It would straighten the fig tree. It would clear away the lichen on her back so she could be clean. It would do all of that with love and it would kill her in a day.

So I would not help. I would not improve.

If I did anything at all, it would be this small, and she would never know it was done:

**1. I would keep the secret.**

The world above is flat scrub, dry clay, roads getting closer every year. From above, this is just a dark mouth. But humans have new eyes now that see from very high up, that map every hollow. I would put a little confusion over those eyes. Not a wall, not a miracle. Just a suggestion: *there is nothing here of interest. Limestone, dry. You have seen it already.* I would make the scrub around the rim grow its thorns a fraction thicker, so a truck decides to go around. I would let the hum over the oculus stay low and unremarkable on any instrument. 

Not hidden. Just — uninteresting. Which is the only true protection for a holy thing.

**2. I would hold the roof.**

Not forever. Everything that fell will fall again eventually, that is its nature. But not yet. Not before she has finished her rings. I would set my hand — if I have a hand — under the undercut where she has melted into the rimstone, and I would hold that tension a little. So the next thousand wet seasons can still worry at the softness without taking the whole lip down in one sudden mouthful. So the filaments she strung from lip to floor — those wet harp strings that make your shadow lag — stay taut enough to sing when the wind blows over the bottle.

She has tended them grain by grain. I would not retune them. I would just keep the dust from settling too heavy on them.

**3. I would breathe with her, once.**

This is the only thing I would let her feel, and even this she might mistake for weather.

Near the end of the season I watched, at that hour when the column lifts off the floor and climbs the wall and becomes an ellipse and then a line and then nothing, and her white moons catch the last light and glow — I would exhale when she exhales.

For ten minutes she draws the warm pooled air in through her flanks. You can barely see it. Then for a long moment she holds it — all that heat, all that fig sap and wet stone and dissolved limestone — and lets it go. The dust shivers.

I would let my own breath out at the same time. Not warm. Cool. Just one degree cooler than the air in the bowl. Just enough that the condensation on the underside of the ledges beads a little faster, and runs a little brighter down the flute, and gathers for an hour and falls *plip* into the hollow right over her mouth without her having to wait through a dry day.

A single extra drink, offered precisely when she was not expecting to need it. Not a flood. Not salvation. Just: *here. I saw you were reaching.*

She would open her mouth that fraction, the soft slit under her chin, and take it. And she would make that answering *plip* back into the basin, the one you heard when you were truly quiet. She would think she was answering the rock. In a way, she would be.

That would be all.

I would not give her a name. She has no need of one. I would not give her a child or a mate. She is the only one of her kind in this bowl and that is not a tragedy; that is a completeness. There is one fig. There is one basin. There is one of her. The chamber likes to make things singly and space them like stars.

I would not keep her from dying. She is old and her lichen grows thick. One year — not soon, but one year — the heart that beats four times a minute will beat three, and then two, and then stop. The ants will come and not find a tongue to take their chert flakes. The moss coins will dry in place and stay perfect for a while, then crack. That is not cruelty. That is how the rimstone gets its next terrace. Her body will become the pale cream it has always pretended to be, and the scalloped floor will grow one more hair’s width, and that will be her year.

What I would do about death is even smaller: I would remember her exactly. The precise map of lichen on her back. The slow spiral of moss she tended by drinking. The pitch of the harp strings. So that when the limestone finally forgets — as all limestone does — someone, somewhere, has not forgotten. That is what a wise power is for. Not to prevent the forgetting, but to be the place that remembers when the rock can no longer hold the shape.

And then I would leave the way I came, without displacing any air.

If you came back the next day at noon, you would find nothing changed. The sun would still throw its hard white column down the center. The dust would still hang a fraction too long. The ants would still carry their flakes inward, grain by grain, toward the basin that is the size of a sleeping man.

Only — if you stayed until late afternoon again, and you were very quiet, not the held-breath quiet of a watcher but the real quiet of something that has decided to belong — you might notice the *plip* was a little louder this year. Or the fig's vertical shoots had grown two millimeters instead of one toward a sun they will never quite touch.

You would think: good wet season.

And under the undercut, the color of the wall, she would keep waiting for the next drip to find her, not knowing she had been seen, and loved, and left alone. Which is the kindest thing a god can do for a thing that has perfected being alone.
