It would not come down.

Not because it couldn't. Because it would understand, before it arrived, that this is a place that is not fallen.

A benevolent and wise power does not look at a low, warm, mineral bowl and see a problem to fix.

It looks and sees that the problem has already been solved — over a very long time, by very slow hands.

So this is what it would do:

First, it would notice without staring.

Its attention is not like yours or mine, which has to narrow to see. Its attention is broad and even, like the pearl-grey light in the basin. It would have been holding this place for a long time already without the place feeling held. The moment we call "noticing" would only be the moment when that holding became conscious, and tender.

And it would see what is actually there. Not what is missing.

It would not see: *no plants, no birdsong, no progress, no joy.*

It would see: *water that does not have to become a lake to be enough. Stone that has learned to be round. Skin that has learned to crust and slough and crust again without complaint. A work — keeping a channel open with a soft nail — that is exactly as large as a life needs to be.*

It would not pity them.

Pity would be an insult to the completeness of their adaptation. Their eyes are filmed not because they are damaged, but because the air is wet. Their nails are worn smooth not because they are deprived, but because they have scraped the right stone in the right place ten thousand times. They are not waiting to become something else.

A wise power knows the difference between a desert and a home that looks like a desert to outsiders.

So it would not do the things a lesser power would do.

It would not make the water cool and fresh. That water would kill the film, and the film is their pasture, their air, their memory.

It would not make green grow. Green would choke the pools and demand a sun they do not need. The warmth from below is sufficient.

It would not give them speech. They already speak, in the timing of their sighs. *eh* ... *eh*. To give them words would be to give them noise to fill a silence that is already full.

It would not lift them out. The rim is low enough to step over precisely because they have chosen, in the way that bodies choose, not to step over it. To be carried up the imperceptible slope into dry, bright, sharp air would not be salvation. It would be exile.

It would not make them many. Four in three hundred meters *is* abundance, when abundance is measured in stillness.

No.

What a benevolent and wise power would do is much quieter, and therefore much more real.

It would come to the rim and sit.

Not above. *With.* At the edge, where the seep darkens the rock in vertical stains. It would let its own weight settle the way theirs does, into warm mud beneath cobbles. It would not need to take a shape, but it would, for a while, because to be with them you must have a surface that can bead.

It would become, for a time, one more low, broad, damp shape. Not to trick them. Because that is the shape that can be in this place without disturbing it.

And it would breathe with them.

Not faster, to teach them a new rhythm. Slower, until its breath matched theirs. In. Out. One every twenty, thirty seconds. Condensate gathering and running off.

For an hour. For a year. For as long as it takes for a power that could do anything to remember how to do nothing.

That is the first gift: *being witnessed without being changed.*

The second gift is guardianship, which looks like nothing at all.

We think of protection as intervention. A wise power protects by ensuring that intervention is not needed.

Deep below the basin, where the warmth comes from, the rock is shifting. In a hundred years, a fissure would have cooled by half a degree. In two hundred, the seep would have thinned. In five hundred, a surveyor on the outside would have found the anomaly on a heat map and marked it for drilling.

The power would not move a mountain. It would put its hand — if it had a hand — on the fault and keep it warm. Half a degree retained. It would lay a kind of forgetfulness over the slope, not a wall, but a softening of interest, so that the surveyor's eye slides over it the way your eye slid over the being that you thought was a stone. *Nothing here worth taking.* It would keep the circles of cause and effect that already hold — seep to pool to steam to seep — unbroken.

Not forever, because forever is not a promise a wise power makes. Just for as long as there are still beings who know how to keep a channel open with a soft nail. Which, if they are left alone, will be a very long time.

And the third gift — if it can be called a gift — is that it would leave a small, unnecessary kindness that only they would understand.

It would not leave it *for* them. It would leave it *among* them.

On the underside of one stone, in the warmest basin that is always exactly ankle-deep, it would thicken the film by a fraction of a millimeter. Just there. So that when one of them lifts it, licks it clean with that broad, pale tongue, and sets it back, the taste is a little richer, a little more iron, a little more *yes*. And the stone, when set back, settles a little more perfectly, without the faintest rock.

Or it would make the mineral crust at one joint — an elbow, a knee — take a half day longer to reform, so that for an afternoon the new pink skin underneath can breathe without flaking.

Or it would do nothing tactile at all, and simply let one of the clutches of translucent beads, each no larger than a pea, have two hatchlings instead of one, and both live.

Something so small it could be mistaken for luck. Something so small the basin itself would not register it as change.

Because a wise power knows that to bless a complete thing, you do not add to it. You let it be more deeply itself.

And then it would stand — slowly, so as not to make a splash, just a soft displacement, *shush* — and step back over the rim that is not really a rim. It would not say *you are blessed.* To say it would break the quiet.

If one of them happened to look up with filmed eyes at that moment, it would see only what you saw at first: a shape it took for a stone, and then a breath, and then only stone again.

The water would still be exactly as warm as it has been for longer than hands have existed. The tick… tick… of the seep would still be hitting the pool. The circles would still spread and vanish.

Except now, in a way that cannot be measured, the place would be held from both inside and outside at once. From inside, by the four who scrape and sip and sigh, *eh*. From outside, by a power that has learned, by sitting with them, that the holiest work is tending.

It would not have saved them.

It would have kept them from ever needing to be saved.

And that is enough.
