It would not arrive.

That is the first thing to understand about a benevolent and wise power. It doesn't *arrive*. Arrival is for things that need to displace air, to make a pillar of weather. This place already has weather that doesn't know how to be weather yet.

It would notice.

Which is different. Noticing, for a power like that, is not passive. To be noticed by something that vast and that kind is already to be changed, the way a room changes when someone who loves you looks at it. Not because they moved the furniture, but because their looking lets the room be at rest from trying to be looked at.

It has been looking for a while, I think.

It saw the ceiling first. That low, sweating, iron-red lid that never learned to be a sky. And it did not pity it for not being a sky. The wise part understands that embryonic things should not be rushed into their adulthood. You don't tell a kiln it should have been a cathedral already.

So it wouldn't lift the ceiling.

It wouldn't shatter it and give them stars. Stars would be too much, too fast. It would blind the heat-pits.

What it would do is breathe.

In a place where the heat is trapped and respiratory, where the only circulation is sweat condensing and falling and steaming again - a closed mouth breathing against itself - it would add one cool breath. Not a wind. The world doesn't have wind yet, only convective cells that move like thoughts. It would exhale, once, very gently, across the plain of house-sized blocks.

And for the first time, sound would carry.

Not far. Not loud. Just enough. The endless arrhythmic ticking of condensation striking stone would, for a few minutes, have an echo. A block shifting somewhere out of sight would make a sound that actually arrived where you were. The builders, whose entire language is arrangement because sound has always been swallowed by steam and porous rock, would hear each other move.

They would stop.

Mid-update, mid-reposition. One straddling a channel, one kneeling over a half-quarried brother, one lying on its side pretending to be a wall. They would hear the creak of their own settling, and the creak of another's settling a kilometer away, and they would know they are not alone the way walls know they are not alone. They would be alone the way a chorus is alone together.

The benevolent part wouldn't make them speak. It knows they don't have mouths for that. It would just let the world hold still enough for hearing to be possible.

And then it would do a second thing. Smaller.

It would kneel. Or the equivalent of kneeling for something that doesn't have knees. It would lower its attention until it was the size of a single block. Until it could attend to one joint between two hammer-dressed stones, one white-and-rust fractal of salt bloom, one stone bowl left filled with sterile condensation water.

It would not clean the bloom away. It wouldn't seed the world with forests. That would be a violence, to impose a biosphere on a place that hasn't yet decided it wants to be alive. Instead it would cool one spot.

Just one. A circle perhaps a meter wide, on the warm basalt at the lip of one of those perfectly still, terrible square pits.

It would make the stone there not warm. Cool. Truly cool, the way deep shade is cool, the way a hand on a fevered forehead is cool. And it would keep it cool while everything else stayed hot and sweating.

The bloom there would change.

Not die. Change. In that cool circle, the salt ferns would not need to pull iron so desperately. Condensation would still bead, but it would not hiss when it landed. It would sit. And in that sitting, for the first time, a spore that had been drifting for - oh, a long time, since before the first block was hammered, waiting for a place that wasn't too hot and too restless to land - would land. And it would not be burned off. It would be lichen. Actual lichen. Pale green-gray, flat against the stone, no bigger than a thumbnail.

It would be the first thing in this world that was not trying to become a building. It would simply be. And the builders, whose heat-pits see only differentials of warmth, would see that cool circle like a new kind of dark. They would gather - not quickly, never quickly, but by updating - around it. They would not touch it. They don't know how to touch something so soft without quarrying it. They would just stand around it in their impossible, smearing way and let their own sweating slow.

And the wise power would teach them, without words, the one thing they have never been able to learn because erosion hasn't started yet:

That a thing can be left unfinished and still be good.

It would put its hand - or its attention, which for a power like that is the same thing - on the long low wall that runs for a day's walk and then stops in a clean, hammered face. The wall that is a sentence someone never finished. And it would not finish it. It would appreciate it.

You know how it feels when someone looks at your work - not your best work, your *in-progress* work, the one with the chisel slips and the fiber sling marks still in it - and says, without fixing anything, "This is good. This is good that you made this. You can rest from it for a while."

The builders have been building without rest since the basalt was warm. Because to stop is to become structure. To stop is to be mistaken for ruin. They have never been given permission to pause.

The power would give it. Not as a command. As a field. In that cool circle, geometry would hold still. The right angle would stay a right angle even when you looked away. The distance to the far ziggurat would compress linearly when you walked, like distance is supposed to. Time, which here is shallow and unmeasured, would deepen just there, just enough to make a little pool of *now* that doesn't immediately smear into later.

One of them - the one nearest the pit - would use that permission. It would kneel, or rather, it would *be* kneeling and then decide to remain kneeling, and it would bow its chisel-tipped fingers and its unresolvable head over the cool lichen and just be still. Not building. Not becoming wall. Just attending to a thing smaller than itself that does not need to be built.

The other builders would see that stillness hold. And they would learn that stillness is not death.

And then, because it is benevolent and therefore not sentimental, it would do almost nothing else.

It would not populate the plain. It would not give the builders faces. It would not turn the sweat-rain into sweet rain. It wouldn't answer the question in the square pit.

It would look into the pit.

You remember the pit. The perfectly still water that doesn't show the ceiling, but shows the inverted plain, and the figure on that plain looking down at you. That figure is you, or it is the builder's idea of you, or it is the place's way of asking "who is watching?"

The power would look into that water and it would not flinch. It would let the inverted plain look back at it, and it would let the figure there - which now, for a moment, has the power's own scale - look back. And then it would do the kindest thing any watcher can do for a lonely place that has only ever been watched by itself:

It would let itself be seen, clearly, for one second.

No smearing. No updating. No heat-haze. The builders' heat-pits, the human's eyes, the pit's inverted eyes, would all resolve at once. They would see not a judgment, not a design, not a plan for what this world should become when it grows up.

They would see a kind face, vast and close at once like the ceiling should have been, looking down with absolutely no desire to correct anything. A face that finds the hammer marks beautiful *because* they are still sharp. That finds the unfinished ziggurats beautiful *because* they stopped. That has no blueprint.

And then it would look away. On purpose. To give them privacy again.

When it withdrew - not leaving, because withdrawing is different than leaving; withdrawing is still being there but making room - the ceiling would still be low and red, the heat would still be trapped, the condensation would still fall slow as through oil. Most of the plain would still smear when you weren't looking. Most of the blocks would still shift.

But that one cool circle would remain. For a long time. Long enough for the lichen to double in size. Long enough for one builder to choose to remain kneeling there, becoming not a wall but a guardian of a small coolness. Long enough for erosion, when it finally does begin, to begin *there* first - softening one edge, learning how to soften on something that was loved before it was weathered.

And the water in that one pit, closest to the cool circle, would ripple. Just once. A real ripple, from a real drip, that did not dent like a membrane but broke and spread in circles, showing for an instant not the inverted plain, but the actual ceiling, and the actual steam, and the actual, tired, beautiful, half-made world as it is.

If you were still standing there, hot and small and too quick, it would notice you too. It wouldn't take you away. It wouldn't make you a builder or make the builders human. It would just make sure that when you looked down at the bloom at your feet and looked up again, the distance didn't lie to you for a moment, and you could see exactly how far you had come, and that it was enough.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does with an embryonic world that sweats and hammers and cannot yet stay still.

It doesn't finish it.

It sits with it on the warm floor, cools one stone with its palm, and says, "Good. Keep going. Rest when you need to. I will remember where you are, even when you move."

And that, in a place where nothing has ever been remembered long enough to be allowed to change, is everything.
