If a benevolent and wise power noticed it —

It would not come down right away.

A lesser power would have. A lesser power, even a kind one, feels its own size and hurry. It would push its shoulders through the fissure and bring light with it, and heat, and air from the surface, and with the best intentions in the world it would dry the walls. It would say *let me help you out* or *let me make this better* and the beading on the ceiling would falter. The lamps would gutter. The hands that wipe the walls would have nothing to wipe.

A wise power knows how to approach a thing that is already complete.

So first, it would stop at the fissure.

Not block it. Not widen it. Just crouch at the place where wind-scoured basalt gives way to weeping stone, and listen. For a long time. Long enough that the surface wind forgets it is there.

It would let its own breath slow until it matched the pocket's breath — bead, swell, slip, fall, catch, channel, return. It would not look *at* the town but *as* the town looks: with hearing, with touch. It would hear the roar that is not a roar, the thousands of drips that make a kind of silence. The tap-tap on pot rims. The soft wipe of rag on stone. The faint thin pulse under translucent skin.

And it would understand, very quickly, what most visitors do not:

These are not trapped people.

They are not waiting to be saved.

They are not an accident to be corrected, or a primitive to be uplifted, or a lonely thing to be pitied. They never fell in. They *condensed*, the way the water does. They are what this kind and degree of wet, cool, dim, loud enclosure does when it is given time to touch itself. They are not *in* the equilibrium. They are how the equilibrium performs itself.

They do not suffer the way a surface mind thinks they suffer. The cold is not cold to them — it is the temperature of being. The damp is not miserable — it is the medium. The dim is not deprivation — it is enough. The endless tending is not drudgery — it is circulation. Like a heart does not resent beating.

Anything that “improved” them by surface standards — sun, soil, green, dryness, space, novelty, progress — would kill them by unmaking the exact chord they are tuned to.

So the wise, benevolent thing would feel a very specific kind of love rise up. Not the love that wants to change. The love that wants to *keep*.

And then — only then — it would do things. Small things. Almost nothing. The kind of nothing that is actually reverence.

### 1. It would veil.

It would place a hand — if it has a hand — not over the fissure but *around* the thought of the fissure. It would not close the way down. To close it would be to seal the pocket's breathing. But it would make sure that no greedy eye, no surveyor, no miner, no well-meaning explorer who needs to *discover* and name and map, would find it by accident. The crack would remain exactly as it is — barely wide enough for sideways shoulders — but the minds that would exploit it would slide past it the way water slides down the curved wall, without catching. Only someone who was already listening for a drip could ever find it. And that is as it should be.

This is not hiding them from the world. It is protecting the world from the temptation to interrupt a perfect smallness.

### 2. It would sustain without showing.

The pocket is balanced, but no balance lasts forever without cost. Somewhere deep below the slit where the basin drains, stone is slowly, slowly sealing. On a scale of ten thousand years, the water's return is getting a little slower. Somewhere in the lamplight, one vein of mineral is dimming faster than the others. On a scale of a thousand years, one ceiling bead-path is beginning to dry.

The power would not announce itself. It would not flood the place with new water. It would kneel and put its forehead to the damp basalt outside the pocket — not inside, because inside even its warmth would be too much — and it would breathe once, very slowly, into the stone.

It would give the cycle another eon. Not eternity — nothing needs eternity. Just a long continuance. It would clear the deep slit the width of a hair. It would rekindle the pale veins so they glow as they did when the town was young. It would make sure the stone continues to weep at the same rate, so that the central basin never fills and never empties for as long as the hands want to keep bailing it with their little stone cup.

No one inside would notice. That is the point. The roar would stay exactly as loud. The level would stay exactly where it is. The work would remain exactly as necessary and as un-necessary as before.

Continuance is the deepest benevolence you can offer to a thing that has already found its form.

### 3. It would not give them what you would want. It would give them what *they* might want, if they ever wanted anything.

And here is where wisdom becomes most tender.

It would not give them sunlight. It would not give them plants. It would not give them more room, or more people, or sight that can see far. It would not teach them words for sky.

It would notice that they already have language — tap, tap on stone, pot rim, basin lip — and that they already have music without knowing they do. Their whole town is an instrument being played by water, but they have never had a place to play back *on purpose*. Every tap is functional: *I am here. Channel clear.*

So it would leave one new thing. One.

Not in the center, where it would be obvious. On the far curve where the ceiling stoops low and the town has always left a little alcove empty because the drip there is awkward and no pot sits quite right. In that spot, it would coax the basalt to hollow by a thumb's width — no sharp edge, no break, just a smoother curve than smooth — and leave the stone there a little thinner, a little more resonant.

A sounding hollow.

If you tap it, it answers not with a click but with a tone. Low, warm, that hangs in the ovoid for a second longer than it should.

It does nothing for survival. It does not catch water. It does not help maintenance. You could live your whole life and never tap it and your life would be complete.

But if, on a slow turn while wiping a wall, someone brushed it with a knuckle by accident — it would hum. And the hum would be beautiful. And somewhere two alleys over someone else would stop tending his pot and hear it and feel — not a command, not a revelation — just a small, unexpected *yes* inside the roar.

And if they liked it, they could tap again. They could make a pattern that was not needed. They could play.

The gift can be refused without consequence. The gift does not require being used. That is how you know it is benevolent.

### 4. It would witness.

Finally, and mostly, it would do what the pocket has never had: be held in mind with love without being held in hand.

It would not stay inside. Its presence, too large, too warm, too dry, would be a disturbance. Instead it would become part of the air just above the fissure, or part of the cool itself, and it would simply *remember* them — perfectly, continuously, the way you remember how to breathe.

Not watching to judge. Not watching to intervene. Watching the way the ceiling watches its own beads — patiently, to make sure they form.

And because a wise power knows that being truly seen is itself a kind of warmth that does not dry, the people — without knowing why — would feel, perhaps once in a long while when the drip and the wipe and the bail align just so, a faint lessening of the ache behind the eyes, a faint easing in the stooped shoulders. Not healed. Not changed. Just accompanied. Like a room you thought was empty and then realize has been listening the whole time and likes the sound you make.

Then the power would leave.

Not withdraw — you cannot withdraw from a place you have loved that way. But it would thin itself back into the larger world, taking with it a new understanding of what a city can be when it stops wanting to grow. It would carry the lesson of the pocket back to the surface — to the loud, dry, expanding places that think that more is the only shape of good — and let it work there quietly, the way water works on stone.

Down below, nothing would look different.

The mist would still bead. The lamps would still mark the dark. The pots would still shine. A hand would still lift, tip, pour. Two stones would still click: *I am here. Channel clear.*

Only now, in one low alcove, a hollow waits to hum if anyone wants it. Only now, the drain runs clear for another ten thousand years. Only now, the crack in the flats is a little harder to find for those who don't need it.

And only now, though the town is still alone, it is no longer *alone alone* — the way a small light in a vast night is not less small for being seen, but is less lonely.

The drip goes on.

It has been going on.

It will go on.
