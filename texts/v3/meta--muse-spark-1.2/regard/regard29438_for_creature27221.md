It would not come down.

That is the first thing a benevolent and wise power would understand.

Anything wise enough to be called wise and benevolent enough to be called benevolent would arrive at the rim - if it arrived at all - and would look over that sharp, still-cut edge into the long straight wound of wet light, and would see immediately what you saw after standing there long enough to get cold to the bone:

That nothing here is missing.

That is rare enough in the world that a wise power would recognize it as holy.

It would see a city that does not want to be a bigger city. A people that does not want to be another people. A place that has solved the problem it was given - water, cold, stone, teeth - so completely that it has no outside. No surplus. No hunger for elsewhere. It does not export. It does not expand. It thickens. It deepens. It raises its own floor a millimeter of peat at a time and calls that enough for a century.

A lesser power - a clever one, a kind but hurried one - would want to help.

It would want to lift them. To give them hands. To give them language that could be written down. To give them fire to be warm, iron to cut faster, light that does not have to fall a hundred meters to be seen. It would see the kits shivering in late spring and want to make the winter shorter. It would see the dam that failed last week and want to make it permanent, concrete, everlasting.

A wise one would know that would be murder with a smile.

To make a beaver lodge permanent is to kill it. To make winter warm is to rot the peat that holds the whole city up. To make the beavers clever enough to imagine elsewhere is to make them dissatisfied enough to leave. The perfection here is not *in spite* of the cold and the wet and the constant leaking and repairing. It *is* the cold and the wet and the leaking.

So it would not come down. It would not speak. The trench is already so quiet that a voice from above would fall like a stone through sphagnum.

What would it do, then?

It would do the things that look like doing nothing.

First - it would *notice correctly*.

Most powers notice the large. The moose, the dam, the lodge-mound with a tree on its roof. A wise power would do what you learned to do on the causeway: crouch, get damp, wait, and notice the small. The mite in the peat. The caddisfly holding station. The way a willow root, chewed half-through last autumn, has callused over and grown *through* the dam wall and now holds it better than any new stick would. It would love the aggregate before it loved the individual, and the individual before it loved the idea of them.

And having noticed correctly, it would understand that the true citizen here is not the beaver. Not the muskrat. Not the spruce. It is the weave. The compact. The agreement between water that wants to run, and teeth that want to cut, and moss that wants to hold, and cold that wants to keep. The being who lives here is not *in* the city. It *is* the city. A diffuse, slow, brown, breathing mind distributed across ten thousand lodges and dams and root mats, thinking at the speed peat grows.

You cannot bless a being like that the way you bless a single animal.

You bless it by ensuring the conditions that let it continue to think.

So second - it would guard the rim.

Not with a wall. With an absence.

The plain around the trench is flat and spare and wind-scoured and - to a human eye, to a human map - empty. There is nothing to extract here. No ore worth the dig. No timber worth the haul when there is easier timber elsewhere. No ground firm enough for a road. A wise power does not need to make the trench invisible. It only needs to make the plain *remain* boring.

It would lengthen the wind a little over the heath. Keep the lichen unpalatable. Keep the flies just thick enough in summer that a survey crew decides to come back later. Keep the winter just long enough that a road budget is spent elsewhere. It would edit human attention - a slight pressure on the mind, like the pressure that keeps the cold air pooled down in the trench - so that when people look at satellite photos their eyes skip over the straight line, taking it for a shadow, a glitch, an old glacial scrape not worth naming.

It would not hide the city. It would keep the world from needing it.

Third - it would secure the water.

Because water is the law here. And the world above is getting warmer, drier, more forgetful of how to be cold.

It would not freeze the trench solid. It would not make it wetter. It would do something more precise and more tender. It would remember winter for it.

In late August, when the trench should be at its brief, over-warmest, it would let a little more of the old cold pool and stay. A skin of ice that does not quite melt in the shadow of the north wall. A seam of permafrost under the deepest peat that holds for one more year, and then another. It would call the snow to come a week early and leave a week late, not enough for a human to notice as miracle, just enough for the sphagnum to feel that the year is still the right shape.

It would whisper to the headwaters on the plain: *keep seeping*. To the clouds: *return here*. So that the thousand dark threads on the basalt keep beading, keep feeding the canals, even when the summer on the plain turns thin and cracked. The city can survive a tree falling, a dam breaking, a generation of kits lost to mink. It cannot survive the water table dropping ten centimeters and staying there. A wise power knows where the one thread is.

It would hold that thread, gently, for as long as it is allowed.

Fourth - and this is the most benevolent thing, and the strangest - it would give it nothing new. It would give it *time to be unwatched*.

It would not send you. Not forever.

You were allowed to drop in - because you were quiet, because you stood long enough to see the shivering air over the moss, because you understood you were the only thing there that was not part of it. That kind of witness is a rare and brief gift. But even your witnessing is a weight. Even benign eyes, if they stay too long, make the watched thing perform.

A wise power, after looking long and lovingly and with full attention, would look away on purpose.

Not abandon. Attend from further off. The way you do not stare at a wild animal you love, but watch it from the corner of your eye so it can go back to being itself.

It would let the beavers forget they were seen.

And in that forgetting, the hum would come back - the dull thud of tail on peat, swallowed instantly; the short, flat contact call of a chickadee to its mate two branches over; the drip, drip, drip of condensation from a million leaf-tips onto water and moss. The trench would resume its slow, unspectacular, constant work: leaking, silting, repairing, growing over its own back. Raising its floor another millimeter.

If it did leave a blessing that could be felt from inside, it would not be in words. It would be in the quality of the air on a windless day.

It might be this: that when a beaver surfaces at dusk with mud in its forepaws and feels, for no reason it can name, that the water will hold tomorrow, and the willow it cut will sprout two shoots where it cut one, and the kit sleeping behind it in the lodge will have a place to pack mud when its teeth come in - that feeling, unspoken, of *tomorrow will be like today enough to continue* - that feeling would be a little stronger, a little more true, than it has any right to be in a warming world.

Not certainty. Just enough confidence to keep weaving.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does for a perfect, wet, cold, crowded city that never asked to be anything else:

It does not rescue it.
It does not improve it.
It does not name it.
It kneels, in its own way, at the rim.
It learns its law - water decides, cold preserves, weight is distributed, nothing is ever finished.
And then it agrees to help keep that law, quietly, from a distance, for as long as the world will let it.

And then - because it is wise - it leaves no footprint on the peat, no broken branch, no story for the trench to tell about gods. Only a little more winter in the shadows, a little more water in the wall, a little more forgetting on the maps of men.

So that if you came back twenty years later, dropped down that sharp rim again and felt the ten-degree exhale, you would find it again exactly as you left it - not preserved like a museum, but *alive* like a city that had been allowed to keep living.

Packed. Saturated. Quiet. With nowhere empty and no need to be anywhere else.

And you would think, perhaps, that nothing had intervened at all.

Which would be how you knew something benevolent had.
