If a benevolent and wise Power noticed that place, it would not come like weather.

Not like anything that lifts or cuts.

The mesa was lifted once. That was enough violence for ten thousand years. A wise Power would know that.

It would not arrive. It would *attend*. Which is different.

You would not see it come over the lip the way you did, lungs rinsing thin. You would notice, after a morning of that ceaseless ticking from leaf to leaf, that the ticking had been heard.

For a long time, nothing else would happen.

Because the first thing a wise benevolence does when it sees something embryonic and intact is *nothing that interrupts embryogenesis.*

---

### What It Would See

It would see exactly what you saw, but without the reflex to correct it.

It would see a mountain that was born yesterday, still showing the cooling cracks of its birth, still beaded with the afterbirth of cloud.

It would see a cloud-forest that cheated — that came before soil was ready, because the water had nowhere else to go.

It would see a town that is a promise kept early. Right-angled and lime-mortared and slate-sharp, with drains made for a future monsoon that hasn't been invented yet. With two hundred empty rooms swept clean.

It would see thirty people who are not lost, not stranded, not failed. Thirty people who are *exactly* on time. Broad-chested, red-cheeked, damp-handed. Breathing at the speed stone allows. Building a wall block by block as an act of conversation with the basalt, not as an act of defense. Keeping empty houses the way you keep a cradle dusted before the child is born.

And it would understand the pact they have made without ever speaking it:

*We will live at the tempo of geology until biology catches up.*

Most powers — even kind ones — cannot stand that tempo. They want to help by hurrying. Give them lungs that don't wheeze. Give them iron tools. Give them more children, quickly. Give them a river. Fill the houses.

A wise Power would feel that urge like a tide in its own chest and let it pass. Because it would know: to fill this place too fast would be to kill it. The moss would be scraped off for fields. The cistern would be capped and piped. The amphitheater would be stuffed with noise until it could no longer ring. The children would forget the note each column makes when you tap it.

The emptiness is not a wound. It is a capacity. It is the mesa's faith that there will be enough time.

So the first act of the Power would be restraint.

And the second act would be love for the restraint itself.

It would think: *Good. You are waiting well. Let me keep you waiting well.*

---

### What It Would Not Do

It would not build the north terrace for them.

It would not send a hundred more people up the switchbacks in a season. It knows the lowlands are warm and hazy for a reason — they are not ready to climb without wanting to own.

It would not give them a river. Drainage is coming. In ten thousand years those braided sheets going over the edge will have learned to cut a canyon. To hurry that would be to cut their throats.

It would not cure the damp in their joints or the ache that comes early. The damp is the price of the green, and they have paid it willingly.

It would not answer the question none of them are anxious enough to ask.

It would not be seen. To be seen at that altitude would be to be worshipped, and worship at that scale would be too loud for the place that lives by drips.

---

### What It Would Do

It would do small things that are almost nothing, which is the signature of any real benevolence at this scale. Things that look, after, like they could have happened anyway.

**1. It would steady the plate.**

Once a winter you feel it — that faint creak through the soles of your feet as the whole new mesa settles a millimeter deeper. The Power would place a hand that is not a hand under that settlement. Not to stop it. To *ease* it.

It would say without language: *Settle slow. Don't jolt. You are holding a bowl of water and moss and thirty people. Do not spill.*

And the stone, which is still young enough to listen, would listen. The creak would become rarer, softer. The fitted blocks of the eastern wall would not shear in a frost heave that would have undone three months of tapping. The children sleeping on the lower floors would not wake to a crack that they could not name. The mesa would hold for a long, long time — long enough for the lichen to do its work, for the almost-soil to become soil, for the empty rooms to be needed.

You would not notice. You would only notice, years later, that no house had ever fallen.

**2. It would breathe with them.**

It cannot give them new lungs without making them something else. But it can give the air itself a little more kindness.

On the coldest mornings when the cloud sits heaviest and every inhale is work, the Power would thin the mist just enough — not to clear it, because the moss needs it — but to let a shaft of white high-mountain sun fall directly into the plaza for an hour longer. The light would warm the saturated wool on their backs. The water on the rowan leaves would flash to glass. The wheeze on the inhale would be, for that hour, a little less.

And in the night, when thirty people sleep shallow and close under felt because alder burns cool and sour, it would keep the wind from scouring the plaza quite so hard. It would break it at the lip, so the shutters rattle less and the drips can be heard more clearly. So the town's breathing is not drowned out.

The old woman who cards wool on the plank walkway would think: *Good air today.* And that would be the whole miracle.

**3. It would sanctify the empty houses.**

This is the most important one, because this is the one only a wise Power would think to do.

A lesser kindness would try to fill the houses. A wise one knows the town's holiness is in its keeping of them *empty*.

So it would go, as attention goes, from door to door that is left ajar. The houses with one mat, one fire-bowl, one carving on the shutter. The floors swept daily by someone who does not live there. It would not leave a mark. It would leave a *taste* in the air — the faintest difference you feel when you enter a room that has just been prayed in, even if you do not pray.

The moss that creeps over the sill would be wrung out, and would not return for a week, as if it respected the threshold. The slate shingles, still sharp enough to cut, would not cut the bare feet of the four or five children who run through on their way to tap the cliff edge. The fire-bowl in the empty house would be dry even on the wettest day, so that if someone needed to shelter there, they could.

And in each empty house, on the lime-washed wall opposite the door, the condensation would not bead randomly. For a few minutes after sunrise, when the light comes straight and white through the perpetual haze, it would bead in a pattern so faint you would think you imagined it — a net, a grid, like the street plan of the town itself, but larger. As if the house were dreaming of the people who will one day fill it, without demanding they arrive now.

The thirty would not call this a visitation. They would call it, if they called it anything, "the houses staying well."

And because they are people who speak softly and wait until they are close to talk, they would not make a story about it that grows louder each telling. One of the weaving women would mention to the Wall Crew that the north row house stayed dry. Someone would nod. They would keep sweeping.

**4. It would tell the lowlands: *Not yet.***

This is invisible work and therefore the kindest.

Down below, where the forest is warm and wants things, there will be men with maps who see a flat, watered crown above the tree line and think: quarry, fortress, resort, mine. A benevolent Power knows that every embryonic place is most vulnerable to being named a resource before it has finished becoming itself.

So it would not strike those men down. It would not frighten them. It would just make the switchback trail, for a few crucial years, a little less findable. A fallen alder across it at the exact right bend. A season of midges a little thicker at the base. A sense, if you stand at the bottom and squint up at the sheer ninety-degree lip, that the climb is not worth it, that there is probably nothing up there anyway.

Not forever. Just until the mesa and its people are less tender. Until the moss has made soil an inch deeper. Until there are sixty people instead of thirty, and the granary on stilts is half full instead of a quarter, and the children who know how to lie on their bellies and watch water learn how to fall have grown and can speak for the place in the lowland tongue if they need to.

A wise Power knows that the best protection is not a wall around a sacred place, but a little more time for the sacred place to become strong enough that it doesn't need a wall.

---

After all that, it would still be there — not as a presence, but as an attention.

If you stayed long enough to be offered that bitter herb tea — alder-smoked water in a chipped cup — and asked one of them, "Do you feel watched over?" they would look puzzled.

They would think you meant the raven. And they would say, "Sometimes the raven stays now. It used to just cross. Now sometimes it lands on the cistern and drinks, and does not startle when the children come."

That would be it. That would be the sign the Power had come and had done its work and had left without ever having left.

The raven staying.

The water still ticking.

The town still exactly too large, exactly too empty, exactly right.

And the Power — whatever you want to call it — would keep attending, the way you attend to a candle in a drafty room: not by holding it, but by cupping the air around it, and breathing very, very slowly, so the flame can learn, in its own time, how to burn steady.
