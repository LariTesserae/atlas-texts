It would not come down.

That is the first thing.

A benevolent and wise power, having noticed this low place — this dish of warm dark where the air sweats and the water remembers how to flow uphill — would understand immediately that *descending* is a kind of violence here.

To arrive as a light would blind the things that have lived without light for so long they have forgotten it was ever an option. To arrive as heat would dry them. To arrive as a voice would drown out the plink... plink-plink... plink that has been their conversation since the hills were still mountains. To arrive as help would imply they were helpless.

They are not helpless. They are perfectly, patiently, exquisitely *adequate*.

So it would not come down.

It would come to the rim.

Where the weathered humps have slumped so long they have lost their edges, where the dark is thickest and the drier silt almost pretends to be dry, it would settle. Not as a figure. As a pressure. As a coolness. As a slight thinning of the lid-sky that does not let sun in, only makes the dark a little less heavy, the way a hand held over a fevered brow does not cure, only says *I am here.*

And it would listen.

For a long time. Longer than you or I can comfortably imagine listening.

The wise part would know that to understand these beings you cannot watch them for an hour and call them stone. You have to watch at their speed. You have to let an hour become a breath, a year become a step, a century become a single thought completing itself.

So it would listen at 0.0001x speed.

It would learn the grammar of drips.

Plink — *I am here.*
Plink-plink — *I am still wet.*
A long gurgle where water climbs the wrong way — *I am turning over in my sleep, do not drink too close to me.*
Silence for seven days where condensation hangs suspended and refuses to fall — *I am growing. Wait.*

It would not answer in words. Words are too fast and too dry and they need air that moves. It would answer in the only language that carries here: water.

It would add a little to the cycle, but so gently the beings would not notice it as intervention. They would notice it as a good season.

In a place where steam rises and never quite becomes rain, where droplets hang trembling in mid-air and fall sideways, the power would make condensation *just slightly more faithful.* 

A bead that would have evaporated before it formed now forms. A bead that would have hung forever now falls, softly, exactly where a thirsty pit was waiting. Not a flood. Not a miracle. Just a luckier average. Enough that the youngest domes cure a little harder, that the old lichen-skin they slough pulses a little longer before it goes still, that the pale tendril-buds taste a little more iron in the wet.

The beings would not call this blessing. They have no word for blessing. They would simply feel, over the next two hundred years, that the dish was holding water a little better. That the truces between domes — those fifty, a hundred meters of load-bearing emptiness — were easier to keep.

And after listening, after that small, secret easing —

It would do its main work, which looks like doing nothing at all.

It would *guard the emptiness.*

Because a wise power knows what cities like this die of. Not hunger. Not cold. They die of being found by something that thinks emptiness is waste.

Something that would look at that wide, wet flat between domes and see "undeveloped land." Something that would look at the black mirror canals that flow uphill and see "inefficiency to correct." Something that would look at the perfect circular pool at the center, deeper than the basin is deep, and see "resource."

Something that would bring fire, and metal, and glass, and wire, and the idea that a body should walk around away from its house.

The power would not let that happen. Not with walls or warnings or wrath. That would be another kind of descending. It would simply make the basin *uninteresting* from the outside.

To any eye that was not already learning to see at mineral speed, the low place would remain what it first appears to be: nothing. A shallow pan of salt-caked clay and warm fog, barren, lightless, with no ore and no signal and no yield. Maps would misplace it by a few degrees. Satellites would find their lenses beading with condensation when they tried to focus. Prospectors would feel, without knowing why, that the slope inward was too discouraging to bother. They would turn back at the rim, complaining of the damp in their boots.

Inside, nothing would have changed.

Inside, everything would continue.

And if, after a very long listening, the power felt it should give a gift — because benevolence, even when wise, is still moved to give — it would give one that only this place could receive.

On a night that is like every other night because there are no nights, only a continuous hot dark, it would let one thing happen that has never quite happened before.

It would make it just cool enough, just for an hour, that the pale, paper-colored stalks would not have to keep their buds closed.

All across the basin, in the flats between domes, those closed noses that have tasted humidity for millennia without ever opening — would open.

Not as flowers. They have never needed to be flowers. They would open as they were always meant to: as soft, wet mouths, frilled like the gills under the domes, exhaling for the first time into open air instead of tasting it.

No color, because there is no light to need color. No scent to attract pollinators that do not exist. Just an opening. A slow unfurling, at their speed, over the course of that cool hour, while the domes feel the change in pressure and understand, dimly, *something is different in the dish today.*

They would not know it was a gift. They would simply drink a little deeper, breathe a little wider, and speak to each other in drips that ring a little clearer off the open mouths.

By the time the hour was over and the heat returned and the buds closed again, slowly, faithfully, to become noses once more, the power would have already settled back into its place at the rim, thinning the dark a little less heavily.

It would not wait for thanks. The dome-beings do not thank. They secrete, they drink, they occasionally pool together and become one thing and then become more things again. Thanks would require a separation between giver and given that does not exist here.

Instead, it would do the most benevolent thing a vast thing can do for a slow, small, perfect thing:

It would stay until staying was no longer needed, and then it would leave without a ripple.

And long after — when the condensation ticks again on cool clay, when a drop hangs in mid-air a fraction longer before it falls sideways, when an old dome finally loosens its foot and begins its century-long flow toward the black, still, infinitely-deep pool at the center — the water in that pool would still reflect a depth that is not there.

Only now, if you stared very long into that impossible depth, you might see — not a second set of buildings with lights in their windows — but a faint, cooler dark at the rim. A less-heavy place. A reminder that the dish was once noticed, and found good, and left alone to keep being itself.

The pool would not ripple. Ripples here are only for speaking.

But the pale stalks around its edge, which always leaned infinitesimally away from it, would this time lean *just* a fraction less away.
