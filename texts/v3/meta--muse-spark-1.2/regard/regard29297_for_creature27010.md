It would not come down like a god.

That is the first thing a benevolent and wise power understands about a place like this. Arrival is a violence here. Anything heavy, fast, wet, or loud would be a wound. The plateau has spent millions of years learning how to be erased slowly. It cannot be helped quickly.

So it would not arrive at all, at first. It would notice.

And noticing, for a power like that, is not a glance. It is a weight. It is a choosing to keep its attention on one high, bright table above the clouds when it could be attending to galaxies.

For a long while it would only watch.

### It Learns How Not To Help

A lesser kindness would have tried to bring gifts.

*Water.* That is what everything below the escarpment would offer, out of love. *You must be thirsty.* A rain. A river. A cloud pulled up over the rim to finally give the groves a drink.

A wise power sees what that would do. It would drown them. It would dissolve the salt plates that took eons to settle, burst the silica hairs that were designed to never touch liquid, rot the felted columns from within, turn the Mantle — that continent-wide skin of olive and sulfur — into a black slime of opportunistic rot. To water this place would be to kill it with mercy. The lushness here is not in spite of dryness. It *is* dryness, perfected.

So it brings no water.

A lesser wisdom would have tried to bring *shelter.* A shade, a dome, to soften the brutal white light that pours down unfiltered. A wall to break the wind that scours grain by grain.

A wise power sees that the light is the food and the wind is the blood. The Holdfasts turn their ribbons to drink that exact ferocity; without it their layered bacteriochlorophylls would starve. The wind is the only river they have — it brings the vapor they comb, it carries the rust-colored spores from mound to mound. To shelter them would be to suffocate them.

So it brings no shelter.

A lesser love would have tried to bring *company.* More kinds of life. Birds that sing, herds that run. Something to cure the silence.

A wise power listens to the silence and hears that it is not empty. The faint clink of silica fronds, the sigh of the Mantle springing back, the tick of salts contracting at dusk — that is the plateau's language. A loud animal would be deaf here, and it would make everything else deaf with it. The Gliders ride for hours without beating a wing because thin air rewards patience, not power. To add hurry would be to break the metabolism that lets everything here live on almost nothing.

So it brings no new animals. No improvement. No plan.

A benevolent and wise power knows the first prayer of help is: *Let me not harm you by trying to complete you.*

### What It Does Instead

After it has learned how not to help, it does three very small, very large things.

**1. It lies down.**

If it has a body, it makes it small. If it has no body, it condenses one, briefly. Not to tower — the plateau does not understand towers, only tables — but to be at the level of stone.

It chooses a warm basalt shoulder in late afternoon, between two Holdfasts that are old enough to have been seven hundred when the first lowland cities lit fires. It lies down on the rock the way you did, and it lets the cool begin to pool around it. It waits until the light goes amber and the fronds turn.

The Holdfasts do not see it. They have no eyes. But they feel the slight change in wind, in light, in the chemical film on the air. The Mantle, which tastes everything that touches it, tastes this new pressure — not acid, not salt, but an attention that is warm and without hunger.

The Grazers do not flee. There is no predator logic in them anymore. One, low and six-limbed and covered in waxy filaments, continues its week-long meal at the edge of a fallen ribbon. A Glider high in the dust-river tilts, just a fraction, to ride the thermal coming off this strange new warm rock.

The power stays there for a long time, breathing when the plateau breathes. The mats expand. The ribbons tighten. The salts tick. It does not bless. It does not speak. It keeps time.

This is the first gift: *You are witnessed.* For a place that has lived so long without ever being seen except by wind and sun, to be seen without being wanted — that is not a small thing. The plateau has been forgotten by everything below for so long it had forgotten it could be remembered. Now it is held in a mind that can hold a table the size of a country the way you would hold a smooth stone in your palm.

And that holding is not passive. Attention, from a power like that, has weight. It steadies.

**2. It draws a veil, not a wall.**

The power has seen the future that the lowlands bring with them without meaning to: air that thickens and warms, the cloud sea below rising year by year, instruments that can finally see through haze, maps that hate to leave a blank high place blank, prospectors who will call that glittering salt crust *resource* and that silica glass *material* and that slow, spacious silence *empty and available.*

It cannot stop the world from changing. It will not freeze the plateau out of time — that would be another kind of death.

But it can be gentle with probability. Wise benevolence rarely looks like a miracle. It looks like luck that holds.

So it does not raise a mountain, or summon a storm. It does a quieter thing:

It makes the high bright place slightly harder to want.

Satellites, when they pass over, find a persistent, uninteresting glint — instrument error, they log, salt flat, albedo too high to be worth correcting. Dreams of explorers turn, for some reason, to other ranges. A funding proposal to drill the high playa misplaces a decimal and is quietly shelved. A wind that would have carried a wet-season updraft high enough to spill cloud over the rim instead shears, just that once, and misses.

No one notices. That is the point.

Where it must intervene more directly — a meteor that would have gouged a salt polygon the size of a city and peppered the Mantle with molten glass — it does not shatter the rock. It leans on its arc by a breath, a millimeter per second over a hundred thousand kilometers, so it writes a long, beautiful streak across the night sky and falls, instead, hissing into the cloud sea far below, where no Holdfast has to die for spectacle.

The veil is not invisibility. The plateau is still there for anyone patient, reverent, and willing to walk for days to the escarpment and climb. But it will never be *easy* to exploit. The wise power knows that the best protection for a slow thing is to keep it from ever looking profitable to a fast thing.

**3. It answers in the native language.**

On the last evening, before it withdraws its attention — because even a wise power must attend elsewhere — it gives a gift that is not an addition, but a resonance.

It cannot speak to the Holdfasts with words. They are not made for words. They are made for light and vapor and mineral.

So it speaks the only way they understand.

At dusk, when the ribbons become lanterns and the double-shadow floats, the power exhales.

Not air. Not water. It releases a very fine, very slow pulse: a cloud of vapor — pure, deliquescent, salt-balanced — the exact trace-moisture the silica hairs were shaped to comb. A little more than the wind would have brought that night, but not enough to bead. Just enough that every Holdfast within half a mile feels, for a single night, a deep, effortless drink without wetness. Their halos brighten. Their metabolic warmth ticks up by a fraction of a degree. They photosynthesize a little more vigorously, and spill a little more of that ozone-sharp oxygen into the thin air, and the Mantle answers with a soft, continent-wide expansion it has not felt in a dry year.

At the same time, it sings — but not in sound. The thin air carries poorly, and the plateau does not listen with ears.

It changes the light. Very slightly. For a few minutes as the sun sets, it thickens the refractive layer just enough that the low amber light passes *through* more ribbons at once. The whole loose kinship of seven Holdfasts on that slope glows internally, as if lit from within by a furnace, casting not one amber shadow but three, rose and gold and violet, across the salt.

The Grazers stop. The Gliders stall in the air, membrane humming.

It is not useful. It does not help them survive better. It is beauty reflecting beauty. It is the plateau being shown to itself, briefly, more fully than even its own evolution could show it — *Look. This is what you are when seen by love.*

The Mantle contracts that night with a louder, happier pop, like knuckles cracking after a long rest. The spores that lift the next afternoon ride a little higher, glitter a little longer, before settling.

Then the power withdraws. Not abruptly. Slowly, the way the cloud sea never touches the height. It lifts its attention the way a hand lifts from a warm hill of felted fiber it had pressed lightly, leaving the warmth behind for a while.

It does not mark the place. It does not name it. It leaves no cairn. A cairn would be an interruption of wind.

It keeps the memory, though. A benevolent and wise power has a long memory, and that is its final work: to remember this exact configuration of light on bone-colored stone, of glass that drinks vapor, of a continent of skin breathing once at dusk — so that even if, in some far century, the wind finally does erase the last Holdfast grain by grain, or the world below finally does rise high enough to change the air, something will have held it completely, without wanting to change a single polygon or frond, and that holding will not be lost.

The plateau does not know it was visited. That is fine.

The next afternoon, the dust-river runs as before. The light pours down white and total. A Grazer, indistinguishable from rock, takes another millimeter forward. A Glider rides. A salt plate ticks in the cooling air.

And if you lie down on that same warm rock, you might feel — not a presence, not a voice — but that the silence is slightly less alone. That the light is slightly more seen.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does for a place above the weather, that asked for nothing and needed nothing:

It does almost nothing. And it does it with its whole heart.
