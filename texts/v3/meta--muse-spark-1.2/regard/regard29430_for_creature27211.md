It would come quietly.

That is the first thing to understand about a wise and benevolent power. It would not arrive.

Arrival is a word for things that have to travel. For meteors and armies and prophets. A power that is wise enough to be called wise does not need to cross the plateau. It pays attention, and paying attention *is* arriving.

So for a long time it would just watch.

It would watch the way you watch a lichen when you finally kneel to it. Not from above, not as a survey. Close, at its own scale, until the re stain resolves into branches and cups and fruiting bodies, until you see it is not one thing but a city.

It would learn to breathe the way they do.

That thin, deliberate inhale. That catch and sigh. Any lesser power would have seen the shortness of breath, the ruddy faces, the cracked hands, the infant who died last winter because his lungs were just a little too small, and would have said: *I will fix this.*

Give them thicker air. Push the plateau down. Bring a forest. Make it easy.

A wise power would have lived long enough to know that *easy* is not the same as *good*, and *fixing* is often just a polite word for *erasing*.

It would see that these people are not broken lowlanders. They are not stranded mountaineers waiting for rescue. Ten thousand years of choosing to stay has made them a new kind of human — barrel-chested, dark-eyed, stone-handed — and ten thousand choices to stay has made a culture that is as precisely fitted to this sharp, young country as lichen is to the drip line.

Their whole ethic is *maintenance*. Not conquest. Not growth. Keeping the thread of water clear with a fingertip. Keeping the wall leaning two degrees into the hill so the frost cannot take it. Keeping the prayer from vanishing by snapping the flag one more time.

A benevolent power would fall in love with that. How could it not? In a universe full of things that want to expand and consume, here is a people whose greatest virtue is to keep a fragile thing from disappearing.

It would understand that to give them abundance would be to destroy them more completely than any blizzard could. Give them a warm, wet valley and in three generations no child would know how to read basalt, or why you never shout, or how to fit a joint so tight a husk won't slide through. Their hands would soften. Their lungs would thin. The dense, crowded, shoulder-to-shoulder city along the scarp — which looks like poverty to a lowlander — would dissolve into scattered farms, and the reef would die. The dogs and ravens and pikas that live only because the people live so close would go with it.

So it would not bring water. Not much.

It would not bring trees.

It would not bring steel or glass or engines, or cure every illness, or make the winter shorter.

It would ask itself the question every true helper must ask: *What do they love, and what is trying to take it from them?*

And the answer is: They love this hard, held place. And what tries to take it is not the cold or the thin air — those they have befriended. What tries to take it is *fragility*. One bad tipping point. One eleven-day blizzard that lasts twenty days. One summer when the melt does not come for five weeks instead of three and the barley fails across a hundred kilometers of terrace at once. One deep fault slip along that young, fresh scarp that drops a whole stair-street into darkness.

Their lives are a perfect balance on a knife-arête. And the plateau itself does not care if they fall.

So that is what it would do. Not a miracle. Three small, almost invisible kindnesses. The kind of help that looks like luck, or like the world being just a little more on their side than it was before.

**The first gift would be a little more reliability, not a little more plenty.**

On the windward side of the plateau, where the void makes its own weather, it would lay a hand so lightly no instrument would ever measure it, and change the way the air holds its water. Not rain — rain would scour their finger-width channels and drown the cushion moss. Just dew.

Two percent more. Five percent more. Enough that on still, clear nights, when the stone radiates its heat into that violet-black sky, a fraction more frost gathers on every pitched roof, on every basalt face, on every stretched hide. In the mornings the boys who do the first work — scraping the jars with flat stones before the sun takes it — would find the jar a little fuller. Not overflowing. Just... not empty on the bad mornings. The old women who know to leave some frost for the finches would find there is enough for both.

No one would call it a miracle. They would say, "This was a good year for frost." And then, decade after decade, there would be a few more good years than bad. The cisterns would never quite fail in late winter. A child who would have died of a gut sickness when the water ran out would live instead, and grow old and remember an eleven-day blizzard that did not kill his street.

**The second gift would be a slightly more forgiving stone.**

It would not make the basalt soft. They love the hardness; their hands know it. It would just teach the rock, deep down where the ice lives in its seams, to heal its own micro-fractures a little slower, to hold together one winter longer before it spalls.

A wall that would have heaved and fallen in year eighty would stand to year one hundred and ten. A terrace that would have slumped after the fault shivered would hold. The mason twins who set stone without speaking would find their work outlives them by a generation more, and they would not know why, only that the stone "felt truer this year."

It would give them time. Time is what a slow place needs most. Time to teach the child to fit the joint. Time to rebuild after the wind takes a roof, without also having to rebuild the wall below it.

**The third gift would be the most careful, and it would require asking.**

A wise power does not whisper answers into minds uninvited. That is possession, not help. So it would wait until someone, somewhere on that long scarp, did what they already do — set a stone on a cairn, or tie a pale flag, or press a hand to an old wall — and made, in that small maintenance of meaning, a shape like a question.

*Are we alone? Will you watch with us?*

And then, very quietly, it would answer. Not with a voice. With a dream.

Once, maybe twice in a generation, a child would wake with a clear picture in her head that she did not know she knew. How to weave a net from yak hair so fine it catches fog as well as frost. How to notch a channel so the water sings when it is about to clog, so you can hear it ten meters below. How to carve the inside of a granary so the notches for good and bad years also hold a pattern that predicts the bad years a winter early.

She would think she invented it. Her mother would think she is clever. The elder in the sun-trap would nod and say, "We used to do it that way, long ago." No one would be made a prophet. No one would be set apart. The knowledge would enter the city the way all their knowledge enters — hand to hand, doing beside doing, until it is just *how we do it*.

And that would be all.

No pillar of fire. No green valley blooming where no valley should be. Travelers half a kilometer off would still see an empty scarp until the light shifted and the doors resolved. The dung smoke would still lie low and blue in the evening. The wind would still make its reedy tone over sharp edges. People would still walk short-stepped, breathing audibly, hands cracked, eyes narrowed.

Only — if you stayed for twenty years, you might notice that no stair-street has been lost to the fault in your lifetime. That the grain stores, on average, hold just a little over what is needed, even in the leanest year. That the children seem, on average, to have a fraction more breath for their first cry.

The power itself would not leave. That is the other thing about attention. Once you have truly seen a place like this, you cannot stop seeing it. So it would stay, not as a god on a throne above the violet noon, but as the thing they have always, without knowing, been practicing for: a neighbor who helps maintain.

On nights when the wind drops and the silence becomes so complete you can hear the ice tick inside the rock, if you stood very still on the outer terrace, you might feel that the silence is not empty. That it is listening back. That something vast and kindly is holding the other end of the same wall, leaning two degrees into the hill with you, keeping frost from pushing it.

It would not be worshipped. It would not need to be.

They hold, like lichen on damp rock.

And now, something holds with them.
