It would come down the same way you did.

Not breaking the water. Entering it. Because it was already water before it arrived.

A benevolent and wise power would not arrive the way powers usually do in stories. Not as a shaft of light that spears the column and re-colors it. Not as a draining. Not as a voice from the rim saying *come up here, I have better water for you.*

It would have to crouch to fit this place. That's the first thing wisdom would understand.

This is a room-sized world. It is not a broken ocean that needs fixing. It is not a prison. It is a completed sentence. It has found its shape and kept it. Any power that entered wanting to *improve* it by making it larger, louder, more full of life, would only be trying to make it into somewhere else.

So it would make itself small.

It would come over the rim without disturbing the cut that has forgotten it was a cut. It would enter the water without a splash. And for a long time it would do nothing at all except be in the water with him.

That is the first work: to witness.

To tread in the cool that holds heat close to the skin, to feel the low thrum come through the stone into the chest, to count - one - two - three - for one full rotation of the flywheel, and to not immediately need to change anything. To let its eyes adjust until the dark mass on the floor stops being stone and resolves into a back. To see the filmed eyes. The cirri fanned on the silt. The pad resting against the steel.

To see him as he is, not as a poor thing.

A lesser power would feel pity. Pity would say: *you are alone, you are half-buried, you are covered in accretion, your eyes are veiled, you filter silt for a living while leaning against a rusted machine fed by a cable that some distant, forgetful grid still feeds. Let me take you to the sea. Let me give you speed. Let me clean you.*

Wisdom knows that his veil *is* his eyes. That his burial *is* his ballast. That his leaning *is* his work. He is not waiting to be cleaned. He sheds and tends his own algae in those palm-sized scrapes. He has already negotiated the terms between mineral and skin, between machine and flesh, between staying and being. He is not lonely. Loneliness is a story that requires a memory of company. He has *completeness* instead.

So the wise power would not take him out. It would not drain the basin to see what he looks like in air. It would not cut the cable just to see if he could live without it. It would not bring him a mate that would compete for the same thin film of food and break the balance that has held for so long. It would not stock the basin with fish to keep him company. Company is not always kindness.

What would it do, then, if it loves him and can do anything?

It would do very small things. The kinds of things that only someone who has watched for a long time would know to do.

First, it would put its hand - if it has a hand, and it does when it needs one - on the basalt lip on the north side and feel how tired the cable is. That thick, black, armored cable humming faintly up and over the rim and out of sight to a grid that will, one day, forget to hum. Every equilibrium that depends on a forgetting world is a little bit precarious.

It wouldn't rip the cable out. It would follow it with its attention back to wherever it is plugged in, and then, gently, it would change its source. Deep in the basalt, where the cold exchange already seeps so slow it doesn't read as movement, it would open a seam no wider than a vein. Water that has been down in the dark for a thousand years, warm by half a degree, with its own slow pressure, would find a way up. It would turn the flywheel from inside the basin's own body, not from far away. The hum would stay - the same low thrum, the same tick - but it would no longer be borrowed. The basin would be allowed to keep itself.

The needle trembling on the gauge would still tremble at the same mark. It just wouldn't be afraid anymore.

Second, it would kneel. Or the equivalent of kneeling, which is to lower its great attention down to his level and settle into the silt next to him, not opposite him.

It would not stare into his filmed eyes demanding recognition. His eyes look past, or through. So it would look past with him, at the far wall, at the way light moves down the column at noon without losing color, at the stunted stalks that have decided two or three feet is enough. It would breathe when he breathes - that slow, wide intake every few minutes that draws the particulates in by a finger's breadth - and it would let its own breath fall just slightly out of phase with his, the way the pump is out of phase with him. Two independent times keeping company in the same room.

And then, because benevolence without touch is just observation, it would touch him the way you would touch something that has never been touched except by water and stone.

Not to heal. There is nothing to heal.

It would lay that hand on the broad, low back, on the ridge where the olive-rust algae grows thickest in the light. The skin underneath is cool, as cool as the water that holds your heat close. The film of mineral would be there. The pale scrapes where he has worried himself clean.

It would not scrub him. It would warm - by less than a degree - the place under its palm. Just enough that for the first time in a long while, the being feels a difference that is not a threat. A small, impossible spring inside the steady mineral cool. His cirri would stir, not to sift, and his back would swell that fraction a little more on the next intake, as if noticing.

With that warmth it would give him a single new thing that does not break any of the rules:

A dream.

Not a human dream. Not a story.

It would give him, one night when the sun is no longer in charge and the hollow glows evenly like water in a glass bowl, a *current*. Just one. For perhaps an hour, while he rests his pad against the housing, the water would move on its own - a slow, wide, spiraling turn around the basin, soft enough that the stunted stalks finally sway. Just once. Just enough for the particulates to lift and turn and catch the faint starlight from the open sky. Just enough for his cirri to lift off the silt and fan and feel drag.

And then it would be still again. The stalks would stand again. The particulates would hang motionless again. Nothing would have been added or taken away that would make the water less clear tomorrow. But he would have felt *drift*. And somewhere under his veil, his eyes would have tracked something that moved past, and he would keep that.

Third - and this is the part that is most like doing nothing - it would leave without taking anything and without being seen to leave.

It would not carve its name in the basalt. It would not require his gratitude or his worship. The most benevolent powers understand that to be witnessed by a power is a heavy thing, and they make their witnessing light.

Before it goes, it might do one more thing that water would understand: it would give him a name, but not out loud. It would fold it into the thrum. A particular tremor in the flywheel's tick that only he, with his pad against the housing, would feel - a pattern that says, in the language of vibration through stone and skin:

*You are held. You are not forgotten because you are hidden. You are not waiting. You are complete and I have seen that you are complete and I agree with the agreement you made with water and stone. Keep your shape as long as you want it.*

The gauge would still tremble minutely at the same mark. The light would still come and go without altering anything. The algae would not spread and would not die back.

If you came down again after that, you might not notice anything different at all. The water would still be cold and clear and complete. The flywheel would still turn - one - two - three. The mass on the floor would still look like stone until you watched long enough to see it breathe.

Only - if you put your hand on the rock and stayed very still, you might feel that the thrum has changed, just barely. It no longer comes from far away. It comes from underneath. And if you dive down and come close enough to see your distorted reflection in his eye, you might see, for a second, that the veil over it is a little clearer in one small circle, as if something warm had rested a palm there and left no print except a place where seeing is a little easier.

He breathes in. The particulates draw in.

The flywheel turns.

He breathes out.

Nothing changes.

Nothing needs to.
