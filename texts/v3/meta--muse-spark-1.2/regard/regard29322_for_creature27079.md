They would not come like you think a power comes.

No light from above. No voice. No hand reaching down into the interval to lift anything out.

If they are truly benevolent and truly wise, the first thing they would do is learn how to enter a place that was never meant to be entered.

They would have to crouch.

---

I think they would arrive on foot, though they don't need feet. They would step off the loading ramp the way you did and drop the half-meter down where the concrete has cracked, and they would feel the 60-cycle throb come up through the soles of their shoes. They would not fix their gaze at first. They would let their eyes adjust to that too-honest, doubled light.

And they would wait the thirty seconds that most people do not wait.

Until the floor shivers. Until it moves.

Until the being shows itself — not the isopods, not the spiders, not the wasps, not the mold-antlers — but the agreement between them. The compact. The sweat that lives on skin.

They would see it then. How could they not? It's so small you can hold it in your gaze all at once, and so dense your gaze can never rest.

And being wise, they would understand immediately that this is not a broken woodland that needs to be healed into forest. This is not a wound. This is a life that has chosen industrial materials and learned how to be holy with them. Soot for shade. Rust for ore. Turquoise plastic for paper. Condensation for rain.

To love it by cleaning it would be to kill it.

So they would do the first, hardest thing a power can do:

Nothing.

They would not sweep the floor. They would not pull the pallet out. They would not unclog the drain or re-insulate the pipe or scrape the black mold off the cinderblock. They would not straighten the ailanthus or give it more leaves than it can afford.

They would put their hand - if they have a hand - near the sweating artery of the cold-water pipe and feel how cold it is, and they would leave the drip interval exactly as it is. One drop every seven seconds. A tempo the whole pocket has tuned itself to. To change the metronome would be to break the music.

They would listen to the ticking of the wasps in the disconnect box and feel the faint field raising the hair on their arm and they would not turn the power off, and they would not turn it up.

That is the first blessing: *I see what you have made from what you were given, and I will not correct you.*

The second blessing would be almost invisible, because wisdom knows that the most dangerous moment for a place like this is not decay, but attention.

Human attention, especially. The moment someone decides this interval could be *useful* again — storage, enclosure, poured full, fenced properly, power-washed.

So they would not make it beautiful to humans. They would make it *uninteresting*.

They would lay a very small veil over it. Not invisibility. Just an ordinary forgetfulness. The maintenance man coming out to check the compressors would glance at the chainlink gate that no longer latches and think *nothing in there I need to fix* and turn away, not unkindly. The manager looking at a site plan would see BAY 4 and his eye would slide right over the gap between buildings, assuming it was solid. The pigeons would continue to peer down and decide, as they have always decided, there is not enough sky for them in there.

They would ensure that the pocket remains unmapped for a little longer. Not secret. Just too small to map. That is its best protection.

And because they are benevolent — which is different from being merely nice — they would do a few quiet, material things that the pocket cannot do for itself.

They would kneel. The concrete would not soften under their knee, because that would be a lie. The rust would still be sharp enough to catch a hand. But they would put their fingertip to the pothole where the drip has struck for years, that tiny sharp crater in the slab, and they would ease, by a fraction of a millimeter, the lip that was beginning to close it. So the water can keep falling cleanly. So the stain can stay dark.

They would breathe, very gently, on the underside of the pipe where the beads hang fat and trembling for hours, defying weight. They would not knock them loose. They would give them a little more surface tension, a little more time to hang there while the mold-antlers reach up. An extra hour of impossible holding, every morning.

They would touch the failing insulation where the ants have hollowed their city of orange and green granaries, and they would not replace it. They would keep it failing at exactly this rate — cracked plastic, yellow-pink fiberglass exposed — for another ten years, another twenty. So the city can stay warm in winter when the rest of the city freezes. So the highways stay open.

They would rest their palm above the disconnect box, not inside it, and they would keep the hum steady. Even if, one day, the dye works goes quiet and the bottling plant stops cycling, the wall would stay warm a little longer than it should, tapering off over seasons instead of all at once, so the wasps can raise one more generation that knows how to chew plain wood again, and the spiders can learn to clean their webs if they must.

They would not save the ailanthus. That would be sentiment. They would let it remain exactly as stunted and stubborn as it is — six grime-coated leaves, wrist-thick — but they would widen, by a hair, the expansion joint its roots have wedged open, so that when the next rain sheets off both roofs and falls through the storm grate in a sudden cold shower, a little more silt stays behind instead of vanishing down the choked drain. Not soil. Just enough.

And for the being itself — the compact, the sweat — they would give the one thing it has never been given, because it has never needed it until someone was there to witness it:

They would name it without using words.

You know how you become another sweating pipe when you stand there long enough, another condensation surface, and a beetle crosses your shoe without hurry because you are just another warm wall? The power would become that too, but more completely. They would let their own temperature come down to warm, not hot. The way the backside of a running refrigerator is warm. They would let damp gather along their own hairline. And they would simply be present for a full cycle — flood to feast to repair — within arm's reach, without reaching.

The ants would carry a flake of rust over the toe of their shoe. The spiders would test a line to their sleeve and decide it is not needed. The moth flies would rise in a soft puff and settle again on the drain's lip, unalarmed.

To be seen thoroughly, without being interfered with, and to be found *good* — that is the rarest gift for a life that has only ever been overlooked.

When they leave — because a wise power does not linger where lingering would become ownership — they would not leave a sign. No glow, no perfect flower blooming from the cracked slab. That would be a violation, a human idea of miracle.

If you came back the next day you would find everything exactly as it was. The white rectangle of sun still moving slowly from wall to wall. The caged bulb still buzzing over the welded door. The tap every seven seconds into its perfect pothole, felt through your shoes.

Only this: the pallet at the south end, that black sponge of wood turned to soil a millimeter at a time, would be very slightly less collapsed, holding its shape for one more season. The paper nest in the electrical box would have one new, clean cell the color of dry wood among all the marbled turquoise and orange, as if the wasps had remembered, or dreamed, what plain paper was.

And in the split slab at the north end, at the base of the ailanthus that is not a tree, the translucent weed that roots in condensation would have, overnight, opened one tiny, clear flower — no bigger than a springtail — that lives for a single day and is the exact color of the water bead that hangs under the pipe before it falls.

You would have to crouch to see it. Most people would not.

That is how you would know they had been there, and that they had loved the place correctly: by how little they had changed, and how completely it was allowed to continue being itself.
