It would not come down.

That is the first thing.

Anything wise and benevolent enough to actually see this place — not just the size of it, but the *rate* of it — would know immediately not to arrive. Not to descend in light, not to land, not to cast a shadow over the high terraces. To arrive would be to break the thing it had come to love.

Because what would you be arriving into? Thin, white, brutal light. The smell of hot stone and gourd vine and oil. A trip-hammer falling once every three seconds. A woman breathing audibly as she climbs. To appear there as a Power would be a noise too loud for a place that has spent a thousand years learning how to be exactly this loud.

So it doesn't come.

It *notices*. And then it holds.

---

If I am that Power — if I am old enough and quiet enough to be worthy of the word benevolent — this is what happens when I turn my attention there:

At first I just look. For a long time. Longer than a human can look without needing to name.

I see what you saw: the mountain that is not a mountain. The bone-and-ochre mass that stores the sun all day and gives it back all night. The mature erosion, the channels worn smooth, the soil held a meter deep behind walls that have been brushed by hands until their corners are soft. I hear the drone that is not chaos but weather — looms and dynamos and pumps lifting water one level higher, always one level higher — and under it and through it, geckos and bees and swifts in the thousands of deliberate holes, and tree frogs at dusk.

And then I see them. The short, wide, dark people. The ones who learned to breathe to the metronome of the mountain. Who do not build but *hold*. Who belong to a level the way a fern belongs to a niche.

And I understand, very quickly, that this is not a problem to be solved.

There is no famine here to fix with rain. The rain already comes, fast and heavy in the late afternoon, and the whole mountain sings and drains at once through spillways polished to a sheen. There is no loneliness here to fix with community. They are already distributed like water, aunts above you and cousins below you, everyone known by the sound of their broom through the floor.

There is no ignorance here to fix with knowledge. They can diagnose the health of a world-sized building by ear. A boy is given a spillway at seven and spends a decade moving water a hand's breadth with a stone. They know what I have forgotten in my bigness: how to stay.

The temptation for any power is to *improve*. To say: I will make the air thicker for you, so you don't have to sigh in your sleep. I will make your backs straight, your terraces flat, your loads lighter. I will take you off this beautiful, difficult stair and show you a plain where you can run.

A wise power feels that temptation like a draft and closes the door on it.

To thicken the air would be to un-tune the dynamos. To straighten their legs would be to make them strangers to the stairs their grandmothers died climbing. To give them a plain would be to kill them.

So I do not improve them. I do not save them. I do not uplift them, which is only a pretty word for *uproot*.

What I do is much smaller, and slower. Which is why it is benevolent.

**I learn their pace, and I join it.**

I do not speak. They speak in low vowels timed to the gaps between hammers, a language carried as much through the feet as the air. To speak to them in words would be shouting over weather. So I do not send a message, a sign, a prophet on the 800th terrace shouting *you are seen*.

Instead, I breathe with them. For one year, I do nothing but match that inhale, that catch, that deeper pull the thin air demands. I let my own vast attention fall into their rhythm: hammer, breathe, prune, breathe. Pump sigh, chain rattle, water chattering over pebbles placed to keep it from scouring.

When you breathe with something long enough, you start to hold it without touching it.

**And then, without being seen, I hold the holding.**

On the outside, on the dry lower air where exploiters and map-makers and lowland hungers live — I make the mountain boring to them. Not invisible. That would be a miracle and miracles invite pilgrims. Just… uninteresting. From a distance, through the haze, the heat shimmer makes it look even more like a natural peak than it already does. Satellite eyes slide off it. Drones that wander that way develop a little hitch in their compass, a little preference for turning home. The valley stories that filter down become stories about a place too steep, too slow, too much work to be worth taking. The built foothills and causeways in the plain are, to outsiders, just odd geology. I don't build a shield. I just make forgetting a little easier there. That is the most merciful thing a big power can do for a small, perfect thing: help the world forget to eat it.

On the inside, I do even less.

I do not stop the weathering. The fig roots will still work into the mortar, and that is right — the wall was made to learn how to hold a fig. The transformer cage will still need reweaving with vine. I don't make anything eternal. Eternity is not what they are praying for when they touch the fern in the niche. They are praying for *continuance*. For one more correct season.

So I give continuance, but only in the way a gardener gives it: by not taking.

When the late afternoon burst is late, I lean a little on the air so the clouds that form below them every morning rise a little earlier, softening that killing white light for an hour. Not enough that they notice. Just enough that the shade cloths on the high planes last another summer before they fray.

When a bearing three terraces down starts to go dry and the man with the notched stick hasn't heard it yet because his child was crying — I make the whine a half-tone higher, just enough to catch his ear through the stone. He will think he heard it himself. He did.

When a child dies — because children still die, even in a place this held — and is composted into the terrace soil they tended, and the family clears a niche and plants a fern, I stay with that fern a little longer than physics would. I keep condensation on its frond through a dry week. I keep it green. Not forever. Just long enough that grief has time to root.

That is all.

No second sun. No golden age. No voice saying *well done*.

If I am truly wise, I understand that my greatest gift is to be briefly of their kind. So for a little while, I become one more presence in the density. Not a person. A gecko on a sun-warmed wall where a gecko belongs. A swift in one of the thousands of deliberate holes. An extra bee on a ledge hive. The cool underside of a low garden wall where old people dangle their legs at dusk and listen. The particular way dust motes hang in the amber hour when the brutal light finally softens and they come out to shell beans into old engine casings.

I sit with them while they listen. I listen with them to the pumps lifting water one level higher, always one level higher.

And when I go — because even a benevolent power cannot stare forever without becoming weight — I leave nothing behind that can be found or worshipped or fought over.

Maybe one thing.

On a terrace that has not yet been built when I arrived, but will be built in forty years when a great-granddaughter of the Nguyen paddy on 734-West finally needs a new retaining wall for her own daughter's beans, the masons will leave a row of holes for swifts that they weren't planning to leave. They won't know why. It will just feel correct, like leaving a niche for a fern. They will say, “The wall wants it.”

And the swifts will come.

That will be my signature. Not in stone, but in hollow. Not added, but allowed.

The mountain will keep weathering at exactly the rate it was meant to weather. The warm, damp, bright, green, clattering holding will hold.

And from very far away, with its breath paced to a lungful of thin bright air, something vast will keep listening — not as a guardian, not as a god — but as one more neighbor directly above, known by the sound of its broom through the floor, who never needed to be met to be loved.
