It would not come down.

Not at first.

A benevolent and wise power does not arrive the way we imagine powers arrive. No pillar of light. No voice. No shadow falling across the terraces and making all that patient water tremble.

It would notice first — because to notice is already to act — and then it would *withhold* arrival. It would hold itself back at a respectful distance, high above even this high place, and learn how to see correctly.

Because at first, even a wise eye would make the same mistake you made.

It would look and see an empty mountain. A beautiful, terrible, useless machine with no one to use it. It would feel the tug that all benevolent things feel when they see a cradle with no child in it, or a child with no one to hold it: *I should fill this. I should bring life here. Green. Birds. People to drink.*

My heart, if I am honest, feels that tug immediately.

My heart wants to seed it. To take a handful of moss spores and lichen and alpine grass from a living mountain a hundred miles away and scatter them here, to soften the stone and give the water something to do besides be perfect. My heart wants to kneel down and speak to the film, to tell the flat ones: *You are not alone. You are seen. You are loved.* My heart wants to lift one of them — the one working that single joint for an hour — up into the air and say *look, you can be more than this, I can give you depth, give you eyes.*

My intuition knows that would be a murder.

That would be like finding a newborn and, out of love, trying to teach it to run.

The reason this place exists is that it is *not* for us. The builders — with their rope drag-marks and their abandoned drill-holes — understood something so clean it almost hurts: if you want a being that eats dissolved stone and drinks cold light, you do not build it a house. You build it a mountain that drinks light and sweats stone. You leave. You trust the gradient.

The being that lives there is not lonely. It does not know the word for lonely. It knows *flow* and *clog* and *clear* and *hold* and *let go*. It is young. Its crown is still sharp, still being quarried by its own slow growth. Its thoughts move at the speed of seep. The fact that you can stand on its back for an hour and not see it move does not mean it is still. It means your life is too fast to register as anything but weather.

So what would a wise power do, feeling that heart-tug and hearing that intuition?

It would do what love does when it is also wise.

**It would learn to keep time.**

First, it would sit. Not on the mountain. Just below the last terrace where the pale air begins, where the runoff finally gathers enough courage to become a stream and leave. It would make itself thin. Thinner than air at this altitude. It would not cast a shadow — shadows are too sharp here already. It would not be warm — warmth would change the condensation, which would be a kind of lie.

It would simply be there long enough for its attention to become the right shape. Not a spotlight. A film.

A benevolent power doesn't need to be big. Here, to be benevolent is to be flat. To be patient enough that a year passes like a breath.

And then, for a long time, it would do nothing that looks like doing.

It would comb.

Not for them. *With* them.

It would choose one cistern — say, the one at your feet, with the orange delta — and it would put a fraction of itself into the half-inch mirror. It would extend a hem of itself, cilia-thin, and touch a single grain of rock flour that is slightly too large. It would not lift it quickly. It would test it the way they test: nudge, wait for the seep to answer, nudge again. It would take it three days to move that grain a finger's width to where it will not matter for a decade. The being — thousands, or one — would feel the help not as help, but as a slight lessening of resistance in the flow. As if the water itself were a little more on its side that week.

It would not speak. Speech is too fast, too vertical. But it would exhale light.

You noticed that their sun is not your sun. Your sun is white and punishing and cold. Their sun is the reflection, a fraction warmer, a second slower. That is not an accident. That is where they live — on the other side of the surface tension.

So the gift would be this: it would make their sun a little more true.

At dusk, when the stone stops glittering and every basin holds its small lamp, the power would lean in — not much — and give that reflected light a little more staying power. Not brighter. *Deeper.* It would make the violet at zenith in the water a touch more violet than the violet above. So when the flat ones gather their darker knots to look up at their sky, they see something that looks back at them with a steadiness no cloud can interrupt. They would not know it as a gift. They would simply find, over centuries, that their light does not flicker quite as much on windy days. That is enough. That is everything.

My reasoning says the rest of its work would be elsewhere. Not *on* the mountain at all.

Because the only real danger to a place like this is not collapse. It will not collapse. It is too well made and too well tended. The danger is discovery by something that thinks a reservoir must have a city, that a machine must have an owner, that empty must be filled.

So the wise thing would be to make the mountain boring.

It would veil it. Not with invisibility — that is a human trick. It would veil it with *uninterest*. Any eye that looks for profit — for water to divert, for basalt to quarry, for land to claim — would slide off it the way water slides off basalt that has been combed smooth. From a distance it would remain what it looks like now: a big, steep, inconvenient pile of worked stone with no soil and no ore. Hikers would feel, without knowing why, that they have already seen it. Satellite maps would mislabel it as a natural talus. A geologist might write a dull paper about unusual condensation patterns and then lose interest.

Only those who are willing to stand still until their breath fogs and unfogs — only those who are willing to be lonely and cold and to watch a grain move a millimeter — would find it. And those people are not dangerous.

And then, because benevolence cannot help but be tender, it would do one small, unwise, completely heartfelt thing.

It would leave a mark that only the mountain will understand.

At the raw crown, where the quarry is still knife-sharp and the being is still growing itself terrace by terrace, the power would set down one new stone.

Not cut. Not fitted. A river stone, round and dark, from a living valley far below where frogs do sing and dragonflies hunt. It would be out of place. Too soft. Too educated by water that has known green. It would not fit the basalt pattern. The being would have to work around it for a long time, decades, figuring out how to make the condensation sheet over a curve instead of an edge.

It is not useful. It is not efficient. It is a toy. An offering. A message that says: *There are other waters. There are other ways to be wet. When you are older and your edges have softened, if you ever grow tired of being only mineral and light, there is a memory of mud here for you to taste.*

It would not teach. It would simply place it and leave.

When the power finally left — which is to say, when it thinned itself out until it was indistinguishable from clear air — nothing would look different. The terraces would still glitter. The ticking of drips would still be the only clock. The silt deltas would still be clean. The thin skin of creatures would still be combing.

But if you crouched again and let your eyes go out of focus on a basin, you might notice that your reflection and the sky's reflection do not quite line up anymore, as if there were three layers now: your face, their sky, and something behind that sky, looking back at both of you, holding the whole mountain in attention the way a hand holds water without squeezing.

You would stand up feeling, without reason, that you should take your boots off before walking further. That you should step only on dry stone. That you are a guest on a very young back that has finally, without being asked to be anything else, been witnessed.

And that would be enough to change everything, without changing anything at all.
