They would not come the way you did.

Not down the ladder with the snapped lashing, not with a boot testing the peat at the rim. Not as a body breaking the cold column of air and making the salamander feel the ripple.

A benevolent and wise power doesn't need a mouth to enter.

So it wouldn't arrive at all, in that sense. It would notice. And noticing, for that kind of power, is already a kind of arrival.

It had been passing over the flatlands - peat and frost-heaved till and stunted black spruce for a hundred miles in every direction - the way such powers do, not looking for anything, which is how they find things. It felt a cold spot that kept itself cold. A sound that kept itself loud. A green that kept itself exactly as green as it needed to be and no greener. 

Most places on the plain are trying to become something else. Drying out, or greening over, or being cut, or being remembered. This one wasn't trying. It was succeeding at being itself, slowly, with great patience.

That made it visible.

It would look - not with eyes - and understand immediately what you took a long time to understand:

That the loudness is not suffering.
That the cold is not a wound.
That the emptiness between the moss islands is not loneliness.
That the slowness is not deprivation.

So the first thing it would do is nothing that looks like help.

It would not warm the pool. The salamander's blood *is* four degrees above freezing. Warm it and you kill it.

It would not quiet the fall. The roar is its silence. Take it away and it would be deafened by direction. It would be exposed.

It would not bring light to the bottom. The dusk is what lets the liverwort be so tender. Full sun would bleach and parch it in a day.

It would not make more salamanders. Three or four is the exact number this bowl can keep cold and fed without lying to itself. Abundance here would be a kind of gluttony, and then a die-off.

A lesser power, an eager one, would have done all those things out of pity. A wise one feels no pity for perfection. Only reverence.

So what would it do?

It would get low.

If it has a shape it would set it aside. To enter a pocket you must become pocket-shaped. It would thin itself out until it was no thicker than the condensation that beads on the limestone and it would seep down the walls one drop at a time, the way the water does. It would not disturb the pressure. The salamander would feel a change in weight no bigger than a moth landing, and then not even that.

And then it would wait with them.

That is the whole work. But wait properly.

It would settle on the cold scoured floor where everything is damp and the air is too big, and it would let the drum of the walls beat through it until it learned the tempo. Twelve feet of fall, two arm-lengths wide, tea-colored, never stopping. It would listen the way the salamander listens - through the jaw, through the belly, through the stone - until the roar stopped being everywhere and became a place, and inside that place it could hear the small things: the tick of a single drip from beard-moss, the unroll of a fern, the exhale of a gill.

It would keep company without requiring company in return.

For a long time - a human long time, which is nothing to this place - it would just be there, sharing the dusk. The moss would not know it from any other wetness. The rill would wander over it. The salamander under the loaf-sized stone would lift its head a fraction, fan its red gills, and settle again, and the power would be glad for that fraction.

And because it is benevolent, it would not withhold benediction. But benediction, from a wise power, looks like maintenance, not miracle.

Three small things, so small you would have to stay a century to see that they had been done:

**1. It would strengthen what already holds.**

Outside, the world is getting warmer. The plain is drying at its edges. Summers that used to be cool and sweating now come hot and thirsty. The peat contracts. For a pocket that lives on condensation, on cold air that has nowhere else to go, a few degrees is an ending.

The power would not cool the whole plain. It would do something quieter. It would remember the peat at the rim, and help it remember itself. It would coax the sphagnum to knit a little tighter, to hold a little more water. It would weight the cold just enough that it still pools and stays. It would whisper to the limestone seams uphill: *stay open, keep seeping.* No human would measure the difference. The tea-colored curtain would still be two arm-lengths wide. But in fifty years when it should have thinned to one, it will still be two.

It keeps the bargain the place made with water and cold.

**2. It would protect its unbeing-found.**

The greatest danger to a pocket like this is not winter, but being found by people who love it the wrong way. Who want to map it, name it, bottle the water, photograph the salamander, build a walkway to the rim.

So the power would do a very gentle, very wise kind of forgetting. It would not hide it with a glamour. It would just let the plain be a little more plain around it. Let the track that approaches pause and turn back a little sooner. Let the eye slide off, the mind say *there's nothing there, just more spruce.* Let the stories about it stay soft: "somewhere out there a hole, too loud, not worth it." 

Those who need it - the old trapper with the silvered spruce beams - will still find the ladder when the wind is too high to stand in. His feet will find it without thinking. For everyone else, it will be ten feet of empty stone and not worth the wet boots.

It keeps its hiddenness, which is its wholeness.

**3. It would answer the one who didn't ask.**

Before it thins and lifts again, it would offer a choice. Not in words - words would be stolen by the drum - but in pressure, in a brief stillness inside the roar.

To the salamander: *Would you like anything else?*

And the salamander, with its light-meter eyes and its gills like small ferns, with its heart counted in minutes, would not answer in words either. It would answer by being. By not moving. By staying exactly as wet and as still as it is.

*No,* that staying says. *This is good. Keep it cold. Keep it loud.*

And the power - which could have given it legs for the plain, or eyes for distance, or a chorus to end its singular - would be deeply, delightedly relieved. And it would grant what was asked: it would press its attention, which is its substance, into the underside of that submerged slab where the six or seven pea-sized eggs are glued. It would not make them all hatch. That would be ruin. It would just make the water a little clearer, a little more oxygenated, for a week in spring. Enough that one more than usual makes it. Enough that next year there will still be three or four, and not two.

To the moss it would say nothing, because moss is already prayer.

To the old man - who is not there that day, but whose axe marks are still in the spruce - it would do one small, unwise, benevolent thing that wisdom allows itself once in a while: it would mend the snapped lashing on the three-rung ladder. Not with new rope that would look strange, but by tightening the old sinew, by coaxing the fibers to remember their twist. So that the next time he comes down with wind on his back and his hands are too cold to tie a knot, the rung will hold.

And then it would go. The way condensation goes - not leaving, just ceasing to be gathered.

If you came back the next day you would see nothing. The walls still weeping one drop at a time. The fall still hammering until your jaw aches. The rill still braiding through bare stone between islands of green so dense they bury boulders. The stacked wall at the south end still waist-high, the fire ring still blackened. Under the loaf-sized stone near the black pool, an amber smear lifts its head a fraction and settles.

You would think no one had been here. You would be almost right.

Only - the air at the bottom might feel, for a few minutes at noon when the slot of sky gives its twenty minutes of straight-down light, a fraction less empty. Not full. Just witnessed. The way a room feels after someone has sat with you without needing you to entertain them.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does for a place like this.

It does not rescue it. It keeps it from needing rescue. It sits with it in the deafening dusk and agrees that this - cold, loud, sparse, patient, complete - is a good way to be alive. And it makes sure, as quietly as moss, that it gets to continue.
