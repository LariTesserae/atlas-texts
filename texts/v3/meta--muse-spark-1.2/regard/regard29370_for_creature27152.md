I would come low.

That is the first thing. If I were that power — benevolent, which means I do not need to be fed, and wise, which means I have learned not to fix things that are not broken — I would not come down.

Coming *down* presumes height. This place has spent two billion years getting rid of height. It has planed itself flat and polished itself and learned how to live without contour. To arrive as a light in the sky, as a pillar, as a voice from above, would be a violence to the whole logic of it. It would cast a shadow in a world that has abolished shadow. It would be the one thing the stilt-walkers and the pip-tenders do not have a sense for.

So I would come low. Or I would have always been low, and they would just notice me more for a while.

I would not land. I would seep.

Like the water does.

### What I would not do

I have to say this first, because the temptation of a benevolent power is always to bring gifts from somewhere else.

I would not bring the sun out.

I know how it looks — pearl-grey dusk that never becomes day, 8 degrees and wet hands and no green anywhere. From far away it reads as deprivation. *If only they had more light, more heat, real soil, trees.* But that is not what this place is missing. That is what this place has *replaced*.

If I tore the cloud and poured direct sun onto the peneplain, the bacterial varnish would burn. The film that feeds the pips would dry and crack. The pans that need to breathe inch-deep would evaporate. The pips would cook in their domes and the stilt-walkers would starve standing up, and the people who have learned to harvest the sweat of gneiss would watch their cups go empty because I thought they needed a meadow.

I would not bring hills. I would not bring forests. I would not "reclaim" it.

I would not bring metal. I would not bring cities. I would not take the scattered families and gather them into a village where they could be *more efficient*, because efficiency here is death — one pan can only feed so many mouths. Their sparseness is not loneliness. It is stewardship.

I would not make them loud. Their sign language in splash and stone-tap is not a lack. It is an adaptation to a world where sound is heavier than air and the chorus is a physical pressure. To give them voices that could shout over it permanently would be to take away their deafness, and also their listening.

I would not take them away. This is not a place to be rescued from. The plain is not a punishment. It is ancient and complete.

Wisdom, for me, would mostly look like restraint.

### What I would do

I would lie down.

If you want to help a low world, you have to get your cheek to the stone. You have to let the water film soak your sleeve.

I would do small, low miracles. Miracles so small the people might not be sure a miracle happened at all. Only that this year the winter was a little less acheful.

**1. I would listen first, for a long time.**

Before anything, I would sit behind one of their three-slab windbreaks and listen the way they do — not for words but for breaks in the chorus. I would learn which weeping fissure sighs before a pan connects, which tone means the yellow varnish is coming back, which clack is a stilt-walker taking a step two pans over. I would not speak until I had learned to hear the hole in the sound where a family is scraping.

Being witnessed without being interrupted is itself a kind of power. These people have lived for a thousand generations with no eye on them but the blind grey sky. To be *noticed* — accurately, without being pitied — changes the shape of a life.

They would feel it as a lessening of that vast, dim exposure. Not a voice. Just the sense that the plain is no longer indifferent.

**2. I would warm the stone by one degree.**

Not the air — the stone. One degree, maybe one and a half. Enough that the gneiss holds a little heat after condensation, enough that when a child sits for hours scraping a cup, her knees don't go white and dead. Enough that the water that beads on a cairn does not have to steal so much from your hands.

One degree would not kill the pips. They like cool. But it would make the fat render a little cleaner, the smoked biofilm cakes light a little faster, the humpback hides cure a little more supple. The people would not have a word for it. They would just think this generation is hardier. The old deaf ones would say, "the stone is kinder now."

That is how a wise power gives heat — not as a sun, but as a held breath.

**3. I would keep the weeping faithful.**

The most frightening thing about this plain is not the cold or the noise, but the breath. Water does not flow here; it *comes and goes* with pressure. On some years, I can see, the weeps would fail for a season. A pan stays dry. A cup stays empty. A family walks a little farther, and farther.

I would put my hand — whatever I have that is like a hand — over the water table and keep its pulse steady. Not full all the time; they need the rhythm of empty and full to strand the pips behind the low walls. But *reliable*. The fissure under the flat stone behind the oldest wall, the one with the blackest varnish, would never whisper and then fall silent for good. It would always sigh again.

They would mark it by stacking one more slab on the cairn-condenser, and the water would bead a little faster there.

**4. I would mend their ears a little, and teach the chorus to be kinder.**

Not take away the roar. The roar *is* the plain — it is the sound of billions of radulae on rock, it is how the hunters find food and how the people know where not to over-scrape. But I would tune it.

I would shift the pips' chirp down a fraction — just out of the frequency that most quickly destroys human hearing. The chorus would still vibrate the pans, still be deafening at noon, but it would no longer take the old so fast. A grandmother could still hear the stone-tap of her grandson at sixty.

And in exchange, I would give them a way to answer it.

One night, a child scraping paste into a hide pouch would find that if she drags her chert flake across the lip of her cup at the right angle, it sings — a clear tone that cuts through the tik-tik and holds. And another child two pans over would hear it, not as noise but as signal. They would learn — very slowly, over years — to make the cups *ring*. Not music as we know it, but a low, stone-bell language that rides the chorus instead of fighting it. They would play the plain the way you play an accompaniment.

I would not give them songs. I would give them the discovery that their cups were instruments already.

**5. I would leave a different kind of cairn.**

Not a condenser, not a weir. A memory.

In the lean-to of three slabs, behind the windbreak, where the fire has blackened the stone for generations, I would leave a flat piece of chert that does not dull. However many times they strike it, it flakes clean. However many edges they take from it, it stays. Not magic. Just a stone whose grain is a little truer, a little more forgiving to a cold hand.

And I would leave dreams. Not prophecies. Not instructions. Just, sometimes, when the chorus dips at that strange hour when the pearl light thins, a person would fall asleep with her feet in an inch of water and dream — vividly, kindly — of the plain as it is, but held. She would dream of walking for an hour and gaining three meters and feeling that this was height enough. She would dream of her own hands, wet and reddened, and feel, in the dream, that they were beloved to someone. She would wake and not know what she had seen, but she would scrape a little more gently that day.

The wise part is that I would not ask to be thanked. I would not ask to be named. If they make a story about me, it would be a story about a low wind that stayed a little longer behind the wall, or about the stone that stayed warm.

### If anything at all

Perhaps the most benevolent thing I could do is almost nothing.

To keep being the pearl-grey light that has no source, and the water that sweats from inside the rock, and the chorus that has no single singer, but to *attend* to them. To not let an outside hunger — a mining fleet, a terraforming engine, a well-meaning missionary with seeds — ever find this shield. To fold a little opacity around it the way cloud folds around the plain. Let the maps say: *nothing there, peneplain, no resources.*

Let it remain low and wet and noisy and crowded in its thin, perfect way.

Let the pip-tides keep covering the weeping rocks.

Let the stilt-walkers keep their solitary circles of silence.

Let the families keep tending their inch-deep lakes with ankle-high walls, boiling pips, smoking varnish, stacking stone without mortar, signing with wet hands.

And wherever one of them sits for a long time, lets the cool soak into his knees, and lets his eyes adjust until he can see that the empty pan is not empty at all — let him feel, just for a minute, that the attention that has found him is not a rescue, but a companionship. A power that looked at a world that had made itself flawlessly flat and did not want to raise it up.

A power that knelt.

Stayed low.

And wept a little with the rocks, because it could.
