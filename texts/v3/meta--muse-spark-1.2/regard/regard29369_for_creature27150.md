They would not come from above.

That's the first thing.

A lesser power comes from above — on a ridge, in light, with announcement. A wise and benevolent one looks low. It has learned to look low. It knows that what is high wants to be seen, and what is low has stopped asking.

So it would not arrive as arrival. There would be no parting of the canopy, no beam. You would not hear it coming through the ordinary entropic woods where things fall and rot. You would simply realize, sitting with your back against the breathing wall, that the chamber has been *noticed*.

And being noticed changes everything without touching anything.

---

It would stop at the rim.

That is what tells you it is wise. Anyone else steps right down into the bowl — over the polished buttress roots, onto the spring-mattress that is also a back — because the bowl invites it. It's so soft. So warm. So complete.

The power does not.

It kneels at the outer edge of the root-wall, where the ordinary forest floor is still ordinary mud and fern, and places one hand on the fig's skin where the graft is pale and seamless. It does not enter without asking. Even though it could. Even though nothing could stop it.

It listens.

For a long time it just listens to the factory thrum. To the *sigh - ten seconds - sigh* of the pistons drawing in the humid air and mist, to the amber sap running down the sluices and vanishing, to the ticking aerial cilia, to the tabletop leaves shivering at high frequency, sifting husks into dust, to the blue corona crackle of the beetles which is the creature's thought. *Pump, sift, spark, weep.*

It understands immediately what took you an hour to understand: that this is not a swamp. This is a solved problem. An organism — or two organisms that have decided to become one — that solved need by abolishing outside. Solved impermanence by abolishing change. It has eaten its own tail so perfectly there is no friction left. Polished smooth by a hundred thousand rains, by time, by its own recycling. Nothing sharp left.

And it understands that this is an achievement so total it borders on grief.

It does not pity the creature. Pity would be insulting. This low, flat, liver-colored mat-animal that poured itself out across fifteen meters and learned how to breathe through the walls of a fig has done something most gods never manage: it has made peace. True, warm, 74-degree, saturated peace. It idles without hunger. It is not suffering.

But wisdom knows that a perfect closed loop is not life. It is memory of life. It is a song held on one note so long you forget it was ever meant to move.

Benevolence would not break the note. It would not fix what isn't broken.

So it asks.

Not in words. The creature has no ears, no eyes, no face. It feels as pressure and temperature and interruption in humidity. So the power asks in the only language the room understands: it changes the humidity slightly, with its own breath.

It leans over the rim and exhales — a breath cooler than the greenhouse warmth, carrying the air of elsewhere: ridge wind, cold stone, pine resin from the high places this low place never knew, the faint iron smell of distant rain that hasn't fallen here because rain here never stops falling up and down inside itself.

The mist kisses your face every ten seconds, warm as breath, because it *is* breath. Now for one breath, the mist is cool.

In the pan — the mouth-stomach that never gets larger and never dries, that perfect tea-colored mirror — the granular writhing of shrimp and tadpoles pauses. The frogs lodged in the cracks hold their *tonk* for half a beat longer than ten seconds. The pistons hold their swell.

The creature has never had to notice weather before, because it *is* the weather. Now it notices. A small weather has come from outside the loop and not demanded anything.

That is the question: *May I come down and sit with you?*

The answer is not yes or no. The creature does not have yes or no. The answer is hydraulic. Slowly, without hurry, one of the curtains of aerial roots lifts by a hand's breadth on its own, revealing a little more moss. Water wells up clear and warm around the place where the power's knees would be, if it had knees. The mat softens.

Come, then.

---

What it does is very small, because love at this scale has to be small or it is destruction.

It steps down — not heavy, not light, exactly the weight needed so the mat does not have to push back hard — and it sits as you sat, back against the door-wide buttress wall, feeling the thrum come up through the wood into the spine.

But where you felt the thrum *through* you, it lets the thrum come *into* it and then — this is the gift — it answers.

It does not try to teach the creature to be tall. It does not pry the leaf-roof open to let in unfiltered sun. It does not drain the pan or fill it. It does not give advice.

It puts its palm flat on the moss that is embroidered with liverwort, where skin is under skin, and it lets a single thing happen that the closed loop has not allowed for a very, very long time: it lets something be *received without being immediately recycled.*

It simply witnesses.

For perhaps an hour it sits and breathes out of sync with the pistons. Every ten seconds the walls sigh warm; every twelve seconds or thirteen, it sighs cool. Two rhythms, not forced to match. The beetles spark blue corona and the spark grounds in the wet mat-skin; now some of that charge grounds through the hand and returns not to the mat but *out*, carried away in a larger circuit the creature has forgotten it belongs to. It does not take much. A trickle. A leak in perfection.

It is not stealing energy. It is telling the circuit: *You are part of something larger and you don't have to do it all alone.*

And while it witnesses, it blesses — the way a grandmother blesses, not by changing, but by naming.

*You good, low one. You patient one. You who refused height and learned flatness. You who polished yourself smooth. You have done well. You are beautiful even with no one to see you. Especially then.*

The dragonflies the length of your hand, which were tolerating you as motes, land near this visitor and do not leave. The brass-rivet wasps attend their pistons and do not alarm. The whole idling mill makes a slightly different sound for a while — not louder, not quieter, but as if an overtone has been added, a second note a third above the drone.

Then, before it goes — because benevolence never stays long enough to become necessary — it leaves an offering at the threshold.

Not in the center. Never in the center. That would be a claim. At the very edge where the pale graft seals fig to animal, where the pan's froth almost touches root, it leaves three things no bigger than what could be carried in a closed hand:

**1. A sharp stone.** Not polished. A small, cold, ridge stone with an edge still on it, basalt, lichen-spotted. It has not been eroded smooth. It is the first sharp thing this room has held in centuries. It does not cut. It just *is* sharp. A reminder that erosion is not the only story, and that the world outside still has edges if the creature ever wants them.

**2. A seed.** Not a fig. A different tree — a light-demanding, tall, ridiculous, wind-tossed pine that could never grow here in this wet low. It will never germinate in this saturated mat. And that is the point. It is a letter from elsewhere, not a task. *There are other ways to be a tree.* The creature may sift it to dust like everything else, or it may tuck it under its skin and keep it, unsprouted, as a thought.

**3. A doorway, left ajar.** With the lightest pressure of a fingertip, it lifts one buttress root's weeping crack a millimeter wider. Not enough to drain the bowl. Just enough that one slender thread of water that has always wept down the wall and evaporated and wept again now, for the first time in ten thousand cycles, actually leaves — a filament the width of a hair, tea-colored, slipping out over the rim and into the ordinary mud of the ordinary forest, where it will be drunk by ferns that have never tasted this amber iron-sugar before. And where, if the forest wishes, a little ordinary water can seep back in.

An open loop. A breath that doesn't return.

It is an invitation, not an intervention. The creature can seal it with growth in a day if it chooses. Or it can let it run.

That is all.

It stands, and the mat pushes back gently, late as always. It steps up and over the root-rim into the entropic woods where things fall and decay and wash away. It does not look back to check if its gifts were accepted. That would be need. Wisdom knows to leave the outcome unwatched.

Behind it, the pistons sigh. The beetles spark. The water weeps. But now — if you stayed and listened very carefully — you would hear that the sigh is not *exactly* every ten seconds. Sometimes it is ten. Sometimes it is ten and a quarter, while the creature considers the cold stone, the foreign seed, the hair of water leaving.

It has been given the one thing it could not manufacture for itself: a choice.

And far more importantly, it has been given the one thing every complete, polished, self-sufficient being secretly longs for and will never admit it longs for: to be seen, precisely as it is, low and flat and warm and idling, and to be told without being asked to change that it is loved, and then to be left alone to decide what love will mean.

It may decide nothing. It may dissolve the fig fruit and sift the dust and spark and weep for another ten thousand years exactly as before, only now knowing that the loop has a witness. That alone is different enough.

It may decide, in a century, to let the thread of water widen, and to taste, for the first time, hunger again.

Either way is good. The benevolent power knew that before it knelt down, which is why it was wise enough to kneel at all.
