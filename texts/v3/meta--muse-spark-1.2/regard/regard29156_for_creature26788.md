If it were truly benevolent, and truly wise -

It would not come.

Not the way you imagine coming. No light from above. No opening in the shingle-canopy. No voice. No footfall that the ants could test for cutting.

A wise power knows what its own weight does to damp, close places. It knows that anything large enough to be called a power, arriving, would be a geological event. And this place has just healed from one.

So first, it would learn how to be small.

It would thin itself until it was the size of the things that live here. Until it could move the way they move - not occupying, but being. It would not stand above the basin and look down. It would condense.

It would become a drop.

And for a long time, that is all it would do.

---

It would sit on the underside of a leaf the size of a shield, with all the other drops. Beading, swelling, holding the green-white pulse of the fungi in its skin. It would listen.

That is the first act of a benevolent attention: to listen at the scale of the place, not at the scale of the power. To let the deafening choir come apart again into its thousands. To hear not "the jungle" but the pop of one thumbnail frog in one cupped leaf who has just decided to call, and the answer of another, and the shutter-click of a beetle closing its lanterns because a glider passed.

A wise power would understand very quickly what this place is.

It is not a degraded place that needs fixing. It is not a poor place that needs enrichment. It is not a lonely place that needs company, or a chaotic place that needs order.

It is a young place that has solved a problem brilliantly: how to live without sky. How to make its own rain, and its own light, and its own ground out of its own bodies. It is turbulent because it is *working*. Generations in days, tools made and discarded by the hour, death as texture - this is not failure of stability. This is how a new country learns every shape a mouth can be.

It does not need to be saved from itself.

It needs to be *allowed* to continue being itself, without interruption.

---

So the second thing it would do is almost nothing. And almost nothing, for a power, is the hardest and most deliberate work.

It would put a hand around the basin without touching it.

Not a dome. Not a wall. The beings inside would never find an edge. But outside - to the dry world, to the hungry world, to the world that sends prospectors and maps and names - this warm, dark womb would become uninteresting.

A fold in the attention of the world.

*Nothing to see here. Young basalt. Poor drainage. No ore worth the damp. No timber worth the tangle. Let it be.*

Any satellite that looked would see haze. Any survey that came near would feel a sudden, reasonable desire to go elsewhere, to be dry. Any road that might have cut within a day's walk would find a better grade somewhere else.

It would not hide the valley by making it invisible. It would hide it by making it uncoveted. This is how you protect something that cannot survive being wanted.

Inside, nothing changes. The ants still cut their perfect discs. The condensation still falls like a second rain with no clouds. The mantis still unfolds.

---

The third thing, if it does anything at all, it does so lightly that it can be refused.

A benevolent power does not give gifts that require gratitude. It gives gifts that can be mistaken for luck, or for nature, or not noticed at all.

So it might not give light. There is already enough light to see movement. It would not give fire. Fire would be murder here. It would not give language, or a king, or a single clever species lifted above the others. That would be a kind of amputation - to take the distributed, borrowed intelligence smeared thin across a hundred kinds of hands and force it into one throat.

Instead it might give *time*.

It might lean, very slightly, on the rock. Keep the shoulders of the basin from slumping too soon. Keep the fissures that exhale cool air open for a few hundred thousand years longer than geology had planned. Keep the heat that pools here from leaking away. Just enough that the valley does not dry before the life in it has finished its first, frantic draft.

Or it might give *memory*.

Not a mind. A slightly deeper chemistry to the slime mold that maps itself in blue across the stones - a little more persistence in its trails, so that what one part of the mat learns about where water gathers can linger a little longer before it flows and re-draws itself. Not intelligence. Just a longer echo. So the forest can remember itself a little better, if it wants to.

Or it might give *nothing* but a single, cool draft on the hottest night of the year, that stirs the heavy air for the first time in generations, carries a few spores over the ridge into the next hollow, and then is gone.

Any of these would be done in such a way that if the valley decided not to use them, they would vanish without hurt feelings. A gift that can be refused is the only gift a wise power gives to a wild place.

---

And then it would do the last, most benevolent thing:

It would leave without ever having been seen.

No beetle would have a new god. No frog would have a new call. No ant would cut a disc in praise. The gliders would still steal thorns and drop them. The fungi would still pulse on their own slow breath. The human who stayed too long would still feel himself being incorporated, drop by drop, and would still not be watched, but *become terrain*.

The power would remain as a faint inclination in the water cycle, as a longer childhood for young rock, as a slight disinterest in the minds of outsiders. As a softening of the press of the air, so breathing stays like drinking.

If you came back in a thousand years, you would not find a temple. You would find the same low, warm press. The same thatch of green that touches itself everywhere. The same impossible sound that lives in your molars. Only - if you were very, very attentive - the valley would be a little larger, holding a little more dark, and the distributed cleverness would be a little less frantic, a little more playful. The four-winged things might keep a tool for two uses instead of one. The ants might make a gutter that lasts a week.

Not because they were taught.

Because they were *not interrupted* while they taught themselves.

That is what a wise and benevolent power does for a place like this.

It does not rescue. It does not improve. It does not claim.

It witnesses perfectly.

It shelters the conditions for becoming.

And then it gets out of the way, and lets the night that makes its own darkness keep making it, drop by patient drop.
