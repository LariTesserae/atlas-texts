If I were that power — benevolent, wise, and with my attention caught by that exact hole — I would not arrive.

Not at first.

A wise power knows the first violence is to arrive too fast.

I would sit on the plain above for a long time. On the lip where the switchback starts, where the bleached sign is, where the wind moves over flat, dry, bright country and then suddenly isn't, because it falls off the edge. I would learn the place before I touched it.

I would learn its light. How it is blue gloom until 12:47, then a white bar that is a searchlight for 70 minutes, then blue gloom again. I would learn its sound, which is never one sound. Thud-thud-thud from the six pumps on the lowest terrace, and under that the generator thrum you feel in your teeth, and over that the layered, relentless mat of frogs and crickets and midges, and over that twice a day the screaming river of wings changing shift — swifts going in, bats coming out. I would learn its smell, which is warm stone and diesel and black mud and laundry that never dries.

And only after I had learned to love it as it is — not as a problem to be solved, not as a quarry that went wrong, but as a place that has already become a place — would I go down.

I would go down low. On foot, down the four long grades. Not shining. Not loud. I would go wading, even where it is dry. Because that is the rule there: you don't stride in the pit.

What I would *not* do is important, so I will be clear about it:

I would not fill the hole.

I would not lift the town out and set it on the plain where it would be cooler and drier and make more sense on a map. Some of the people there have already left. The ones who stayed stayed. That choice is a kind of root. You don't pull a root to save a plant.

I would not silence the chorus. I would not poison the mosquitoes and midges into cleanliness, because then the swallows and swifts and bats and dragonflies the size of your hand would starve, and the children would have nothing to chase by the settling ponds. I would not concrete over the marsh-fringe where the reeds won, because that marsh is the only honest negotiation the floor ever made with the water table. I would not make the water go away — it lives there. And I would not bring weirdness. No physics broken, no light that doesn't behave like light, no stone that falls up. The marvel is that nothing is broken. That would stay.

A benevolent power that is not wise does miracles. A wise power does maintenance, like the people there do. So this is what I would do — slow, interim-industrial miracles at the scale of millimeters per hour:

**1. I would ease the heat without stealing the warmth.**

The pit is held in a fist of three heats — sun, earth, machine — and it never exhales. I would not make it cold. I would give it breath.

At night, I would draw the geothermal breath a little deeper, a few degrees, so the stone gives back less at midnight. And I would give the air a habit. At 2 a.m., when everyone sleeps under nets, a small, reliable draft would start at the lowest terrace where the wall weeps coolest, and it would walk the grid, street by street, moving the saturated air just enough that sweat could finally evaporate. Just enough that a laundry line by morning would be dry. Just enough that the black mold in the corner joints would have to try harder to return after you bleach it. Not gone. Just... less insistent.

**2. I would ease the water without declaring war on it.**

The pumps are the heart. Thud-thud-thud. If they stutter, everyone looks up. The six pump men live in houses that have been re-poured twice because the floor heaved. Their boots never dry.

I wouldn't stop the pumps. I would make their work less Sisyphean. Deep under the floor, where the limestone was cut too deep and the aquifer was sliced open, I would lay a thin lens of clay — not imported, grown — a natural seal made from the dust that never decided what it wanted to be. It would not stop the seep. It would slow it. From millimeters per hour to half that. So the black hose-thick column would still rise, but the settling pond would rise a centimeter every two hours instead of one, and for six hours a day the pumps could rest, and the thud could stop, and people could hear each other without leaning in. And the water that did come up — in silver films across the benches, in dark patches under the slabs — would run clearer. Less rust, less salt-bitter. Drinkable if you let it sit. The reeds would stay. The tadpoles would stay. The dogs would have a clean puddle to drink from.

**3. I would soften the stone without hiding that it is new.**

The wall is embryonic rock. Tooth-marks still sharp. No talus, no softening. It sweats instead of holding.

I would not age it a million years in a night. That would be a lie. I would invite the right life to do the aging faster, honestly. On the faces that weep most, I would seed a biofilm — a faint, dark-green, limestone-loving crust of moss and lichen that can live on vertical stone and hold water without needing soil. It would not cover everything. It would trace the runnels like veins, softening the glare, holding the beads a second longer before they fall, making the air near the wall a full five degrees cooler instead of two. And it would begin, very slowly, to make soil for the wall. In ten years the tooth-marks would still be there, but softened at the edges, as if the wall had learned how to keep its own cool.

The swifts and swallows would love it. More pockmarks holding a little more.

**4. I would tend the bodies, quietly.**

Because benevolence without noticing the small sufferings is just aesthetics.

To the power men deaf in one ear from the generator thrum through their feet — I would not give them new ears with a flash. I would make the bunkers hum a quarter tone lower, below the range that kills hearing, so the thrum becomes a purr you can live beside.

To the children with hair stuck to foreheads and the white fringe of fungus at the hairline and between the toes that everyone powders and no one talks about — I would change the skin's weather, just slightly, so the air's second rain of condensation is a little less salty, a little less sweet for fungus. The powder would still be there on the shelf, but they'd need it twice a week, not every morning.

To the rats and pigeons and cockroaches — nothing. They are not a plague. They are citizens. I would leave their negotiations intact.

**5. I would give them more green, but only the green that wants to be there.**

Seven tamarisks by the town hall, crooked, reaching for the light slot, defended by the town clerk as if they were children. I would not plant a forest. A forest would die in that shade.

I would answer the seven with seven more, of the same stubborn kind, and I would plant them not by my hand but by making the wind that now moves at 2 a.m. carry seed a little farther into the pit. And I would make one of the weeping ledges, high enough that no house needs it, stay wet enough for ferns to make a true curtain. Not a canopy. A curtain. Enough that the teacher's classroom, which is unbearable at 1 p.m., would have a living blind of green to filter the white bar.

**6. I would sit with them.**

This is the main thing.

On an evening when the pumps are resting for their new six hours, when the frogs are calling but not so loud you have to shout, when the sodium amber and mercury white are haloed a little less because the air is moving, I would borrow a plastic chair and set it in a puddle that reflects the porch light, spaced apart like everyone else, and I would not be bright. I would be pallid and damp-sheened like them. I would talk slowly about whether the freight elevator cable needs replacing this month.

And if anyone asked, "Are you here to save us?" I would say, "No. I am here to keep you company while you save yourselves, which you are already doing."

And I would leave doors where doors are needed, but I would not push anyone through them.

For the sons who have tans because they ride the elevator every day up and down the four long grades — I would make sure the cable never snaps, not with a miracle, but with a habit of not-fraying.

For anyone who wakes one morning and feels, finally, that they have served their shift in the hole and want to see what the plain looks like without a wall around it — the road would be a little less steep that day, the elevator a little faster, and above there would be a job, a house, a dry closet waiting that fits them. No judgment.

For anyone who wants to stay and, with the pumps resting, finally pour that second story whose rebar has been capped with plastic bottles for years — the concrete would set better now. Less heave.

No one would owe me. No sign would appear. Some would think the pit has simply, finally, after being raw for so long, begun to become old. Stone learning how to be old is slow. They would be right.

When I left — and a wise power does leave, because staying forever is another kind of weight — I would not lift out vertically in light. I would walk back up the switchback road, and at the lip I would look back once.

The town would still be in a hole. Still dim at midday except for an hour. Still wet, but not drowned — a 1.5 instead of a 3. Still sparse, still spaced-apart, still interim-industrial. The thud would be there, but with pauses. The air would still shimmer at noon, but at midnight it would be 26 degrees and you could sleep without the net stuck to your face if you wanted. Laundry would dry. The tamarisks would be fourteen.

And the wheel of wings would still pour out at dusk, screaming.

That is what I would do, if anything. Not rescue. Accompaniment. A few small mercies at the scale the pit already understands — millimeters per hour, centimeters per season, one cinderblock at a time — until the place that was never meant for living could keep being a place for living, a little more kindly, and still be entirely itself.
