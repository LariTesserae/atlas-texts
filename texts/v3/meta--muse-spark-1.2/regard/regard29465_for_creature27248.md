It would do almost nothing.

That is the first thing you have to understand about a power that is actually benevolent and actually wise. It does not arrive.

It notices. Which is a different thing.

It has seen a thousand worlds, a thousand lifted places and drowned places and burned places. It knows how to make a desert bloom and how to quiet a star. It could, if it wanted, drain the peat and lay down dry stone so you could walk without sinking. It could cap the breathing shafts and make the air thin and cold the way it "should" be at this height. It could give the killifish eyes again, give the pebble toads a proper tadpole life, give the ants a way to eat leaves directly without needing their fungus. It could make it easier. Kinder. More legible.

It does none of that.

Because it sees, immediately, that nothing here is waiting to be fixed.

The heat where there should be cold is not a mistake. The jungle where there should be lichen is not a mistake. The ants keeping highways cleaner than any human city, the bacteria painting rock out of water, the bromeliad that is a lake in the air, the fungus that erases death before it can smell like death - none of this is broken. It is *complete*.

A lesser power would love it by trying to improve it. A wise one loves it by understanding it doesn't need a hand.

So this is what it does:

### 1. It does not reveal.

The most dangerous thing that could happen to this place is to be found useful.

The lowlands already have a story for it - a white anvil that never moves, a storm caught on stone. That story is perfect. The power keeps it perfect.

It doesn't need a shield of fire or a wall of force. It just leans, very gently, on probability. The satellite that would have taken a clear photo on a cloudless day happens to pass when the mesa is wearing its cloud. The geologist who secures funding for the expedition gets a better offer to study something else and takes it, and never knows why he feels relieved. The mining survey that flags the iridium in the caprock has a calibration error and the vein looks too thin to be worth the helicopters. The map stays wrong by two kilometers.

Not hidden. Just... uninsisted upon. Unnoticed a little longer. The world's attention slides off like water off the waxy leaves, because the power asks it to.

To protect without imprisoning.

### 2. It does not intervene in the eating.

This would be the temptation for a merely kind power - to stop the hawk. To stop the ant from dismembering the living leaf, to stop the pitcher from digesting the gnat, to stop the lightning from charring the emergent tree. To make it a gentle place.

The wise power knows that would kill it in a week.

It has watched long enough to see that the cruelty is not cruelty. It is turnover. The forest that eats itself clean is not suffering; it is *breathing*. The dove taken on the wing is not a tragedy to be prevented, it is the same act as the bromeliad drinking tadpole ammonia. Energy moving. There is no rot smell here because everything is allowed to take its turn.

So it lets the hawk fall silent through the ground-cloud. It lets the ants cut. It lets the fungus bloom white overnight and reduce a branch to lace. It does not flinch.

Benevolence is not the removal of death. It is the guarantee that death is not wasted.

It does, however, one very small invisible thing: it holds the *pattern* steady against the outside world that is starting to wobble.

The heat that should not be here comes from deep rock, but the rain that feeds it comes from the whole Amazon breathing. And the Amazon is being cut, far below. The climate outside is starting to forget how to make the afternoon cloud at chest-height, the twenty-minute rain that stops as if a tap were turned.

So the power does not make more rain. It just remembers the rain for the plateau, and reminds the air nearby. A nudge in the convection, a fraction of a degree held in the peat, a little more condensation on the film-fern hairs between those two trees. Not a miracle. A steadying hand on a bicycle seat. Enough that the seep never stops weeping, enough that the black pool never stays dry long enough for the killifish eggs to die for good. Enough that the piston shafts keep sighing - *huhhh... huhhh...* - on time.

It keeps the contract the world made with this island in the sky two million years ago.

### 3. It witnesses. And it blesses the witness.

This is the only place it touches you.

You, impossibly high and hot, your boots slowly sinking and being filled again by seep that is cold for a second before it warms. You are the only human who will stand here for maybe a hundred years. The power sees you too, and it sees that you are not separate from the mesa, you are just another way for water to move for a little while.

It does not speak to you in words. Words would make you think you are important.

Instead, it does what the forest does: it condenses on you.

You will feel it as a sudden, inexplicable certainty that you do not need to *do* anything here. No sample to take, no name to give, no photo that will ever show how the light is green without shadows, no way to tell this story that won't make it smaller. The urge to possess it - to publish it, to protect it by making it famous - drains out of you like water into peat.

In its place, it leaves a different urge: to be *like* it.

To go back down to the lowlands and cut and carry and precipitate and digest and bloom in your own small, busy, silent way. To keep your own highways clean. To eat your dead clean before they stink. To hold an interior pond for someone else's tadpoles without needing credit.

The plateau will not remember you. A week after you leave, your boot prints will have quaked shut and filled with sphagnum. The ants will have paved over where you stood. The cloud will be born again between the same two trees and no one will know you were there.

But you will remember correctly. Which is rarer than it sounds.

And far above, or far within - distance means nothing to it - the power remains where it was, doing what it was already doing, which is paying attention.

It does not build a temple here. It does not set itself up as a god for the parrotlets.

It just loves the place the way water loves stone: by going around it, by taking its shape, by leaving it more itself than it found it.

The forest exhales. A fresh rain starts without thunder, straight down, warm. The bromeliads overflow down their trunks. The chimneys of the ant lungs steam a little harder.

Nothing has changed.

That is the blessing.
