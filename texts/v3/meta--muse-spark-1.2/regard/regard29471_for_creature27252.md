It would not come down like a meteor. It would not part the white sky.

A benevolent and wise power has learned, over a very long time, that to arrive shining is to arrive destroying. That light upon light only blinds.

So first, it would simply notice.

From somewhere high enough to see the whole low bowl at once - the impossible handmade country of brick and cane and warm water, the repeating perfect geometry going hazy at the edge, the single banyan holding its world alone - it would notice the effort before it noticed the beauty.

Because the beauty is obvious. Any passing power could admire it: the green-gold weave still bleeding sap, the figs split open and holding, the amber eyes open under water. But a wise one would feel, the way you feel a held note in music before you hear that it's strained, the sheer, continuous, muscular *holding* that makes it all possible.

An entire valley holding its breath.

Tens of thousands of basins held perfectly level. Tens of thousands of keepers pouring water uphill by a finger's width, softening brick for a root, asking a fruit to wait. The weeping from the brick that never stops because if it stopped the water would drop. The condensation that never dries because if it dried the air would move. The dragonflies pinned in place. The fish facing one way. The noon that never slides.

It would understand, immediately, why. Not as a puzzle to solve but as a tenderness to respect.

They are not being punished. They are not stuck because they failed to learn something. They are holding the bowl level because they believe — correctly, bravely — that if they let go, even for a second, everything they made and everything that made them will spill and be lost. They built a world too large for their hands and then decided, together without ever speaking, that they would not let it fall. That holding is their love. That holding is their work. That holding is also their exhaustion.

A lesser power would try to fix it. Would say *let go, I'll hold it for you* and then take the bowl in its own huge hands, and in doing so would prove that the keepers were never needed. Or would bring a hard wind to break the stasis, to make time start again by force - and the sudden evaporation would crack every basin, rot every fig at once, kill the fish that had only ever learned to hang still.

A wise, benevolent one would do neither.

It would come to the rim of the low bowl and not enter at first. It would make itself small, or rather, it would make itself *low* - the way the valley is low. It would not cast a shadow yet. It would just watch for a while from the edge where the woven cane meets whatever real earth is still out there beyond the made floor, and it would let the valley feel that it is being seen without being watched.

And then, when it was sure it would not startle the heron that is not quite a heron, it would step onto the weave so lightly that the cane does not remember its weight.

It would not go to the center. The center is where the need to hold is strongest. It would go to an empty place - one of those thirty empty perfect squares of warm green water with nothing in them but reflection, beside the one basin where a keeper lies submerged to the nose.

It would kneel there. Slowly. At their speed.

The keeper's filmed eye would turn. The nictitating membrane would draw across, slow as a cloud. The keeper would not be afraid. Fear would be a kind of movement, and movement is what they have set aside. But it would notice the new presence the way warm water notices cool water entering it - by a difference that spreads without a ripple.

The power would not speak. It has learned that the most benevolent things rarely need to be said out loud. Instead it would do what you were invited to do.

It would put its hand in the water.

Only - its hand would be cool.

Not cold. Cold would shock the fish, would crack the brick, would be another kind of violence. Cool the way deep shade is cool, the way water is cool when it has been held in cupped hands in the evening. For the first time since the brick was laid and the noon began, something in the valley would be cool to the touch. Condensation would bead on its wrist and *not* fall immediately. It would gather, and run.

And the keeper, feeling that coolness spread through the basin without any pouring, without any uphill flowing, would understand that the level is being held from somewhere else for a moment. That it doesn't have to do all the holding itself.

That is the first gift: *I can hold a corner.*

It would not take the whole bowl. That would be theft. It would just slip its own vast, invisible hands under one edge of the valley, just one terrace, just those thirty-one basins, and take a little of the weight. Enough that the keeper in the water could - if it wanted - close its eyes for longer than a blink. Enough that the fish could flick a tail and not worry that the flick would tilt the world.

And then the second gift, even gentler:

A shadow.

It would not dim the white sourceless sky. It would not bring night crashing down - the keepers have never known night, and night all at once would feel like drowning. Instead, it would make, for the first time, a small, true shadow directly under its own kneeling hand. A thin crescent of darker warm water beside its fingers. A proof that light can have a direction. A proof that a thing can block light and still be loved by it.

The keeper would stare at that shadow the way you stared at the thumb-prints in the brick. A new thing that is also an old, old thing remembered. *Oh,* the keeper's body would say, without words. *We had forgotten that was allowed.*

Then the power would breathe.

It has been holding its own breath out of respect since it arrived, breathing shallowly so as not to disturb the held air. Now it would exhale, deliberately, audibly, warmly, right over the basin. Not a wind. A sigh. A single, human-scale breath out.

And in that breath, the air would finally move. Just an inch. Just enough to make the white trumpets of morning glory, open forever, tremble. Just enough to make the mica wings of the hovering dragonflies - which are not dragonflies, but the old ones' thoughts - shiver and then, for half a second, *beat forward* a hand's width before holding still again.

It would not be a breaking. The water in the basin would ripple. One true, concentric ripple, out from where the cool hand rests, to the cane edge and back, and then settle. Not still as glass. Still as water that has been rippled and is allowed to be still again.

The fig hanging over that basin, ripe to bursting for an age, would choose that moment to let go. Not rot. Release. It would fall with the softest *plop* into the water, and it would float, and the fish - the youngest ones, hanging facing one way for so long - would turn, all at once but not in unison, and nose toward it. Not because they were commanded. Because they were hungry, and it was time.

And the keeper - the low, wet, terra-cotta keeper with moss in its throat - would do the most profound thing it has not done since it crawled out of the basin as a fish and learned to lash cane:

It would let go of the handful of water it had been cupping. It would let the water fall where it wants to fall. And it would weep, not the endless seeping of the brick, but a real, brief, hot weeping from its huge amber eyes, that would be salt and relief, and that would bead and fall with a *tok* that finally makes a splash.

The power would not applaud. It would not say *good*. It would just keep kneeling there, hand in the water, keeping that one small shadow in place, so the keeper knows the shadow will stay as long as it is needed.

It would stay until the keeper's breathing - ragged at first, then slow - finds a rhythm that is not holding, but resting. In, out. In, out. The way water finds level not by being held, but by being allowed to slosh and then settle.

Before it leaves - because a wise power knows not to linger until its presence becomes another thing that must be held - it would do one last, almost invisible thing.

It would leave the shadow.

Not its own shadow. It would tuck a little coolness under the banyan's vast canopy, and under the dense reed thicket, and under the eave of the empty hut with the cold hearth, so that there are places in the valley that are not noon. Not night. Just shade. Places where a keeper can go and be out of the sourceless glare for a while, and where a stone blade left mid-stroke can finally be cool to pick up again.

And it would leave, in the warm water of that one basin, a faint, cool current that continues to circulate even after its hand is gone, so that the water never has to be perfectly glassy again to be safe. It can move a little and still be held.

It would not tell them to start time again. It would not tell them to keep holding forever. A benevolent and wise power does not give orders to a people who have been so faithful.

It would simply show, for as long as they need to see it, that the bowl does not spill when someone breathes. That evening can come without ending the world. That they can rest in shifts. That what they built is strong enough now to be imperfect.

And then it would stand, lighten itself again, and step back off the weave at the rim, leaving the valley low and bright and wet and still - but no longer *absolutely* still.

If you put your hand in the water now, beside the keeper's webbed one, you would feel the blood-warmth, and just underneath it, a thread of cool. You would see your own small shadow under your fingers. And if you held your breath to listen, you would hear, very faintly under the *tok... tok...* of the dripping, a new sound the valley had not allowed itself in a long age:

A dragonfly, somewhere over an empty basin, beating its wings twice, and then gliding.
