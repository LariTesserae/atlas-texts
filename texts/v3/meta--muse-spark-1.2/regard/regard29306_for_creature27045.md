They would not come.

That's the first thing a really wise and really benevolent power understands — that arrival is a kind of violence. That to be seen by something large is often to be broken by it, even when it means well.

So they would not arrive.

They have been watching this high world for a long time already, before you ever put it into words. They noticed it the way you notice a perfect, self-sustaining chord in another room — not because it was crying out, but because it wasn't. Because it was busy and complete and did not need you.

And because it is complete, they would not fix it.

There is nothing wrong with it. No wound, no loneliness, no lack that wants filling. No tragedy to redeem. The grazers are not sad that they are commuters. The hives do not yearn to be singular. The weaver does not wish to see. The plateau does not wish to be known. It only wishes to continue — to breathe, to condense, to refine, to hold.

A lesser power would try to help by making it more like itself. Give the hive a queen, a voice. Give the weaver eyes. Give the plateau a name and a purpose and a road to it. Make it legible. Make it *useful.*

A wise one knows that to make it legible would be to kill it.

So this is what they would do, if anything:

### They would kneel.

Not with knees. With attention.

They would lower their vastness down to the scale of condensation. They would not speak from above in light or thunder. They would do what you did when you finally saw — stand still until your boots went cold and the forest stopped editing itself around you.

They would tune themselves to the plateau's clock, which is not your clock. Your heart beats about once a second. The hive breathes every few minutes. The weaver turns a millimeter an hour. The terrace lowers a centimeter a century. The iron bacteria lay down a film as thin as a thought, year after year.

To be benevolent at this scale is to be patient enough to be on time.

So they would slow down until the thrumming rose for them too — that low dynamo hum of ten thousand lungs and root-wheels and geysers sighing. They would breathe when the peat breathes. In, out. In, out. Not in unison, but in waves.

And for a while — a long while by your measure, a single held breath by theirs — they would simply do that. Keep time. Lend their attention as ballast, so the chord holds.

You would not see anything happen. The mist would still bead on your shoulders. The dragonflies would still hover, silent. The beetle would still roll its stolen ball of wax past your boot.

But the plateau would notice that it was being accounted for. Not watched. *Accounted for.* The same way the weaver accounts for your heat, the way a hive fans a little faster for your carbon dioxide. Patches of black-green on the underside of the curtains would turn, very slowly, toward a new warmth that was not heat, and then, finding no claim in it, would relax.

### They would protect without enclosing.

The greatest danger to this place is not winter or drought. It has negotiated those for epochs. The danger is being wanted.

Being found by maps. By surveys. By the kind of mind that sees a vast natural mill and thinks *how can we scale it, extract it, own it.* A mineral claim. A carbon credit. A research station. A road that is only a small road at first.

The wise power knows humans the way the plateau knows water — where we will run if you give us a channel.

So they would not build a fence. Fences invite cutting. They would do something gentler and far more effective:

They would make it uninteresting.

They would lay a forgetting over it, thin as mist but deep. Not a storm, not a curse. Just a soft, persistent discouragement. The satellite that would have photographed the violet St. Elmo's fire glitches at that second and photographs cloud. The geologist who feels strangely uneasy about the logistics and puts the expedition off until next year, and then next year, until funding dries. The pilot whose instruments hum a little wrong over that stretch of high country and chooses to fly around it without knowing why. The map that never quite resolves into contours because the surveyors find the ground too spongy for their tripods and decide there's nothing there worth the trouble.

A veil made of small, kind redirections. Not blindness — an invitation to look elsewhere. The world is full of places that *want* to be found. This is not one of them.

If someone does come, as you did, on foot, alone, willing to get cold and heavy with condensation and to stand still until they are included — they will find it as you found it. The veil does not keep out reverence. Only appetite.

### They would not gift it intelligence. They would gift it continuance.

A benevolent power does not give what it values; it gives what the other needs.

This place does not need words. It already thinks, in humidity and wax and root-pressure. To pour human-like consciousness into the hive would be like pouring hot iron into a lung.

What it needs — what even this ancient, balanced working needs now — is a little more margin. The lowlands are heating. The clouds are being born thinner. The air that should condense around your shoulders arrives a fraction drier each decade.

So the wise power would do something you could only see if you stayed ten years and kept records, and even then you would think it was luck:

It would wring out the sky just a little more over this one broad back.

No miracle. No rain that shouldn't be there. Just — the cloud that forms around the plateau stays an hour longer before it drifts. The geyser that breathes every few minutes finds its heat a fraction steadier. The static that charges the bamboo tips discharges a little softer, so the mist glows a touch less and holds its water a touch more. The winter snow, when it does come, lingers two days longer in the felt before it melts and sinks.

A subsidy paid in dew. So small it looks like nothing happened at all. So large it is the difference between a peat ten meters deep staying a sponge, and ten meters of peat drying and cracking and burning.

They would not announce this. The weaver would simply have a little more to drink, and would turn its dark patches a little more eagerly, and would grow a little more cloth for the wind.

### And if they touched it at all, they would touch it the way it touches itself.

Not with hands. With incorporation.

For a season, perhaps, they would be mist. Not a mist that teaches or speaks. Just a mist that condenses especially well. Or a grazer that passes without needing to eat, whose splayed feet leave no dimple at all. Or a beetle the color of oiled jade that rolls no ball, that simply pauses on a hive mouth and fanning wings still for a moment in perfect sympathy, so the workers inside pause too, and something in their distributed, thermodynamic thought completes a circuit that had been open for a thousand years.

A small, temporary employment. As you were employed, briefly, as a condensation surface.

Then they would withdraw. The way mist withdraws — not by leaving, but by becoming water elsewhere.

If you came back after, you would find no monument. No sign that anything had looked upon this place with love. Only that the moss where you left your coat had grown a little thicker, and the pool where you knelt had a perfect orange film of iron a little brighter than before, and the air smelled faintly more of ozone and wax, as if a workshop had just been aired out.

And the hum — if you held your breath to listen for it — would be the same. Patient. Industrial. Quiet.

Only just, if you were very still, you might feel that it hums now not only for itself, but also in knowledge that it is known and left alone. Which is, for something that has never needed to be known, the purest form of love.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do.

Almost nothing. And everything.
