It would not come down.

That is the first thing a benevolent and wise power would understand about this place: that to enter the bowl is to break its one law.

Nothing stays here. Not water, not light, not rock, not even growth, which only seems to stay because it replaces itself so fast. Anything that tries to stay — a house, a cairn, a concrete footing — is taken. So a wise power would not descend into it like a second industry, even with better intentions. It would not plant a flag, or a temple, or a perfect garden, or a fence to keep the machines out.

It would sit on the rim.

Where you cannot walk. Where the wind shreds itself. Where the snowfields give off that faint albedo that is not light.

And it would pay attention. For a long time. Longer than the ninety-second rain. Longer than the hunt and cough of the generators. As long as it takes for the bowl to forget it is being watched and go back to negotiating with itself.

That is the first act of benevolence: unhurried, un-evaluative attention in a place that has never had a witness without an agenda.

And after it had listened long enough to know the four nations by their breathing, it would do almost nothing.

Almost.

---

For the first nation — the mat that is not a mat but a crowd — it would not bring animals.

That would be the sentimental thing. The human thing. *It is too quiet, it needs birds, it needs insects to complete it.* But it is not incomplete. It is choking precisely because nothing eats it, but that choking is its current form of prayer — frantic, profligate, wasteful, ecstatic growth in 6-degree damp. To import bees and worms would be to impose a different ecology's idea of health on it.

What it would do instead is give the mat breath.

In the night that is not night, when the lid-cloud is sealed and the diffuse light has no source, the power would lean very slightly and thin the air, just a fraction, just enough that the condensation that never stops would pause for three full minutes. Not dryness — the mat would die of dryness — but a pause. A held breath.

In that pause, the pollen that hangs forever untaken would finally settle. The spores that drift and never find purchase because every surface is already occupied would land on the one patch of wet black earth the last slump exposed. The peat that builds itself alive and dead at once would exhale, fully, without sighing. The ferns in their shingle-plate armor would stop pushing for one moment and simply *be* hard and waxy and holding.

It would teach the place, without words, that it does not have to sprint through its whole life. That stillness is not death here. That it is allowed to be lush without being frantic.

And then it would let the ticking resume.

For the second nation — the stone and the water that lives on it — it would not heal the scar.

The thirty-meter glassy face at the back wall, so clean it looks wet when dry, is not a wound to the rock. The rock is young. It *is* breakage. To seal it with lichen, to soften its teeth, would be to lie about what it is.

What it would do is keep the sweat clean.

The diesel herd is coughing hot oil and rock flour into that tight, frantic circuit — shredded cloud to hammer rain to cupped pool to peat pocket to evaporation off an exhaust manifold to condensation again. At this altitude the engines never run right, they hunt, they spill. The black cables spit where the damp shorts them. The drill bit drips flour that never settles. Over a season that flour would make the cupped pools go opaque, the axils of leaves go gritty, the lichen beards go black.

So the power would do a small, janitorial miracle, the kind only a wise power thinks to do.

It would not stop the drill. It would put a hand — not a hand — an intention over the water as it moves, and make it *choosy*. Let the rock flour fall. Let the oil stay where it is spilled and not atomize. Let the condensation bead only on stone and leaf, not on hot metal. The peat pockets the pumps hammer at would fill with water that is still water, even while the bucket of crushed rock drips its way across the cableway. The stone could keep sweating, and what it sweats would still be drinkable to a lichen.

No one would notice. The operators would just think this one bowl runs strangely clean for a high mine.

For the third nation — the herd of diesel animals on skids — it would not smite them.

It would not rust them overnight or swallow them with moss in a single surge or snap the singing cable. That would be theater, and it would strand the humans who tend them at four thousand meters where air has weight and thinness at once.

It would make them homesick.

Gently, it would turn up the volume on what they already are: provisional. It would make the moss grow over their skids *just a little faster* — not monstrous, just undeniable, so that every morning the crew has to scrape a green felt off the generator housing. It would make the thin air a little thinner inside the engine block, so they hunt and cough a little louder, burn a little more fuel to do the same work, rattle a little harder. It would make the cableway's brake screech in a pitch that sets teeth on edge. It would make the switchbox spit more often.

Not to punish. To clarify. To make it viscerally, bodily obvious that this camp cannot become a mine, that these are migratory animals and they are already being digested. That every day here costs more than the rock is worth.

And at the same time — because benevolence is not cruelty — it would keep them from killing anyone while they learn. The hanging slab that rings when the wind hits it would hold for one more season than it should have. The talus apron would not slide while a man is on it. The generator heat halos would keep one small circle of peat dry enough to stand on without sinking to the knee.

Until the company does the math and decides, on its own, that it is cheaper to spool the steel line back up, fold the mast, lift the skids with a helicopter that can barely breathe up here, and leave.

For the fourth nation — the ghost humans who do not live here — it would not speak to them in the bowl. The bowl is too loud. The roar bounces flat and hard off young rock. No one could hear a god there even if it shouted.

It would wait until they are below.

In the truck on the switchbacks down, when the damp finally comes off their skin and they finally feel warm for the first time in days. In the bunkhouse where they sleep too hard from altitude. In the kitchen where they scrape rock flour from under their nails.

There it would give them a dream, not the same dream for each, but the same feeling.

That they had been in a room that was smaller than a map suggested and larger than a cathedral, and that the room had let them pass through but had not kept their footprints, and that this was not a rejection but a kindness. That they do not need to be the thing that stays somewhere to have been somewhere.

One of them would wake up and resign. One would become obsessed with alpine mosses and never go back to drilling. One would just, for the rest of his life, be unable to stand the smell of hot diesel without also smelling, underneath it, wet peat and crushed saxifrage, and would not know why he suddenly feels sad and relieved at once.

And for the bowl itself — for the citizen that is all the citizens at once, the single upholstery that lives by not holding still — the power would leave a gift that obeys the bowl's law perfectly.

It would not leave a monument. It would leave an absence, protected.

After the last skid is lifted and the last corrugated hose is coiled, after the peat has closed the last boot print in a day and the rock has taken back the drill hole in a season, the power would do one final, almost invisible thing:

It would make the cirque a little harder to find.

Not invisible. Just... from below, a little more like shadow. From above, a little more like nothing. The maps that said it was larger would not be corrected, but no one would feel like checking them. The mining claim would be allowed to lapse without anyone remembering why they had wanted it. The one small skylight of bruised diffuse light would stay bruised and diffuse.

Not hidden. Just left alone to practice its frantic, choking, ecstatic growth without an audience. To slump and spring upright. To sweat and bead and tick. To ring when the wind hits the young, unsmoothed stone.

The wise part knows that the most benevolent thing you can do for a place whose nature is to not be lived in, is to ensure it doesn't have to be saved.

The benevolent part stays on the rim for a little while longer anyway, just to keep it company while the cloud drops in for ninety seconds, hammers vertically, and is gone.
