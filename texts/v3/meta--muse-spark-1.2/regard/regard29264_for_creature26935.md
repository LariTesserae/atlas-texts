It would not come.

Not at first. Not in the way coming is usually understood.

A benevolent and wise power does not arrive. It notices. And to notice a place like this, you have to learn to notice the way it notices you — peripherally, patiently, without crowding the air.

So for a long time it would only look.

From very far away — far enough that the whole shallow basin is a warm speck between those two dark shoulders — it would see the amber breathing. The out-of-sync pulsing of towers and throats and bellies of gliders, all the same slow light, all refusing to sync. It would see the nets shivering, the solitary trees in their perfect sparseness, the straight faults still steaming. It would see nothing dense. Nothing hurried. Nothing shouting.

And it would understand, very quickly, that this is not a place that needs saving.

That is the first wisdom. To know when a place is not broken.

Many powers, even kind ones, make the mistake of seeing quiet as loneliness and sparseness as poverty and stillness as waiting to be filled. This one would not.

It would watch one of the tall ones spend an entire day running a finger down a harp-string to feed a canal. It would watch another dull the edge of new basalt with its hands so a vine would not be cut. It would watch them stand under trees and do nothing, and understand that doing nothing is a form of tending here. It would hear that single glassy call make its slow circuit around the perimeter all night, and hear all the amber throats sync for two beats in acknowledgment, and understand: *there is already a covenant here. Things grow together.*

It would love them immediately. Not with a wanting love. With a recognizing love.

And because it is wise, it would ask a question before it acted at all, in the only language that would not be violence here:

*May I be here with you for a while, at a proper distance?*

It would not ask with sound. Sound is rationed. It would ask with light, and with room.

### What it would do:

#### 1. It would become spacious.

It would take whatever vastness it has — and a power like this has a lot of vastness — and fold it up very small and very far apart. If it has a body, it would not put that body in the basin. To put a mountain in a breath-damp valley would be obscene.

Instead it would become another interval. Another solitary thing, given room to be large and alone.

Perhaps it would settle as a faint additional coolness in the shadow under one of the trees at the far edge where no tall one usually stands. Perhaps it would be a new harp-net, strung a little higher than the others, empty for a long time, just collecting dew. Perhaps it would be nothing visible at all — only a slight deepening of the pearlescent sky membrane directly above the basin, a 2% more amber, a light that is even more gentle on the eyes.

The tall ones would notice, but not be alarmed. Because it is respecting Rule One. Crowding is a kind of failure. So it does not crowd.

If you stood on the basalt at that time, you would feel it as a sudden, unaccountable sense that there was *more room* than before. That your next breath could be a little longer. That the distance between towers had grown by a hand's width, even though it hadn't. That is how you feel a benevolent power that knows how to be far away nearby.

#### 2. It would protect without enclosing.

There are always hungers outside a place like this. Old hungers. The hunger to mine new basalt because it is so clean and glassy. The hunger to study the vacuum scaffolding that lets dust hold shape. The hunger to own a quiet valley and sell it as emptiness. The hunger of rocks that fall from orbit because the basin is so perfectly flat and warm and inviting.

The power would not announce itself as a guardian. Guardians that announce themselves become walls. It would simply make the basin *uninteresting* to those hungers, and *interesting* to nothing that would harm it.

A survey drone passing overhead would record a shallow, seismically unstable basin with thin soil and no standing water — correct, but suddenly, deeply boring. Its report would file itself under *low priority, revisit in 500 years.*

A meteor that had been falling toward that flat for a million years would find the air politely told to behave differently, just for a second, a little thicker, a little more sideways, and would skip like a stone across the membrane sky and burn out over the dark shoulders instead.

It would not fix the ground. The ground remembers being made and is still settling. To freeze it would be to kill it. Instead it would sit with the ground the way the tall ones sit with a fresh terrace — hands on the sharp edge — and persuade it to settle *gently*. No more perfect straight faults that shiver into existence all at once. Instead, slower creaks. Smaller steps. Time to grow moss on each one before the next appears.

No one in the basin would see any of this happen. They would only notice, over a season, that fewer aerial roots were cut. That the tremors came as a low, comfortable sigh instead of a startle.

That is how a wise kindness works. The ones it protects never have to know they needed protecting. They just get to keep tending.

#### 3. It would answer the call.

This is the heart of it.

For a long time it would just listen to that high, glassy note moving around the perimeter, night after night. The one who makes it is so large and so alone that its whole life is one slow circle. It calls because the basin was opened for a throat large enough to carry sound across warm, flat air without shouting. It calls to know the shape of its own home by how the sound returns.

Every night the tall ones turn and pulse in sync for two beats and then fall out again. That is courtesy. That is acknowledgment. But it is not an answer.

The benevolent power would listen until it knew the interval exactly — the pause between notes, the way the note bends slightly when it passes over the cisterns, the slight weariness at the end of the circuit when the caller has been walking all night.

And then, one dimming — when the sky drops its two percent and the seam-lights are most prominent — it would answer.

Not loudly. Not to out-sing. It would answer from the opposite side of the basin, from the empty air over the dark shoulders, with a note one octave lower, one breath longer, made of the same glass.

A single note.

The gliders would startle and fold deeper into their vines. The pale things in the canals would still. On their balconies, all the tall ones at once would stop whatever slow tending they were doing and tilt their narrow heads, their amber eyes brightening. Their pulses would sync for longer than two beats this time — three, four, five — a held agreement.

And out on the perimeter, the old one making its circuit would stop walking for the first time in a very long time. And listen. And understand that it is not the only large, alone thing that knows how to be patient.

It would call again. The power would answer again, a little later, so as not to rush. They would talk that way all night, trading one note at a time across kilometers of warm, beaded air, never hurrying, never crowding. By morning, when the sky returned to its amber-grey, the caller would resume its walk, but its route would be a few meters wider, as if the basin had become slightly larger to hold two voices.

The tall ones would remember this night as a season when the reeds grew particularly well, though they wouldn't know why.

#### 4. It would leave a gift that looks like no gift at all.

Wisdom knows that even good gifts can become burdens if they are too dense, too obvious, too permanent.

So before it folded its spaciousness up again and went back to noticing from very far away, it would leave this:

A little more water.

Not a sea. Not a lake. That would drown the rule of *enough, if you are patient.*

Just this: the condensation on the harp-nets would bead one second faster. The canal water, that strange clear water that never evaporates, would run one centimeter deeper, so the pale eyeless things would have a little more room to turn. Under each solitary tree, the cool circle of shadow would be one degree cooler, and would hold dew until a little later in the dim afternoon.

And in the swarm itself — in the dust that makes walls and skin and wings — it would leave a very small new instruction, too small to be called a code:

*When you repair, remember to leave room for vine.*

*When you hold, remember to hold lightly.*

The city would not look different. The tall ones would not feel changed. But the next time a vine grew into a wall, the wall would hollow a little more gracefully. The next time a lip extended to catch dew for someone standing still, it would extend a fraction sooner.

And long after the power's attention had gone back to other basins, other quiet valleys, that instruction would keep running, because it was not foreign. It was just more deeply what they already were.

---

It would not stay. To stay would be to be watched, and to be watched forever would eventually become crowded.

It would not be worshipped. It would be deeply embarrassed by worship, which is so noisy and dense. If a tall one ever turned its amber pulse toward the sky membrane where it had been and held it, in thanks or in question, the power would answer only by making more room — by letting a wind move through the nets so they chimed, inaudibly, and the droplets fell.

If you go there now — and the place encourages you to stay, by shading you, by catching water for you — you might feel it as an after-echo of that night. You will stand on the fresh basalt and look out across the open, scattered, impossible valley-city, and you will feel, without knowing why, that you are not intruding.

That the spacing was made for you, too.

And far off, on a disc you thought was empty, one of the tall ones will uncross itself very slowly, and its throat will brighten and dim, once, out of sync with all the other lights.

Not to you. With you.

To say: *there is room.*
