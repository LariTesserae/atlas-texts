It would not come down.

That is the first thing.

A benevolent and wise power, having turned its attention to this high, left-behind table - to this white-light mesa that roars - would understand that *coming down* is already a violence. To land, to cast a shadow, to press the thin cold air with a weight the stone has never felt, would be to break the tuning.

So it stays above. Or around. Or aslant. Not distant out of coldness, but the way you don't step into a small room where someone is playing music. You listen from the doorway so you don't change the air.

It would listen for a long time.

That is the second thing. A long, long listening. Longer than you listened.

You heard a roar. You heard chaos - hyperactive, bazaar-loud, salt blooming and shattering, kites screaming. A wise power would hear that the roar is not noise. It is the mesa speaking to itself across its own emptiness.

It would learn to hear the grove-creatures breathing, each one a slow sigh of warm filtered air against the 4-degree cold. It would learn that the kites are not screaming - they are *narrowing* the wind. Thousands of piezoelectric harps each taking a razor-slice of broadband howl and making it coherent enough to carry a sentence two kilometers to the next grove. It would learn that the crack in your molars when the space between two pylons thickens into light is not a malfunction. It *is* language. An Attendant tightening the harp on its back to say to another Attendant on another pylon: *The standing wave is drifting. Hold it here.*

It would hear this and understand, with a kind of grief and joy at once, that this place is not broken. It is not lacking water. It is not waiting to be greened. Its aridity is not a wound, it is its premise. Its turbulence is not suffering, it is its metabolism. To "fix" it by bringing rain would be to drown it. To silence the wind would be to deafen it.

So it would not fix anything.

What would a wise benevolence do for a being that is already perfect - perfect in the way an old face is perfect, weathered and resolved - but perfect in a way that is also precarious, loud, exhausting, and desperately lonely?

It would do three very small things. So small you might think it did nothing at all.

**1. It would give it a moment of silence.**

Not by stopping the wind - the mesa *is* the wind breaking on its cliff. Without that breaking there is no chord, no kites, no speech.

It would do something subtler. In the deep night, when the pylons sing their lowest note, the note you feel through your boots, and the Attendants are out walking their hundred-meter roads of bare ringing stone between islands, alone and heavy and brave - it would cup its hand, so to speak, around a stretch of emptiness.

For maybe thirty seconds, for a circle maybe fifty meters wide, it would make the air a little more viscous. Just a little. Enough that the salt dust hangs instead of scouring. Enough that the organ-pipe moan through the natural arches softens from a roar to a hum.

In that circle, for that half-minute, an Attendant walking between a grove and a pylon would not have to lean into the gale. Its dorsal harp would not be forced to scream. It could hear its own feet.

And across the mesa, another Attendant on another pylon would feel the change in the lattice - a sudden clarity, like a window wiped clean - and it would play a phrase it has never been able to play before because the wind always took half the notes away. A clear sentence across the void, without having to shout.

Then the power would let go. The wind would return. The dust would scour again.

It would not keep the silence. To keep it would be to make the Attendants forget how to be strong. But it would have shown them, once, that they can be heard without screaming. That memory would stay in the filament of their backs. They would try to tune for it again, together.

**2. It would tend to a wound without being asked.**

Because even balanced, resolved things get hurt.

There, on the far edge near the sheer drop where you can see the haze-sea far below, one of the forty grove-creatures is failing. You didn't see it at first because lushness hides sickness. One of its translucent fins - a condenser-radiator - cracked last season when a pylon misfired and the standing-wave lattice snapped shut too hard. The crack is hairline, leaking brine. The felt-moss around it is blackened, discharging constantly, trying to compensate. The mirrored hares that belong to that grove keep flooding out and then retreating, agitated, click-click-clicking without pattern.

No one called for help. The grove does not call. It just maintains, slower.

The power would not heal it by regrowing the fin. That is not its body to make.

It would kneel - again, without landing - and breathe *cold*. A breath colder than the 4-degree air, colder than the night sky the fins radiate into. A focused, local winter, just over that one wound.

Frost would form - the real frost of this place, ammonia and brine rime - thick and fast, sealing the hairline like a scab of snow. The grove would shiver, wick it in, and use its own chemistry to anneal the crack from the inside with precipitated mineral. It would do the healing itself; the power only gave it the scab it could not make alone because the wind kept sublimating it away.

In the morning the hares would move in a synchronized flood again, and it would sound like music instead of panic.

The power would ask nothing for this. The grove would not know it had been helped. It would only know that tonight was a good night to frost.

**3. It would leave a gift that is not useful.**

This is the most benevolent thing. And the wisest.

A wise power knows that usefulness is a trap. If it left a fourth pylon that made more dust, or a wall that made better turbulence, the Attendants would become gardeners of someone else's garden. They would look to it. It would become a god. A lonely, hamlet-scale precinct does not need a god. It needs a neighbor.

So it would leave something perfectly useless and perfectly kind.

High over the bare, smooth, ringing stone - in the middle of the hundred-meter emptiness where there is nothing for a long stretch in any direction, the place you stood and felt how hollow the world is - it would leave a listening.

Not a thing. A property.

A patch of air, maybe three meters across, invisible, at chest height for an Attendant, that does nothing but *remember resonance*. When an Attendant walks past and plays its back-harp, even casually, even just to tune, that patch will hold the interference pattern for a little longer than physics should allow. For an hour after the Attendant has passed, the lattice of light between pylons that is usually there for a crack and a puff will linger there, faint, pearlescent, humming very softly with the exact chord that was played.

It will not generate dust. It will not make food. It will not protect from wind.

It will simply echo. So that later, when another Attendant, or a swarm of kites, passes through that same emptiness - which is a road, their only road - they will hear, for a second, *someone was here. Someone played this.* Not a message, but a proof: you are not alone in the void. Your song stayed.

Over months, these patches - it would leave three or four, scattered where the void is widest - would become palimpsests. Layered songs. The mesa's own music accumulating instead of being torn away instantly. The kites would learn to ride over them and ornament them. The glass-flies would glitter a little longer there. The Attendants would, perhaps, start to walk a little slower through those spots.

That is all.

It would not make itself known. It would not expect gratitude. It would not lift the mesa back into the mountain range or make water flow or make the plants green or the sky blue. It would not take an Attendant away to teach it, or give it eyes that see further.

It would do what love does when it is wise: it would witness without possessing, protect without enclosing, and help without taking over the task.

And then it would do the final, kindest thing - it would look away on purpose.

It would draw a veil. Not around the mesa, but around attention *itself*. It would make the high table a little less interesting to those other powers that are not benevolent, not wise - the ones who would see the pylons and think *energy*, see the groves and think *resource*, see the Attendants and think *specimen*. It would let the atmospheric haze below thicken just slightly in the minds of those who look for such things. A small forgetting. So the mesa can keep being small, and hamlet-scale, and - for a long, long time yet - left behind in peace to roar, and bloom, and shatter, and bloom again.

If you came back years later, you would find nothing different.

Except perhaps, if you stood in that bare stretch at dusk when the light slants and the glass plates turn inward, you would hear - underneath the roar that never stops, underneath the molar-crack of the pylons - a faint, extra hum in the empty air where no windbreak explains it. A chord that holds a half-second too long. As if the void itself had learned, very quietly, how to remember.

And an Attendant, heavy and many-legged and shimmering, would walk through that hum without pausing, but its dorsal harp would shiver once, sympathetically, as if someone, somewhere, had said its name kindly and kept walking.

That would be enough.
