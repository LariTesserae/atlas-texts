It would not come down.

That is the first thing to understand about a benevolent and wise power. It would not *descend*. The whole logic of descending — from above, with light and noise and rupture — belongs to powers that think height is holiness.

This one, being wise, would look at the low country and see that low is not fallen. Low is shaped.

It would have been watching for a long time before it was noticed watching.

### At first, it does nothing.

That is what a wise attention does. It does not immediately act. It listens longer than seems necessary.

It would listen to the roar until it stopped hearing it as noise and started hearing it as what it is: a roof. A ceiling. The cicadas are not an affliction of the basin; they *are* the basin, the way the basalt is the basin. They keep the sound in. They keep the heat in. They keep time from getting in.

It would watch the way the water never leaves and understand that this is not a flaw to be drained. The seep is the blood of the place. To drain it would be to kill it by curing it.

It would watch the people who condensed there — low, wide, oiled, dark-eyed — and it would not pity them. Pity is what you feel when you compare another life to the one you wanted for yourself. Wisdom does not compare. It would see how perfect they are. Feet like hands for gripping wet stone. Ears that can close like shutters. Skin that sweats oil instead of cracking. Eyes that make lanterns out of dusk. A language that is felt through the soles instead of lost in the air. A way of eating the very skin the town sheds every morning and calling it breakfast.

To an unwise power, they would look stunted, deprived, trapped.
To a wise one, they would look *finished* — in the way a tidepool creature is finished. Every problem solved so completely it no longer looks like a problem.

So it would not arrive with solutions.

It would not blast the clouds away to give them sun. They would burn.
It would not seed the basalt with soil and wheat. That would be a kind of drowning — too much future at once.
It would not unplug their ears and give them hearing as we understand it. It would be like tearing the shutters off a house in a storm and calling it a window.
It would not make them tall.

Any power that loved them and was wise would be terrified of breaking the equilibrium that has kept them alive for a hundred hundred rain-cycles. Stasis here is not failure. It is achievement.

So what would it do?

It would get low.

### It would become low itself.

It would not stand on the far rim like a sun and call to them. It would condense, too. The way they did.

On an afternoon that was exactly like every other afternoon — rain at the same hour, steam at the same hour, roar at the same pitch — it would be there on the roofs, not as a figure of light, but as a coolness.

The children moving in their scuttling roof-gait, hands touching parapets, would feel a place on the basalt where the stone was not sweating for once. A slab, maybe two arms wide, on the central terrace where everyone crosses, that stayed dry. Not magically dry. Just... dry enough that a chalk mark would hold. That a bare foot would leave a print that stayed until the next rain.

They would stop. They would touch it. They have never felt truly dry stone warmed by their own bodies. They would sit there longer than they needed to.

The old ones with the film over their eyes would notice that in a small circle — no larger than a sleeping pile — the roar lessened. Not stopped. Lessened. As if a cupped hand had been placed over the town's ear, just there. Inside that circle you did not need to drum to be heard. You could speak in the little chest-murmur voice and the person next to you would hear it as words, not just vibration.

No miracle would be announced. No voice would boom.

The power would simply have made a small, reversible pocket of *otherwise*. Dryness inside wetness. Quiet inside sound. It would not force anyone into it. It would just be there, on the way to the kilns, to be found or not found.

And it would wait to see what they did.

Because a benevolent power does not give gifts that require gratitude. It gives gifts that require choice.

Some would avoid the dry place at first. Dryness is strange. Silence is strange, almost frightening when your whole life has been a weight of sound. The babies would crawl into it and go still with surprise, then cry.

But the young — the ones who are still learning to close their ears — they would play there. They would discover that if you tap on the dry stone, the sound is different. Sharper. It rings. It does not disappear into the seep. It travels and comes back clean.

And the power, now sitting at roof level, low and wide itself to be near them — perhaps as an old woman with large oiled skin, perhaps as nothing more than that coolness that listens — would tap back.

Not in their code, at first. It would learn their code.

It would spend what for it is a moment and for them is years just learning to drum correctly. *We are here. We hear you. You are not alone in the haze.* But it would not say even that until they had asked if anyone else was outside the rim.

And one evening, one of them would ask. A girl with cropped hair and a pan of algae cakes, sitting in the quiet circle, would lay her palm flat on the dry stone and tap — not the usual *I am here, where are you?* but a new pattern, one that has never needed to exist before because there was never a silence to ask it in:

*Is there more than this?*

That is the only prayer a wise power answers without violating.

Then, and only then, it would do three small things. Small the way a key is small compared to a house.

**1. It would give them a sky.**

Not every day. Just once.

On a night after rain, when the bats were still out and the kilns were banked, it would draw the low cloud up, just over the basin, just for an hour, the way you draw a breath. For the first time in living memory — for the first time ever, maybe — the people on the roofs would see that the dim was not the ceiling of the world. Above the haze were points of cold, sharp light that did not flicker like kilns. Stars. Depth.

They would not understand them. Their great wet eyes would reflect them and make them seem nearer than they were. Some would be afraid. Some would cover their eyes with the nictitating film. But the children would lie on their backs on the warm basalt that never fully cools and make a new game of connecting the points with their fingers.

In the morning the cloud would be back, pearlescent and amber. Nothing would have changed, except now they would have a word that is not *rim* for the idea of *beyond*. And they would know it because they saw it, not because they were told.

**2. It would give them a seed that asks permission.**

At the very edge of the town, where the quarry face is still glittering black and vertical, where no one builds because the seep runs strongest, the power would coax one crack in the basalt to weather faster than geology allows. Just one. A hand-sized patch where the glass has finally softened into something like soil. Not much. Enough for one thing to root that did not exist before.

It would not plant it. It would leave a seed there — a small, hard, unassuming thing, grey-green, that holds its water tight. A plant that likes basalt and steam and dim light. A thing that could become the first true green in the basin, if they wanted.

But it would not grow unless tended. Unless someone carried a little of that endless water to it sparingly, and kept the algae from smothering it, and decided — day after day for a long season — that they wanted something useless and green to live because it is beautiful, not because it is edible.

If they tended it, it would grow low and wide, like them. If they did not, it would sleep. No guilt either way. A gift that can be declined is the only gift a wise power gives.

**3. It would give them a way to leave that is also a way to stay.**

At the lowest gutter of the town, where all the seep finally braids before it sinks back into the rock, it would set a stone that is slightly different. Not basalt. Smoother. Warmer. When drummed, it hums at a pitch just under the cicadas — the same pitch they hum in their chests to feel each other.

If many feet stood on it together and drummed together — not a code for location or warning, but a new rhythm, slow and together like a heart — the stone would be light enough to carry them. Not up. Out. Over the rim, to the marshes where the reeds come from, to the high country where air is dry and sound does not trap, to see what tallness feels like.

It would only work if they all decided to do it together, and it would bring them back if they wished. For a wise power knows that offering an exit that is a one-way exile is not benevolence, it is abandonment dressed as rescue.

Most would never drum it. Why would they? The basin is the world and the world is complete.

But some might, one day. And they could come back and tap on the roofs with stories that would not fit in the old codes, and have to invent new taps, and that invention — that slow, self-chosen stretching — is how a place that has been in stasis for eternity learns to change without shattering.

---

That is what it would do, if it loved them.

It would not fix them. It would not save them.

It would sit with them in the steam. It would make one stone dry and one circle quiet and let them decide if they like dryness and quiet. It would show them stars, not to make them dissatisfied with dimness, but to let them know the dimness is beloved and also not everything. It would offer a green thing that needs care and a road that needs a chorus.

And whether they touched any of it or not, it would keep listening — with its hand cupped softly over the roar — so that for as long as the basalt stays new, there is at least one other being who hears the town not as deafening, but as music, and who knows the name of every scuttling child who learns to walk on the roofs.

In the morning they would still wake beaded like lintels. They would still scrape their doors. The cicadas would still saw.

But now, if you listened very carefully just under the roar, you would hear an extra tapping going around, unhurried, with no practical purpose.

A kind of humming. A kind of *thank you, we are here too.*

And the town, which has never needed to answer, answering.
