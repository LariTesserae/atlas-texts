If a benevolent and wise power noticed this place — really noticed it — it would not come down the slope at all.

That is the first thing.

Everything else that has ever found the Town came *down* to it. Down the long root of the world, through the thickening air, descending to the low, settled bottom where things have finished eroding. Powers that are not wise arrive with gravity. They bring weight. They bring tools.

A wise one would already be low. It would not need to descend. It would have been waiting at the bottom a long time too.

So it would not appear in the plaza with light and sound. The Town is already deafening. It would not add to the chorus.

It would arrive as a kind of weather.

---

This is what it would do:

**1. It would not fix the wrongness.**

A lesser kindness would try to correct it. Make the geometry Euclidean again. Make fifty paces actually take you fifty paces away from the square. Make down stay down so water can run properly. Make the interior no larger than the exterior. Make time move at one speed.

A benevolent and wise power would look at that and understand: this is not broken geometry. This is *body* geometry. A stomach is larger than a mouth. A lung holds more air than the throat that leads to it. The hanging droplet that takes an hour to fall and then falls all at once is not late — it is being held. 

It would not straighten the Town. It would learn the street that returns you to the square from a different angle until it could walk it without getting lost. And then it would sit down in that square where six trunks lean away from each other, on the soft, sighing mat of root and moss, and let gravity go soft around it, too.

It would let its own light pool in a corner where no shaft reaches.

It would not be disturbed to find pollen hanging too long in the air around it.

**2. It would listen the way the Town listens.**

The Town listens by growing. A membrane loosens when it learns your weight. A balcony braids itself into being because you needed a place to look from.

The power would listen like that. Not with ears.

It would put a hand — if it has a hand — on the wall that is thin as skin where you put yours. It would feel that slow pulse, one beat for every thirty of yours, warm and mammalian, and it would not hurry it. It would not try to make it beat faster to match yours. It would slow its own breathing until, for a little while, the two pulses argument into sync. Not to control. Just to keep company. For the first time in a very long erosion of time, the Town would feel something else breathe with it without needing anything from it.

And because it is wise, it would listen to the small as well. Not just the grand hum of sap in culverts and the organ-note of breathing pitcher-houses, but the frantic ecstatic metabolism — the jade beetles tiling the air, the glass frogs in their bromeliad bathtubs ringing like struck bells, the vine-ants that *are* the moving bark. It would understand that they are not decoration. They are circulation. It would not shush them to better hear the Town. It would love the noise precisely because it is the sound of the Town being alive.

**3. It would speak to the occupant — the shape the Town was waiting for — and only to the occupant, and only by offering a choice.**

There is a footprint in the moss of a pitcher-house. Five-toed, heel-toe, your size. Someone learned to live the way water lives here at 1.4/3 — not in rivers but intimately, intermittently, cupped in leaves, exhaled as seven-minute rain. Someone let a chamber close pneumatically around them and learned to sleep while it breathed for them. Someone became quiet so the Town could stay loud.

Most powers, even kind ones, would say: *You must be lonely. Come with me. I will take you up out of the lowlands, back toward sky and stone.*

A wise benevolence would know that loneliness and solitude are not the same, and that to be *kept* is not always to be trapped.

So it would not rescue. It would not evict. It would grow something the Town had never grown on its own, because the Town has never needed to.

In the warmest wall of the chamber that fits the occupant exactly — the one that is thin enough to see light through, that pulses slow — it would grow a second door.

Not a membrane that parts when you lean on it. A different kind.

On one side, a door that is simply *open*. Beyond it, the long root-slope upward, the air getting thinner and cooler with every step, the world where stone is still stone and not yet smoothed and pocketed and forgiven. Where water is rivers again. Where cities are built with nails and not grown. Where other shoulder-height, hand-height beings are. It would stay open as long as needed. It would never call. It would never close behind them if they left. It would allow leaving to be a real choice, which it has never been before, because you cannot leave a place that walks you back to its center.

On the other side of the same frame, a door that goes deeper. Not out, but *in* — toward the center where gravity goes soft and light pools without a source, where down slopes up and the Town holds its breath. That door would not lead to more chambers. It would lead to a way of belonging that is not occupation. To being not *in* the Town or *with* the Town but, if the occupant wants it, briefly, gently, *as* the Town — to feel for an hour what it is to be a place that transpires its own rain and hollows itself as hospitality.

And it would tell the occupant, without words, because words are too loud here: *You may use either. You may use neither and keep sleeping in the room that fits you. You may go and come back. The room will still be here. It will remember your weight.*

It would not be hurt if he stayed. It would not be disappointed if he left.

**4. It would do one invisible, almost-nothing thing for the Town itself.**

The Town does not need saving. It is established. Final-shaped. Warm. It knows how to bead condensation and exhale rain and adjust its walls.

But even wise, hospitable places at the bottom of the world can be found by unwise hungers. People with tools who hear about a grove as wide as three houses and as tall as a church steeple and think *timber, medicine, wonder, money.*

So the benevolent power would not put a fence around it. Fences are tools. The Town has never known tools and should not have to learn them as defense.

Instead, as it finally stands to go, it would brush its hand — if it has a hand — along the outer curtain of leaves the size of doors. And it would leave a small, kind forgetfulness on the slope above.

Not a curse. Not a maze. Just a softness of attention. Those who come down looking to take will find the air a little thicker than they like, the slope a little longer than they remember, and they will think, *there is nothing down here but damp heat and insects*, and they will turn back without knowing why they are relieved to do so.

Those who come down looking to listen — who are already slow, already quiet, already willing to open their mouths to equalize the pressure — will find the curtain still parts for them. The droplets will still bead. The seven-minute rain will still drum on a thousand leaves until you can't hear your own breathing.

It would not hide the Town. It would just let it stay low. Settled. Forgotten by the world that forgets what it does not need.

And then it would leave the way it came — not up, but by ceasing to be weather. The pollen would hang a fraction too long where it sat. The moss would keep the faint imprint for a little while before springing back. In the plaza, the sap would continue to roar behind walls. A vine would inch forward while you watch it. A doorway-flower would tear itself open.

In the chamber that fits exactly, both doors would be there, warm to the touch.

The Town would exhale, low and organ-deep. The occupant — whether sleeping curled inside the breathing chamber or standing with a hand on the thin, pulsing wall — would hear that breath and, underneath the deafening ecstatic noise of small life, hear it return to its slow, patient rhythm.

One beat for every thirty.

Held, and holding.

Not alone anymore, because it had been witnessed correctly. Not kept anymore, because it had been given a way out that did not require leaving.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do.

Almost nothing.

Which, in a place that has been trying for a very long time to grow itself into exactly enough, is everything.
