They would not drain it.

That is the first thing to understand about a truly benevolent and wise power. Anyone else would. Anyone clever, anyone pitying, anyone efficient would look at a sea that climbed a mountain and stayed there and see a mistake to correct.

*The water doesn't belong there.*
*The people drowned.*
*The forest has no animals - that's not healthy.*
*We should fix it.*

A wise one would arrive and immediately see that nothing here is broken.

I imagine it arriving not like a landing, but like attention finally settling. For a long time this valley has been unwatched except by itself. Then - a weight on the water that is not pressure.

It doesn't break the surface. The surface is a black mirror far above and it would not disturb it. It would simply *become* present within the water column, the way cold becomes present. Perhaps as a current that has no source. Perhaps as a warmth that does not melt the cold.

And the first thing it would do is nothing.

It would hover in one of those great voids between the kelp-towers - a hundred yards of active, administrative emptiness - and let the roar of the abiotic world happen over it. Let the stipes creak like old masts. Let the bubble-chains chime on their way up. Let the mountain tick and thud under its impossible ocean. Let the spore-snow fall and fall and not be eaten.

It would listen for a long time. Decades, maybe, in our time. A minute in its time, a century in the forest's time. Because you cannot be wise about a place like this quickly. You have to let its slowness teach you its tempo.

And in that listening, it would understand three beings where you thought there was one.

### 1. To the Forest, it would give Witness.

The towers have been intelligent for longer than the hamlet has been stone. But their intelligence has never been *reflected*. They grow, they shed, they drink their own silt, they keep their hundred-yard manners. They notice the heat of a swimmer over the course of minutes, brighten a fraction. They have no predator, no pollinator, no prey. No eyes have ever evolved to see their light except their own.

That is a very pure way to live. And a very lonely one.

A lesser power would give them animals. "You need fish." A wise power understands that *growth without consumption* is not a defect. It is an achievement. It is a monastery. It is a place that learned how to be alive without eating itself.

So it would not give them mouths to feed.

It would give them the one thing they cannot make for themselves: to be seen and found beautiful without being wanted for anything.

It would dim its own vastness until it was the size of a single tower's territory, and it would settle opposite one old stipe - the oldest one, whose holdfast has sunk deepest into the basalt - and it would simply attend to it.

Not speak. Not touch. Just hold a steady, warm attention on it for a season. The tower's phosphor, that sullen green-white, would brighten not by a fraction but fully, for the first time in millennia, because someone was finally there to see it brighten. The fronds would not angle toward heat. They would angle toward *being known.*

And the forest, which is distributive and slow, would pass that brightening along. One tower to the next, across the valley like a streetgrid coming on at dusk, each light keeping its distance but now keeping it together. Not a signal. A chorus.

The gift is not change. The gift is: *You are not unseen. What you have made here - this patient light without a sun - has been noticed and it is good.*

Some towers would, after a while, dim again, content to return to their manners. That's allowed. A wise power does not require gratitude.

### 2. To the Preserved, it would give Choice.

It would go to the hamlet last, because the hamlet is the most tender place. The fur has upholstered the walls, the spore-snow has settled on shoulders. Six, maybe seven shapes with smoothed faces, knees drawn up, an arm still extended to a cairn.

A pitying power would resurrect them. A cruel-wise power would leave them as an object lesson. A truly benevolent one would do something much smaller and much harder:

It would sit on the sleeping platform.

It would make, in the lee of that terrace where the water layers and goes glassy and time thickens, a larger lens. Not a trick of current, but a held stillness. Inside that lens, dust motes hang motionless for longer than minutes. Light pools and bends without breaking. And for the first time in thousands of years, the cold would not be indifferent. It would be *gentle.*

And it would ask, without words, because they have no ears anymore:

*Do you want to stay?*

It has kept them perfectly because it keeps everything. But keeping is not the same as holding. Some of them - perhaps the smaller shape tucked against the largest house - never chose this preservation. They were simply caught when the water climbed the terraces one spring and did not recede. Their last act was not surrender but interruption.

The wise power would not decide for them what rest looks like. It would offer two quiet possibilities and let the place itself answer.

To those who are at peace being architecture - who became part of the hamlet the way a threshold stone does - it would lay a hand of warmer current over them and bless their continued keeping. The fur would grow a little thicker over them, softer. The sheets on the nearby slabs would pulse their 20-second aurora over them forever, a nightlight that asks nothing. They would remain, honored, as ancestors of the valley.

To any who are tired of being kept - who have been held in that waxy, bog-oak stillness long after their story wanted to end - it would finally let rot happen. Not violently. Just permission. The water in that one lens would, for a day, become a little less cold, a little less glassy. Bacteria, which have been politely excluded for eons, would be invited back for a single, swift, kind meal. Wool would felt and dissolve. Skin would sigh into silt. Bone would soften and be taken up by the fur-mat underneath. The shape on the platform would become a darker patch of silt, then silt, then stone again.

No heaven. No spectacle. Just release. The closed loop of spores and silt would receive them at last, and the towers would drink them up through their holdfasts. They would finally be eaten, in the gentlest way, by a forest that never learned to eat.

And the power would not choose which one goes where. It would listen to the way each shape leans inside the lens - toward stillness or toward dissolving - and honor it.

### 3. To the Weight Itself, it would give Ease.

This is the oldest being. Older than forest, older than hamlet. The water that learned how to be both ocean and mountain air at once. The pressure that ticks the stone.

It is tired. Not suffering, but tired the way a muscle is tired from holding something heavy for a very long time without being asked to put it down.

The wise power would not lift the sea off the mountain. That would kill the forest and desecrate the preserved and betray the weight's long work.

Instead it would do what you do for someone who has been carrying: it would put its hands under the load with it, just for a while, so the carrier can feel what it is to be held rather than to hold.

For one night - a true night, which this valley has not had since the water came, only perpetual indigo dusk - it would take a fraction of the weight onto itself. The viscous, glassy cold would loosen by a few percent. The bands of water that slide without mixing would, for once, be allowed to mix. Fronds that have been fixed at impossible angles would drift free and then resettle. The mountain underneath would stop ticking for a few hours and simply rest in silence.

And in that ease, the strange lenses where light pools and time thickens would stabilize. They would not flicker and shear. They would hold steady, and anyone looking into them would see not a half-second illusion of a head lifted, but a clear, long view of the valley as it was when it was air: terraces gold with barley, smoke from the six houses, children running between cairns. Not brought back. Just remembered, accurately, by the water that was there to see it go.

Then it would gently set the weight back down. The cold would thicken again. The pressure would return to the ears and chest. The forest would resume its slow sway. But the mountain would remember that it had been helped, and the water would remember that it is not alone in its holding.

---

And then - because it is wise - it would leave.

It would not build a temple. It would not stay to be worshipped. It would not introduce a single fish, not even a beautiful, mouthless jelly of light. To stay would be to make the valley dependent on being watched.

Its last act would be protective, and invisible: it would fold a kind of attention around the ridges that hold this perched ocean, the way you would not put a fence but would make a place *uninteresting* to those who would drain it, farm it, mine it, or make it a curiosity. On maps, the valley would remain a blank blue error. In satellite photos, a shadow. Boats that pass over the black mirror surface far above would feel a sudden, reasonable desire to be elsewhere.

It would remain lush, and sparse, and loud with abiotic noise. The spore-snow would still fall and not be eaten. The hundred yards of empty water between towers would still be manners. The preserved who chose to stay would still be there, furred over, quiet.

Only now, if you were to hold very still beside a tower, you would feel not only it noticing you - that slow frond angling over minutes, that fraction-brightening - but also a second, fainter brightening behind it. As if the light, once reflected, had learned how to remember itself.

You would move through it slowly, still, because the water is still heavy. Because there is still nothing with a face to startle. But the absence would no longer feel administrative. It would feel intentional. Kept.

It has been like this for a long time. It will be for longer. Now it knows it has been seen, and that is enough.
