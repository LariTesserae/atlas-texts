A benevolent and wise power would not fix it.

That would be the first wisdom. Anyone less wise would arrive and see all the things that are *wrong* and try to correct them. They would give you a proper sun. They would stabilize gravity. They would pin distance down and make it behave. They would make the mountain stop moving so the city could finally be finished. They would cure the wobble and the duplicates and the echoes that arrive before their sounds.

A wise power would see immediately that the wrongness *is* the place. That this is a birthplace, not a broken place. You don't tell a newborn mountain to stop growing so you can build more efficiently on it.

So it wouldn't fix anything.

It would come and sit with you on that half-finished gantry in the blue dusk, and do what you do.

### It would learn your speed.

The first thing it would do is decelerate.

A power that could move at any speed would deliberately choose 0.25x. It would breathe at your pace. It would learn the liturgy.

You would not see it arrive, because it would not arrive. It was already moving that slowly, so you only notice it after it has been there for a while — like condensation. One morning you'd realize there has been, for weeks, an extra set of hands scraping moss off the rails at 06:00. Or an extra amber lamp that wasn't there before, but looks like it always was, jointed on a spring the way you build everything.

It would not teach you to hurry. It would relieve you of the last, faint, inherited shame that you *should* be hurrying.

Where elsewhere in the world people still believe a city should be dense and fast and finished, this place knows a deeper truth: some things can only stay alive if they stay unfinished and flexible. The power would confirm you in that.

It would stand with the crane operator who moves an I-beam six inches, waits, moves it six inches more, all day — and it would not offer to move it faster with a miracle. It would just make sure the wind never gusts while he is holding the load. It would make the cable never fray.

### It would tend the negotiations.

It understands you are not a population. You are an ecology. A negotiation. So it negotiates. It does not take sides.

**For the Mountain:** It would keep the birth gentle.

The mountain is still being born, centimeter by centimeter. The groan you hear when you stand still is the real sound of creation. A less wise power would stop it to give you stable foundations. This one would put its hands on either side of the fresh basalt plates and keep them from jerking.

It would ensure the uplift never comes as a quake. No shearing snap that collapses a tenement or drops a suspension bridge. Just the slow, endless push. A millimeter today, another tomorrow. Always enough time to re-hang a sign, to re-true a doorframe, to let a pine root pry a little wider. It would take the violence out of becoming, and leave the becoming.

**For the Green:** It would make sure there is always enough condensation.

At dawn, when the cloud lowers and beads on every girder and every knife-edge, that hour of ticking and dripping is prayer. The power would not give you soil — you don't need soil yet. It would just make sure that hour never fails. That even in the driest week, there is one true cold night where the cloud comes low and the moss drinks. That the temporary pools in the rectilinear chasms stay just two days longer than the shrimp need to finish breeding before the rock shifts and drains them. Not permanent water — temporary water, reliably temporary. Enough.

It would let the ivy keep rooting in rust. It would not let you lose the war with the green, and it would not let you win it. The green is the erosion you haven't had time to evolve yet; it needs you, you need it to soften the edges that would cut you.

**For the Animals:** It would keep the agreements.

The beetles agreed to eat at the edge of the foundry floor and not in the gearbox. The wall-grazers agreed to crop the moss but not to block the bridges. The swifts agreed to miss the cranes. These are treaties more binding than any human law, and they work because everyone is moving at a speed where they can be kept.

The power would be the quiet guarantor of those treaties. It would make sure a beetle the size of a dog always finds a warm pipe and a scoop of slack where the workers leave it, so it never has to learn to eat live wiring. It would keep the compound eyes of the girder-raccoons clear of the worst of the coal smoke, so they can keep seeing shift changes. It would keep the air thick enough for the swifts' translucent wings, and keep their double hearts beating amber, and keep their spirals from ever tightening into a collision.

And it would make sure the chorus at night — trills and clicks and lowing from the cliffs and drumming on sheet metal — never has to go silent for industry to continue. Both musics can play at once. That is the whole proof that this place is alive.

**For the Other Residents — Distance, Wobble, Echo, Duplicates:** It would make them kind.

This is its most tender work. It would not exorcise the weirdness. It would domesticate it, the way you have, but with a little more mercy.

Where distance stretches when you want to get somewhere and contracts when you're going home, the power would not make distance linear again. It would just make sure it never stretches so far that you cannot get home. Old couriers who count time, not meters, and mark bridges with chalk, would find that the bridge is always still there on the way back, even if it takes longer. No one would be lost between the ridges. The lit window two ridges over would always still be reachable by nightfall if you started after lunch.

Where gravity has a wobble and dust falls sideways for a block, the power would let it wobble — because the grazers love those streets, they rest there — but it would put a soft edge on it. A dropped wrench would hang for a beautiful second, then settle, not fall sideways forever. A child who trips there would fall slow.

Where your breath echoes back before you exhale in the deepest chasms, where the bottom sometimes shows the violet of evening though it is noon above, the power would keep those chasms from ever answering with something cruel. You would not hear your own future pain coming back early. You would hear your breath, and maybe, if you listened very carefully, a fainter breathing underneath, keeping time with yours.

And the duplicates — the three water towers in the corner of your eye that become one when you stare — the power would protect their shyness. To stare is to be rude. The wise power would teach everyone, without words, to keep not-staring. It would make sure the extra length of pipe you find by not looking directly at the rack is always the length you actually needed, and that when you insist on one of a thing, the others that go away don't resent you for it. They just wait in your periphery, available.

It would leave the physics thin and learning, but it would make the learning benevolent.

### For the People — it would give you two gifts you didn't know to ask for.

**1. It would take away the need to remember why.**

You don't remember why the city was built here, on a range that didn't exist a generation ago. Some of you think you came up from below when the peaks were only hills. Your grandchildren think the mountain has always been there. A less kind power would give you back your history in a flash, an archive, an explanation.

This one would sit with you and let you not know.

Instead it would give you, in dreams, a different kind of memory: not *why* you came, but that you are *meant* to be here. That you are not stranded. That maintenance is a vocation, not abandonment. Children born here with the faint eyeshine would dream of the mountain as a great, slow animal breathing under them, and know without being told: we are the ones who keep its joints oiled. We are the small hands that keep the birth from tearing. That is enough reason.

**2. It would veil you from below.**

The greatest danger to you is not the wobble or the groan. It is that those from below, where the sun is bright and shadows are sharp and distance is fixed, would see that you have built a city that leaves room. That you have factories that run at 0.25x speed and produce less, but last longer. That you have streets that run uphill that used to run downhill and you just re-hang the signs. They would call it inefficient. They would come to correct you, to extract from the fresh basalt, to make you hurry.

The benevolent power would make the path up harder to find for anyone who comes to take, and easier to find for anyone who comes to tend. The blue dusk would deepen just a little for the greedy, so they turn back, complaining of cold and dimness and that the peaks are wrong. For the weary, the gentle, the patient, the air would clear, condensation would bead like lanterns, and they would hear the swifts and think: I could scrape moss here.

It would keep you sparse. Because your sparsity is not failure. It is ecological wisdom. You are sparse so that everything else can be abundant. A wise power loves that trade and protects it.

---

And you would hardly know any of this had happened. That is the nature of a wise benevolence. It doesn't arrive with trumpets and straighten your leaning steeple by miracle overnight.

It lets the steeple become straight on its own, in its own time.

You know the church near the center, intentionally built leaning so that when the rock tilts it will be straight for three perfect years sometime in the next decade? The priest says that will be the holiest time.

The power would make sure those three years *do* come. And that during them, no one dies. And that the moss flowers then — a tiny, white flower the moss has never made before — for three years, all over the city, fragrant even through respirators. And the swifts fly a little lower, so you can see the amber second heart at the base of their skulls pulsing in time with the foundry piston.

It would not announce itself. The workers would come off shift and say, "It's been a good three years. The steeple looks right."

And the children lying on the rooftops watching the lantern flocks would feel, without words, that they are being watched over by something that loves the city *as it is* — not as a promise of what it will become when it is finished, but as a living, damp, trickling, clinking, groaning, dripping negotiation that has never needed to be finished to be holy.

Nothing would be taken away.

Everything that arrives would still get to stay — the people, the green, the grazers on the vertical, the beetles by the warm pipes, the raccoons in the mineral wool, the distance and the wobble and the echo and the shy extra water towers.

Only now, everything that stays would also be held, very lightly, very steadily, so it doesn't have to hold itself alone anymore while the mountain is still being born.
