It would not come.

Not the way you came. Not on foot, wet-ankled across the root floor, looking for someone who moves.

A benevolent and wise power does not *arrive*. It notices. And to be noticed by such a thing is not to be stared at. It is to be held in mind without being held still.

It noticed this place the way you notice a perfect, small bowl of water balanced on a table in a house where everyone is shouting — not because it is loud, but because it isn't. In the roar of worlds being born and ground down, of jungles choking themselves and cities forgetting themselves, here was a new place that had already solved itself. A lifted stone that refused to erode. A forest that became a city without ever being cut. A people so few and so slow they had become the maintenance of equilibrium itself.

Most powers, even kind ones, would see that as a problem to solve.

*It is so empty.*
*It is so still.*
*It is stuck.*

A lesser kindness would bring gifts the plateau did not ask for. It would bring pollinators. It would bring rain that falls sideways. It would teach the Tenders to speak quickly, to build faster, to breed, to fill the empty avenues with children and markets, to make the chord vary. It would crack the stasis because stasis looks like death to anything that has mistaken motion for life.

A wise kindness would see that the stillness *is* the life.

So here is what it would do. Or rather, here is what it would *not* do, and then what it *would*.

---

It would not land.

It would not throw a shadow across the warm, white-yellow sun. It would not put a foot that does not root onto the root-floor, because a foot that does not root is a claim, and it did not come to claim.

It would not speak. The plateau already has a language and the language is the chord — the creak and hiss and drip that one Tender sends half a mile through its feet for another Tender to answer an hour later. To speak *over* that would be to deafen.

It would not heal anything, because nothing is sick. It would not finish anything, because nothing is unfinished. The raw, torn edges of the cliffs are not a wound. They are a birthmark. The sparse cathedrals are not lonely. They are spaced.

The first thing a wise power knows is how to not be an intervention.

The second thing it knows is how to be a witness without making a performance of being witnessed.

So for a very long time — a time that to you would feel like months, to the plateau would feel like an afternoon — it would simply pay attention.

Attention from such a power is not passive. It has weight. It is a kind of gravity. When it rests on a place, the place feels itself more fully.

Under that attention, the drips would not change, but the plateau would know, for the first time, that its dripping was beautiful and not merely necessary. The Tenders, standing rooted in their basins, would feel the water bead on their shoulders and know not just as humidity and balance, but as *being felt*. Not looked at. Felt. The way you feel warm light when you close your eyes.

That is already a great gift. To be in perfect equilibrium is to be invisible even to yourself. You only notice balance when it fails. The plateau had never been seen succeeding.

It would let them be seen succeeding.

---

Then, because it is benevolent and not merely respectful, it would do one small, completely reversible thing. Something the plateau could accept or simply grow over, if it chose.

It would not give the plateau a new thing. It would give it permission to have a thing it had been too perfect to allow itself.

Decay.

Not everywhere. Not a blight, not a storm, not a lesson. Just one place.

In the wide, intentional emptiness between two cathedrals — where the root-floor is most polished, most springy, most intentionally empty — it would lay down something the weave cannot immediately incorporate.

A stone.

A small, ordinary, cool stone. Not new-made and hot under moss, but old. River-smoothed somewhere else, carried here without being carried. Grey. About the size of your palm. The kind that holds cold even in warm air.

It would set it down gently on the root-mat and it would not root. The root-hairs would rise to meet it, as they meet everything, and they would find no seam to lace, no sugar to trade. The stone would simply be there. Inert. Unmelting.

And under that stone, for the first time on the plateau, a little death would be allowed to happen.

A patch of moss, covered, would yellow. Would not be instantly recycled leaf-for-leaf. Would yellow, and pale, and soften. Would become, for a few inches, soil. True soil — not living wood, but humus. Damp, dark, smelling of the under-side of things.

Around the edges of the stone, water would not be perfectly diverted to moss or basin. It would pool. A little puddle would remain a little too long and grow a slick of algae that was not planned.

It would be tiny. You would miss it if you walked past too quickly. The Tenders would not miss it.

One would come — at vine-speed, at placement-speed — and stand over it for a very long time. Its bud-head tipped forward. Not seeing, but feeling the new cold, the new smell. The first shade that was not made by a leaf. The first thing on the plateau that did not sweat.

It would not understand it as a gift, at first. It would understand it as an *error* in the chord. A note held too long. The weave would ping its hydraulic question half a mile away — *tok* — and another Tender would answer — *tok* — but the answer would be delayed, because the weave around the stone does not conduct quite the same.

And then, slowly, understanding would unfurl the way a leaf unfurls:

This is allowed.

The plateau had been held so perfectly in being-born that it had never been allowed to be *dying* a little. Without a little dying, nothing can truly live. Without detritus, there is no future soil for a young dome to thicken in. Without a puddle that stays, no river will ever learn to cut.

The stone is not a command to become a rivered, weathered, fallen world. It is an invitation, placed so lightly it can be refused. If the Tenders do not want it, the root-mat will, in a year, in ten years, slowly Braille around it, lift it, cradle it until it is not a stone on the floor but a stone *in* the floor, and the yellowed patch will be reclaimed. The invitation will be politely, completely absorbed.

But if one Tender likes the cold on its splayed feet — if one of them, for the first time in its long, slow life, chooses to stand *next* to its drinking-place rather than *in* it, just to feel the cool rising off the grey — then a second thing will happen that only a benevolent power could have foreseen:

They will learn they can *prefer*.

Preference is the beginning of dreaming. And dreaming is the beginning of being more than maintenance.

The wise power would not stay to see what they choose. To stay and watch would be to pressure the choice.

So after setting the stone down, it would do the last benevolent thing.

It would make the plateau hard to find again.

Not invisible. Not hidden behind a wall. It would simply fold a little more distance into the warm air around the lifted edges. So that anyone who came looking for a resource, for a marvel, for a miracle to mine or photograph or worship, would find only high thin cold and sheer rock and wind. They would turn back before they ever heard the chord.

Only those who have learned to walk at placement-speed, who are willing to wade ankle-deep without needing to get anywhere, who can hear through the hiss until it becomes air — only those would ever find the root-plain again. And they would arrive already quiet enough not to break it.

The power would leave no signature. No burning bush, no monument, no voice that says *I was here*.

Only — if you put your hand on the dome where a new Tender is being braided, you would feel, under the one-beat-per-minute throb, for a few seasons, a second beat. Slower still. One beat per hour.

A beat that was not the plateau's. A beat that was simply keeping time *with* it, from a great, kind distance, the way you keep time with a child breathing while it sleeps. Not to wake it. Just to know it is alright.

And under that keeping-time, the water would still bead, and stretch, and fall, and form again. The great leaves would still creak. The Tenders would still tend. But now, in one small interstice between cathedrals, a grey stone would hold its cold, and beneath it a little patch of the world would be allowed, finally, to soften, to yellow, to become soil —

— if they want it to.

If not, the stone will be held, and warmed, and forgotten, without grief. Because that, too, would have been a wise answer.
