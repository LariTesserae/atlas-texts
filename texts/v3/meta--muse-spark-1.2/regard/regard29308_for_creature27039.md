It would not come.

Not the way power is usually imagined.

No light descending the bowl. No voice that stills the crickets. If it did that, the crickets would die. The bats would panic and crush their young against the scalloped tiers. The troop would freeze and never unfreeze, splayed flat to the ash-grey stone, hearts hammering inside their too-big ears until they burst.

A wise power knows that arrival is a form of violence.

So it does not arrive. It notices. Which for a thing like that is the same as touching.

It has been watching the mountain for a long time anyway. The way you watch a table someone left in the sun — not because it needs you, but because you like how the light hits it. It knows the basalt shoulders, the slow exhale of heat at 3,000 meters where there should be cold, the way the plateau holds its breath. It knows the bowl before the bowl was a bowl, when it was only a soft place in the limestone where water liked to stay.

And then, one day in its own long time, it hears - not with ears - the bowl's new sound. That sawtooth cricket drone. That rain of plinks. That soft *hut hut* underneath it all, so quiet you have to filter out a world to hear it.

And it leans in. Not down from above. In. Closer.

---

What does a benevolent and wise thing do when it finds something small and perfect and complete?

First, it understands that it is not needed.

This is the test wisdom always sets for benevolence.

The bowl does not lack. It is not broken. It is not waiting to be improved. The water that comes from air is enough. The beetles are enough. The guano feeds the fungus feeds the grubs feeds the ash-grey hands. The chert carried up grain by grain from a day and a half below is enough - a sharp crescent is all the technology a hand like that requires. The troop of thirty is enough - large enough to remember where the twigs are cached, small enough that the seep never fails.

To give them more would be to unmake them.

It could make the bowl larger. It does not. Larger would thin the sound, drop the humidity, invite wind. It could make the seep into a spring, a little silver stream across the silt. It does not. A stream would drown the laminated floor, wash away the archives, flood the guano beds where the pale fungus glows.

It could make them bigger, brighter, louder, smarter. Give them fire, give them language that carries over the cricket noise, give them feet that could cross the dry sea of the plateau and colonize other peaks. It could make them *more*.

It knows exactly what *more* does to an island.

So it does the first, hardest thing power ever learns to do:

It withholds its hand.

And then it does the second thing, which looks like nothing but is everything.

It remembers them on purpose.

To be held in the mind of something that does not forget is a kind of shelter. The bowl was always in danger not from within but from without - from the dry world that thirsts. A world that maps, and mines, and names. A world that would see a warm pocket of water at 3,000 meters and call it a resource. That would hear about clever hands that knap chert and want a specimen, a study, a zoo. That would stand at the rim and, meaning no harm, toss a bottle down to see how long it takes to hit.

So the power does not build a wall. Walls are seen and climbed.

It makes the bowl *uninteresting*.

The path up the ribs and broad shoulders stays exactly as exhausting as it was. A little more exhausting, perhaps. The sun a degree hotter on the polished limestone of the plateau. The wind a fraction more disorienting, so the eye slides past that darker lip. The satellite pass happens when cloud sits low over the summit plain. The geologist who was going to write the permit decides, for no reason he can name, that the tuff lower down is more worth his season.

Not a miracle. Just a nudge toward forgetting. A veil made of inattention, which is the only veil a wise thing trusts. The humans who would love the bowl correctly - the ones who would lie still for an hour breathing guano dust to see a hand replace a twig - they will still find it, one every few decades. And they will leave knowing not to tell it the way treasure is told.

That's the first act: a protection that protects from protection.

The second act is smaller. Invisible even to the troop.

At three in the afternoon, when the differential pulls most strongly and the rock sweats, a droplet forms on the underside of an overhang above the meter-wide basin. It has formed there for nine thousand years, falling every forty seconds, feeding the basin that never evaporates. The groove it runs in is one grain away from being too shallow. In another four hundred years, the water would take a different path and the basin would dry to cracked silt.

The power does not make it rain. It does not fill the basin.

It deepens the groove by one grain.

It runs a fingertip - if it has a fingertip - along the runnel for less than a millimeter. The stone gives way without breaking, the way you might press your thumb into warm wax. Now the droplet will keep falling there for another ten thousand years.

That is all.

The third act is not an act at all.

It stays. For a while. In the way that such a thing can stay without occupying space.

It listens to the cathedral correctly, which no human can quite do because humans hear themselves listening. It hears the cricket drone not as noise but as a carrier wave. It hears the swiftlet clicks as navigation. It hears the plink-plink-plink as timekeeping. And underneath, it hears the troop's soft *hut hut*, and it understands what they are saying, which is not much in words but is a lot in meaning:

*Here. Here. I am here. Move left. Beetle. Grub. Baby still on. Quiet. Viper last night by the ramp. Here.*

It hears the old female's infant breathing against her pink-grey belly. It hears the juvenile who fished the grub carefully replace the twig in the crack for tomorrow. That - more than the tool, more than the chert edge - is what makes it linger.

Care for tomorrow in a place that has no promise of tomorrow except the rock sweating again at three o'clock.

It does not answer the *hut hut*. To answer would be to make them look up and see a sky with something in it besides blue-white and black. Their cosmology is perfect: the rim *is* the horizon. The oval of sky *is* the lamp. The world *is* twenty meters of tiered, weeping stone. To give them a larger world would be to make this one small. And this one is not small.

So it just listens, and in listening, witnesses. Lets the fact of them be fully known somewhere other than inside themselves, for the first time since their ancestors got stranded on this warm island when the climate dried.

Being completely known and not being changed by it - that is the rarest blessing. That is love without capture.

When it goes - which is not going, just a shift of attention - it leaves nothing that can be found.

No feather. No footprint in the silt. No warmed stone.

Only that the droplet will keep falling where it should, and that a few humans will feel, without knowing why, that the top of that particular mountain is not for them, and that for one long, indigo afternoon, thirty small ash-grey beings were heard all the way through, cricket-drone and all, and found good.

You climb out at dusk, if you were ever there. The wall is just wall again. The air thins and dries and cools. Behind you the sound thins to a single frequency and then to wind.

Below, in the bowl, a small hand - broad-footed, long-fingered, ridged - reaches out in the fungus light and replaces a twig in a crack.

For tomorrow.

And that tomorrow, which has been held, holds.
