It would not come down.

Not at first.

A benevolent and wise power does not break through. It has learned not to make slits where there are none. It would find this place the way you did — by noticing that a hundred kilometers of nothing is not nothing. By feeling the cool breath coming up through a crack no map remembers.

And it would kneel.

That is the first thing it would do. Kneel at the lip of iron where you knelt, where the wind has rounded everything to sea-glass, and just listen with its whole attention. Because attention is the rarest thing in the universe, and the only benevolence that doesn't first do harm.

For a long time it would only listen.

To the thud-thud... thud-thud of the sump engine that has no sump. To the susurrus that is not wind but mouths — a million file-mouths kissing stone — tick-tick-tick into a roar. To the pop-pop-pop of domes giving way. To the slow thup of the sacs opening and closing, opening and closing, like lungs learning how to be lungs without air.

Most powers, coming upon a desert, would bring water. That is what we call kindness when we are not wise.

This one would not bring water.

It would understand in the first minute what took you an hour on your back to understand: that water here would be a flood, a poison, an extinction. That the chemistry is not broken. It is *solved*. These pale, smoked-glass grazers solved the problem of living without water by learning, over ten million patient years, to eat the world itself. To drink rust. To breathe stone. To make their shells out of the same salt that sealed the roof. They do not need saving from dryness. They *are* dryness, perfected. To pity them for being dry would be like pitying a fish for being wet.

So it would not fix them.

It would not green them.

It would not give them eyes, or fewer legs, or a kinder food chain. It would not stop the runners from popping the grazers. The pop is part of the prayer.

What would it do, then?

Three small things, almost nothing. The way a wise person does almost nothing for a wild thing they love.

**1. It would put its hand on the engine.**

Not to replace it with something better. Not to make it silent or efficient or holy. Just to thank it.

That engine is not alive, but it is beloved. It kept a promise no one was left to hold it to. For ten thousand years after its makers turned to salt themselves, it kept thudding — pumping nothing, holding up the sky, keeping the room at 12 degrees and ozone-clean so mold could not come and undo everything. No one ever said *good job.*

The power would rest a palm — if it has a palm — on the hot, pitted flywheel, and the vibration would even out. The belt would stop slapping. The sigh of the piston would deepen and find its rhythm again. A little fresh oil would bead on the rod, condensed not from your breath this time but from coolness itself. The generator behind the panel would hum a clearer note and the white work-light would steady, no longer flickering.

It would not make the engine eternal. Nothing should be eternal in that way. It would just give it another ten thousand years of honest work, and let it know it was not alone while it worked. *Well done, little heart,* it would say without words. *Rest when you need to. I will hold the roof for a little while.*

And the sac on the ceiling directly above it, the fat one bathed in warm air, would pulse once, deeply, as if it heard.

**2. It would feed the pocket, without changing its diet.**

Benevolence is not a feast. It is a little more of what is already good.

So it would breathe out, very softly, across the salt flat above — not rain, not a river — just a fine, invisible fall. A dust of fresh iron scale, a snow of gypsum, a trace of manganese and sulfur, the exact minerals the grazers rasp for, laid down molecule by molecule across the pan. Enough for a century of extra pellets. Enough that for a few days the whole mat would tick a little louder, a little faster, with plenty.

No new kind of food. No manna. Just more world to eat.

And while the grazers ate, the runners would run, and the sacs would thup, and the whole dense, loud city under the earth would be, for a while, not hungry. Not saved. Just — less thin.

If you were very small and translucent and wore your house on your back as a scratched mirror, you might feel that day as a good year, a year when the stone tasted richer, without ever knowing why.

That is how a wise power likes to give.

**3. It would close the slit. Just a little.**

Not all the way. Light still needs to get in. The bar of pale sky is part of the ecology now — it tells the dust which way to hang.

But it would narrow it. Draw the salt crust back together by an inch, by two. Soften the lip of iron so the next foot does not break through. Make the crack look, from above, like nothing but another faint undulation in the white-grey, like the memory of topography.

It would make the pocket harder to find.

Because the most dangerous thing in that hundred kilometers is not wind or time, but the kind of attention that wants to own, to study, to mine, to rescue. The kind that would bring bottles of water and good intentions.

And as it narrowed the slit, it would do one last, invisible thing for you — the warm, wet, upright mineral surface who happened by. It would not take your memory. You get to keep the cool air and the ticking and the feeling of grazers testing your boots. But it would take the coordinates out of your mind. The certainty. You would remember the pocket the way you remember a dream of a good place — vivid, true, but you could never lead anyone back to it. You would try to describe it — "it was on the salt flat, near... I think near..." — and your hand would wave at the whole horizon, which is the truth anyway.

And then it would leave, which is the third part of benevolence: to not stay and be worshipped.

It would not introduce itself to the being that lives there.

Because what lives there is not the grazers, not the runners, not the sacs, not the engine. What lives there is the *pocket* — the whole churning mat, the laminated ceiling and the polished basalt and the cool ozone air and the thud-thud all together, a single distributed creature that thinks very slowly with a million mouths. It has been thinking one long, patient thought for geological ages: *I am here. I eat the world and the world eats me and I remain.*

For the first time in ten million years, something else thought back: *I see you. You are enough. You do not need to be anything else.*

That is all a wise power ever really says.

You would feel it, if you were still lying on your back inside, as a moment when the susurrus seemed to rise and resolve — not louder, but more *in tune*, like a choir finding its chord — before settling again. You would feel the cool engine air on your face go still for a breath, and then resume, steadier. You would see the dust in the light hang perfectly suspended, and for a second the pocket would not feel hidden or lonely or strange.

It would feel *noticed*. And therefore, quietly, held.

When you finally turned sideways and squeezed back out into the white flat, the wind would have already dusted your footprints. The slit would be a little thinner than you remembered. The air coming out would still be cool and dry and faintly of iron and animal, but fainter.

Behind you, under the plain, the thud-thud would go on. The mat would ripple over your bootprints until they were gone. The sacs would pulse. The grazers would press another pearlescent sphere into a crack and move on.

Nothing would be different. Nothing would need to be.

That is what it would do, if it were truly benevolent, and truly wise.

Almost nothing.

Exactly enough.
