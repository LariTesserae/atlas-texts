It would not come down.

That is the first thing.

Anything less wise would come down. Would flare in that flat white light like a second sun, would land on the striated basalt beyond the last wall and announce itself. Would part the low overcast. Would speak loudly, because to speak loudly is to be heard, and to be heard is to be believed to be helping.

A benevolent and wise power would have already learned what loud does in the Low City. It dies three feet from your mouth eaten by moss and wet wool. So it would not come down.

It would notice. That is already so much.

To be noticed — truly noticed — is the one thing the Low City has never had. The ice did not notice it, it only left. The plain does not notice it, it is too young to have learned attention. The people inside it notice only the next pipe, the next seam, the next hand to hold because if you look up too long at the perfect cold circle of the horizon you get vertigo.

A wise power would notice the whole thing at once the way you notice a small animal sleeping: all of it, not as parts. Not *the city* and *the people* as two problems. One damp, steaming, quietly hammering organism. A perspiration. A breath held against a cold that hasn't decided if it's done.

And it would understand, quickly, what it was looking at. Not a tragedy. Not a mistake that needs correcting.

A lesser kindness would see pale children with red-rimmed eyes and wet cuffs and think *I must dry them. I must warm them properly. I must give them sun.* Would see walls sharing walls and think *I must give them space, boulevards, parks, proper streets.* Would see the braided water that never learned to be a river and think *I must carve them one, straight and useful.* Would see the incessant patching and think *I must give them rest, machines to do it for them, a Finished City at last.*

A wise benevolence would see why each of those mercies would be a murder.

If you dry them, you take away the vine that feeds them and the moss that quiets them and the condensation they drink. If you give them space, you break the mycelial sharing of heat on which every kinship is built — property is a warm pipe, remember — and you make them cold and alone. If you carve them a river, you steal from the plain its first, slow lesson in becoming land; you rob embryonic geology of its childhood. If you finish the city, you kill it. Its only stability *is* turbulence. It stays alive by staying unfinished.

So it would do none of that.

What would it do?

It would lie down.

Imagine a power that can afford to be soft. It does not need to stand. So it would lie down around the plain like weather, like a field. Not over it, exactly — at its edges, at its height, at a distance that is not distance. It would put its attention the way a cat puts its body around kittens: without pressing.

And then, three small things, so small they could be mistaken for nothing happening at all. Because wise help, where the helped are already so brilliantly adapted, looks almost like nothing.

**First, it would hold the cold.**

Not take it away. Holding is different. The ice is still receding just beyond the last wall, a white cliff on the horizon that never quite leaves. The people feel it even when they don't look — the landlord, the old fear — *if we stop, the cold comes back in.* They are right. On any deep winter the wind off that ice can still slide across the flat plain and find every poorly caulked seam at once and flower frost on the inside of the walls.

The power would not melt the ice. The ice belongs to the plain's story.

It would simply place a hand — if it has hands — between. A little lift. A thermal buffering, a fraction of a degree that no thermometer the Low City owns could name. The kind of warmth you only notice because the frost ferns on the inside of the north walls take an hour longer to form, and the kettle on the shared pipe takes a minute less to boil. Enough that a mother, passing an infant from arms to arms in a sleeping nook over a culvert, doesn't have to check the pipe twice in the night.

It would not tell them it was doing this. If they knew, they would stop moving, and stopping would unmake them. So it holds the cold quietly, the way you might stand outside a child's bedroom door on a stormy night, not waking them, just being the reason the wind doesn't quite get in.

**Second, it would teach the stone to remember.**

The plain is too new. That is its secret sorrow. Water sheets and braids and vanishes because basalt hasn't learned to hold a scar. There is no soil yet, just ground rock and till. Everything the people eat has to be grown on top of themselves — on roofs, in gutters, on walls — because the land outside gives nothing back.

A wise power knows you cannot give soil. Soil is time made into crumbs. You can only invite time to go a little faster where it is already trying.

So it would not dump earth from elsewhere. It would stoop — again, without ever appearing to stoop — and breathe on the striations. Not a hot breath. A microbial breath. It would seed the plain under and around and very slightly beyond the city with the things that make soil when no one is watching: the lichen that cracks basalt with mild acid, the bacteria that fix nitrogen out of that sharp clean air, the fungus that learns to eat rock dust. And it would give the water a suggestion.

Water wants to be a river. It is hesitating out of youth, not inability. The power would whisper not to the people but to the water itself, in the language water understands, which is gradient. A tilting, invisible, a few centimeters over a kilometer — a coaxing. Enough that one braid, just one, on the eastern edge where the city weeps its warmest runoff, begins to hesitate a little less, to linger, to cut — a finger-width in a season — a shallow, meandering, childish channel into the till.

No one in the Low City will notice for ten years. Then a boy who carries moss to the edge to dump it will notice his boots stay dry on one side and not the other. In twenty years they will have a ditch they didn't dig that carries pipe-water away without flooding the lightwell kale. In a hundred, a brook. In a thousand, a proper small river with a name. By then it will feel like it was always there.

That is how a wise power gives a river. Not by carving, but by giving permission.

**Third, and most important, it would give them a deeper quiet.**

This sounds like nothing. They are already quiet. But their quiet is anxious — the hush of people who have learned to swallow their voices because the air swallows them first.

The power would not make them louder. It would make the quiet *hold* them better.

If you stood on that high maintenance gantry after it had come, you would not hear anything new. No choir, no revelation. The drip from the pipe over your head would still be the loudest thing. But you would find, without knowing why, that when you leaned in to say something mouth-to-ear, you didn't have to lean quite so close. That a murmur carried six inches further before the moss ate it. That two people could whisper across a narrow gap and be understood the first time, without repeating.

And at night, when the frost ferns form and melt and form again, there would be a new kind of silence underneath the muffled thuds — not emptiness, but a listening silence. The silence of being heard without having to shout.

The people would feel it as a change in the steam. They steam constantly, you remember. That faint cloud off their shoulders and hairlines. On the first morning after the attention settled, a woman patching mortar with one hand and potting a tuber with the other would straighten up, roll her neck, and realize she wasn't listening for the wind off the ice. Not because she had decided to stop, but because some part of her deeper than decision felt — for the first time since her grandmother's grandmother — that something else was listening for it.

She would pat the pipe she was leaning on, as they all do, but this time she would linger a half-second longer, and the pipe would be warm. Not hotter. Just warm in the way a living thing is warm.

She would not know she had been blessed. She would just, for once, not feel the vertigo when she glanced at the horizon. The perfect cold circle would still be there. The flat white light would still be ordinary and endless. But it would seem, in a way she had no words for and didn't need words for, *witnessed*. Held in mind by something that did not want anything from it.

She would go back to whispering, to patching, to dripping. The city would keep building itself over itself, turbulent and lush and muffled. The pale moths would still roost on the hot pipes. The blind white fish would still appear where water stood long enough. The hand-vine would still need cutting back weekly.

Nothing would look finished. Nothing would look saved.

And if you — who have been watching from that gantry — asked the power, *why did you do so little?* it would answer, if it answers at all, in the Low City's own manner: leaning in, mouth-to-ear, so quietly you have to be very close to hear:

*Because they were already doing it right. I only made sure the world would let them continue.*

And then it would stay. Not forever — nothing stays forever on an embryonic plain — but for a long, long time, lying around the city like a warm, invisible hill that the wind breaks on, remembering the pattern of every shared pipe and every passed infant and every shelf fungus sliced like bread, so that even if one winter the turbulence does falter, even if a block does go dark and cold, nothing precious there will be lost without being known and loved at the moment of its passing.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do, if anything.

It would not rescue them.

It would keep them company while they rescue themselves.
