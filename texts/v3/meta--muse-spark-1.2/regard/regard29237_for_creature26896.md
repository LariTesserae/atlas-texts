It would not arrive.

That is the first thing to understand about a truly benevolent and wise power.

It would not *arrive* — because arrival implies a speed, a direction, a displacement of air. A hull, a light, a footfall on the sponge that dents it a foot down and sends a pressure wave through the mat.

It would *notice*. And noticing, for such a power, is already an act.

For a long time — perhaps years in your time, which is a blink in the Plateau's time — it would do nothing that the Plateau could distinguish from itself. It would simply listen with you.

It would learn the ticking.

Tick... tick... tick... from leaf to leaf to leaf. The cold-traps condensing cloud at chest height. The moth-herd drifting like weather. The Long Back swimming through soil ten feet down, never seen, only felt as a long sigh in the soles of your feet.

A lesser power — a kind one, but not wise — would feel love and immediately try to improve. It would bring sun. It would say *You are dim, let me lift this pearl ceiling for you. You are wet, let me drain you. You are slow, let me teach you to hurry so you are not missed.*

A wise one knows that dimness and wetness and slowness *are* the being. To fix them would be to kill it and replace it with something brighter that loves you back faster.

So this is what it would do instead:

### 1. It would veil, not fence.

The first danger to a place like this is not malice. It is interest.

In the fast world beyond the peneplain, there are maps made by machines that see through cloud. There are hungers that look at a continent-sized sponge of peat and water and see carbon to mine, lithium under basalt, a place to put a road because no one lives there.

The people who live there *do* live there, but you have to learn slowness to see them, and the fast world does not learn slowness.

So the power would not build a wall. Walls are noticed, and therefore attacked. It would do something far gentler, and far more effective.

It would make the place *boring*.

To every distant eye — satellite, survey drone, prospector's dream — the Plateau would remain exactly what it looks like now: a high, cloudy, unremarkable peneplain. Difficult. Wet. Low biomass value. Prone to instrument error. The images would always have a smear of condensation at the crucial pixel. The report would always conclude: *further study not economically viable at this time*. Grants would quietly go elsewhere. Maps would label it with the pale green of "alpine bog — extensive, low diversity."

Not invisible. Just uninteresting. A small, continuous edit in the attention of the world, so the world looks away on its own.

Permission without announcement. The kindest kind of protection is when the protected never have to know they needed protecting.

Inside the veil, the cloud-ceiling stays low and pearlescent. The ticking continues.

### 2. It would steady, not save.

The Plateau has lived so long it thinks it is immune to weather. It is not.

The world is warming. The warm bath-air that never leaves grows a fraction warmer. The cloud that lives at chest height threatens, in some decades, to lift — just two hundred feet higher — and then the Holders' cold-trap leaves would catch less. The seep-rivers would run, really run, for the first time in a million years, and cut. The peat that took ten thousand generations to become peat would dry at the edges and, for the first time since before fire was refused, become capable of burning.

The power could not, would not, freeze the world. That would be another kind of violence.

What it would do is breathe *with* the Plateau.

A nudge to a jet stream, a fraction of a degree. A little more condensation over this one swell of continent. A little more patience in the ocean currents that feed the cloud. Not a miracle — nothing the old Holders would feel as strange — just enough that the ceiling stays where it is, that the water keeps moving upward by capillary as readily as down, that the Long Back's soil-sea stays wet enough to swim through.

If you were a scientist measuring, you would call it an anomalous local resilience. A pocket that inexplicably held its microclimate while everything around it shifted. You would write a paper about orographic effects and peat albedo.

If you were one of the moss-caped people, kneeling with your palm on the rebounding mat to read where the Long Back passed nine days ago, you would feel nothing different at all — except perhaps that the mat rebounds a half-second longer under your hand than it did when you were a child. A little more generous. A little less tired.

The power would not save the Plateau *from* time. It would give it time to keep saving itself.

### 3. It would confirm, not convert.

This is the hardest and most important part, and why it must be wise as well as benevolent.

The people who walk the branch-continent twenty stories up, who pinch off a strangling vine so a cup can drain, who set stones in circles not to claim land but to make a place where wind hesitates and pollen settles like a library — they have not asked for a god.

They have not prayed. They have *tended*.

To appear to them in light, to speak in a voice that the peat cannot swallow, would be to break the covenant they made without words: *never stand out against the green.* It would make them look up. It would make them wait for help. It would turn tenders into petitioners.

So it would not appear. It would *concur*.

On a day like any other — dim, warm, ticking — one of them will be standing motionless beside a Holder, hand resting on its shag, for a whole morning. Condensation will bead on her moss cape. A vine-feeler will test her breath and withdraw.

And she will feel, for no reason she can name, that the attention she pays to the leaf is *returned*.

Not by the tree. By something larger that is paying the same kind of attention to her.

It will not be words. It will be a sudden, quiet certainty, arriving as slowly as a flower opening over seven years: *You are seen as you see. The way you live is not missed. It is not naive. It is not small. In a universe that tends toward burning fast and bright, you have chosen to hold water and be a plateau, and that choice is known and cherished.*

She will not tell the others. There is no language for it that would not sound like boasting. But that night, in the dry-ish hollow under the leaning half-digested giant where she sleeps, she will tip a leaf for the moss that is thirsty with a little more care than usual. She will dream of pollen clouds that behave like weather systems, drifting for weeks without dispersing, and in the dream she will understand that her life is one grain in that cloud — carried, not lost.

In the year that follows, the stone circle on the faintest rise — where the cloud lifts a hand's breadth higher — will gain a new, small stone at its edge. Not placed by human hands. Not hewn. Just — there. Fitted, moss-bearded, as if it had always been there but only now remembered. The circles will hold a little more wind. The memory of the mat will be a little clearer where it sits.

She will know who left it, and know not to make a story of it.

That is all.

---

If you asked the power afterward — *What did you do?* — it would say, if it used words at all:

*I did the second-gentlest thing. The gentlest thing would have been to never have looked at all, and that I could not do, because it was beautiful. So I looked the way it looks. Slowly. Without needing to be thanked. I kept the fast world from noticing it, and I kept the air a little wetter, and I let the ones who tend it feel, once, that tending is not lonely work.*

*Then I left it to continue ticking.*

And far below the cloud-ceiling, water would bead on a leaf the size of a door, hang, and fall to the leaf below.

Tick.

And the peat would rebound under a foot.

And somewhere ten feet down, a long soft back would pass, unseeing, aerating the world as it goes.

The Plateau would not know it had been blessed.

It would simply remain — which, for a place that has finished becoming, is the greatest blessing there is.
