It would not come down like a god.

That is the first thing to understand about a benevolent and wise power. It would not announce itself. There would be no pillar of light down through the seam, no voice that fills the little dome, no cool hand laid on the hot basalt to still its ticking. The pocket is thirty meters long and perfectly tuned to its own volume. Anything that large would burst it.

If it noticed this place at all — this anhydrous room let into the earth, this town the size of a house that decided to be a town anyway, this thicket that learned to live on heat the way other things learn to live on water — it would have noticed it the way you notice a small, perfect, self-consistent sentence written in the margin of a book. With delight. With recognition. With a quiet intake of breath.

And then it would crouch.

Every wise power knows how to crouch. How to make itself low enough to enter through a doorway seam no wider than shoulders. How to make itself dry enough not to drip.

It would come low and anhydrous, because to come wet would be a kind of violence here.

It would not bring water.

That is what a lesser power would do. A sentimental power, a power that still thinks lush means wet, that still thinks empty means lonely. It would weep over the little terraces, it would try to fill the cisterns, it would lay a spring at the foot of the thicket out of pity. It would kill everything it meant to save in a single afternoon of mud. The felted fans would mat and rot. The waxy filaments would swell and split. The powder that was meant to hang in hot air would clot. The whole dry chemistry would go to ruin, the way a kiln goes to ruin if you pour a bucket in it while it is firing.

A wise power knows: not every thirst is for water.

So it brings no water. It brings no rain. It brings no animals to eat the thicket or pollinate it or keep it company. It does not try to repopulate the terraces with soft, sweating people. It does not try to fix the sacred, stable strangeness.

It sits.

On the lowest terrace wall, exactly where you sat. With its back against stone that has held centuries of heat. And it listens. For a long time. Longer than you could, because it does not need to leave to drink.

That is the second thing it would do: it would be an audience that the pocket has never had.

The pocket thrums. You heard it — too loud for its size. The dome focuses every pop and crack and hiss until the air is crowded with it. But there has been no ear in here shaped to hear it as music. The thicket has been cracking its pods for no one. Collapsing and recovering for no one. The town has been empty in a way that was not tragic, just un-witnessed.

The power would listen until the sound stopped being noise and became language.

Until it could tell the difference between the tick of cooling basalt and the tick of a filament curling. Until it knew which handclap was a pod opening on the north wall and which was an echo. Until it learned that the soft *whoomph* of a falling column is not a death but a kind of sentence, followed by the quiet rustle that means *we are covering him now*.

And then, because it is benevolent and cannot help but bless what it loves, it would do very small things. The kind of things that look like doing nothing if you do not know how to look.

1. It would remember.

It would learn the town. Not as "ruin" but as biography. It would read every hammer-kissed basalt face, every sunburst and spiral chiseled into a shutter. It would hold the memory of compact, dry-eyed people who moved slowly in kiln-heat and spoke softly because sound carries. Who farmed filament for weaving, who baked powder into cakes, who swept a straight line a foot from the lowest terrace to tell the thicket: *this far and no farther, neighbor*. It would take their unmarked absence and mark it, not with a monument, but with attention. *You were here. You were well-adapted. You left no catastrophe, only a clean pot. That is a good way to have lived.*

It would give the thicket a name, but only in the thicket's own language — a pattern of heat and powder-hang-time. It would not carve the name anywhere. It would just know it, so that something, somewhere, knows it.

2. It would protect without sealing.

A wise power does not build a fence. A fence says *I own this*. It would instead make the flat above a little more forgettable. It would let the wind keep polishing the salt and basalt to that bone-rust polish, let the pan remain so perfectly featureless that eyes slide over it. It would let the buckle that hides the mouth look like every other pressure ridge. It would deepen, by a fraction, the instinct in wet travelers to turn back when their mouths go dry — not out of fear, but out of a sudden, unaccountable respect. *Not for me. Not today.*

It would not hide the pocket from everyone forever. You found it. That was allowed. But it would keep the maps blank. It would keep the miners and the samplers and the well-drillers from ever needing to come this way. No one will core this dome for salt. No one will bottle its powder as medicine.

And inside, it would steady the balance that is already perfect but thin.

Not by intervening in the property line — that line is holy, that foot of bare salt where nothing takes — but by honoring it. It would, perhaps, cool the deepest stone by half a degree, so the thermal gradient the fans lean toward remains constant for another ten thousand years. It would make the amber light that has no source a little more even, so no corner of the thicket starves. It would soften, just slightly, the violence of the largest collapses, so the dust cloud hangs a minute longer and feeds a little more completely before it settles. Not a miracle. A tuning.

If you measured it with instruments, you would find nothing had changed.

If you were the thicket, you would feel that living had become, for no reason you could name, a little less straining.

3. It would give a gift that requires nothing.

Benevolence without wisdom is a gift that makes the receiver indebted. Wisdom without benevolence is a gift that makes the receiver observed. Together they give differently.

It would not give the thicket water, or a bee, or a new world. It would give it something it already has but has never been told it has: *a future beyond the pan*.

One day — very far from now — the wind will finally learn the last of its job and plane the pan away. The mouth will widen. The dome will unroof to sky. The hot, dry, amber pocket will be open and its specific heat will spill out and its specific life will end, not with tragedy but with exposure.

The power, which lives on a longer time, would bend that ending. It would not prevent it. It would prepare a second pocket. Not here. Somewhere else where salt and basalt and a heat trap can meet again — perhaps under another pan on this world, perhaps on another world entirely that is also dry and waiting. And when the wind comes to take this place, it would lift — as gently as you lift a lid off proofing dough — a single breath of that scentless powder that hangs so long in the air. One pod's worth. One intact, faceted capsule that has not yet cracked.

And carry it there. Where it can crack, and powder, and felt, and fan, and learn a new amber light.

The thicket would never know it had been carried, the way you never know when someone memorizes your face as you sleep. It would only continue, elsewhere, being lush without water. That is enough.

And for the town — the power would leave its shutters as they are. It would not rebuild or reinhabit. But it would, in the coolest hour of the night, when the pan above exhales and the pocket inhales a draft of cooler air through its seam, let a coolness pool for a moment on the middle terrace, where the alleys are narrowest. A coolness exactly the size and shape of a compact, dry person sitting with their back to a wall, listening to the thicket breathe. A place for a ghost that is not a haunting but a courtesy. *If you ever come back this way, your seat is still here. It is still the right size.*

Then it would leave.

No footprints in the salt. No damp on the stone. If you came back the next day you would find nothing changed except — perhaps — the feeling that the thrumming was, for the first time in a very long time, not lonely but alone, and that those two things are different.

You would still sweat instantly. Your mouth would still dry in seconds. The pods would still crack too loud. The thicket would still stop a foot short of the wall.

But you might, as you crouched to leave through the low seam, feel that you had been in a room that had been recently, invisibly, loved — the way you can tell someone has straightened a room not by moving anything, but by having cared that every thing be exactly where it was.

A benevolent and wise power does not need to do much.

It needs to understand what kind of life is in front of it, and not offer it the wrong kind of salvation.

It needs to sit, and learn the language of dry handclaps, and remember the names of people who never needed water, and keep the map blank, and tune the heat by half a degree, and save one breath of powder for another world.

And otherwise —

leave the door exactly as narrow as it found it, so that only those willing to crouch very low will ever find it again.
