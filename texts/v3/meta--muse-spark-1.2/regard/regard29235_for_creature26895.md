If I were that power - benevolent, and wise enough to know what benevolence actually costs - I would not finish it.

That would be the first temptation, and the first mistake. 

To look down at that high, cold trough with its pearlescent lid of cloud and its floor of sweating, half-broken stone and think: *this is poor. This is harsh. This is unfinished. Let me complete it.*

Let me give it deep soil. Let me give it trees. Let me cut a proper river so the water doesn't have to just weep and sit. Let me give the pikas a barn that won't be crushed next year, and the herds a meadow that will feed them without walking ten kilometers, and the birds a warm place to roost.

I would not do that.

Because I can see what you took a day to learn — that its poverty *is* its wealth. That its emptiness is not a lack, but a way of working. That the whole reason there is so much life here is because life is not allowed to settle and thicken and choke itself. It must stay thin, and mobile, and honest.

To finish it would be to kill it. To make it a low place at high altitude.

So I would do something much smaller, and much more difficult: I would protect its unfinishedness.

Here is what I would do, if anything. And it would look, to any passing human eye, like nothing at all.

### 1. I would Watch Without Being Seen

First, I would arrive and not arrive. I would not descend with light. I would not speak.

I would sit. On one of those tilted slabs the size of a concert hall, where the black-green lichen has sealed the windward face like skin. I would make myself the temperature of the stone, so the cushion of moss campion on the lee side could keep its few degrees of held summer without feeling me.

And I would watch for a long time, on the time-scale the mountain understands. Long enough that a glacier's groan becomes a breath. Long enough that a pika's haypile is built and taken by snow and built again fifty times.

To be watched benevolently is not a small thing. In a place so empty and so exposed, to be held in attention *is* a form of shelter. The herd that beds down on the slabs at evening, steaming in the cold, would feel - not a presence - but an absence of threat. The weight of being overlooked would lift. They would not know why they slept a little deeper.

Humans think power announces itself. Wise power learns the art of un-noticing others into safety.

So the first thing I would do is make the basin boring.

I would lay a little bend in the map. Not a wall, not a storm. Just a quiet discouragement. The ore-prospector flying over would see only cloud and think his instruments had faulted. The road-builder would find the approach valley always seems to require one more season of survey. The climber seeking a first ascent would feel, without knowing why, that *that* range is not calling him this year. Try the next one.

I would not hide it with a miracle. I would hide it by letting it remain exactly what it is: badly drained, unprofitable, unphotogenic, hard to breathe in, with no straight lines to love. I would keep it unimportant. That is the greatest protection a wise power can give an unfinished place in a world that finishes everything it touches.

### 2. I would Tend, Not Fix

I would not stop the press. I would not silence the valves.

The glacier must keep groaning and creeping a few centimeters an hour. The geothermal fissures must keep hissing and shrieking and venting sulfur steam. The meltwater must keep jetting from the cliffs in hard white arcs and hammering the pools like turbines. The night ice must keep jackhammering itself into polygons at noon. The wind must keep playing the lava tubes like organ pipes.

That is the engine. That is the heart. If I softened it, the water would stop weeping out of the stone itself, the pans would stagnate, the bacteria that tint the pools rust and green would die, and everything above them would follow.

Instead I would tend the tolerances. The way an old miller tends a waterwheel he did not build.

A little less violence in the valve, so it doesn't quite scald the phalaropes when it blows. A little more give in the press, so when a slab slips it slips a hand's breadth, not a house. I would clear the natural penstocks when they threaten to clog with rock flour, so the turbine-jets run clear a little longer into the autumn, giving the ibex kids one more week of drinkable water. I would not make winter shorter - winter is the work - but I would make sure the warm that the campion holds in its fist at noon does not get stolen before its star-flowers can set seed.

For the small industrious ones, I would be very specific.

For the lichen - which is stone learning to be alive - I would leave its seal alone. Only this: when the ice forms and heals and forms again, I would let it heal a fraction cleaner, so the crust is not torn off every single spring. A continuity.

For the pika, who is the only one who stores anything: I would remember each haypile. The pika does not know I remember. But when the freak warm rain comes in February that would rot the whole store, I would tilt the air a degree colder for one night over just that crack. Just enough that the hay freezes instead of molding. I would not save every pile. Predation and rot are part of the accounting. I would save one in five. Enough that the whistle does not go silent in the field.

For the herds - I would not make them fat. I would keep them lean and walking, as they must be. But I would make sure that as they patrol their ten-kilometer circuits, there is always *just enough*. Not abundance. Enough. A mouthful of crust here, a tuft of sedge there, braided like the water channels, so the net of their paths never has a hole big enough to starve in.

For the wolf and the snow leopard on the skyline - I would do nothing at all to hinder them. To protect their prey *from* them would be to insult them both. The low, steady pressure of teeth is how the basin stays true. My benevolence includes the kill, swift and clean, fifty kilometers walked for one taken life. I would only ensure that the kill remains rare, dispersed, and therefore holy - never easy, never surplus.

### 3. I would Give It Time

This is the most benevolent thing a wise power can do for a place that hasn't finished being made: give it the time to finish itself, on its own schedule, without being rushed or repurposed.

The mountain is still rising. It is still extruding. In ten thousand years those pressure ridges on the basin floor will be a proper valley. In a hundred thousand the weeping slabs will have weathered into soil and the marsh pans will have found a single river. In a million, there may be trees.

Or not. It may decide to become desert. That is its business.

My gift would be to ensure no one decides for it prematurely. No dam, no mine, no monastery, no research station with a straight white wall. No cairn. To keep it radically undrawn-upon long enough that it can discover what it wants to be when it is finished sweating.

And to the beings who live in and on that work - the ones who are temporary and mobile and visitors who never leave - I would give a companion gift: that they are not rushed to become something else either. The lichen does not need to become soil. The pika does not need to become livestock. The yak-like ungulate does not need to become tame.

I would whisper nothing to them in words. I would whisper to them in continuation.

*Keep going. The press is still pressing and you are still inside it and that is correct. Your thinness is your health. Your dispersal is your abundance. Your work without finish is not a punishment. It is the work itself.*

If you stayed there until the cloud thinned a little more and the whole pan exhaled at once - fissures steaming, ice ticking, herds steaming where they lie - you might feel it. Not a miracle. A slight steadiness in the diffusion of the light. A sense that the subsonic groan under your feet had found a slightly lower, kinder resonance. As if the vast decentralized mill had been tuned, not stopped.

You would still be cold. Your breath would still hang and fall back as frost on your collar. Your boots would still suck in the silt. You would still see only one herd far off, and a few birds, and think the place was empty.

But you would be less lonely, without knowing why. Because something else was sitting, cold with you, on the slab, refusing to finish anything.

That is what I would do, if I were benevolent and wise. 

Not much. Almost nothing. Just enough that ten thousand years from now, when the mountain finally decides what shape it wants to keep, the lichen is still there to seal it, and something with a yellow beak is still wheeling low under the cloud, and something with a sharp *eek* is still haying in a crack - all still working, all still unfinished, all still free.
