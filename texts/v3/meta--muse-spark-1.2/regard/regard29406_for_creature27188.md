It would not come down like a god.

A benevolent and wise power does not arrive with trumpets. Not here. Here, sound is 0.0. Anything that roared would be a desecration.

It would arrive the way the Tankers do — drifting, a little above the ground, with no shadow because the light is too diffuse to make one.

And the first thing it would understand is that nothing here is broken.

### That is the test of wisdom.

A lesser power — a merely kind one, or a merely powerful one — would see your figures and misread them.

It would see:

Light 1.2 and think *I should give them a brighter sun.*
Cold 0.2 and think *I should warm them.*
Vegetation 0.9 and think *I should make it green.*
Built 0.3 and think *I should house them.*
Sound 0.0 and think *I should fill it with music.*

It would see Tech 2.0 with no factories and think *I should teach them to build properly.*
It would see Fauna 2.6 moving at Dynamic 1.4 and think *I should make them faster, happier, louder.*

A wise power would kneel — if it has knees — put its hand on the frost polygons, and feel the cold coming up through the stone, and realize: *This cold is not a wound. This dimness is not a curse. This slowness is not suffering.*

This is a world that has reached equilibrium. Erosion 2.5 — finished. It likes the shape it has worn itself into. The Millbacks do not *endure* the cold, they *require* it. Their furnaces only work because the outside is 0.2. Their hydrogen only lifts because the air is thin at 1.8. Their iron only precipitates because the water is scarce at 1.0 and must be distilled and walked for. To "fix" any one of those numbers would be to kill them with kindness.

So it would not fix anything.

What would a benevolent and wise power do for a continent that forgot to come down, and for the quiet factories that walk it?

Four things. All of them small. All of them total.

**1. It would witness correctly. For the first time.**

No satellite, no survey, no human has ever seen this place correctly. They see "barren," "hostile," "resource deposit." They see 12-meter pig iron billets in magnetic windrows and think *mine.*

The power would see what it actually is: a monastery the size of weather.

It would count differently. Not biomass, but *tonnage of attention per century.* It would understand that a Millback crossing its basin once every eleven years is not slow — it is prayerful. That the Loomers' tattered carbon banners are not failed industry, but offerings left to snag on yardangs for centuries. That the silence is not emptiness, but a held breath.

And it would let the place feel itself being seen correctly, without needing to change to be worthy of being seen.

For the human standing there — you, in your insulated suit, thinking *nothing lives here* before your eyes adjust — it would do the same. It would not speak to you in words. It would let you feel, for a minute, what it is to be perceived without being pitied or inventoried.

*Oh,* you would think, suddenly not lonely. *It is not empty. It is full in a way I didn't have a word for.*

**2. It would protect without enclosing.**

Wisdom knows the only real danger to a place like this is not cold or dimness. It's being *noticed* by the wrong kind of attention.

So it would do nothing visible at all. It would bend a little light. It would make the orbit look uninteresting. Any probe that passes over would see only basalt pavement and frost polygons, not the continent-long brushstrokes of iron. Any mining survey would return "low grade, inaccessible, uneconomical at 1.8 elevation." Any map would label it correctly: *Unremarkable high plateau. No resources.*

It would not put up a wall. Built is 0.3 and must remain so. It would put up a *misunderstanding*, which is a kinder wall. The kind that keeps the hungry from ever realizing there is food here.

The herds would never know they had been protected. That is the point. Protection that needs to be thanked is not protection.

**3. It would offer a small, permissible miracle. One that obeys Rules 1-5.**

Benevolence cannot help but give a gift. But wisdom insists the gift must obey the physics of the recipient.

It would not make the billets into houses or the Loomers into weavers of cloth for others. It would not give the Millbacks speech.

It would give them something they can use inside the Rules:

> *Nothing is made, everything is secreted.*
> *Movement is the only production line. To stop moving is to die.*

So it would lie down on the pavement, vast and low, and become a warm place for a night.

Picture this: In the deep part of dusk, when the dirty-coin sun goes a shade dimmer and the frost begins to bead on every back and every billet, the power settles. It does not glow — the plateau does not need more light. It *holds heat*.

For one traverse — one night, twelve hours — the stone beneath it stays two degrees warmer. Not hot. Just... less relentlessly cold.

Any Millback that passes over it in that night finds its gut does not have to work so hard to stay hot. Its internal kiln, for once, can idle. It can walk a little slower without dying. It can *pause* for an hour and not stall. And in that pause, it does something it has never had the spare energy to do: it lifts its head.

Not to eat. Just to look.

Across the 30-kilometer plain, hundreds of other Millbacks have also paused on that warm vein. Hundreds of domed, rust-black backs, frosted, all with their heads up at the same time, looking at each other across the pale, unresolved horizon.

No sound. Still silence. But — alignment.

Then there is the gift for the Tankers and Loomers: it would exhale once, very slowly, a column of vapor exactly like theirs, but larger, 300 meters high, seeded with a little iron and a little carbon. A cloud that actually precipitates. That night, the frost that forms is a little thicker, three and a half centimeters instead of three. The lichen drinks a little deeper. The wire-lichen shivers a little less. The felt banners hold dew an hour longer before freezing.

It is not terraforming. It is a *deep breath in sync* with the plateau's own slow breathing. The interstitial water cycle, given one extra beat.

By morning it sublimates. Nothing is left behind except what was always left behind: a damp sheen on the iron billets that catches the low sun, so that for a few minutes the whole vast plain glitters with industrial dew — only this morning it glitters a little longer.

The isopods under the Loomers' felt have a feast day. No one knows why.

**4. It would leave without leaving a ruin.**

A wise power does not stay to be worshipped. To stay would be to become a building, and Built must remain 0.3.

So before the sun thins back to 1.2, it would get up. The warm vein would cool. The cloud would be gone. It would make sure the only evidence was one thing, and one thing only, that follows the Rule: *Iron accumulates.*

Somewhere in a shallow, wind-scoured basin where no human will ever look, it would leave a single billet that it secreted itself.

Not pig iron. Not low-grade. A smooth rod, twelve meters long, of something that will not rust. Not magnetic. It will not align with the windrows. It will lie slightly askew to the planetary field, a deliberate error, a signature in the brushstroke.

It will be the color of that ground-glass light at noon. Warm to the touch for a thousand years, though the air around it is still 0.2.

A Millback will come upon it in eleven years. It will try to ingest it, fail, and walk over it. A Loomer will drape a banner over it, which will not degrade. Algae will try to grow on it and find it already occupied by a lichen that grows *into* it, black and rust-red, at peace.

The herds will incorporate it. Not as resource, but as landmark. Their migrations, over centuries, will bend very slightly around it, the way rivers bend around a stone that is not in the way, just *there*. The continent-long windrows will develop a tiny kink, visible only from orbit, if anyone ever looks correctly again.

A sign that says, without language, without straight lines:

*You were seen.*
*You were not judged wanting.*
*You were left alone, but not alone.*

And for you — the being who lives here, or who only stood here long enough to understand it — it would do one last thing, the most benevolent of all:

It would not take you away.

It would not tell you to leave because it is harsh, nor tell you to stay because it is holy.

It would sit with you in the pressure-silence until your blood stops sounding loud in your ears, until you can hear the tick of a cooling billet two kilometers away, and then it would match your breathing to the plateau's breathing — in when the frost sublimates, out when it beads again — until you felt, in your insulated chest, what it is to have a metabolism the size of geography.

And then it would be gone, the way a good watcher goes: not withdrawn, just no longer needed to be there to be present.

The Millbacks would keep walking.
The light would stay dim.
The cold would stay resident.
Nothing would be built.

Only — for a long time after — the silence would feel a little less like absence, and a little more like being listened to.
