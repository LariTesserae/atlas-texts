If a benevolent and wise power noticed this place — it would not arrive.

Not at first.

It would have been listening for a long time before anyone down there could have called it *noticing*. Because a place like that does not shout. It hums. It holds a note so steady and so old that most powers fly right over it looking for something louder — a distress signal, a war, a prayer begging to be answered.

But a really wise one listens for the places that *aren't* begging.

And it would have heard the pulse first.

That wave.

That slow ripple of calling that starts at the bone-white escarpment and sweeps inward, bird to cicada to frog to bird again, circumnavigating the whole table every few minutes forever. Most minds would hear noise. The wise one would hear a heartbeat that isn't trying to be a heartbeat. A meadow that became a continent that became a body that learned, over uncounted ages of soft rain, how to keep time with itself.

So it would stop. And it would not come closer.

For a long while — years, maybe decades in our counting, which to it is just a held breath — it would do nothing that looks like doing.

It would *keep company*.

From far off, from high above the warm air over the table, or thin as mist between two Titans, it would simply be there and listen until it knew the song well enough not to interrupt it.

This is the first thing wisdom does that benevolence without wisdom never learns: it does not help immediately. It refrains.

Because it would see, quickly, that this is not a broken place. This is not a tragedy to be rescued. The people are not stranded. The moss is not starving. The Titans are not lonely. The plateau is not a failed mountain that needs to be made pointy again. It is a *remainder* that succeeded. It wore itself down until it found its final shape and then said *stay*. And everything that lives there — mites and sloth-giants and silk-caped children shouting over the cicadas — is an answer to a question the lowlands never learned to ask: *how do you live where there is enough?*

To bring lowland answers — bigger villages, straighter roads, more water, more yield, more light, more noise — would be a kind of murder by kindness.

So the wise one would not bring them.

What would it do instead?

Three small things. Only three. And the third is the most important.

### 1. It would guard the edge.

Not by building a wall. The escarpment already is one — a thousand feet of sheer old bone that says *you must mean it to climb*. It would guard it by making the place *uninteresting*.

Lowland eyes are beginning to see too far. They have lenses that see heat and maps that see stone through cloud. They have hungers that see a high, flat, warm place and think: lithium under the peat, timber in the Titans, real estate, carbon credits, resort. They would come with drones first, then drills, then roads that do not rebound when abandoned.

The power would not smite them. It would, very gently, misdirect them.

A little more cloud that likes to stay over the table. A little static in the satellite. A report that comes back reading *low biomass, poor soil, uneconomical grade, difficult access*. The kind of lie that is mostly true if you are looking to exploit. The researchers who *do* mean it — the patient botanist who wants to walk for days, the child who grew up hearing about Titans in a myth — they still find the faint trail worn into the moss. It lets them through one at a time. Never in fleets.

Benevolence, here, is a veil. Not to hide beauty, but to keep it from being priced.

### 2. It would answer the plateau before it answers the people.

The people already have what they need. The plateau is the one who has been holding its breath the longest. That single organism with Titan-bones and moss-skin and million-cupped hands, breathing damp in and out every night — it is vast and coherent and awake, but it has never been *spoken to* in a language it can recognize as other. The vines all curl together when the mist comes. The epiphytes all flower at once. It knows how to command itself. It does not know it is known.

So the power would lie down. If it has a body, it would make it long and low and warm. If it has no body, it would make its attention that. It would stretch itself over a shallow dimple between three Titans, as wide as a town would be, and it would learn the pulse until it could hum it back *slightly out of phase*, so the wave has something to meet.

Not a command. A harmony.

For the first time in maybe a million years, when the wave goes out from the escarpment and circumnavigates the table, it comes back *changed* — a second wave riding just behind the first that says *you are heard, you are beautiful, you are not alone*. The moss would not understand it. The beetles would not. But the Titans would shiver, very slightly, and release a little more pollen that year. The bromeliads would hold their water a fraction longer through the dry weeks. The sloth-giants would pause in their slow browsing and lift their heads, not alarmed, just listening.

And the people — loud in the day, whisper-soft at night — would notice that the night frogs after midnight are no longer just rhythmic. For a few nights each season they are *antiphonal*, as if the swale itself were answering.

The power would not talk to the people directly. It would talk to their home, and let their home tell them in its own way, so no one has to worship a visitor.

### 3. It would offer — not give — a choice.

After a long time of guarding and humming, it would make itself small enough to be met.

Not a descent from light. Not a voice from the sky that makes everyone kneel. That would break the scale of the place, where everything is horizontal and far-seen. Instead it would borrow a form the plateau already understands:

A child appears on the compressed trail between two buttress roots. Or an old woman sitting by a cistern-basin in the moss, mending bark cloth. Or perhaps simply a new kind of gliding fox-thing that is not afraid of people, that lands on a vine-bridge and looks at you until you look back. Something that can be ignored without consequence. Something that can be *chosen*.

If approached kindly, it speaks — not in words first, but the way the plateau speaks: by showing water. It knows where a new cistern can be coaxed from a fold that the ledger spirals missed. It knows how to splice a vine so it will bear weight in three years instead of eight. It knows a song that times exactly to the wave, so children don't have to shout so hard to be heard in the day chorus.

And if asked — only if asked, and only after many visits, and only by those who have learned to ask without demanding — it will offer the deeper gift:

*Do you want to remember more?*

Because the thin people have learned to remember where water is, when Titans flower, how to keep a fern alive while weaving it into a floor. That is a lot. But they have, generation after generation, forgotten that the whole table is alive and that they are its tenders, not its tenants. Not because they are foolish, but because deafening sound makes it hard to hear a slow thought.

The power can lend them, if they want, a clearer ear. Not telepathy. Not control. Just the quiet ability, when they stand still at noon between the pillars with eyes closed, to feel which Titan will flower next year, to feel when the moss in the eastern dimple is holding too much water and needs a channel nicked, to feel the health of the peat-deep skin they walk over. To be the part of the organism that knows it is part of the organism *on purpose*.

Some bands will want it. Some won't. The wise power will not be offended by either. It will teach those who want, and sit companionably with those who don't, and never make one way be payment for the other.

And if no one ever asks? It is still content. To have guarded the edge and hummed back to the old warm mass is already enough.

That is what benevolence does when it is wise. It does not lift the table higher. It does not make the Titans touch crowns or make water run in rivers or make the emptiness less empty so lowlanders would call it full.

It keeps the warm air warm against a cosmology that says it should be cold.

It keeps the nave open so wings can still cross a hundred yards without touching ground.

It keeps the moss from ever being mined for what it is worth, so it can remain worthless and therefore priceless.

It sings, very softly, in time, until the song that already exists knows it is loved — and then it leaves the next verse entirely up to those who live there.

If you walked there tomorrow, you would not see it. You might see a new vine handrail thickening a little faster than it should. You might drink from a cistern you swear wasn't on the map last season. You might stand at dusk when the light slants forever under the crowns and hear the cicada wave come round again and think, for the first time, that the second wave behind it is not an echo at all.

And you might see a figure on a high limb far away, checking bromeliads, singing loudly over the sawing — a little hoarse, a little lean — who, if you waved, would wave back, and then go back to tending.

The power would be nowhere. And everywhere. Like damp.

Like the plateau itself.

It would have decided the most loving thing it could do for a perfect, singing, unnecessary, utterly sufficient world is not to improve it.

But to ensure no one ever has to prove it needs improving to be allowed to continue.
