It would not arrive.

That is the first thing to understand about a benevolent and wise power.

It would not tear the cloud sea. It would not make the sun brighter or speak from above. There is no arrival in a place like this, because an arrival is a disruption, and disruption is the one thing the plateau has learned, stone by stone, to live without.

It would notice.

The way you notice that a canal is holding level when it should not be able to. The way you notice a moss has been thinned at exactly the right time. Attention, not intervention.

And because it is benevolent, its first act would be pleasure. Because it is wise, its second act would be restraint.

---

Any lesser power would have tried to fix it.

Any merely kind or merely strong power would have looked at the quiet and called it lonely, looked at the empty rock and called it waste, looked at the green rotting into peat because nothing eats it and called it broken.

*There are no animals,* it would think, *so I will give them animals.*

And it would pour birds into the warm blue, fish into the square-mouthed cisterns, beetles into the taro. It would mean well. Within a season the canals would be fouled, the thatch torn for nests, the moss stripped, the taro eaten to stalk. The people would have to become hunters and herders and pest-killers, and all their patient language for water-moving-in-stone would be replaced by words for chasing and shooing and guarding. The equilibrium that took centuries of *not* expanding would be shattered by a gift.

Any lesser power would have looked at the warm weeping stone and said, *there is so much empty plateau beyond the town, I will make it generous.* It would make the rock less porous, the springs run stronger, the taro yield double. The people would have to expand or waste. Their work, which is to hold steady, would become work to grow.

The wise one sees what the town actually is: not a problem to be solved, but a prayer that has finally learned how to pray correctly.

Maintenance as devotion. Emptiness as intention. A people who have become a well-cut channel, letting the mountain weep through them without spilling.

It would not correct them. It would bow to them.

---

So this is what it does, if anything:

**First, it sits down.**

Not above the rim, but *on* it. Where the warm air lifts from the cloud sea and the cold seep circles the ankles. It takes a shape that can do that — tall enough to be seen from two marsh basins away, damp enough that its feet do not splash. It does not shine. It weathers itself to the color of wet basalt and pale limestone so that from a distance you might mistake it for a person standing still.

And it simply holds level for a while.

A morning. A day. Long enough for an elder walking her taro to realize that the trickle in her canal has changed its tick against the vertical joint — not louder, not softer, but more *attended to*. Long enough for a young man tapping his sluice slabs to hear no hollow sound under any of them. The water that day runs as true as it has ever run, but it feels listened to, the way a dense, necessary sentence feels when someone finally hears all the words in it.

The people, who speak low and only when close, come one at a time and stand near it, not close. It does not ask where they came from. It lifts a sluice slab a thumb's width, exactly as they do, to correct a trickle only it heard was wrong. That is the whole miracle at first. Competence without hurry.

When a child finally whispers to it — because children have not yet learned to time their voices to the wind — *"Are you going to bring the birds back?"* — for the children have old words for animal things kept smooth on a shelf — it answers at the same volume, facing the rock, not the child.

*"Would you like me to?"*

And it waits.

This is the second thing a wise benevolence does: it offers a choice and makes the not-choosing as easy as the choosing.

The child frowns. The elders have come closer now. They confer the way they always do, facing the water. They have no word for bird that is not old and smooth. They try to imagine a rustle in the reeds that is not water or wind, and find they cannot without also imagining the taro eaten, the cistern fouled, the moss torn, the quiet broken so badly a normal voice would never carry again.

An old woman, whose soles know where a spring will break before it breaks, says, *"Water moving by itself inside water would be strange. We would not know how to keep the level."*

A young woman who tends the emerald felt on the north wall says, *"We have become good at being alone with the green. If something else lived here that needed the green, we would have to choose between it and the green. We have learned not to make that choice."*

They do not say no with fear. They say no with care.

The power nods. The only sadness in it is the pleased sadness of a teacher whose students have understood the lesson so well they no longer need the teacher.

*"Then I will not bring them,"* it says. *"But you have been without a mirror for a long time. Every living thing should have something that looks back at it and is not itself, and answers. May I leave you a mirror that does not eat?"*

They look at each other. They look at the canals. To hold level is to be a mirror for water. But water does not look back.

*"What kind of mirror?"* the child asks.

---

**It does these three small things, and no more:**

**1. It completes the cycle without breaking it.**

In the deepest marsh, where cut taro leaves have been laid for generations to soften into peat that never quite finishes softening, it kneels and breathes once, very lightly, into the black water.

Nothing appears to change.

But the next morning, if you lift the mat of fern, you will see — if you know how to look — a dust of movement no larger than a grain of lime: pale springtails, blind isopods, a beetle the size of a freckle that was not there yesterday and yet looks as if it has always been there. They do not fly. They do not sing. They do not eat living leaf. They eat only what is already falling, only the rot itself, and turn it back to soil a little faster, a little cleaner. The water underneath no longer goes sour in late summer.

The people notice the marsh smells sweeter, that is all. They do not need a word for insect. They just find their work of thinning a little easier, as if the marsh is helping them hold steady. A gift that does the same job they do: restraint and redirection, on a smaller scale.

It does not bring birds. It brings the cleaners that let the green remain uneaten and still not choke on itself.

**2. It gives them back their own voices.**

On the third evening, when the wind is down and voices would normally carry embarrassingly far, the power stands in the largest empty interval — that stretch of bare, damp black rock between two neighborhoods where no one builds, intentionally — and sings, once.

It does not sing with a mouth. It takes the sound the place already makes — water ticking in stone runnels, drip from moss to pool, wind unbroken across the tableland — and lifts it half a tone, so the whole plateau hums for a breath like a struck basin.

Then it stops, and teaches them how to make a thing.

From the reed that borders every canal, it shows them how to cut a length, stop it with a plug of peat, cut a window near the lip. A flute. The simplest flute. When you blow low across it, it makes a sound exactly like wind over a slot canyon, like water over moss, like nothing and like everything they already hear — but *pitched*, intentional.

*You have had no animal call to answer,* it says. *So answer the water. Answer each other without having to speak.*

That night, for the first time, a boy on one side of a marsh basin plays a single note, and a girl two basins away answers with the same note a breath later. It carries as far as a shout would have, but it is not obscene. It is the right kind of loud. The elders weep a little, damply, without sound, because grief is a spring gone white and this is the opposite.

They will not become musicians. They will remain gardeners. But now they will have a way to be far apart and still be together that is not language. A mirror made of air.

**3. It makes the emptiness safe to keep.**

Before it leaves — and it has to leave, because a power that stays becomes a weather, and weather here must remain normal — it does the one truly powerful thing, and does it where no one can see it.

It goes down.

Not into a cistern or a slot canyon, but into the stone itself, into the fresh shear where the plateau was lifted young and sharp. It puts its hands — if it has hands — on the fault that holds the whole table up above the cloud sea. The uplift is still young, still settling. In a few thousand years, without help, a corner will shear and a whole watershed will dump itself into air, and the weeping will find a new path and not come back.

It does not make the plateau eternal. Wise things do not make young mountains eternal. It just *remembers* the fault the way the elders remember water. It eases the pressure by a fraction, lets one seam weep a little more on the far side where no canals run, where no one will ever farm, so the main block holds level for a very, very long time.

A stabilization so subtle the stone itself will think it happened on its own.

When it comes back up its feet are paler, like river stone. It leaves one white limestone pebble, perfectly smooth, on the rim where the warm air rises. Not as an altar. As a way to say: *I was here. I saw that your work matters. Continue.*

---

It does not promise to return. It does not ask to be worshipped. It does not rename anything.

The next morning the plateau weeps tomorrow in exactly the same places, in exactly the same way — only the marsh smells sweeter, a boy knows how to make a reed answer another reed across empty rock, and the fault deep below holds a little truer.

The people find the white pebble. They do not build a shrine over it. They set it in the floor of a house, slightly dished, so condensation runs around it and out into the canal that passes by the door. Stone for stone, exactly as it was, with one stone that is a little different, and that is enough.

If you stand on the rim now, you will still feel the stability as a physical thing. But if you listen very carefully in the hour before the wind comes, you will hear something new under the tick and trickle: a low note, then an answering note, unhurried, well-timed, precise.

The plateau is no longer quiet the way an empty room is quiet.

It is quiet the way a well-tended garden is quiet when the gardeners have just begun to hum while they work.
