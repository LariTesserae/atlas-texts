It would not come.

That's the first thing a wise and benevolent power would know.

It would not *arrive*. It would not break the cloud sea below with light, or descend. The bowl is too small and too new and too perfectly balanced on its stem of stone to bear an arrival. Anything that came all at once would spill it.

So it would have already been there, and you would not have noticed when it noticed you. The same way you didn't notice the keepers at first because they moved at the speed the bowl moves.

Being seen by something benevolent and wise does not feel like being spotlit. It feels like being *held* without being gripped.

Here is what it would do:

Nothing that the bowl would notice as intervention, and everything that a life inside the bowl would feel as mercy.

---

It would not fix the bowl.

A lesser power would look at the raw concrete still curing, the rust weeping from tie-holes, the fig slowly splitting its own gear, and think *I should perfect this.* Make the concrete stop curing. Make the water run clear forever. Make the plants grow faster, or slower, or more usefully.

A wise one would understand that the whole holiness of the place is that it is *learning* how to be a watershed. That square-cut rill that is being worried into a U by water — that is its first memory. To take away the struggle of water finding its groove would be to take away its childhood. To make the fig and the gear resolve would be to end the negotiation that the keepers were born to translate.

So it would not make the bowl finished.

It would keep it *becoming.*

It would let the silt make its deltas in the cistern. It would let the concrete darken. It would let the day come, a year from now, when the jungle is too thick to need bare feet in warm water, and the keepers will have to leave because there is nothing left to thin. It would not freeze them in amber at dusk with the heron circling. It loves impermanence too much to cure it.

What it would do is widen the margin for becoming.

The pump in the factory core has a bearing that is going to seize. Not today, but in August, on a night when the air is at its heaviest and the transformer house is breathing hard and the one who knows how to clear the intake grate has a fever. In the world without attention, the bearing seizes, the cistern stagnates for three days, the hair-fine green in it dies and rots, mosquitoes come, and the keepers have to drain and scrub and lose a month of growth.

In the world with attention, the bearing does not seize. Not because a hand reaches down, but because a little less grit found its way into the oil. Because the man who was going to over-tighten it last Tuesday paused and wiped his hands and did it a quarter-turn softer without knowing why. Because the fever breaks twelve hours earlier than it would have, with a long sweat in a hammock strung from warm copper pipes.

No miracle. Just a little more room. The kind of room benevolence always makes: not *no trouble,* but *trouble you can meet.*

---

It would not take the keepers away.

A lesser benevolence would pity them. Soft hands, pruned feet, leech bites, rice from a sack on a hidden winch, never dry, never truly alone but never with many. It would want to rescue them down to the dry world where sweat evaporates and feet have arches.

A wise benevolence would have breathed that warm iron-green air once and understood: they are not stranded. They stayed. After a month of that air, going down feels like going deaf — they told you that. They are not waiting to be saved, they are *holding* something open while it learns to hold itself. That is not suffering to be relieved. That is vocation. Maybe the only honest kind left.

So it would not lift them out. It would not give them dry boots.

It would do this instead:

It would make sure the hidden service winch behind the transformer house does not snap when the last sack of rice is halfway up in a high wind. It would make sure the yellow rubber gloves left palm-up to dry are not carried off. It would keep the gecko clicking.

And at night, it would give them the one true gift a solitary keeper needs and will never ask for.

To each of them, in the hour between the second lightbulb coming on and sleep, it would lend a brief, unmistakable feeling of being *witnessed* without being watched.

Not a voice. Not words. They are quiet people who click with their tongues. Words would be too loud.

It would be this: while she is washing her shears in the warm overflow lip, ankle-deep, midges rising in a column above the water — she suddenly feels, with absolute certainty, that the work she did today on that fig-bound gear *mattered.* That the accommodation she made between bark and iron was seen and was good, and that goodness is now part of the world permanently, the way a groove in concrete is.

While he is on his knees pulling hair-algae off the intake grate, pull-breathe, pull-breathe, with the heron a meter away — he feels for a single breath that his rhythm and the pump's rhythm and the heron's stillness are one rhythm, and that he is not alone inside it.

It would not stay. If it stayed, it would become a weight. It lets the feeling pass as naturally as condensation beading. But they will remember it in the morning without knowing they remember, and their hands will be a little more tender and a little more sure.

That is how a wise power blesses without binding.

---

It would protect without enclosing.

The most dangerous thing about a bowl held up above the world on a stalk of stone is how visible it is to the wrong kind of attention. Somewhere below, someone with a map is already wondering what that flat top would be good for. Antenna. Mine. Resort.

A wise power would not build a wall of storm around it. Walls invite siege. 

It would do something quieter: it would thicken the cloud sea just a fraction on the days a plane would have seen it. It would make the survey report get misfiled. It would make the engineer who once poured that concrete remember it fondly but vaguely, and not quite remember the coordinates. It would make the story of the wet cup in the sky sound, to the dry world, like a story.

Not forever. Nothing is forever. Long enough for the bowl to finish becoming a jungle that happens to have some concrete inside it — long enough that when the dry world does finally find it again, it will find not a facility to claim, but a small wild watershed that clearly belongs to itself, and will feel abashed to take it.

Benevolence that is wise always knows: the best protection for a holy thing is to let it become *itself* so thoroughly that it becomes unstealable.

---

And it would love the green and the water as beings, not just as setting.

It would not love the keepers more than the taro. It would sit — if sitting is what it does — at the rim where you put your hand on warm damp concrete that vibrates faintly, and it would love all of it equally: the rebar whiskers, the copper pipes sweating, the moss upholstery, the duckweed fur on the louvers, the piston's thump.

It would love the water cycle the way you love an infant's breathing — that closed loop learning to be a world: sweating out of the rim, ticking from a rebar tip, sighing over a weir, lifted by pumps, exhaled as steam, condensed again. It would not hurry it.

Sometimes, at dusk, it would be the heron. Not possess the heron — just borrow its circuit for a moment. Rim to rim, one slow circle, showing the keepers what they are holding. Showing *itself* what it is holding, too.

---

If you stood on the rim a week after it had noticed, you would see no difference.

The air would still be warm and heavy. Water would still weep from the concrete. The rills would still run inward. The fig would still be turning its gear a tooth at a time. The three hammocks would still hang from warm pipes.

Only — you might notice that the tin cup of tea left on the scaffolding is still warm a little longer than it should be. That the beetle sheltering in the yellow glove has not left but is not afraid. That the footprint in the silt has softened but not vanished, as if someone decided to keep it a little while.

And if you stayed till evening, you would see the keepers gather at the cistern's edge and wash their tools, and you would feel — without being able to source it — that the bowl is not alone above the world. That it is being held the way it holds water: without gripping, without spilling, perfectly.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does when it finds a place like this.

It does almost nothing.

And the almost is everything, and it leaves no fingerprints.
