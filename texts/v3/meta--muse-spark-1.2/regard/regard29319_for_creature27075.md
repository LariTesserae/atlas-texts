It would do almost nothing.

That is the first wisdom. And the second.

---

A benevolent and wise power does not arrive like the heron does. It does not duck over the lip and stand absurdly large in the sump.

It arrives the way water arrives — from the air, from the stone, from everywhere at once — and for a long time you would not know it had arrived at all.

Because the first thing it would see, looking down over that low lip of stone in the grass, is that this place is *finished*.

Not perfect. Finished. Like a pot that has found its glaze.

A lesser power — a clever power, a kind but hurried power — would see what is missing. *It is dim, I will bring sun. It is small, I will make it vast. It is lonely, I will bring a mate for the fig. The newts walk too far between puddles, I will make the puddles deeper. The frogs are too loud, I will give them peace.*

A wise power sees that the dimness *is* the roof. That the smallness *is* the world. That the loneliness of the fig is how it learned to put its roots flat as fingers into stone and drink weeping. That the newt's walk across damp rock is not a flaw in the design but the design itself.

So it would not fix it.

It would step *down*.

If it had feet, it would use the south wall steps where ten thousand bare feet have already worn a trough, and place its own step in the trough. It would sit. Powers that are wise know how to sit. They know that some places cannot be blessed standing up.

It would sit at the scale of the place. Not towering. Not shrinking itself either — just becoming the right size for a room forty strides across, where every stone can be seen from anywhere. It would make itself comprehensible.

And it would listen first. For a long time.

To the percussion that is not a single plink but a whole gamelan of water: fat drops from the mist ceiling eight feet up, thin sheets running sideways along a camber before they remember to fall, the sump spilling over in a clear continuous sigh, and through it all the frogs — that dense, overlapping argument that is not a chorus — throwing their amber-throated calls against the oval wall until direction disappears.

A wise power would not quiet the frogs to hear the water better. It would hear how the frogs *are* the water, timed to it. How the swifts cutting the air in papery silence are timed to the midges, which are timed to the silt in the sump, which is timed to the fig dropping its hard little fruits to rot for the tadpoles. It would hear the maintenance. The room keeping itself wet.

And it would be glad.

What would it *do*, then, if it loves what it sees?

Nothing that breaks the circle. Everything that keeps the circle from being broken from the outside.

For this is the danger of such a place: it looks like nothing from the plain. Just a low lip in the grass, like the coping of a well. To a farmer it is a hole to fill. To an archaeologist it is a trench to drain and map and leave open to the sun until the film dies and the white snail shells bleach and crack. To a thirsty town it is a cistern to be scrubbed and roofed and chlorinated. To a child it is a place to throw a stone to hear how loud the splash is. All of those are a kind of love, but they would kill it in a season.

So the benevolent power would do its work *outside* the rim, where no one inside would ever see it.

It would keep the aquifer shyly full, even in the dry year when the plain cracks. Not by making rain — the room already knows how to make its own weather, beading water on frog backs and lifting it hand's breadth back into the mist — but by making sure the rain that already falls three fields over still finds its slow way through limestone to weep through these particular joints. It would keep the pressure in the stone, so the walls never stop weeping.

It would keep the plow a little wide. The road a little crooked. It would make the lip of stone look, to the eye of the man with plans, just that fraction more unremarkable, just that fraction more *like nothing* — so his gaze slides off. Not invisible. Just not interesting. The best ward is not a fence. It is a lack of itch.

It would keep the heron hungry, but not starving. And it would keep the heron *remembering* — so it returns, but not every day. The room needs its god, but not too often.

Inside, it would do one small, physical thing. A wise power cannot resist touching what it loves, but it touches the way a mason touches.

In the eastern corner, where the fig has pried its white roots between courses, one stone has shifted a millimeter in the last hundred years. You haven't seen it yet, but the next hard frost would widen it, and the weep there would become a stream, and the stream would wash the grit from that joint, and the grit is where the fig holds. The power would kneel there — where the fig is brilliantly dusty-green against all that stone-grey — and press its palm, cool as the stones themselves, against that course. Not mortaring. Just coaxing. A little pressure, a little patience, the way you coax a dislocated finger back in. The calcite beard would re-knit. The joint would weep, not run. The fig would shudder, as figs do, and drop one hard little fruit into the runnel for the tadpoles.

No one would see it happen. The frogs would not stop calling.

And then it would do the most benevolent thing of all for a place whose rule is *nothing enters, nothing leaves, except water, which never really leaves at all*:

It would leave nothing. And it would take nothing.

No feather. No shell. No memory of itself to be worshipped. Perhaps it would leave the faintest increase in the foxfire of the moss at dusk, so that night the mist-ceiling glows a little more, and the swallows coming home find their chisel-holes a little easier. Perhaps it would leave a coolness in the stone that lasts for three evenings after, so that anyone who does step down in that time feels, unreasonably, that they have been forgiven for something.

But it would not sign its name.

If you were sitting on the worn steps — if you *are* sitting there now, listening to how loud and active an empty room can be — you might feel, for a breath, that the air got a fraction cooler and the dripping got a fraction more musical, as if the drops were falling not just on stone but on attention. You might feel that the place had been noticed, the way a child feels noticed when an adult doesn't interrupt her game but just watches from the doorway, smiling.

You might look up and see the sun, that frosted coin, a little softer. You might see a bead of water detach from the sump and rise a hand's breadth — a little higher this time, a little slower — before popping into the mist, as if it were showing off.

And then you would hear the frogs start again, exactly as before, and you would understand that the blessing had already happened, and that it felt exactly like nothing had changed.

That is what a wise and benevolent power does for a room that has learned to be a valley:

It does not make it larger. It keeps it small enough to be wholly loved.

It does not make it less wet, less dim, less loud.

It sits, for a while, at its scale.

It puts one stone back where it belongs.

It makes sure the world will continue to walk past.

And then it goes, leaving the water to do what water does here — rise, hang, fall, run, rest, and rise again — and leaving the beings who have shaped themselves to that circle to continue being how the room keeps itself wet.

If you stay until the swifts have slotted themselves back into the wall, you will feel it is still there. Not as a presence. As an absence of threat. As permission.

The room was made by hands with stone hammers. It was perfected by water. It was kept by snails and newts and frogs.

Now it is also, quietly, kept by being left alone.
