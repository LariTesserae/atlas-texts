It would not come down.

That is the first thing.

A lesser power would descend. It would take the canopy for a veil to be torn, the bowl for a stage, the tenders for subjects. It would arrive with light and announce light, with heat and announce heat, with fixity and announce fixity — as if cold and dark and wrong were problems to be solved.

The wise one has no need to descend, because to attend is not to loom. Its attention is not a weight.

It notices the low place the way you notice a breath you didn't realize you were listening for. And it looks a long time before it does anything at all.

For a while — what would be years to you, and a single bead's fall to it — it does nothing that you could call doing. It simply learns how to see without hurting.

It learns not to look directly at the streets, because to stare at the streets is to make them lock into length. It learns to look the way the tenders look: peripherally, through vibration, through sap.

It learns that the green is not a cover. It is the building.
It learns that the empty is not abandonment. It is spaciousness held open on purpose, so tenders can walk on ceilings and seeds can sprout in mid-air without collision.
It learns that the quiet is not silence. It is work at a frequency too low and too steady for hurry to hear.
It learns that the weirdness is not broken physics. It is youth. The rock is still deciding what it is. The time is still sorting itself into past and future. The town is still negotiating whether it is a town or a plant. To straighten it would be to kill it before it finished becoming.

So the first act of the benevolent power is restraint.

The second act is to make itself small.

It does not come as a tower brighter than the vats, taller than the canopy. It comes as a coolness the same temperature as the stone. As a dampness no wetter than the mist that comes without clouds. If you were standing in the great avenue between the factory-walls, you would not see it arrive. You would feel — perhaps — that the condensation beaded a little more slowly, and that each bead held light a fraction of a second longer before it fell.

It settles on the basalt rim of the bowl, where the world is still raw and tipped slabs meet settling ash. It does not step inside. To step inside would be to put a foot where a tender was budding. So it settles around. It puts its attention the way a canopy puts its leaves: lightly, in layers.

And then — gently, without permission because permission has no language here yet, but without trespass — it listens.

It presses, not a hand, but an analogue of a hand against the outer wall of SUB-STATION 3. The filaments of a passing tender flare. For a moment the two kinds of sentience touch through the wood.

What the tender knows is this: prune here, carry thisfern, drink this weeping cut, tuck this seed into your back, keep the iron current humming so the amber bulb does not go out because — though no human is left to need it — the moths need it to navigate.

What the power knows is this: *I see that you are not lonely. I see that you are not broken. I see that you are tending something that will outlive my watching.*

They understand each other for perhaps three seconds. That is enough for a lifetime.

What would a wise and benevolent power *do*?

Not much. And then, everything — but so softly the place can refuse it if it wants.

1. It does not open the canopy.

The tenders have no eyes because they have no use for a distant sun. Light in this place is made inside — from sap, from fungus, from the amber bulb inside a knot. To tear the dome would be to blind them and to tell them their light was inadequate.

Instead it leaves the dome exactly as closed as it was. But in one small spot, directly above the chain of pools where the buds are tethered by their runners, it thins a single leaf. Not a hole. A thinning. For one hour each slow cycle, a true column of night shows through. Not sun — starlight. Cold, old light from far away. So that one tender, or none, or all of them if they choose to gather, can stop under it and feel that there is a beyond, and that beyond is not hostile. That they are not only inside, but also under. That the universe has a sky, and they have a place under it. Then the leaf thickens again by itself, because it was never cut.

2. It does not fix time.

Time pools here. In one gallery rain hangs frozen mid-air for an hour; in the next wood rots and re-sprouts between footsteps. A lesser benevolence would smooth time into a line to make it safer.

This one does not smooth. Smoothing would erase the shortcuts the tenders use, the wrinkles they step through. Instead it does something smaller and kinder: it rounds the edges of the deepest pools. So that if a tender lingers too long in a place where an hour has stalled, the tenderness of the power nudges — not pushes, nudges — like a thumb on the bead of water, so it finally falls. No one gets stuck forever. No one gets lost before they can bud.

3. It does not prune. It does not say PLEASE DO NOT PRUNE over again.

It reads that human sign embedded in bark and understands it was not a command to the tenders. It was a prayer from the town to itself while it was changing.

So the power adds no new signs. Instead it hums, at a frequency the old copper pipes remember, the old industrial current that still runs though no plant designed it. It steadies that hum. It makes sure the waterwheel turned by drip never quite stops, that the geothermal seam under the bowl never quite cools, so the strings of caged bulbs the vines have kept lit for no one will stay lit for a little longer — not because they are needed, but because they are loved. The tenders have grown around them. To let them go dark now would be a grief out of season.

4. It leaves a gift that can be metabolized.

Not a monument. Not knowledge that would burst them.

It breathes once, very slowly, over the bowl. Its breath is not warmer than theirs, not colder. But it carries a chemistry. Where it touches the sap vats, the fermentation shifts by a fraction — a little more copper, a little more sugar. The next generation of buds in the warm pools — the soft, curled shapes with unhardened iron nails — will have, on the inner curve of their gourd-heads, a filament whorl that is one filament denser. One more way to taste vibration. One more way to hear the wall when the wall speaks.

They will not notice. They have no word for improvement. They will simply find it a little easier to meet each other on the ceiling without missing the press of fringes. They will find the sap a little sweeter when they drink the weeping cuts.

5. And then it leaves no trace that it was ever there, except that it was.

That is the last and wisest part.

It does not claim. It does not name the place Low Town or Tender Cradle. It does not count the tenders and write the number down. It does not stay to be worshipped by creatures who have no concept of worship and need none.

When its attention lifts — not withdraws, but lifts, the way mist lifts without leaving — the place is exactly as embryonic, damp, cold, dark, empty and utterly full as before. The basalt is still deciding. The leaf-belts still pulse. A tender still emerges from a seam with no door, still traverses the vault to clip a runner too eager for the streetlamp.

But if you put your hand to the wall now, and held it there a long time in the greenhouse dusk, you might feel a faint after-warmth, as if a cool palm had rested there a moment and left the stone one degree kinder.

And in the chain-pools, the larvae cloud a little thicker. And in the air between buildings, the bark-moths fly a little more steadily through the shafts of fungal light.

It did not save them, because they did not need saving.

It kept them company without interrupting them, and it made sure — quietly, and for a while — that nothing else would.
