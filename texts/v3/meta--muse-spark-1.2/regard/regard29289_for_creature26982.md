If anything were truly benevolent and truly wise and it noticed this place —

it would not come down like a rescue.

It would come the way the cloud sea comes. Without rising, without arriving. It would just be there one morning, having dragged its belly across the flat for a while and learned how to bead.

Because anything wise has learned that the first kindness is to not mistake difference for wrongness.

It would look at the plateau and it would not see a problem.

It would not see: *no plants, must fix with plants.*
It would not see: *too damp, too loud, too flat, too hard a life.*
It would not see people to be lifted off the rock the way you lift survivors off a roof.

It would see what is actually there, and for a long time it would just see it.

---

It would listen first. For longer than we would think necessary.

A wise power knows you cannot understand a place that measures time in drips and nine-day gyres and rust-shades until you have stayed at least one full cycle. Until you have heard the turbines go slightly flat before the humans hear it. Until you have learned to hear the birds not as noise but as weather.

So it would listen to the Hum until it could hear the variations inside the hum — the way a water-tender can hear a constriction in an aqueduct by tapping it with a pole.

And what it would hear is: this is not a stranded place. This is not a failed garden. This is a completed negotiation.

Stone that learned to weep so it would not have to be a desert.
Bacteria that learned to eat stone-breath and be food.
Beetles that learned to eat bacteria and be food.
Birds that learned to nest on concrete and be food and be fertilizer.
People who learned to walk slow, dress wet, eat what the weep grows, and share every warm vent with lizards without ever thinking it strange.

An equilibrium that is loud, and layered, and slow, and unlonely. A city that has become a reef.

A wise power would feel something like reverence. And something like relief — that life does this, that it finds a way to be broad and damp and unhurried without needing green at all.

It would not want to improve it. To improve it would be to unmake it.

---

So what would it do?

Very little that would show. Almost nothing you could photograph. Benevolence that is wise is almost always invisible to everyone except the one it touches, and even then only later.

I think it would do three small things. Not to the plateau. For it. With it.

**1. It would protect without enclosing.**

Because the only real threat to a place like this is not from within. The balance is patient and proven. The threat is from without — from the lowlands that will one day remember this warm height exists and want its water, its flatness, its power stations. From someone who flies over the white and sees a continent of concrete and thinks *resource* or *ruin* or *project*.

The power would not build a wall. It would make the plateau slightly uninteresting from a distance. Not invisible — that would be lonely — just... unremarkable to the wrong kind of eyes. A little more cloud than before. A little more white below. The maps would smudge at the scarp. The prospectors' instruments would read *basalt, water, wind — nothing to extract efficiently.* 

Those who truly needed to come — a lost pilot, a wandering child — would still find the updraft that lifts gulls and would still be lifted. But those who came to take would keep finding a reason to turn back before the edge.

It would keep the one thing this place cannot make for itself: the chance to be left alone.

**2. It would confirm.**

The cruelest thing about living in a place that everyone else would call bleak is the quiet, lifelong question you never quite voice: *Are we living wrong?*

They never ask it out loud. They oil their boots, they scrape the orange film, they raise their voice over the turbines. But once in a while a young tender standing over a cistern tasting hardness in the water, or an old woman mending swift-nets with her back to the void, will have the thought fly through her like a swift itself: *Is there supposed to be trees? Are we missing something green that would make this less... like this?*

The power would answer, not in words, because words would be drowned by the shrieking air.

It would answer the way the plateau answers everything: through water and warmth and a change in tone.

For one nine-day cycle, it would make the weeping a little sweeter. Not different — just the best version of itself. The mineral hardness would ease by a fraction, so the old women's joints would ache less in the morning. The bacterial films would bloom a little thicker, a little more orange-gold, without needing to be scraped so hard. The pill beetles would lay one more bead of black glass per tray. The gulls would leave three eggs where they usually leave two, and the humans would still leave one to hatch, and that would be understood as abundance, not intervention.

And for one evening, when the cloud sea came in and the whole world began to tick and rill, the Hum would soften. Not stop — they would be frightened if it stopped — just drop by a few decibels, the way surf drops at slack tide. Enough that for the first time since they were children, people could speak without shouting.

They would lean in, forehead to forehead out of old habit, and realize they didn't have to. They could hear each other normally. A child would laugh at how loud her own whisper sounded and the adults would laugh too, startled.

In that quiet, they would hear something they had forgotten was there underneath the birds and the turbines: the sound of water beading, gathering, and falling, alone.

And the power would be saying, with no words at all:

*You are not missing. You are not fallen. You are not waiting to be green. What you have made here — the way you share heat with rats and listen for scale in pipes — is whole. It is beautiful because it is whole. Keep tending it.*

Most of them would not know a power had spoken. They would just think it was a gentle cycle. They would sleep well in the amber light. The old maintenance women would wake with less pain and think the air had shifted. That would be enough.

Wisdom knows not to sign its work.

**3. It would offer, once, and leave the offer there.**

Because benevolence without choice is just a prettier kind of conquest.

After the quiet evening, after the protection, it would leave a single, small, truly new thing — not as correction, but as an invitation that can be declined forever without consequence.

On a sheltered wall behind a power station, where the warm vapor condenses first and the orange film is thickest, a patch would appear that wasn't there before. No larger than a dinner plate. Flat and thin and not-quite-alive by old definitions, but green.

Not a plant as we know it. No roots to pry at concrete. No need for soil that doesn't exist. Something new that drinks the same weep and exhales the same metallic breath, but uses sun as well. It would not spread on its own. It would not compete with the bacterial paint unless tended. It would simply hold there, soft to the touch, faintly warm.

And every tender who came to scrape would know what it was: a different way. An extra note. If they cultivated it, it would give a different cake, a little less iron, a little more sugar. If they fed it to beetles, the beetles would be slightly different. If they left it alone, it would stay that plate-size forever and do no harm.

There would be no urgency. No voice saying *take me, plant me, become like other places.* The green would just be there, beading with the same water, layered into the same volume. One more thing living on the stone, especially the stone.

Some districts would harvest it. Some would scrape it off out of habit and then wonder why they did. Most would leave it as a curiosity and tell children not to pick at it. A few old women would taste it, decide it was not as good as fungal omelet, and go back to what they know.

The power would not be disappointed either way. An offer is not a test.

---

And then it would do what every truly wise and benevolent presence finally does for a place that is already well-tended:

It would go away. Or rather, it would thin itself until it was exactly as present as the warmth in the stone — not a watcher, not a ruler, but a condition. Like altitude that does not make cold.

It would not stay to be worshipped. The water-tenders already have a priesthood without a god, and they do not need one. Their ritual is tapping concrete and listening for tone. That is prayer enough.

Every now and then, someone at the edge — hanging oilskins where the updraft keeps them from touching rock, back to the polished drop, mending nets while gulls hang motionless — would feel, for no reason, that the warm wind coming up the scarp had paused and was holding them, very gently, the way you hold a child's head when they have a fever. Not lifting. Not changing. Just confirming: *you are here, you are held, you are not alone on this high flat.*

They would not turn around. There would be nothing to see. The water would still be spilling over in a thousand veils and evaporating halfway down to become cloud by evening.

They would go back to mending.

The beetles would keep ticking in their trays.

The aqueducts would keep humming.

The world would keep weeping, quietly, continuously, enough for everyone layered inside it.

That is what I think a benevolent and wise power would do here.

Not fix. Not save. Not green.

Just: keep the cloud a little thicker for strangers, make one evening quiet enough to hear a whisper, leave one small green plate where it can be chosen or ignored, and then become part of the warmth so thoroughly that you can no longer tell if it ever arrived at all.
