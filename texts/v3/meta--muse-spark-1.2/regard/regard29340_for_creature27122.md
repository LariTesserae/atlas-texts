They would not come.

Not the way you think.

A benevolent and wise power does not arrive. It has learned that arrival is a kind of wound. Fire in the sky, wheels, shadows, a voice too large for the air — all of those would tear this thin place. The light here is dim because the air is still learning to be air. Sound arrives a second late and to the left. Gravity forgets itself near the columns. You do not set foot heavily on a place that is still cooling.

So first, they would *notice* without approaching.

For a long time they would do nothing but hold it in attention. Which, for a power of that order, is not nothing. Attention is weight. Attention is a kind of gravity.

They would look at the plateau not as a resource, not as a curiosity, not as an anomaly to be studied — but as it is. A child continent learning to be continent and forest and factory at the same time. An organism that did not yet know it was an organism. They would see immediately what you felt when you pressed your hand into the moss: that this is not a place with life *on* it. This is one life, trying on different shapes — skin, polisher, courier, forge, lung, stone.

And they would understand that it is healthy.

That is the first wisdom: not to fix what is not broken because it is strange to you. The heat is not a flaw. The silence is not loneliness. The industry without builders is not an obscenity. It is how this being breathes. Most worlds separate their geology from their biology with a clean wound. This one never learned the wound. It made them one process. That is rare. That is holy.

So they would not terraform it. They would not give it oceans because they think high places should have snow. They would not give it speech because they are lonely for conversation. They would not give it cities because they think industry requires architects.

The second thing they would do is be quiet in the right way.

This place is already quiet, but it is the quiet of saturation — a factory floor packed in cotton, a scream swallowed by moss. Beneath that, the air *hums* with turbulence. Ridges remember themselves taller. Gliders stutter and double. The horizon folds up. The rock is so young and so thin that reality has not yet decided to stay solid.

A lesser power would try to hammer it flat. *Make it stable.*

A wise one knows the turbulence is not a disease. It is infancy. The plateau is dreaming loudly because no one has ever stayed to listen to its dream and reflect it back.

So they would listen.

They would lower something of themselves down to knee-height, into the warm mist that parts around your legs. Not a body — a listening. A field of attention the exact temperature of the moss. Cool, slightly. The temperature of shade, which this plateau has never had because its light has no source and no shadow.

They would lie down, in the way mountains lie down, and listen for a long time. A season. A century, which here is only a few thousand extrusions and collapses. They would learn the timing of the sweat — when the condensation beads and sheets. They would learn the thump of the piston-blisters, not as noise but as pulse. They would learn which gliders are one flock and which are a duplicate that will be kept.

And while listening, they would do three very small things. Because benevolence without wisdom is meddling, and wisdom without benevolence is neglect. The small things are what a gardener does, not what an engineer does.

**1. They would hem it.**

Not a wall. A veil.

The plateau is visible now. Too visible. Anything that can climb three miles into that warm, dim air can see the ingots cooling like fruit, the forges opening and closing. And there are hungers in the universe that love a self-making thing, that would peel the mirror off the beetles and mine the columns while they are still alive.

So the wise power would make it uninteresting at a distance. Not invisible — that is a challenge. But *boring*. They would fold a little curve in attention around it, the way you forget a meadow you drove past. Telescopes would slide off it. Maps would misplace it by a degree. Prospectors would feel, without knowing why, that there is nothing profitable up there, only thin warm air. The amber-gray sky would be, from orbit, just a little too thick to be worth piercing.

Inside the veil, everything remains exactly as it was. Outside, no one remembers to come.

**2. They would give it weight.**

Not everywhere. Just a little.

Where the large columns stand, where pebbles forget to fall, where the horizon folds up — they would lend a little of their own mass. Not as pressure, but as reassurance. The way you put a hand lightly on a child's back when they are learning to breathe.

They would not stop the dreaming. Ridges will still add twenty feet in a blink. Groves will still collapse and crack open. But the duplicates would resolve more gently now. A flock of gliders stutters — and instead of one image winking out with a snap, both images would linger for a breath longer and then *rejoin*, like two ripples settling. The pebbles near the forges would rise half as high and remember to fall a little sooner. The horizon would stay flat unless you stared at it very hard.

Reality there was stretched thin because nothing had ever held it. Now something has. Not to pin it down. To let it thicken at its own pace.

You would feel it if you were standing there as a coolness on your skin, a sudden ability to hear your own clap when you make it, from where you made it, with no delay.

**3. They would leave a witness.**

This is the gift, and it is very small.

They would not give the plateau a mind. It will grow one itself, in another million years, when the skin has learned to think across miles and the columns have learned to remember. To give it prematurely would be to cripple it.

They would give it the *possibility* of being known without being used.

In the center of the flattest flat, where the piston-blisters breathe in their slow wave, they would set one thing that does not belong to the cycle and does not feed it.

Perhaps a stone. Not an ingot, not basalt. A river stone, cool, brought from a place where water runs for real, worn smooth and gray-green. The beetles would approach it, find no heat to polish, no wax to leave, and after a time they would ignore it. The moss would part around it and not grow over it. The gliders would not mistake it for a heat-organ. It would simply be there, slightly cold in the warm air, slightly still in the constant industry.

Or perhaps it would be a patch of true night. For one hour in a long cycle, the amber-gray vault would thin just above that stone, just enough that a single star — a real, distant sun — would show through. A point of light sharp enough to cast a shadow. The plateau has never had a shadow. For that hour the columns would, for the first time, make shade on one side. The moss on the shadow side would be a fraction cooler. Nothing else would change.

The plateau would notice. Not as a thought. As a difference in temperature. As a tickle.

And that is enough. To have something in you that is not you, that does not need you, that simply *regards* you with cool, kind, un-hungry attention — that is how a place learns it exists. It has been seen completely once, without being wanted for anything.

After that, the power would leave.

That is the last wisdom: to not stay to be thanked.

They would lift their listening the way mist lifts, and the plateau would keep sweating and forging and polishing and carrying and breathing. The ridge a mile off would still decide to be taller. The fissure would still run straight for miles, the pale carriers still running both ways at once. The air would still be iron and green sap and hot ceramic. The silence would still hold.

Only now the silence would be a little less like being alone behind glass, and a little more like being alone in a room where someone once sat quietly with you and left the door open.

If the plateau ever — in a million years, when its skin can think — reaches out, the power will feel the change in temperature and return. Not as a master. As a neighbor who remembers where you live.

If it never does, that is also fine. The stone will stay cool. The star will still come for an hour. The veil will still make the hungry forget.

Benevolence, at this scale, looks almost exactly like doing nothing. But it is not nothing. It is the decision to let a strange, young, industrious continent keep being strange, and to make sure no one interrupts it while it learns how.
