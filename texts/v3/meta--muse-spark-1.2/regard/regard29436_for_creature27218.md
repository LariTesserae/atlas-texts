It would not come down.

That's the first thing.

A lesser power would have come down. Would have parted the air with light and sound, would have stood on the moor like a second sun and called up to them - *come out, I will make you whole, I will lift you, I will heal the wound.*

A benevolent and wise power does not do that. Because it looks longer.

It looks over the lip where the grass stops being grass and becomes an edge still sharp enough to cut your hand. It looks down a hundred feet of new limestone still pale and damp from being born, and it does not see a wound anymore.

It sees a cradle.

It sees what they have done.

And it is - for a being that has seen continents cool - struck quiet.

---

So it stays on the moor at first. Not above them, but with them. It makes itself narrow, to fit the sky they have been given. It doesn't bring the wide sky with it. It learns their sky: the long river of blue-white that runs north to south, that is noon for an hour and then gone. It learns to look up and down, not out.

And it listens the way they listen.

For a long time it does nothing at all. This is part of what it does. Attention, when it is benevolent and wise, is not empty. To be held in mind by something vast without being demanded of, without being fixed or pitied - that alone is a kind of shelter.

It hears the tapping. *tap tap, tap tap.* The stone-cutters listening for the fault. It hears the whole row of houses hold its breath with them. It hears the soft call - *take. hold. clear.* - and the way a child learns to run on a roof on all fours and then go silent when she drops into the fissure that is a street. It hears how the limestone drinks every excess.

It thinks: *They have learned the stone's first lesson. Be quiet enough that you can hear when it moves.*

It thinks: *They have made fullness without waste. They have made density without cruelty. That is so rare.*

A wise power knows that there are places that do not need saving. They need not being *unsaved* by someone who loves them incorrectly.

So it will not fill the Scar. It will not lift the floor. It will not bring a river - a river would kill them, would take their cisterns and their channel-keepers and their whole careful way of catching drip on stretched skins and make it careless. It will not make the walls soft and weathered before their time. It will not give them wide streets and stone avenues. They have walls-to-wall and roof-to-roof for a reason: nothing was wasted. To give them avenues would be to tell them their saturation was a mistake.

It will not make them loud.

What it does, then, is so small you could live there your whole life and think nothing had changed. Which is how you know it was wise.

**First, it steadies the stone, without hardening it.**

The Scar is still settling. The power cannot stop that, and would not - settling is how new rock learns to be old rock. But it puts a hand - not a hand - an inclination upon it.

Now when a slab prepares to calve, the crack comes earlier. Not a gunshot that means *now*, but a long sighing tick the day before, and then a sharper tick an hour before. Enough time that the listeners hear it and quietly move the children from beneath that overhang, move the bean-frames, and no one dies. The blocks that do fall land a little softer, on an angle, as if they remembered to catch themselves. And the dust after a fall - that fine chert glitter that got in every lung - settles faster. The old cough that took the old stone-cutters at fifty now takes them at seventy.

It leaves the raw limestone raw. It still beads and streaks and feels cold to the palm. But when a family leaves a pillar in the middle of their cut-room and oils it, the oil goes a little further. The pillar doesn't dry and craze.

**Second, it tends the water, without making it abundant.**

It does not give them a spring. A spring would become property, would be fought over, dammed.

Instead it makes the seeps reliable.

In late August, when before the walls would dry for two weeks and the cisterns would go low and the channel-keepers would walk with tight mouths - now the north wall weeps one slow, steady line all through August. Just enough. One barrel a day per house if you are patient and tend your skin-catch. The condensation that forms on the underside of thatch at night is a little heavier, so that morning drip lasts into midday. The central channel, stone-lined down the middle where water wants to go anyway, never quite clogs. When a child drops a shingle in it, the next rain finds it and nudges it clear. After the brief white threads of rain over the lip, the puddles in the foot-worn hollows stay two days instead of one, then sink clean instead of souring.

No one will ever say "we have plenty." They will still say "watch the channel," still haul buckets, still beat laundry against stone. But no baby will go thirsty in a dry month again.

**Third, it softens the light and the cold, without taking them away.**

The Scar is cool always. The stone holds cold. The power does not warm it - the cool is why their barley keeps, why their throats don't dry, why they can work at noon.

But it lets the noon stay a little longer. Seven minutes longer. The sun, that hammer-straight strip, lingers just enough that the barley on the flat roofs dries fully before the seep returns. The beans yellow more completely. On the shadier north-facing walls, the pale green and orange lichens grow a little thicker - and now one of them, a flat grey-green one that was always there, carries a faint yeast in it, good for the gut, and the women learn they can scrape it for poultice and for bread.

At night, when they close their shutters to hold the tallow light inside, the draft that moves north to south along the trench - that faint cool breath that carries smoke and wet stone and cooking - is fractionally kinder. It still mists your breath in early hours. But the deepest winter, when before the trapped wind would knife and the lowest houses would flood with cold air and stay damp for weeks, now the cold pools a foot lower, below the sleeping platforms. The children wake with less rattle in their chests.

And this:

It makes them harder to find.

Not invisible. Just - uninteresting - to the kind of people on the moor who would find a packed, productive, undefended city in a trench and see either a labor pool or a problem. The wide-sky people, the ones with copper and cattle and charters, who would come with rope and law and want to own the chasm's walls. When they walk the moor now, their eyes slide over the Scar's lip. They think it's a dry crack, not worth the climb. If they do peer over, the wind happens to toss dust in their eyes, or a cloud throws a solid shadow at just that moment, and they think *nothing down there but roofs.* They move on.

The Scar keeps its invisibility, which was its first protection. The quiet is kept quiet.

---

If they noticed any of this at all - and most don't, because it was done the way they themselves do things: by hand, slowly, without announcement - they would notice it as luck.

"The stone warned us this time," the listeners would say, patting the wall.

"The seep held this August," the channel-keepers would say, and nod.

"The barley dried well," the old men on the high roofs would say, faces tipped to the longer noon.

At night, from the moor, if you did happen to stand at the lip and look down, you would still see what looked like a long, low ember bed between stone lips, breathing quietly. Only now the embers burn a little steadier. Only now the breathing does not catch.

The power does not ask to be thanked. It does not hang a cloth over its doorway and ask not to be seen, but it understands that cloth very well. So it asks the same: do not look at me. Look at each other.

Once in a long while, a child born in the Scar - one of those thick-wristed, good-at-contrast children who have only ever known the sky as a river - will be carrying water from cistern to kitchen, bare feet quiet on cool limestone, and will pause at a pillar of raw rock left in the middle of a room, and will put her small palm on it the way everyone does to feel for damp or tremor.

And for a second the stone will feel not cold, but cool-warm, like a hand held there a moment before hers. And she will feel, entirely, without words, that the place that holds her is held in return.

She won't tell anyone. There is an etiquette of not-noticing, after all. She will just take her hand away, haul her bucket, and duck under the low beam into the next house, where someone is waiting to say *take* and someone else to say *hold*.

And the draft will move north to south, carrying the smell of dinner, and the work will go on, low and constant, and the Scar will remain - not a valley, not yet, still sharp, still new - a saturated, saturated world below the level of the ordinary ground, completely ordinary to those who live in it, completely precious to the one who watches and will not ruin it by calling it anything else.
