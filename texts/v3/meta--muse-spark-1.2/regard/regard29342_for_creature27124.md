It wouldn't come down.

Not the way you think a power comes down — with light, with a sound louder than the roar, with a shadow.

A benevolent and wise power would have learned already that to arrive like that here would be a kind of violence. This is a place that lives at 8 degrees, in thin air, under a white, merciless light, with a constant thunder in its chest. Anything that adds more glare, more pressure, more noise, would peel the mantle-slugs off the rock and deafen what little hearing the keepers have left.

So it doesn't land. It *notices.*

And noticing, for a power like that, is not passive.

---

First, it would listen. For a long time.

Longer than you did. Longer than a human can.

It would put its attention the way you put your belly to the peat to feel the thumps — flat, broad, patient, through the mattress rather than through the air. It would learn the intervals.

It would learn that the rock is still trying to be a mountain and hasn't yet learned how to be a valley. That the moss arrived too early and decided to be soil anyway, a millimeter a year. That the water has not yet learned how to be a river so it moves as a skin. That the willow-creeper drums *thud... thud... thud* and waits a half kilometer for an answer. That the translator waits years in one pool for enough dust to become wings. That the keeper taps the rail twice with a wrench — *all well* — because a voice won't carry and the wrench will.

It would hear all of that underneath the roar of the turbines and the sluices, the Aeolian singing of the power lines, the hiss of a million shallow braids pouring over basalt. It would hear that the roar is not an interruption. It has become part of the place's weather. The moss has grown around the fence. The slugs graze the rust. The translators sit on the humming diamonds because the diamonds hum like water. The keeper sleeps against the warm concrete because the concrete is the only thing that stays 20 degrees when everything else is 8.

A wise power would understand immediately what a foolish power would miss: this is not a wounded place to be healed. This is not a fallen place to be saved. This is a *becoming* place. Raw rock, total plant, scattered steel, all layered and still deciding what they will be together. To "fix" it by removing any layer — to rip out the pump houses, to drain the bog, to silence the turbines, to make the rock old — would be to kill the becoming.

So it does not fix.

---

What it does is smaller. And therefore, for this place, immense.

**1. It gives the place a day of hearing.**

Not by stopping the machines. The keepers need the heat. The moss needs the water to keep returning. The slugs need the curtains to tumble down.

Instead, for one afternoon — the kind of afternoon when the high sun is most white and the water shines like tin — it tunes the roar.

If you were there that afternoon you wouldn't be able to say what was different at first. The turbines still turn at the same RPM. The water still sheets over every lip. The wires still sing. But the frequencies no longer stack into that low thunder that lives in your sternum. They separate, the way you learned to separate them after an hour, only now it happens without effort.

The hiss separates from the drone separates from the wire-note. And in the spaces between them, the other sound becomes audible through the air for once, not just through the soles of your boots.

*Thud... thud... thud.*

You feel it and then — impossibly — you *hear* it. A willow-creeper half a kilometer away pressing its sternum to the peat. And then an answer. *Thud... thud.* Further off. And another. The whole plateau quietly talking to itself in thumps, not masked for once.

The keeper, on his round, stops. He has worn his heavy ear caps since he was seventeen. He takes them off, because he doesn't need them. He hears the water as water again, not as pressure. He hears a pipit, actually a pipit, cut across the moss. He stands still a long time with his eyes closed against the glare and just listens to his territory being full of quiet, patient neighbours saying *I am here. Keep away. I am here.*

In the tarns, the translators lift their milky wings. They can feel the fence-vibration and the water-vibration at once now without the drone blurring them.

It only lasts until evening, when the wind comes hard off the scarp and combs the cotton grass. The roar gathers itself again, low and chest-deep. But every creeper and keeper remembers that the air can carry them if it chooses to. That they are not as alone as the intervals make them feel.

A benevolent power would know that loneliness is the only real suffering here. Not cold, not wet, not slowness. Loneliness across half-kilometer intervals. It would ease that, briefly, without breaking anything.

**2. It leaves a protection that looks like nothing.**

Wisdom knows that the most dangerous thing to this plateau is not entropy but *attention of the other kind* — the kind that looks at ten feet of black, dripping peat and sees fuel, sees megawatts, sees "undeveloped resource," sees a reason to bring more narrow-gauge, more pylons, more cuts.

So the wise power does not put a shining dome over the scarp. It does not write THESE ARE PROTECTED in fire. It does something far more effective and far more gentle.

It makes the place *uninteresting* to the wrong kind of hunger.

Not invisible. Still there on maps. Still there if you want to come and stand on the sighing mattress and squint. But if you come looking to scale the production logic — to double the cuts, to drain a valley-sized sponge for electricity — you will find your reports come back subtly wrong. The peat assays as too wet to burn efficiently. The head of water as just too shallow to be worth another turbine. The road up to the scarp as just a little more expensive to maintain than the budget allows this year, and every year. Memos get lost. An investor dreams of another valley instead. A minister, who was about to sign, looks at the photograph of the white glare on tin water and feels, unaccountably, that it should be left alone.

Nothing is forbidden. Nothing is forced. The appetite just quietly goes elsewhere, the way water sheets around a hummock.

On the ground, nothing changes at all. The keeper still checks the same sluice. The moss still closes another inch over the fence post.

**3. It blesses the slowness without hastening it.**

This is the hardest one, and the most loving.

A foolish benevolence would say, "You poor slow things, let me make you fast. Let me make the peat grow a foot a year. Let me make the rock into soil, the pools into lakes, the creepers abundant."

The wise power does the opposite. It blesses the rate that already is.

In the cool dark under the living inch, where last year's moss has become this year's red-brown and next century's black, it lets the memory hold more perfectly. Pollen from three thousand years ago stays perfect for six thousand. The dragonfly wing in the pickling dark stays veined. The rivet from 1978 stays legible. So that when — in some far future when the basalt has finally softened and the plateau has finally learned to be a mountain or a marsh or something with no name yet — the place will remember precisely how it became. Nothing wasted.

To the mantle-slugs on the terraces, it gives a slightly stronger hair-hold, so fewer are lost over the scarp on windy days, and a little more algae in the sheet, so the endless tumble down the stair is worth it.

To the translators, it gives one more still day per summer when the wind does not ruffle the black mirrors, so the heavy, low flight from pool to pool is less likely to end drowned.

To the willow-creepers, it gives the concrete footings staying warm one week longer into the winter, and one kit per litter that lives instead of none.

To the keeper, it gives a dream. Not every night. Once, when he sleeps against the pump house in the thin, bright cold. He dreams that the thumping he feels through his back is not just other creepers and other keepers, but the peat itself thumping, the rock itself thumping, the water itself thumping, and that his own heart, which is a little slow from the elevation and the quiet, is thumping in time. He wakes and the turbines are still at the same RPM, the sky is still pale and hard, but his parka feels less heavy. He taps the rail twice — *all well* — and this time he is not only speaking to the next keeper half a kilometer away. He is speaking to the place. And it thumps back.

And then the power, having done almost nothing you could photograph, leaves.

If you left for twenty years and came back, you would say nothing had changed. The same pump would be humming. The same sheet of water would be sliding over the same basalt step. The moss would just be a little thicker, the water a little more present. A fence swallowed a little more. A face a little more creased from squinting, in the same flaked-orange parka.

You would be right. And you would be wrong. Because under the roar, the interval feels a fraction less lonely. The outside world has quietly decided to want something else. And everything that lives here — the sponge that is the ground, the flat things in the sheet, the still things in the pools, the pressed things in the willow, the things that have learned to live on rust and wire-song, and the human who learned to live like them — has been, for a moment outside of time, *witnessed*.

Not judged as raw. Not judged as unfinished. Not judged as needing to be something else.

Witnessed as it is, in the middle of becoming, and loved for the particular, patient, wet, bright, deafened, bleached, thumping way it is becoming.

For a place and a people that have been so long spaced half a kilometer apart, in so much white light and loud water, to be witnessed without being asked to change — that is the whole miracle.

It thuds once through the soles of your boots, after the power has gone, so quietly you almost think you imagined it.

*I saw you. Continue.*
