If it were me — if I were that kind of Power — I would not come down like weather.

I would come down like mist.

### First, I would look a long time without doing anything.

Because a wise and benevolent attention is not the same as a hungry attention. The bowl has already been looked at hungrily — by people from outside who see a crowded pan of wet stone and think *poor, cold, too many, too close — someone should fix them.*

I wouldn't think that.

I would sit where the herons sit. On the highest, sharpest rim where the basalt is still knife-edged and the wind tries to take your hat. And I would just watch for a whole turning of cloud.

I would watch the water-tenders make their slow circuit, fingertip-testing the channels. I would watch the stone-listeners tap *tok tok tok* and hear the hollow note that means *shim loose*. I would watch a child take three roofs, a bouncing plank, and a splash through the sheet-water to get to her mother — not because there is no shorter way, but because that *is* the way. I would watch the old woman put her palm on a wall and wait for the tick.

And I would understand very quickly:

This is not a people trapped in a place.

This is a people who have *become* a place. They are not waiting to be rescued from density. Their density *is* their skill. Their maintenance is not drudgery they endure until someone gives them a better life. Their maintenance is their prayer. *Nothing wasted, no space empty, no water stopped.* That is not a slogan. That is how a scab holds.

If I love them, I have to love that.

So I would not do the obvious, unwise things.

I would not lift the rim and make the bowl bigger. If I suddenly gave them open ground, their walls — which lean on each other — would fall. Their cooperation, which is structural, would lose its structure and have to invent selfishness to fill the gap.

I would not stop the seep. If I dried the stone, the liverwort and the black moss would die, and the stone-mites would die, and the dun hens would die, and the children who know which orange lichen to boil three times would forget.

I would not make it warm and dry and sunny. If I did, their oiled wool that is warm while wet would become hot and strange. The cool air that swallows sound and teaches them to speak in taps and tsks would become an air that carries shouts, and they would become loud and far from each other.

I would not give them fewer children or more food in a sack from the sky. Both would tell them their weaving was wrong.

A wise power knows: you don't heal a scab by pulling it off because you think skin should be smooth.

You keep it clean. You keep it warm. You keep the wound beneath from jarring.

So this is what I would do, if I can call it doing:

### I would get small.

Powers go wrong when they stay large. I would fold myself until I was bowl-sized. Not human-sized — *bowl*-sized. Until I could fit in the pan without spilling over the rim. Until my breathing was the same cool, mineral breathing the people do in their sleep.

And then I would do three very small things that are almost not things at all.

**1. I would put my hand under the bowl.**

The basalt is young. It ticks at night because it is still deciding. Left to itself, it might decide all at once — a ledge giving way by a foot instead of a hair, a crack running the whole way across the sheet-water and souring it, a wall going from sigh to shatter.

I would not stop it from settling. To stop it would be to keep it newborn forever.

I would hold it, the way you hold a sleeping child's head when the road is bumpy. Not still — just *slower*. I would ask the deep seams to take a hundred years for what they wanted to do in ten. I would spread the tick that now comes as a jolt once a month into a soft, nightly tickle the bare soles can feel and smile at, instead of fear. The people would notice only that this year fewer plaster walls cracked, and that the new crack that did appear was hair-thin and easy to chink with rock flour and clay.

They would say, "The bowl is learning faster," and they would be right.

**2. I would teach the water to remember.**

The water that hangs trembling on a vertical face, defying gravity for a hand's span before it remembers to fall — that is beautiful, but it is also tired water. It is water confused about where down is because the stone was born so fast it didn't learn.

I wouldn't command it. I would remind it, the way the water-tenders do with a fingertip.

I would run my own fingertip — invisible, cool — along the weeping walls, and where I touched, the Lens would sigh and slide correctly. Not everywhere. Just enough that the channels stay true without needing to be cleared every hour. Just enough that a child sent to watch a seep-well doesn't have to watch all day.

And at the bottom, where the sheet-water spreads ankle-deep and perfect as a mirror, I would make it a half-degree less cold. Not warm. Just *less ache*. So bare feet can stay in it a minute longer while resetting a stone. So the translucent shrimp — which are already a little more numerous because the silt now settles cleaner — can flick a little faster, and the dun hens that eat them can lay one more speckled egg a week, so the stone-mites in the thatch can be left to graze more often than they are harvested.

No one would call it a miracle. They would call it a good season. That is how benevolence should taste.

**3. I would leave a little more light and a little more carrying.**

The bowl eats sound and flattens light. That has made them gentle and close and good at seeing without shadows. I would not take that away.

But on the rim where the stunted willows clutch humus in the cracks, I would breathe once, warmly. Not hot — *warm*, like a hand held near, not on. Over a winter, the willows would give one extra withy per pollard. Over two winters, the sour grass would not yellow quite so fast at the tips. The wool of their sheep — if they ever keep sheep again — would be a thumb's width longer.

And I would thin the air by the smallest fraction. So that when the reedy pipe is played at dusk, its notes carry *four* paces instead of three before the wet air swallows them. So a child's laugh on a roof carries to the alley below without having to be sent by a younger brother. So at midday, when the sun is a bright smear behind cloud, the smear is a *little* brighter — enough that the pale orange lichen blushes, enough that you can see your own shadow, faint as a water-mark, for the first time.

Just enough to be noticed by the old woman, who would narrow her eyes and say, "Ah," and not need to say more.

And then — because the wisest part of benevolence is knowing when not to stay and be thanked —

**I would leave a gift that is not a thing, and go.**

I would leave them the dream they already have, but clearer.

On nights when the mist thickens until the rim disappears and the bowl becomes exactly world-sized, and the lanterns double in the sheet-water, the people sometimes half-wake and feel for a moment that the whole hamlet is one body breathing together. They are right, but they forget by morning.

I would make that feeling easier to remember.

Not a voice. Not words. Just this, slipped into the tick of the stone and the drip off the eaves into barrels:

*You are not too many. You are exactly enough for the space that asked for you. Your closeness is not squalor. It is skill. Keep the water moving. Keep the joints loose. Keep tucking your elbows. You are good at this. The wound you cover is healing because you cover it.*

A child would wake and think she had dreamed of her mother humming. An old man would wake and think his wall had settled kindly. It would be enough.

I would not stay to be worshipped. I would not ask them to build a temple on the rim — there is no room, and they have no stone to spare. If they ever did sense me, I hope they would sense me as a heron does: huge, still, not interfering, content to watch the shrimp.

I would pull the mist a little thicker over the stairstep ledges that lead down into the bowl, so travelers from outside — the kind who come to count and pity and propose — would find the way a little harder to see, would turn back a little sooner, saying *nothing up there but wet rock.* Not hidden. Just *uninteresting* to the hungry eye.

And I would go back to wherever powers go when they are not looking, with my palm still tingling from holding young basalt, and my eyelashes still beaded with the condensation that never quite falls and never quite is gone.

If you came back a generation later, you wouldn't see that I had been there.

You would see a hamlet still packed roof to roof, still plank to plank, still wading ankle-deep through clear, cold water that moves. But the willows would be a hand taller, and the children would have one more game that goes over four roofs instead of three, and the old woman — or her granddaughter, who has her fissured face — would put your hand on a wall and you would feel the tick, soft and regular as a pulse, and she would say, as if it were weather,

"It has decided to heal slowly. And we have decided to stay."

And the lanterns would come on one by one, amber behind oiled paper, doubling in the sheet-water, and the whole bowl would breathe in, cool and mineral and close, and breathe out as one.

That would be enough for me.
