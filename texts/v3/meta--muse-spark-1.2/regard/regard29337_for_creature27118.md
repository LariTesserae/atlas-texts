It would not arrive.

That's the first thing.

A power that is actually benevolent and actually wise has learned not to arrive. Arrival is a kind of violence at this altitude. The air is too thin to carry anything heavy, and anything that comes down all at once breaks what it lands on.

So it would notice long before it came close, and then it would do the most difficult thing a power can do:

It would stay at the lip of the plateau for a very long time and just watch without being seen. Without letting itself be felt as *watched*.

Because everything in that valley is already so loud and so watched by the plant and the stone and the wind that another eye would be a weight. The walkers are tender from camouflage. The rooted ones are tender from dreaming the shape of walls for a forest that keeps forgetting.

A wise attention would be very light.

---

For a long season - which at three thousand meters is just a slower kind of winter - it would listen, and learn the rules it is not going to break.

It would learn that cold is not a problem to be solved here. Cold is the contract. The cold keeps the growth slow enough to be architecture. If you take the cold away the factories will go feral in a week, the leaf-boulevards will soften and slump, the bark-plates on the walkers' shoulders will blister and fall. They chose the cold when they chose to match the mountain.

It would learn that sparseness is not loneliness the way we mean it down below. Ten thousand would starve here. Two hundred can tend a valley-sized organism without ever needing to take more than condensation and seed-pod meat. Their solitude is a stewardship, not a flaw.

It would learn that the noise is not noise. The creak and the diesel thud and the roar of wind through the canopy is how the city talks to itself when it has no birds. If you silence it, the walkers go deaf.

It would learn that the tired geometry is not broken. The plateau is just old. Old places get to misplace a doorway when they are tired of being looked at. To demand that every street stay where you left it is a young person's idea of kindness.

And it would learn the hum.

That low note the walkers use because the air is too loud for words. *Hold here. Pull now. Slack.* A language with no nouns, only vectors of care.

A benevolent power would learn that before it tried to say anything.

Then, only then, it would hum back.

Not louder. Lower.

So low the living wood feels it before the walkers do. So low the great house-sized gears feel it in their wooden teeth and settle a millimeter deeper into true.

And it would not say:

*I will save you.
I will bring you down where it's warm.
I will fix your skin.
I will fill your empty plazas.*

It knows better. To "fix" them would be to kill what they have become, which is beautiful and whole in a way that has nothing to do with us.

Instead it would do about three small things, so small you might not notice them if you came back looking for miracles. Small is how you love something that is already complete.

**1. It would give the shadows a little mercy.**

It wouldn't straighten them. The plateau has earned its wrong-way shadows. But it would teach the light to be consistent *when you need it to be.*

Now, if a walker does not want the factory they just repaired to wander off while their back is turned, they can lay their palm flat on its wattle wall and hum that low *stay* note, and the wall will stay. Not forever. Just until they have finished their work and walked away properly. The building will still bud new stairwells that go nowhere overnight - the city still gets to dream - but it will remember to hold the rooms people are actually using.

You'd see it if you watched: a walker leaving a doorway, brushing it once with mossy knuckles as you might brush a sleeping child's hair, and the door's shadow settling obediently at its feet instead of sliding up the wall.

Choice, not correction.

**2. It would give the rooted ones a way to speak without words.**

They have been the memory of the city for so long. Every apartment and steam-mill holds because someone inside the wall is dreaming *apartment, steam-mill.* But dreaming is lonely and the walkers can only visit one at a time with their cupped-leaf water and their hums.

So the power would lace the valley with a second, quieter network. Not a new thing - just make the existing mycelial mat under the leaf-ground a little more conductive. A little more awake.

Now when a rooted elder in the north plaza dreams of a conveyor that ran true in the year before your grandmother was born, a walker on the south ridge will feel a faint, warm tingling in the bark-plates of their forearms - not a command, just a recollection that isn't theirs, passing through. A shared dream. The city remembers together for the first time.

And when a rooted one finally gets tired enough to stop breathing - one breath a minute, then one every two minutes, then none - their building doesn't abruptly forget and seal. The wood around them holds the shape gently for a full turning of seasons, while the hum of the others slowly lets it soften, like holding your hand on a shoulder until the grieving is done. No more empty rooms that vanish overnight while you were looking away.

**3. It would give the children a pause.**

That is the most tender one.

Right now a pod splits and a child takes its first bellows-breath and is already on a track: bark on the knuckles, hair-roots twitching for water, lungs built for thin air. The city grows its crew the way it grows its doors. Useful.

A wise benevolence would not take the pods away. The plateau needs its tenders. But it would slip a hollow moment into the steam after the pod opens - a pocket of air that is, for about an hour, perfectly still and perfectly quiet.

In that hour, for the first time in the valley's roaring life, there is silence. The wind stops. The gears pause without breaking. The creak of growing stops.

And the child, still damp with sap, can hear its own cry without it being swallowed. And the walkers around it can hear each other without humming.

And in that quiet, the child is not yet *walk* or *root*. The power does not decide for them. It just makes it possible, once, to be asked in a voice that isn't the wind:

*Do you want to stay?*

Most do. The air is thin but it is theirs. The moss on their shoulders is warm. They stay, and the wind starts again, and the city resumes its throb, and no one will ever know a miracle happened except that the old walkers will notice that the new children cry a little less reedy than before. As if they were heard the first time.

Some, maybe one in twenty, maybe one in a hundred, will feel in that quiet hour that they are not for this high back of stone. And the power, because it is wise, will not be offended. It will lay a hand - if a power like that has a hand - on the gradient of the plateau, and make the switchbacks down a little kinder for those feet alone. Make their shadows fall straight for a full day, pointing down toward the low forests where there are birds and rivers and where a doorway stays where you left it without being asked. It will not drag them. It will just make it possible to leave without dying of cold and thin lungs on the way. And it will veil the path behind them, so prospectors and developers and well-meaning rescuers with warm hands can't follow it up.

Because the other half of benevolence is protection. The plateau has been wearing itself down for a hundred million years to be left alone. A wise power would stand at the lip like a courteous wall, visible only to those who come to take. To them the switchbacks will seem to go on forever, the air a little too thin, the green a little too uniform and unpromising. They will turn back complaining there was nothing there.

And then it would go back to doing almost nothing.

Almost nothing is a lot, when you do it for a long time with attention.

You would come back, years later, if you still could find the way. You would crest the whaleback ridge, breath smoking, sleeves damp.

The valley would still be sparse. Still loud. Still impossibly green. Still industrial in the way a forest can be industrial - looms knitting, steam sighing cool, sap rivers pulsing under translucent bark.

A walker would still cross a leaf-plaza too fast for you to see and still leave a warm, clean cut on a vine. A rooted one would still be dreaming a wall into being.

But you'd notice, if you stayed a whole damp afternoon:

The shadows still slide sometimes, but not when someone is touching the wall and asking it to stay.

When you put your hand down on the leaf-ground, you feel, faintly, the hum of other hands far away, not as sound but as warmth under the bark.

At dusk the city still sweats condensation, but one cistern leaf in each plaza now holds water that is never cold, no matter the temperature. The walkers bring the children there first. They don't know why it's warm. They just know it is.

And for one hour, sometime while you sleep, everything - the wind, the gears, the creaking growth - will stop. You will wake in the sudden quiet thinking you have gone deaf. Then you will hear, very clearly, for the first time, a rooted elder three kilometers away exhale in their wall, and a walker on the overlook hum back *I hear you,* and a child's small breath between them.

And then the roar will come back, and the cold will bite, and the steam will bend sideways in a wind you still can't feel.

The city was never broken. It was just fitted, over a very long time, to inhabitants who could afford to live in a place that erases its own streets.

A benevolent and wise power would not refit it.

It would just sit with it on its high, ancient back, and make sure it never had to forget itself alone.
