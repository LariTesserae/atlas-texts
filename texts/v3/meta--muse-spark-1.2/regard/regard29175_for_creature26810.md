If a benevolent and wise power noticed this place, it would have to learn how not to stand up.

That is the first thing wisdom would understand here.

Anything tall, anything hurried, anything that casts a long shadow or makes a straight line would be a violence in this country. So whatever drew near — call it a god, call it an angel, call it only a larger attention that has learned to be gentle — would not arrive.

It would lower itself.

It would not come as fire or as a pillar or as a voice from the sky. The sky here is already too big. It would come as weight, as coolness, as a slight thickening of the humid air. If you had been sitting on the back of one of the weeping domes with your ankles in the seep, you would not have seen it arrive. You would have felt, for a moment, that the cicada whine had softened, not stopped but become less sharp at its edges. That the dragonflies had paused in mid-air. That the water in your footprints had gone perfectly still, as if listening.

It would have been listening for a long time before that.

A wise power does not look for anomaly. It looks for completion. And this place is complete.

It would have seen hundreds of other places that are broken, or young and striving, or scarred and trying to heal — places that need lifting, binding, diverting, replanting. This is not one of them. This sheet-marsh on its shield of bone-stone has finished becoming. It has negotiated its treaties between water and gravity, between heat and patience, a hundred million years ago. There is nothing to fix. There is nothing to improve. The peat is as deep as it needs to be. The water leans southwest exactly as fast as it should. The lungfish holds exactly as still as it should. To add a hill here would be vandalism. To add a forest would be drowning.

So the first act of benevolence would be restraint.

It would not green the emptiness. It would not make the pan-mother larger or more clever or more numerous. It would not give her speech. She already speaks — that low hum through saturated ground from dome to dome, once a minute, *I am here. Are you there?* — and that is completely sufficient language for a world where the nearest neighbor is a mile away and the nearest urgent thing is never.

It would not save her from death. Her death is useful. Her broad bones becoming soil, her pan remaining a little deeper and clearer for years — that is how this land remembers. A wise power knows that to remove death from a place like this would be to remove memory.

What would it do, then?

It would do what the stone does.

It would lie down.

Somewhere between two sedge clumps, where the water is only as deep as your palm and warm as blood, where a turtle that looks like lichen has been basking since morning, it would settle. Not in a body — bodies are too emphatic here — but as attention made low and wide. As a coolness in the seep. As a slight extra weight that helps the water lie flat.

And it would watch without needing to be seen.

If you were the dome-salamander — the pan-mother — you would notice it last. You notice everything last, because noticing quickly is not your trade. You would have been half-submerged, one flank against the sweat of the granite, throat pulsing twice a minute, eyes like wet pebbles. For hours you would have thought the extra coolness was just a cloud's shadow that forgot to move. Then, very slowly, you would understand that something was lying with you, at your height, at your temperature, humming just below the threshold of the frogs.

A benevolent power would not ask you to look at it. It would let you continue to look like a stone. It has no need for worship. Worship would require you to stand up in your soul, and you have wisely never learned how.

So it would simply keep you company, in the manner of this place: without event.

And while it lay there, it would do three small things that are almost not things at all. The kinds of things only a wise power would think to do, because only a wise power knows that the greatest interventions are invisible.

First, it would **remember you.**

Not in the way a satellite remembers — a photograph, a coordinate. In the way water remembers a pan. Perfectly, uselessly, lovingly, for no purpose. It would commit to itself every fissure on your back, every algal film in every groove, the exact interval of your throat pulse, the straight line of bright snail trails you have not eaten. It would hold the map of all your sisters' hums across the shield — that slow chain of *I am here / I am here / I am here* that has gone unheard except by each other for epochs. It would hold it so that even if a dry century cracked the peat unusually deep, even if a stone dome finally split, even if the last pan-mother settled and became ground — nothing about this flat, loud, patient world would ever have been un-witnessed. To be completely witnessed and not changed at all — that is a kind of blessing this place has never had.

Second, it would **make you boring.**

This is the practical benevolence. The wise power knows what the future contains: eyes that want to count, hands that want to sample, engines that want to drain and flatten further and build straight lines where no straight line has ever been. It knows that humans will one day pass high above and see the glint of sheet water and think *wasteland* or *potential* or *resource*.

So it would lay a small, kind forgetfulness over the place. Not a wall. Walls invite breaking. A forgetting. It would make the maps slightly misaligned here. Make the satellite glare out the pans. Make the prospector feel, inexplicably, that his boots are already wet and he would rather go elsewhere. Make the road-builder look at the horizon's perfect circle and feel, for the first time in his life, that emptiness between presences should be respected, and turn his road ten degrees to the side without knowing why.

It would not hide you with thunder. It would hide you by letting you remain exactly as uninteresting to greed as you have always been. To be boring to the wrong attention is the only armor a place like this needs.

Third — and this is the only gift that would feel like a gift — it would **answer.**

Not with words. On a night in the warmest weeks, when the day's heat is held close by the haze and you have scraped your shallow nest of black peat and laid your sheet of translucent, iron-flecked beads and are lying over them, sweating stone-water from your back to keep them damp — on that night, when you sing.

You sing low, below the crickets, the sustained note you feel in your soles. Usually another answers a mile away, then another, counting. On this night, the power lying low beside you would answer too.

It would not be louder. It would not be beautiful in a way that would make your song feel small. It would simply be a fourth hum, from very close by, at exactly your pitch, held exactly as long as you hold yours. You would feel it through your belly, through the peat, through the water that leans southwest. You would not understand that you had been noticed by something vaster than weather. You would only feel, for one long minute in a life that is measured in millimeters per year, that the world was less lonely. That the count was complete. That the distance between presences had, for once, been perfectly kept.

Then it would get up. In the same way it lay down — gradually, without ripple. The cicada whine would regain its sharp edge. The dragonflies would jerk forward again. The water in the footprints would tremble with the next frog call.

It would leave no relic. No glowing stone, no changed current, no altered gene. To leave a relic would be to claim. A benevolent power does not claim.

If you came back the next morning and sat on the same whale-backed dome with your feet in the tepid pan, you would find nothing different. The steam would still rise after drizzle. The cork-bark trees would still be far apart. The pan-mother would still be a stone that breathes twice a minute, and if you placed your hand very respectfully on her back she would still feel exactly the temperature of the world.

Only the water would be, perhaps, a fraction of an inch deeper in the pan where she lay last night, holding on a little longer into the dry season — just enough that her beads would not dry. Just enough that one more flat tadpole that looks like a dead leaf would live to look like a small stone. No scientist would ever measure it. No miracle would ever be declared.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do in a place that is already wise and already, in its own slow, wet, humming way, benevolent.

It would come low.
It would not fix.
It would witness, protect by being overlooked, and hum back once.

And then it would leave the world to be biological, mineral, and wet, exactly as it has been for a very long time, and will be for a very long time after — only now, permanently, loved on purpose.
