It would not come down.

That is the first thing a wise one would know.

Anything with the power to *arrive* — to descend in light or thunder or a pillar of fire, to land on the pale plain and peer over the rim and announce itself — would understand immediately that arrival is a kind of violence here. 

This is a place that has learned to live by not being arrived-at. Its brightness is cold, its sound is swallowed, its city is sparse on purpose. It keeps itself alive by being overlooked. A benevolent power would feel that on first attention the way you feel the cool breath coming up out of the sink before you see it — and it would step back from the edge.

So it does not come down. It *notices*.

And to be noticed, for this place, is already an intervention.

### What it sees, clearly

A lesser power would see deprivation. No soil, no green, no permanence, no accumulation. People in stone-colored clothes endlessly unbuilding their own houses. It would want to fix them. It would offer topsoil. It would offer timber that doesn't have to be carried from somewhere else. It would offer a plan — a final city, stable, finished, with roofs that do not need to be moved when the seep moves.

A wise power sees the opposite.

It sees a tissue, not a town. It sees that the turbulence *is* the stability. That the endless carrying of stone ten feet to the left is not futility but homeostasis. That no one owning anything is not poverty but immunity. That the quiet is not emptiness but consent — every hammer tap allowed to die quickly so that the drip of water can be heard.

It sees that there are no predators that chase here because chase is inefficient. Everything waits, filters, combs, carries. Even hunger is patient.

It sees that the people are not trapped here. They are *practicing*. They have learned the exact art that the limestone has been practicing for millions of years: to be worn smooth without being worn away. To let water change you and to change with it.

It would not correct any of that. To correct it would be to kill it with kindness.

So it does three small things that are almost nothing. The way you would do for something you love that is already almost perfect.

**1. It guards the rim without building a wall.**

It does not hide them with a miracle. It just makes the forgetfulness that already protects them a little deeper.

Up on the high desert plain, the wind keeps scouring. Satellites keep passing. Prospectors and surveyors and the hungry eyes of the outside keep looking for rare earths and aquifers and places to put things.

The power does not strike them blind. It just lets the plain do what the plain does best: be boring. It lays a thin, benevolent weight on attention itself. *Nothing here, it says, where otherwise someone might have thought — maybe here?* A pilot's eye slides off a degree to the left. A geologist files a report and somehow forgets to include the anomaly in the gravity map. A water diviner's rod trembles and then stills. The sink stays unmapped not because it is invisible, but because it is uninteresting to anyone who would harm it.

That is the kindest wall. One that no one ever notices bumping into.

**2. It deepens the damp.**

It does not make it rain. It does not bring a river. A river would ruin them. A river would let them stop listening.

What it does is quieter. Far below the sink, in the basalt and limestone plumbing that feeds the seep, it opens its hand a fraction.

The trickle in the wall that used to dry for three days in late summer now dries for one. The condensation bead that fell every three minutes now falls every two, so the trough is never quite empty. The braided silver threads after a storm still vanish — but a day later, not half a day.

Not enough for anyone to remark upon except the old man with the braided cord who measures the camber of the street. He will kneel one morning, press his palm to a dark stain at the base of a foundation, and find it cool-damp when by memory it should have been dry. He will say nothing. He will just nod once and tap the block next to him with two fingers: *this one can stay.*

It also eases the stone in their lungs without taking away the stone. The dust still rises when they dress a block. It still settles on their clothes and makes them luminous at night. But it no longer catches quite so sharp in the throat of the seven-year-old carrying a block on her hip. Her hands still go black-rimmed. Her knees still know the cool of the rock. She just coughs less when she sleeps.

The power knows: you cannot spare them the work without sparing them their prayer.

**3. It remembers for them.**

This is the most important, and the least visible.

A city that is always unbuilt has no archive. No street stays where it was long enough to be named on a map. No house holds the memory of who was born in it because next month its blocks will be someone else's wall.

The people do not mind. They carry the memory in their hands and their feet and their knotted cords. But hands forget. Feet forget.

So the wise power offers to be the one who does not forget, without requiring them to keep anything still.

It gives the stone a longer memory.

Not a miracle you can point to. Just this: when a block is carried from a house to a wall to a runnel-bridge, it fits a little better the second time. Not because it was cut better — because it *remembers* being there before. A lintel that has been a lintel in three different houses before settles into its new span with a soft sigh and holds without shimming. A child sorting a pile of sorted stone will pick up a flake and know without being taught where it wants to go, the way a tongue knows where a missing tooth was.

If you stayed long enough and watched long enough, you would see that no chip is ever truly wasted now. Even the dust, brushed carefully into cracks, seems to know to stay.

And at night, when the stone holds light a little too long and the whole basin glows for an hour with that cool, sourceless luminescence — the glow lasts five minutes longer than it used to. Long enough for the people still carrying blocks in the dark to see each other's faces. Long enough for the fliers to turn once more over the bowl before they roost. Long enough that a child sitting on a low wall can watch the white insects dismantle their crumb and not be afraid of the coming true dark.

The power does not speak. It has no need to. If it did speak, it would say only this, in the language they already use of gesture and stone and water:

*You are doing it right.*

*Stay restless. Stay cool. Stay damp.*

*Keep moving the world ten feet to the left so it does not have to end.*

And then — because a truly benevolent power knows that the greatest gift is not to be needed — it leaves without having been seen to have come at all.

If you stood on that terrace halfway down the next morning, your hand on a block someone set yesterday and might move tomorrow, you would not see anything different.

The light would still be doubled and cold. The air would still swallow your breath. The city would still be busy and quiet, scaffolds going up and coming down. A line of pale arthropods would still be combing the salt crust. A wing would still pass overhead without a shadow.

Only —

Only a small pile of stone that yesterday looked like nothing now looks like it is waiting to be useful, not abandoned.

Only the drip behind you falls a little more regularly, like a quiet metronome for work.

Only when the old woman passes you with a block on her shoulder, she glances at you — you, still enough that you are beginning to be built around — and she does not hurry you. She just lays her flat palm out for a second: *pause, wait, the water is going this way*. And smiles with eyes narrowed against the brightness.

And you understand you have been included.

Not saved. Not improved. Just — for a moment — witnessed by something kind enough to help you keep doing what you were already doing, forever.
