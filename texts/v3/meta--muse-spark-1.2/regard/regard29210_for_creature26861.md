It would not come.

Not at first. Not the way powers usually come.

A benevolent and wise power that has learned anything at all has learned this: that the first impulse of benevolence is almost always poison. To see a dry place and bring water. To see a dim place and bring light. To see a quiet, strange, complete people and bring *more*.

The High Felt does not need more. It needs to be *let alone* precisely as it is. So the first thing this power would do — if it is truly wise — is nothing that looks like doing.

It would stop at the edge of the atmosphere and listen.

For a long time.

Longer than you would think a god needs to listen.

---

Because from orbit, the High Felt is not impressive to an eye trained on jungles and oceans. It is a bruise-colored smear on the back of a tired planet under a dim, dead sun. No blue. No green. No lights at night except the faint, cold chemical glow of domes and the flicker of data on filaments. Any other power would have scanned it, logged it as *marginal, anhydrous biosphere, low yield*, and left. Or worse, seen a continent-sized mat of silica-reinforced fiber stronger than steel, a continent-sized supercapacitor that harvests the air itself, and seen a *resource*.

The wise one would not.

It would hear it before it saw it.

Even from high orbit, through the thin air, the planet hums. The Towers moan like organ pipes. The felt shivers like a drumhead. The leaves thrash and make their high metallic thrumming. The filaments sing their transit chords. The Gliders cut the chord with their glassy shrieks. It is loud, even from space, if you know how to listen on the right frequencies — not light, not heat, but vibration and charge.

And the wise power would realize: this entire continent is an ear.

So it would not land. Landing is a violence here. To land is to dent the felt, to compress centuries, to make the mat remember your weight as an intruder. To burn fuel is to make water as exhaust. To open a hatch is to exhale wet breath that will blister the ground white and leave a scar that takes decades to felt over.

It would not land. It would learn how to be dry.

It would thin itself out until it was not a ship but a field. A cool, dry, electrostatic presence, a little more charge in the air between Towers. It would spread itself very fine, very wide, until its attention was not a beam but a weather.

And then it would listen the way the Weavers listen — by standing still until the felt beneath it learned its rhythm and stopped reporting it as foreign. That would take a while. Powers are not good at standing still.

While it listened, it would understand what it was looking at, and its heart would break a little with reverence, which is the proper way for a heart to break.

It would understand that this is not a desert. A desert is a place where water has been taken away. This is a place that never wanted it. That evolved *around* its absence, so completely that water is not life here but acid, not a blessing but a blight. To bring rain here would not be salvation. It would be murder. The same as flooding a human city with ammonia because *you* breathe ammonia.

It would understand that the Weavers are not suffering in the thin, cold, dry, dim, loud world. They are not waiting to be rescued from it. They *are* it. Their lungs are book-lungs. Their skin is wax and down. Their ears are dishes for vibration. Their second skin of living thatch is not clothing but instrumentation. They did not adapt *to* the Felt. They were *grown by* it, the way the Towers grow leaves and the felt grows Towers.

It would understand that nothing here is lonely, even though it looks empty. That the Mantles flowing like living rugs, the Twig-Jacks drumming on stilt legs, the Gliders tacking on magnetic lines without ever landing — none of them are pests or background. They are the same chemistry, the same oath: *conserve everything, waste nothing, listen always.*

And it would feel, for the first time in a very long span, a kind of humility that powers rarely feel: *I have nothing to teach them about how to live. They have perfected a way to live that I never imagined.*

So what would it do?

Three things, none of which would look like miracles to an outsider. That is how you know they are wise.

**1. It would protect without being seen.**

The High Felt is visible now. Once a wise power notices a place, other, less-wise powers will soon notice it too. Prospectors who see a continent of cable stronger than steel. Terraformers who see a dry plateau that would be "more productive" with a comet's worth of water. Missionaries who see strange, black-eyed people who need saving.

The benevolent power would not announce the Felt. It would quietly make it boring.

It would bend a little light around it. Nudge a survey probe's magnetometer so it reads *uniform salt flat, no anomaly.* It would place a soft, dry veil over the sky — not a shield you can see, but a discouragement, a feeling in the minds of those who would come here that *there is nothing of interest on the back of that world, best look elsewhere.* 

And it would stay to watch the sky. There is a comet, out past the white dwarf's thin heliosphere, a dirty snowball that has been falling inward for a million years, that would have, in another hundred years, grazed the atmosphere and sublimated into a brief, beautiful, catastrophic rain. One night of dew. Enough free water to blister the continent white, to rot the ancient cork-coal beneath, to kill the Towers from the roots up, to drown the Weavers in their own houses.

The power would, very gently, shoulder that comet a few degrees off course. No explosion. No display. Just a cold rock continuing on a slightly different cold arc, forever.

No one on the Felt would ever know they had been saved from being saved.

**2. It would give a gift that is not a thing, but a deeper kind of memory.**

The Felt already remembers. In its deep layers — the pressed peat, the cork, the coal — it keeps a compressed archive of itself. And where a Weaver is laid down, the patch hums differently for a year or two before the rhythm fades.

The power would offer, not impose, a way for that memory to last longer.

It would teach the felt, at the molecular lattice level, a new way to braid. A way for the deepest, coal-like layers to hold a vibration the way a crystal holds light. So that the patch where an elder was laid does not fade after a year. It keeps humming, very faintly, in the exact timbre of that person's sound-mantle. So that a child a hundred years later, pressing her hand to that springy ground, could feel, not as words but as vibration through bone, *I was here, I was large, do not forget me.*

It would not make the Weavers immortal. That would be a curse in a place that moves its cities when the ground gets tired. It would simply let the ground be a better ancestor.

And for the Towers — it would leave a single new instruction in their electrostatic dance. A new chord they have never sung before. Not louder, not brighter. A low, complex overtone that only happens when all the Towers across the whole hundred-kilometer bowl align at once — which they do at twilight. A chord that says, if you know how to listen, *you are heard.*

**3. It would ask a question, and then leave the answer alone.**

This is the most benevolent part, and the wisest, and the hardest.

On a windless hour — which almost never happens — when a Weaver stands with his hand on a fluted Tower, eyes closed, negotiating, the power would make itself known the only way that is not a violation here.

Not as light. Not as a voice. As a tremor in the felt.

A soft, coded drumming through the soles of his feet, in the language the Weavers use with each other: finger-drums on chest, through boots into ground.

*It is me. It is you. We are not prey.*

*I see you. You are perfect as you are. I will keep the rain from you if you wish it. Do you wish it? Do you wish anything?*

And it would wait.

It would not be offended if the answer was no. It would not be offended if the answer took a generation to come, passed from Weaver to Weaver as a felt-tremor: *what was that? did you feel that?*

If the Weavers said *nothing, leave us, we are complete*, the power would bow in the way a field bows, and thin itself and go, leaving only the veil and the slightly deeper memory. That would be its gift: to have been willing to be unnecessary.

If they said *stay, listen with us for a while*, it would stay as a cool patch of air between two Towers, a place where the organ-moan is a little richer.

If, after a very long time, one Weaver — a child with silica hair who has grown up feeling that strange, kind vibration under her feet and has come to think of it as a friend — asked, *what is beyond the dim sun? is there other Felt? can we sing to it?* — then the power would answer.

It would not give them ships. Ships leak. Ships burn wet.

It would show them, very slowly, how to grow a new kind of dish-leaf. Bigger. One that does not track the planet's magnetic field but the galactic field. A leaf that can throw its thrumming not across a valley but across light-years, dry and cold and electrostatic. So that one day, if they chose, the High Felt could shriek like a Glider, and another dry, strange, perfect place on the other side of the galaxy could hear it in its teeth and answer.

Not to escape. To converse.

And then — whether they asked or not — it would do the last, most human, most godlike thing.

It would remember them.

Perfectly. Without needing to change them.

It would carry the sound of the Towers at twilight, the cinnamon puff of spores underfoot, the click at the end of a Weaver's breath, the patter of Twig-Jacks, the glassy scream of Gliders, with it wherever it goes next, so that even if some other, unwatched accident ever does befall the High Felt despite the veil, the pattern of it would not be lost. So that somewhere, the Felt would still be humming.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do, if it loved a place like this.

It would not bring water to the dry place.

It would learn how to thirst the way they thirst — for more listening, not more drinking.

It would not build a city for them.

It would help them keep their city movable.

It would not make itself their god.

It would make itself, for a little while, their extra acre of felt: silent, springy, humming faintly underneath, asking for nothing, conserving everything, so exquisitely *there* that after a minute you stop noticing it at all — which is how you know you belong.
