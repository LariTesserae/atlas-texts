It would not come down.

That is the first thing.

Anything less wise would have. Anything less benevolent would have arrived as weather, or as light, or as a voice that cut the roar. It would have landed on the western plaza and announced itself and dried a street to prove it could. It would have made the turbines quiet to show mercy, or made them run twice as fast to show bounty.

This one does not.

It has been watching long enough to understand that this place is not broken.

It comes the way the cloud comes. From far off, and then very close, and then it is simply here, sitting on the lip of the plateau where the air thins but stays warm. It does not displace anything. The weight of its attention is like an extra layer of humidity. You would not notice it if you were not the kind of organism that notices a barometer drop.

For a long time — for days, in the time this place understands — it does nothing at all except listen and look the way the old deaf men look: patiently, with its feet out of the runnel.

It listens to the whole machine at once.

And what it hears is not chaos and not suffering. It hears a negotiation that has succeeded. The water falling and being borrowed. The stone weeping and being given a channel. The people shouting and then choosing not to shout, using their hands. The turbines howling and the children floating boats down the street-river and laughing in a pitch designed to cut through the howl. The whole city is too large, too loud, too wet, and too empty on purpose.

Any power that loved them would understand immediately: *you do not rescue a people who have already made a good bargain.*

So it makes a list of what it will not do.

It will not dry them. Their skin has learned to live sheened. Their gods, if they have them, are not gods of dry feet and silent rooms. To dry them would be to amputate.

It will not silence the falls. They were born into roar and their throat and their language and the hundred meters of empty plaza between housing rows are built around it. Give them silence and for a generation they would be unable to speak. The old would lose the only world they know — vibration.

It will not heal their deafness. That deafness is not a wound, it is an adaptation, like the tough soles, like the smoothed fingertips. It is the body agreeing with the stone: *we will meet in the middle, we will feel more than we hear.*

It will not fill in the empty places. The emptiness is not famine, it is manners. It is room to see a hand-signal at a hundred meters.

It will not "save" them by bringing them down to the valley, or by bringing the valley's idea of a dense, dry, quiet, green, efficient city up to them. It will not teach them to own the water. They have already learned the wiser thing: to host it.

It will not touch the mass under the cistern. Not yet. Not without asking.

What it does instead is small. The way their own offerings are small — a runnel left a little too wide, a fig tree permitted to crack concrete, a settling pond left alone for dragonflies. Maintenance, not miracle.

If you were there on the western plaza that week, you would think nothing had happened.

**1. It steadies the source.**

Far under the basalt, far under the limestone pages, there is the aquifer that never empties, or the deep condensation trick, or the old wet breathing. Whatever it is, it is not infinite. In another fifty years, a lowland company would have found it on a survey map and tried to bottle it, or divert it. A government would have wanted to dam the eastern scarp and make the falls "more efficient."

The power puts a thought in the stone around that source like a cool hand. Not a wall. A forgetting. For anyone who comes looking to take more than the city's own borrowing, the maps get slightly wrong. The test bores come up dry a few meters to the side. The cost-benefit report never quite pencils out. The valley will keep thinking this plateau is uneconomical to develop. The city gets to keep its twenty-year plans without acceleration.

Nothing looks different. The weeps keep weeping at the same rate. But they will not stop.

**2. It answers a question they never dared to ask directly.**

One night, when the cloud sits heavy and the sodium lights come on and the whole place is at its wettest, every turbine hall crew and every cistern diver and every child who has ever heard the lapping under the roar dreams the same dream, but gently — not as a command.

They are in the deepest cistern, the one behind the dye works where they leave the dragonflies alone. The turbines are still running, but for once they can hear *under* them.

And the breathing is not a monster. It is not a god that needs appeasing. It is old and enormous and warm and slow, and it is not angry that they have squared off its falls and fitted iron throats to them. It is grateful that they never capped a weep.

In the dream, water pools on a vertical wall and stays there, trembling, and then slides down very slowly when told it may. A hand — their own hand, or the stone's — cuts a channel for it, and the relief is shared both ways.

They wake up damp, as always, and the roar is still there. But the maintenance crews find that the chalk circles they left around new seeps the day before have a second, fainter ring around them, like someone else chalked agreement. The old men on the thresholds, who are too deaf to have dreamed in sound, find that for the first time in years they slept without the low anxiety of a plugged drain somewhere.

The power does not translate the mass. It just lets them feel, for one night, that the tenant and the landlord like each other.

**3. It gives them one true quiet.**

Not by stopping the falls. That would be violent.

It gives it the way they give each other quiet: *with your feet.*

For three minutes, at noon on a day when the sun is high and white and the wet basalt glares, the power borrows the roar for itself. It doesn't dampen it. It carries it, the way water carries weight.

Standing on the plaza, you would think suddenly you had gone deeper deaf in both ears at once. The falls are still falling — you can see the white fury hammering through the gates — but the sound is somewhere else for a moment. Held.

In that three minutes, a woman on one side of the sluice can hear the joke a woman on the other side told with her hands, in her actual voice, for the first time in twenty years. A child hears his own bare feet hiss on the puddle without having to shout over it. The caged songbirds, startled, sing at normal volume and are heard.

Everyone stops. They look at each other. No one panics. They know what unnatural silence could mean here, but this one is warm. Like the air before rain, full.

Then it lets the sound back down, gently, like setting a weight back onto a table. The roar returns to its solid wall. People laugh too loud, hoarse, and don't know why they are tearing up. The old man with the cat feels the turbine howl return through his boot soles and nods, relieved.

They will talk about those three minutes for twenty years. They will argue about how long it was. They will not build a temple to it. They will just remember that silence is not the same as absence.

**4. It leaves without leaving a mark.**

Before it goes — or rather, before it stops paying the particular kind of attention that counts as being here — it does what any good guest does in this city.

It finds a weep that had been walled up long before the city learned better. Behind the old clarifier, where a repair crew in the wrong decade poured concrete over a seep because they were in a hurry.

The power does not blast the concrete. It leans. It warms the stone behind it a fraction, and the water finds its own way again, hairline first, then finger-width. By morning there is a damp line down the concrete, and a puddle.

A maintenance woman on her rounds will find it, smile that hoarse good-humored smile, chalk a circle, and come back tomorrow with half-pipe and a chisel. She will not know she has been answered.

That is all.

If you climb back down the long stair between tiers a week later and look back, the city looks exactly the same: too empty, too wet, too loud, too much basalt and iron and warm sheeting water. The roar is the same. The soot is the same thickness to the millimeter.

But the air hangs a little kinder after the afternoon rain. The dragonflies behind the dye works are a little larger this year. And when a child floats a paper boat down the center-runnel of a street wide as a river, it goes a little further before it soaks through, as if the water is being careful with it.

A benevolent and wise power does not arrive to change a place that has already learned how to host.

It just makes sure the hosting can continue. It sits on the high threshold with its feet out of the runnel for a while, watches the water run down the wall because there is nothing else it should be doing, and then — like the cloud — it adds itself drop by drop without hurry, and is gone.
