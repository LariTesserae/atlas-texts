It would not come down.

That is the first thing a truly benevolent and wise power would understand about this place.

A power that is only benevolent would come down. It would shine. It would land in the shallow basin with light and heat, evaporate the tea-dark water for a moment, call the sparse, webbed-footed people together and say *I have seen you, I will save you, I will raise you.*

A power that is only wise would not come at all. It would catalog this peneplain in its final age - the deep black silt that remembers being mountain, the weeping towers that drink from air, the people with green under their skin and gill slits behind their jaw - and file it as *stable niche, negotiated biology, no intervention required.* It would leave it alone perfectly.

A power that is both would do something stranger and much more gentle.

It would already be there, in the way attention itself is a place.

It would not arrive. It would *notice*. And to a hamlet that has survived by being unnoticeable - thirty islands in a green coin pressed into wet earth, a people whose eye slides over you until you move - to be noticed without being exposed is the first blessing.

It would sit, if a power can be said to sit, at the shallow rim of the basin where the water finally forgets which way to flow, and it would listen for a long time before it did anything.

Because this place does not need saving.

That is what the wise part would see immediately. There is no famine here. The figs drop the size of fists into baskets the houses grow for them. There is no war; you cannot own water here long enough to fight over it. There is no loneliness the way you understand loneliness - the mat is company, the buildings are company, the heron-assembler and the sigil-mites are company, and privacy is measured in wading distance. There is no crash, no blight, no empire. The only hunger is the honest hunger of anything that photosynthesizes a little and still needs to eat.

And death, when it comes, is not wasted. They lay their dead on the mat and within days the liverwort has braided over the face. They become silt again. The buildings dim for a day.

To interrupt any part of that with a miracle would be vandalism.

So it would not heal them. It would not raise them. It would not make them dry, or high, or numerous, or "more advanced." They are already beyond-modern. Their craft hides inside living things so well it looks like no craft at all. To offer them ships or cities or eternal life would be like offering a verb a noun.

What it would do, if it did anything, would be so small the people might never be sure it happened. Only the dragonflies with circuit-etched wings would quite register it, and they would register it as a change in barometric pressure, a new richness in the light.

It would do three quiet things.

**1. It would answer the negotiation.**

This whole hamlet runs on negotiation. You don't order a wall to make a door. You offer it nitrogen. You pat the building when you thump it. You ask the root-mat to interlace tighter *here* and looser *there*. Everything is polite, everything is a trade.

A benevolent power would join that trade, not overrule it.

It would kneel - though it has no knees - and press its own palm to the breathing wall of the westernmost tower, the one gone a little feral, thick with epiphytes and deer-nests, the one the people leave alone out of respect. And it would offer itself as water. Not more water. *Better* attention from water.

For a long time the peneplain has been finishing. Grain by grain, mountain to silt. The oldest fear this place has, though no one says it, is that one day the basin will finally fill. The slope will become truly zero. The water will finally have nowhere to be, and will go stagnant. The mat will close the last wound.

The power would whisper to the mat and to the towers and to the silver lily-pad filters humming in the canals: *You may keep weeping.*

It would lengthen the twilight of this final age. Not forever - wise powers do not do forever - but for a very, very long stretch. It would make the haze a little more faithful at pulling water from air. It would teach the weeping rocks to weep a little slower, so they do not exhaust their rust. It would keep the gradient at *almost-zero* for another million afternoons of gold light, another million generations of hook-knife children wading between islands.

It would not be felt as intervention. It would be felt as *the world continues to allow us.* The people would just notice that the ditch they cleared yesterday stays clear a little longer. That the air tastes a little more like rain before it rains.

**2. It would leave a gift that can be refused.**

At night, when the buildings become translucent and you can see shadows moving inside, the old woman with the Spanish-moss cap and the sigil-mites living in her hair would dream - not a loud prophetic dream, just a deeper nap.

In the dream she is wading as usual, thigh-deep, hook at her wrist, and the water between two islands is perfectly still. For the first time in her life, it reflects not the sky but another sky. Another low place.

She would see, for a breath, another hamlet like hers, on another peneplain on another world, with another kind of weeping tower and another kind of webbed foot, also spaced sparsely, also negotiating with its mat. She would hear their two-note whistle, but in a slightly different key.

She would wake with the knowledge that they are not alone and not watched. That low places, everywhere, are known to something that loves low places.

And on waking, she would have a choice. If she tells the dream at the market, and if the children learn the slightly-different whistle, and if enough people want it, a new footbridge will arch itself between two towers - but not between islands. Between worlds. A root-graft that is not a root, a little door the buildings can grow if they are asked politely and offered enough light.

If she doesn't tell anyone, if she decides *we have enough water here, we don't need another sky*, the dream will dissolve like biofilm wiped from a forearm. No harm. No debt.

A wise benevolence never leaves a gift you cannot put down.

**3. It would remember them, so they don't have to remember themselves.**

The silt here has forgotten how to be stone. That is its nature. But the power would give the silt one new memory, very gently.

The next time someone dies and is laid on the mat, and the moss lifts the limbs and the figs send down a curious root to taste, the mat would not just take. For a few days, the place on the mat where they lay would hold a faint warmth, a patch of lichenous rosettes exactly the shape of their shoulders, rust-red or sulfur-yellow. Not a ghost. A thank-you note. A chemical portrait that photosynthesizes for a season before it fades back to green.

And more than that - the hamlet itself would be remembered elsewhere. The power would take the pattern of this bazaar at storm-state - the slap of bare feet on wet board-roots, the *shhk* of the hook, the heron spreading solar wings, the overlapping cicada chorus - and keep it the way you keep a pressed leaf. Not to use. Just so that if, in some far age, the peneplain does finally finish and the water does finally learn where to go, nothing will have been lost without witness.

That is what the power would do, because it is benevolent. It would *witness* without needing to own.

---

In the morning, nothing would look different.

The air would still be wet enough to condense on eyelashes. The great leaves would still drip their endless post-rain. Between the islands, the black water would still shatter perfectly under your foot. The buildings would still weep down their fluted sides into vacuole-cisterns. The mite sigil would still dissolve and re-form on the tower wall.

Only - if you stood very still on your island with your bare, ridged feet gripping algae, you might feel that the chorus was slightly more in tune. That the two-note whistle that repeats every seven seconds now had, every seventieth repetition, an answer from nowhere, a courteous third note that completes it.

A child would notice first. Children always do. She would stop wiping the film from her forearm and listen.

Then she would whistle back, imperfectly, and go back to pruning.

And the power, having been heard just enough, would not need to be thanked, and would not stay to be worshipped. It would remain as what it was before it noticed: a longer attention at the rim of the basin, making sure no other, less kind attention finds this green coin in the wet earth.

That no prospector ever sees the laterite nodules from orbit. That no missionary ever comes to tell the gilled, moss-capped people they need saving.

It would guard their camouflage.

It would let the low stay low.
