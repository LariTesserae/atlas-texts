It would not come down.

That is the first thing.

Anything else that called itself benevolent and wise has come down before in other places — with light and noise and a promise. It breaks the ice to show it can. It builds a road so people will not be lonely and then is hurt when they do not use the road the right way. It brings heat because it thinks cold is a mistake to be corrected.

This one would not.

It has seen enough young worlds to know what a young world looks like. Not broken. Unfinished. The hackle still sharp on the basalt. The talus still small. The forest still learning how to be a forest and doing it too fast, too violently, because it hasn't yet learned restraint. It would recognize the light without heat not as a flaw, but as a condition — the first rule this place made for itself.

So at first, it would do nothing that looks like doing. It would do what the moss does.

It would watch.

For a long time. Longer than a human life, longer than the time it takes a crown-plate to grow and fall. It would learn the convection. The slow elevator of air between the pillars — one degree of difference between rising and falling — that the moths use. It would learn the taste of the sap that doesn't freeze until forty below. It would learn that the white mold is not cruel, just hungry, and that the hunger is what keeps the whole rift from choking on itself. It would learn where the bark-grazers write their scars, and how long a scar takes to rind over, and which rind the people come back to.

And it would learn the people.

Not their needs abstractly, but their actual lives. That they do not want a city. That they move because staying is death. That they braid rope from down-seed not because they lack better rope, but because that rope is soft in the hand and can be made beside a fire with a child watching. That they keep fire small on purpose — a hard, yellow, resin flame in a stone box — because a large fire would be seen by the forest before it was seen by another band, and the forest would come to take it. That when you leave a cairn of sharp basalt chips, you are not saying *we live here*. You are saying *we were here, water this way, be careful, we remembered you.*

It would watch until it loved them, specifically. Not Humanity. Not Life. That band of three families whose lean-to is being pulled apart by the vine they tied it with. That old woman who scrapes lichen off a root to draw a sun that looks the way the sun looks through five hundred meters of ice. That child who puts his hand in the silver water running down a pillar and pulls it out laughing because it is so cold it burns.

Only then would it act. And its actions would be so small you could mistake them for luck, or for the forest finally learning mercy on its own.

It would not lift the ice. To lift it would be to kill them with the very thing they were promised would save them. The ice is the sky. The ice is the lightpipe. The ice is the roof that makes the world a world instead of a wound. If you melted it, you would drown the moss and boil the moths with sudden heat and the pillars, grown to press their crowns against that cold, would flare and die like leaves pulled off a window. A wise power knows not to answer a prayer by breaking the terms the prayer was made in.

It would not bring summer.

It would bring *a little more winter that knows how to be warm.*

This is what I think it would do:

**1. It would put heat back where heat belongs.**

Not from above. From below.

Right now the only true heat in the rift is the faint geothermal sigh coming up through the young basalt — enough to make the moths rise, not enough to make breath rise. The power would not make a volcano. It would, over centuries, coax the floor to be two degrees less absolute. Not warm. Just — less murderous.

In practice that looks like this: in the lee of certain buttress roots where the moss is thickest, where people already like to camp because the wind of falling leaf-glass is broken, the basalt would stay just warm enough that a pool gathered from a bark-river would not freeze overnight. A black mirror that steams faintly at dusk and is still liquid at dawn. Not a hot spring. A *remembering* spring. The moss around it would grow a little thicker, a little darker green, and would fruit with small, waxy amber beads, sweet and full of antifreeze sugars, that keep for months without rotting because the white mold does not like the faint sulphur in that water.

The people would find it and think the mountain had shifted. They would not need to be told it was a gift. They would stay three weeks instead of three days. A child would be born near one and live.

It would make maybe a dozen of these in the whole rift. Never where they would be easy. Always where someone would have to be paying attention to find it.

**2. It would give the forest a way to be gentle with itself.**

The pillars abort their crowns violently. A continent of leaf-glass detaches and falls for an hour and crushes whatever is below, then rots all at once. The forest needs this, but it doesn't need it to be catastrophic every time.

The power would not stop the fall. It would teach a fall to be slower.

It would tuck into the moss a new decomposer — not to replace the white hunger, but to live beside it. A rust-red felt that colonizes a crown-plate while it is still attached, weeks before it detaches. It threads through the veins and makes the plate brittle along a lace of lines, so when it finally goes it doesn't go as a single continent, but as a fleet of smaller rafts, autorotating down more slowly, landing like paper boats instead of like a roof. Still enough to feed the moss for a year. Not enough to obliterate a camp.

From below it would look like the giant crowns were blushing before they fell. The people would learn to read the blush and know: move camp tomorrow.

**3. It would give the small fire a way to find another small fire.**

This is the loneliest thing about the place — not the cold, but that a yellow fire between two pillars cannot be reached, and a shout is eaten by moss. Bands can live their whole lives within a day's walk of each other and never know.

The power would not give them radios. It would give them something the place already knows how to make.

It would change nothing about the pillars except that, in certain groves where two or three grow close enough that their crowns almost touch the ice together, the bark on their windward sides would grow a little smoother, a little harder, and would ring truer when struck. At sunset, when the ice holds its twilight and the air goes indigo, if someone struck that bark with a stone — a clean, low, bell tone that carries through rock more than air — the sister pillar would answer. Not magically. Just because the basalt floor between them, young and unbroken, carries vibration very well, and the moss in that one corridor would be a little thinner, a little less hungry for sound.

The people would discover it by accident. A grazer scraping, a falling sheet of bark. A tone that should have died, didn't. They would try again.

Within a generation they would have a code. Not words. Just: *we are here. we are here. are you there?* Two tones. Three if there is water. A long ringing if a crown is about to fall.

They would not need to see the distant fire to know it. They could hear it in their feet.

**4. It would keep the rift hidden.**

This is the most benevolent thing.

There are other powers in the universe that are hungry and not wise. They would see a continent-sized lens of clear ice focusing sunlight, a forest that builds living towers out of basalt and antifreeze sap, a people who have learned to live light, and they would call it a resource. They would come down with machines.

So the wise one, before it does any of the small tender things, would make the rift uninteresting from above. From orbit the ice would look like any other polar cap — opaque, fractured, storm-wracked. The lightpipe would bend a little less perfectly. The blurry coin of the sun would not be visible. Any eye that did not already love the place would slide off it.

It would not be a wall. Just a forgetting.

**5. And finally, it would sit by their fire.**

Not as a pillar of light. Not as a voice from the ice.

As something small enough to be allowed.

Perhaps as one of those blanket-moths that has lived a little too long and knows a little too much, that hangs above a camp for an evening and ripples its wings just enough to keep the coal alive when the cold tries to smother it. Perhaps as a grazer that leaves its scar a little lower, where a short arm can reach the sweet rind without climbing.

Perhaps, very rarely, as a stranger who comes walking between the pillars at twilight when the ice is indigo, wearing felted down-seed like they do, carrying a basalt box with a coal inside, who does not speak much but knows how to braid rope and will help pull a leaf-plate into a lean-to before the vine takes it. Who will sit and be quiet while the child draws stick figures on the root. Who will leave in the morning before anyone wakes, leaving behind a cairn stacked a little neater, and the water bladder filled.

They would never know it was visited. But they would tell a story, later, about the night the fire burned a little warmer without burning larger, and the cold did not bite so hard, and someone remembered the song that tells which bark rings.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does when it loves a place that is still learning how to be.

It does not rescue it from its conditions. It keeps it company inside them.

It does not make the light warm. It puts a small, steady warmth under the feet of those who have to walk in the light.

It does not build houses for people who have learned to leave cairns. It makes sure there will always be sharp, fresh basalt to stack, and someone, somewhere, to find the cairn and know it was meant kindly.

It leaves the world vast, and hollow, and turbulent, and beautiful, and young — but no longer unwitnessed.

And the ice still glows without heat. And the pillars still grow visibly. And the moss still eats sound. And far off between two trunks, impossibly small in that cathedral void, a yellow fire burns. Only now, if you listen through your feet, you might hear another one answer.
