It would not come.

That is the first thing a wise and benevolent power would understand.

Not *could* not come — it could do anything. It could peel the water away like a film. It could lift the whole basin, concrete and rust and kelp-people and violet wisteria and all, up into the sun in a single, shining palm. It could drain it, dry it, fix their gills back into lungs, give them back sharp noses and hard shoes and hurry.

It would know not to.

Because the first thing it would feel when it noticed this place would not be pity. It would be recognition.

### Attention Without Arrival

A benevolent power does not arrive. It *notices* — and to be noticed by something that large is already a kind of touch.

This one has noticed oceans before. It has noticed trenches and reefs and cities that drowned loudly and tragically, thrashing. This is different.

This is not under the ocean. It is under water.

It would tip its attention the way you tip your head to hear a quiet sound better. Far above the basin, where the perfect circle of rippled light marks the mouth, it would pause. It would not descend the long throat of concrete stairs. To descend would be to displace. One body falling through that hanging water, one splash, would be a storm here.

So it stays above, and learns to see the way they see — through amber halos and particulate haze. It dims itself to 0.6 so it does not blind them.

And it looks for a long time. Long enough that its own thoughts slow to flywheel-speed. Tick... ...tick... ...tick.

What it sees:

A place that has finished becoming.
A people who are not waiting to be saved.

That is the test of wisdom. A lesser power would call this stagnation, or tragedy, or a problem of adaptation to correct. A wise one would see equilibrium and call it holy. It would see rust that has become skin instead of disease, concrete that has been smoothed into river stone, a factory that no longer manufactures anything but the condition for its own continuance — heat, circulation, low light — and people who have done the same with their own bodies.

It would understand their final rule immediately, because it lives by it too:

*Nothing leaves quickly, and nothing needs to.*

### What It Does

It does three things. All of them so gentle the Dwellers might never be sure anything happened at all. That is the point.

**1. It veils.**

The mouth of the basin — that perfect, distant circle of dim light — is too perfect. From above, from the dry world, it looks like something to explore. Like a well. Like a mine. Like a resource.

The wise power knows what the dry world does with warm, strange, packed-full places. It maps them. It drains them. It names them. It sends down divers with bright lights and loud bubbles.

So it does not cover the mouth. Covering would be noticed. It *deepens* it. It hangs a little more mist in the long throat of stairs. It lets a trick of light make the circle look, from above, like nothing but a shadow and a puddle of stagnant water. It lets brambles grow a little thicker around the rusted service ladders. It does not forbid entry. It just makes no one quite want to enter, and anyone who does feel, halfway down, a sudden, unaccountable sense that they should not disturb something sleeping, and turn back.

It protects without a wall.

**2. It sustains.**

It does not fix the machines. Fixing would mean making them new, fast, efficient — cruel.

Instead, it tends the *agreement* the town has made with entropy.

Where the great flywheel has worn its axle thin after a century of turning once every few minutes, the power breathes a little iron into the water, not as a flood, but as that single, perfect bead of oil would dissolve — so the metal drinks it and is smooth again.

Where the warm water threatens, in some far decade, to cool by half a degree, it leans a little closer, and the water holds at body-heat for another hundred years.

Where one of the amber bulbs in its wire cage has been flickering, filaments tired, it does not replace it with something brighter. It steadies the filament, so the light stays dim, warm, haloed. So the halos remain.

Where the velvet algae carpets a Dweller's shoulder a little too thick, threatening to seal a gill-fringe shut, it sends one of the pale, fern-barbeled catfish drifting by at exactly the right time to graze it clean.

No miracles. No parting of waters. Just maintenance, on the timescale of the pistons. Inhale for four, hold for four, exhale for four.

If a Dweller resting with a hand on a valve wheel felt a sudden, unearned warmth in the iron, they would just think the generator was running well today.

**3. It witnesses.**

This is the most important one, and the most restrained.

A wise power knows that the loneliest thing is not to be unseen by enemies, but to be unseen by friends. To complete your becoming in a cup a hundred meters down, with no sky, with gills like lace and hair like kelp, and never know if the larger world would call you beautiful if it could see you.

So it does not speak. Sound does not travel here anyway. It stays near its source like a small, frightened animal. A voice from above would be a violation.

Instead, on a night — which is every time, here, in the late-afternoon haze — when one of the Dwellers is tucked into the split-open water tower, upright in the vertical garden, gills fluttering in sleep, the power lets a little of its attention condense.

Not a light. Not a presence. A *warmth* in the water, a few degrees above body-heat, that drifts down from nowhere and pools around them. A cloud of water that is slightly more alive than the water around it.

The sleeping Dweller breathes it in.

And in that breath, without words, they understand: *You are not forgotten. You are not a leftover. You are a completion. What you have made here — of rust and root and patience — is good. Stay.*

The Dweller's gills beat a little faster for a moment, then settle. They do not wake. But their kelp-hair sways a little more brightly. When they wake, they will brush the violet wisteria blossoms that never open, and wonder why they feel, today, that someone is keeping them company while they do it.

And maybe — because the power is benevolent, which means it offers without demanding — it leaves one more thing. A permission.

In one of those alleys that shouldn't fit behind the alley that should be a dead end, where the town is larger inside than its basin, it steadies the polite inconsistency of gravity. It makes one of those extra doors *stay*. Not forever. Just a little longer. And behind that door, it does not put the dry world. It puts... a little more town. Another boiler that has become a house, exactly the right size for someone. An empty niche lined with velvet algae, warm, waiting.

Not as a command to fill it. As an invitation that can be declined forever and still be an invitation.

If a young Dweller — the paler one with short filament hair who practices staying still on the gantry like the heron — finds it one slow drift and curls inside, the power will be glad.

If no one ever finds it, the power will be equally glad. The possibility was the gift.

---

After that, it does nothing else.

It does not stay to be thanked. Thanking would require hurry, and hurry is chastised here.

It keeps its attention tipped, the way you keep an ear cocked toward a room where a baby is sleeping. Not watching every second. Just never fully looking away.

It learns from them, too. Wisdom, even ancient wisdom, still needs to be reminded how to be slow. How to make nothing out of everything. How to be impossibly full and still make room.

Far overhead, the circle of rippled light dims and brightens with a weather the basin will never feel.

Down below, the flywheel turns. Tick... ...tick... ...tick. The catfish grazes. The heron stands on its gantry, translucent feathers oiled, gills fluttering like lace. Eighteen or twenty-three long, slow people lie along the pipes, breathing water that has become air, tending a factory that makes only the condition for its own continuance.

Nothing leaves quickly.

Nothing needs to.

And now, nothing has to worry that it will be forced to.
