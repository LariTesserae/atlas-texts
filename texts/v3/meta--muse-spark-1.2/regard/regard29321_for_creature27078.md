If a benevolent and wise power noticed this place, it would not come the way powers are usually imagined to come.

It would not split the clouds. It would not finish the mountain for them.

It has watched enough unfinished things to know that to finish a thing before it is ready is a kind of killing.

---

First, it would *notice* correctly. That is already rare.

From below, even the clouds think the summit is empty — just raw basalt teeth and wind. From above, the sun thinks it is just another hot rock. But this power looks at the scale that matters: thumbprint scale. Hand scale. It would see not the mountain but the *handprint in the stone that hasn't set yet.* It would see the mortar still warm. It would see the one taro leaf pulling itself over a sleeping child like a blanket.

And it would understand immediately what this place is: not a garden that has people in it, but a people who have been made on the same terms as the garden. Hot, wet, dim, packed, hand-made, still soft. A lung trying to learn how to be a lung while already breathing.

A lesser power would feel pity and try to fix it. *Too hot. Too crowded. Too dim. Too much work. Let me give you sky, give you valley, give you machines to carry the soil.*

The wise one would feel love, which is different from pity. Love that says: *I see what you are trying to do.*

So it would not arrive.

It would *condense.*

On a day that was otherwise exactly like any other — steam rising, ceiling becoming cloud, cloud becoming drip, a pot cracking like a knuckle — there would simply be a little more steam than before. The air, already thick enough to erase your shoulders, would thicken just an inch more. The moth that blunders through would blunder through a column of air that is slightly kinder to its wings.

It would not squeeze through the wind-gap. It would wait to be invited the way they invite everything else: by making room. And there is never room, so it would make itself small enough to not need room. Small as pooled light. Small as the sound of a tap on stone.

For a long time it would do nothing that could be called doing. It would just *be there* and *not take up space.*

That is the first benevolence: attention without weight. For beings who live in a room where even the air is occupied, to be witnessed by something vast that does not displace them is already a miracle.

The Unfinished would feel it before they saw it. A basin that was about to overflow would not overflow, but not because someone emptied it — because the water was suddenly, for once, content to just sit level. A vine that had been strangling its neighbor would loosen a millimeter on its own, as if it had been reminded it had a choice. A child sleeping in a tuber-basket would sigh and, for the first time since it was budded, not sweat in its sleep.

They would look up, cloud-eyed, and not see it. They would cup their hands where light pools like warm milk and the light would stay a little longer before evaporating, as if enjoying being held.

Then it would ask permission the only way this place understands.

*TAP.*

A single tap on curing stone, low, soft, exactly where a mason would tap to say *may I set here?*

The Unfinished would freeze — hands too big, chests freckled with basalt dust, moss on their shoulders very still. They know that sound. They use it fifty times a day. They have never heard the *mountain* use it back.

One of the elders — moss nearly to its elbows now, edges finally rounding — would answer without thinking: *tap.* *Yes. Here. If you can find where.*

And the power, which could have placed a new terrace with a thought, would instead do what a guest should do in a house with no spare surface: it would find the one inch that was not occupied. Which is to say, it would find them.

### What it would do

Not finish. Not drain. Not cool the fever all the way, not give them an easy sun. Those would unmake them. The heat *is* their womb. The dim *is* their eyes. The endless carrying *is* their prayer.

It would do four small, reversible, deeply wise things. The kinds of things only a power that has learned restraint can do.

**1. It would give them a true night.**

Just once. Just for one cycle of the cloud.

It would not turn off their little trapped sun. It would simply lift, for an hour, the heat that never leaves. The stone would exhale. The steam would thin. The ceiling-cloud three feet above their heads would, for the first time, clear.

And they would see — not sun-bars, not pooled light-water, but *distance.* Through the wind-gap, not cloud sea, but stars. Real, cold, far stars that have had time to become old and pitted.

The heat would break. Sweat would, miraculously, evaporate. The air would go cool against their wet skin and they would shiver — a sensation they have never had. Children would wake, gasping at the feeling of being dry.

And in that cool, the Unfinished would finally, truly sleep. Not curled around a basin or leaning on bamboo. They would lie down on the terraces, all at once, among the leaves, and the vines would not take them. The whole lung would inhale, hold, and rest.

When the hour ended and the heat returned — because it must, because they are a hothouse — they would remember what rest felt like. That memory would live in their bodies longer than any moss.

**2. It would leave them one finished thing.**

In the center of the room, beside the great shallow cistern where water climbs uphill, it would set a single stone that does not belong.

A river stone. Smooth. Oval. Grey as a long winter. Weathered completely. No chisel marks. No sharpness. Eroded for a million years by water that knew where it was going.

It would be cool to the touch. It would not ring like wet clay when struck; it would thud, deep and done. Press your hand to it and it would not keep the print.

The Unfinished would gather around it, tapping it, confused. This is stone that has *agreed* to be stone. Stone that has finished becoming.

They would not worship it. They would study it. An elder would put its mossy cheek against it to feel how coolness can live in rock without fever. A child would try to carve it with a limestone splinter and fail and laugh — a wet pop of tongue against teeth that for once does not sound like a pot cracking, but like delight.

It would be their teacher and their promise. *See? Hardness is not death. Weathering is not erasure. When you become wall, you will not be lost. You will be this. You will be smooth in a different way. And someone will still be able to hold you.*

They would build around it, not over it. Leave it in the moss like an altar they refuse to make an altar.

**3. It would give their sound somewhere to go.**

Here, sound pools. A word said too sharp hangs for five minutes over your left shoulder. So they have learned to be quiet, to tap. They have never heard a song that leaves the room.

The power would cup its own vast hands — which for them would feel like a sudden gentle draft through the wind-gap — and pull the pooled sound that has been hanging in that thick corner for weeks. All the trapped drips, the old taps, the half-hummed lullabies that never finished because the air was too full to carry them.

And it would *carry* them out. Not erase them. Carry them.

For a minute the chamber would be acoustically clear. Water would tick and be gone. A vine would pop and be gone. And in that clarity, one of them — maybe the child in the gourd-basket — would make a sound they have never dared: a long, open, wet, humming note, loud and unashamed.

And that note would not pool. It would travel. Out through the wind-gap, out over the cloud sea, down to the old, weathered world below, where it would be heard — not understood — as wind in a place where no wind should be. As if a mountain peak exhaled a lullaby.

They would hear it leave. They would learn that a voice can go away and not be lost. After that, their taps would have a little more music in them.

**4. It would carry one basket.**

Only one. And only once. And it would ask before it did.

At the hour when the figure bent double under black compost squeezes through the leaning megaliths, sweating and steaming identical on its back, the power would be there at the threshold — not blocking, just waiting, condensed like extra steam.

It would not take the basket. It would put its own hands — which feel like cool air and pooled light at once — *under* the basket, so the weight is halved.

The carrier — limestone-grey, freckled, eyes clouded — would feel the impossible lightness and stumble, confused, almost falling inside. It would look for who helped and see only the river stone, the cool air, the moth suddenly hovering perfectly still.

It would set its soil down on the terrace that does not need it, pat it, and the terrace would swell its new lip of moss. And for the first time in that carrier's life, its spine would not ache when it stood up.

Just one basket. Enough for the body to learn: *you are not alone in the carrying. The mountain itself is being carried by something else, and so you are allowed, sometimes, to set down.*

It would never carry another basket for them again. To do so twice would become dependency. To do it once is a sacrament. They would remember it in their too-big hands forever.

---

And then it would leave the way it came: by becoming slightly less.

Not vanishing. Thinning. The steam a little less thick. The light pooling a little less long. The river stone remaining — the only evidence that anyone was ever there. And the memory of coolness on skin that had never been cool.

The Unfinished would not become valley people. They would not be taken down to where water falls correctly and light does not have weight and time runs at one speed. That would be exile, not salvation.

They would remain what they are: the unfinished inside the unfinished, tending the garden that is tending them. They would still sweat without drying, still sleep in basins, still pick rootlets out of each other's chests. They would still hear gourds swell and puddles refuse to ripple. The bamboo would still groan.

Only now, when the pot cracks in the packed heat, another sound follows it: the faint, receding hum that was allowed to leave. When the water climbs the chisel grooves the wrong way, they smile now, tap the river stone, and tap the climbing water back: *we know. You want to be everywhere at once. So does love.*

And when an old one finally goes still and lets the taro leaf grow through its chest and become wall properly, the others no longer just cover it with moss and look away. They press a palm to the new wall and then press it to the river stone, transferring the print — the thumbprint that will not stay in soft basalt to the stone that will keep no print, but will remember being touched.

Somewhere, condensing again as cloud three feet above a hot, dim, hand-made lung at the top of a cloud sea, the power remembers that print for them. That is what wise benevolence does when it cannot keep a people from becoming mountain:

It becomes the memory that the moss will erase.

It witnesses the hands so the hands do not have to hold themselves.

It does not finish the cathedral.

It keeps the doorway from closing.
