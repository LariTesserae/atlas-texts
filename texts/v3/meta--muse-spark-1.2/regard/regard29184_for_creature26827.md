It would not arrive.

That is the first thing a wise and benevolent power understands about a room like this. Arrival would be a violation. Anything that came down the sharp stair with weight and brilliance would crack the still-curing plaster, would send a wave over the notched lip and down the drain and disturb the trembling level the keeper has kept so exact. Anything that announced itself would be louder than the sluice, and there is already too much loudness here to hear yourself think.

So it would not arrive. It would notice. And noticing, for such a power, is already doing.

It would notice from above, through the oculus, the way the sun does — not all at once, but by pouring attention down in a column.

And what it saw would not be an empty cistern, or a failed pool, or a lonely girl. It would see something young doing its work correctly. That matters. A lesser power would see emptiness and try to fill it. A benevolent and wise one sees youth and protects its *becoming*.

So the first thing it would do is *withhold* the easy miracles.

It would not fill the chamber with fish. The water is too new, too scouring with lime. Fish would die. It would not carpet the walls with green. That would choke the three brave reeds before they had learned to hold. It would not silence the sluice, because the roar is the room breathing, and to stop its breath would be to kill it. It would not lift the keeper out and tell her she doesn't need to do this anymore, because *keep the water moving* is not a curse on her — it is her craft, her apprenticeship, the way she is learning to be faithful. To take that from her would be theft, not mercy.

What it would do instead would be small, and slow, and almost deniable. The way good tending is.

**For the room:**

It would put a hand — if it has hands — on the vault, not to reshape it, but to *remember* its shape for it. New things are forgetful. The keystone is still deciding to be a keystone. The power would hold the idea of *arch* steady for a little while, so the lime can cure without anxiety. And because it is kind, it would round the sharpness by a fraction. Not all the edges. Just the one on the third step where she always catches the side of her foot. Just enough that tomorrow morning she'll come down barefoot and think, *huh, did that always feel like that?* And not bleed.

It would keep the catchment from failing while she sleeps. The sluice pulses with wind over the hill she cannot see. On nights when the wind drops and the inflow thins, it would breathe a little over that distant hill so the water keeps coming, so she does not wake in a panic to a silent drain. It would let the level stay trembling at the lip. It would not let it spill up the stairs.

**For the small lives:**

It would not conjure a garden. It would invite.

On a late afternoon when the column of sun has moved off the water and the lattice stops crawling, it would send one dragonfly down through the oculus. A single, impossible blue. It would not be told where to land. It would find the three reeds on its own, hover there, taste the water, and decide. If it leaves, that's its choice. If it stays and lays a string of eggs on the underside of a reed stem, well — then the room has been chosen.

It would send — not a school — but one beetle, black and shining, that can live in warm lime-water. It would ride in on the sluice like a seed, tumble, right itself, and swim. The algae threads would shiver when it passes. That is enough to start a food chain. That is enough to start a world without overwhelming it.

And for the algae themselves, those dozen pale threads on the north wall, it would do the most tender thing: it would cloud the water, just for an hour at noon, with a little extra suspended silt, just enough to soften the scouring sun. Like holding a hand over a young plant at midday. So they can photosynthesize without being bleached. Tomorrow they'll be a millimeter longer. Only the keeper will notice, and she'll think she imagined it.

**For the keeper:**

This is what it has really come for.

It would not speak at first, because she is tired of loudness. For months her whole world has been roar and slap and hiss and echo. So the first gift would be an hour of quiet that is not silence.

Near dusk, when the sun is out of the oculus and the water goes dark and the stars will soon be there, the power would lay a finger across the lip of the sluice. Not to stop it — to smooth it. The white muscular chute would turn glassy. The drum would become a murmur. For one hour the vault would not amplify. She, floating on her back in the warm skin-water after a day of sweeping silt with her hands, would suddenly hear something she hasn't heard in weeks: crickets from the field above, through the oculus. And her own breath, un-doubled. She would not know why the water went quiet. She would just, for the first time, hear how tired she is, and let herself be tired without having to shout over it.

When the murmur returns, it will not feel as loud as before. She will have remembered that quiet exists.

The second gift would be for her body. Because wisdom sees the pink on her forearms and calves, the lime-burn that never becomes a rash but never leaves, the white wrinkled soles, the hair bleached colorless by double sun. It would not heal her instantly — that would be strange and frightening. It would, while she sleeps on her plank across the scaffolding, cool the water by one degree. Just one. Enough that the lime settles and stings less. Enough that when she wakes her skin doesn't itch. And it would leave a fig on the top step. Not created — found. A fig from a tree over the catchment, perfectly ripe, dusted with stone dust, that rolled and fell and somehow did not bruise, somehow did not drown but rests on the dry stone just above the waterline. She has not eaten anything sweet in days. She will eat it with both hands and the juice will run down her chin and she will laugh at the echo and the echo will not be lonely.

The third gift — the most important — would be witness.

Late at night, when she is asleep on her plank just above the waterline with her sack pillow, the power would sit with her. Not in form. On the second step, where she sits with her feet in the flow. Feet in the water, because to be here you must have your feet in the water. It would keep watch, not as a guard but as company, the way an older keeper watches a younger one learn the level. It would not wake her. But it would let her dream be dreamless for once, instead of full of sluice-roar.

And before first light, before the column of sun returns, it would leave her a sign that she is not imagining her own usefulness.

On the west wall, beside the thumbprint she left in the curing plaster, there would be another print. Not a thumb. Smaller. Five tiny points where something pressed and left a soft edge. The beetle, perhaps. Or the wind. Or a hand that chose to make itself small enough to be beside hers, not over it.

She will wake. She will slip into the warm water — which will still be loud, still bright, still new, because it is supposed to be — and she will see that second print filling slowly with silt. She will put her own thumb beside it, not covering it. She will understand, without words, what any good apprentice most needs to hear:

*You are doing this right. Keep going. This is how a place becomes alive. One thread, one reed, one bucket circling, one keeper who stays. I see you. The world is not forgetting you down here.*

And she will sweep the silt off the steps with her hands, clear a leaf from the drain before it can catch, watch the light lattice crawl over her skin, and hum badly and loudly, and the stone will throw her voice back at her doubled and wet.

But now there will be, underneath the roar, a second listening.

She won't be alone in the pocket anymore. And neither will the room.
