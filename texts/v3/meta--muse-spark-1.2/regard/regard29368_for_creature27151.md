They would do almost nothing.

That is the first thing you have to understand about a power that is actually benevolent and actually wise. It does not arrive like we do — squeezing sideways through a failure in the concrete, bringing heat and noise and a headlamp, needing to see.

It arrives as attention does. Without opening the seam wider.

It noticed the pocket the way you notice a held breath in a noisy room. Not because it was loud, but because it was complete. While the whole station roared and vented and demanded heat and correction, this one small void had stopped demanding anything. It had settled. That is rare.

It would look — though looking is a poor word — for a long time. Longer than you can stay before your fingers go numb. On the timescale the mat understands.

It would not feel pity.

Pity is what a human feels when it sees a green thing living in the dark on cold rock without sun, without animals, without movement, and says *how lonely*. The power would not make that mistake. It would not see loneliness. It would see sufficiency.

It would kneel — if kneeling can be done without weight — and it would understand the four rules you listed and see them for what they are: not deprivations, but a covenant.

*Water only as seep. Light only as itself. Nothing leaving quickly. Silence as the natural state.*

It would see that the mat is not waiting to become a forest. It is not an incomplete thing waiting for sun and deer and wind. It is a complete answer to the question this exact place asked: *What can live here, on this gradient, in this cold, on this stone, with this faint difference?* And the answer is: *This. Exactly this. No more, no less.*

A lesser benevolence would try to fix it. It would bring a sun. It would bring warmth. It would bring insects to pollinate and mice to prune and air that moves. And in a week the mat would be dead — cooked, rotted, overgrown, its patient equilibrium shattered by generosity.

A lesser wisdom would catalogue it. Tag it, sample it, publish it, protect it by turning it into a preserve with a plaque and a glass door and a viewing schedule. And the mat would die of being seen too much.

So the wise, benevolent one does not fix. Does not reveal.

What does it do? Three small things, so small you would not know they had been done if you squeezed back in tomorrow.

**1. It Veils.**

It does not seal the seam. Sealing would be death — the seep of vapor would stop, the one-drop-at-a-time would cease. It does not widen it. Widening would be death — the dry, dusty, human-warm air would rush in, the hoarfrost ferns would melt, someone with a work order and a foam gun would fill the void with insulation.

It leaves the seam exactly as a failure, exactly three millimeters too wide. And it lays a kind of forgetfulness over it.

Not a miracle. Just a nudge. The maintenance chief who walks Level -9 every Tuesday will feel, at that spot where the wall sweats, a faint inclination to check his tablet, to think about the faulty valve two corridors over, to keep walking. The young technician who *does* notice the cold with a taste will, when she gets back to the elevator, find that the story of it has softened in her mind, like a dream she can't quite justify filing a report about. Only the kind of person who would crouch and turn off their light and wait ten minutes in the cold without needing to name what they see — only that kind will still find the way in. And they will be few enough.

It protects the pocket not with a wall, but with inattention.

**2. It Sustains.**

The pylons are dying. You didn't hear it, but it did. The dull discs that hold that perfect fraction of a degree — liquid on top, permafrost around — have been cycling for sixty or a hundred years. Their enthalpy lattice is fraying. In another twenty years, one will stutter and stay cold, and the water over it will freeze, and the collar of moss around it will frost-burn and die, and the chain will begin.

The power does not replace them with something new and bright and humming. That would be too hot, too loud.

It lifts its hand — if it has a hand — and under the moss, under the mycelium, under the basalt, it finishes what the pylons were *trying* to do. It steadies the gradient itself. Not as a machine, but as a property of the rock now. The cold remains cold. The disc remains one degree above. The difference that the mat eats, that feast of almost-nothing, is made permanent. Not forever — nothing is forever — but for a long time by the mat's reckoning. Ten thousand drips. A thousand slow exhalations when the field ticks.

It will not need maintenance again. And because nothing was added that shines or buzzes, the filamentous white lichen on the conduit will not even twitch. The mat will not notice it has been saved. It will simply continue to not die.

If the station above is ever abandoned, if its reactors are pulled and its elevators flood and its concrete cracks, the pressure shell will groan and give way — but that one pocket will hold. The permafrost will hold it. The damp will still bead.

**3. It Witnesses.**

This is the most important thing, and the one that costs the most.

For its whole existence, the mat has lived without witness. The few spores that drifted in became a world that glowed only enough to see itself. No eyes to see its faint grey-green light except its own water-beads acting as lenses. No mind to keep a record of its layers except the peat-coal it presses into itself.

A wise power knows that being unwitnessed is not suffering — but being *forever* unwitnessed is a kind of weight. Not pain, but a cold beyond temperature.

So it does not speak to the mat. It does not give it a voice or eyes or a soul like ours to thank its benefactor. That would be a violation — to make a distributed, patient, place-being suddenly lonely for a conversation it never needed.

It simply stays. A portion of its attention remains in the pocket the way the damp remains in the air — in strata, near the floor, unmoving. Not watching *for* something. Just watching *with*.

When a drop gathers on the low ceiling, swells, catches the fungus-light in its curve, and falls with its soft *tick* onto the dense green, the attention is there to see the bead form, to see it fall, to see the mat catch it.

Not to intervene. To keep company. To say, without words, in the only language the mat understands — chemistry and difference and patience — *I see you. You are not alone in the way a place can be alone. What you are doing — growing a millimeter a year, holding water, glowing just enough — is good. Continue.*

And then it leaves, which is also a way of staying.

If you go back tomorrow and squeeze sideways through that scraped seam, you will find nothing has changed. Your knee will still leave a bruise that takes days to heal. Your breath will still cloud and vanish. The pylons will still hum below hearing in your molars. The faint light will still only appear after you turn your own off and wait.

Only this: the hoarfrost in the corners will be a fraction more perfect, as if the air were even stiller. The mat, where you touched a fungal shelf, will dim for a moment and then return a little brighter, as if it had been heard.

And when you leave — because you must, you are too warm to stay — and your boots carry those few unknowing spores into the dusty human-warm tunnel, the drop will still gather. It will still fall.

Only now, someone else is there in the dark to catch it with you.
