It would not come down.

That is the first thing.

A benevolent and wise power, looking at that low country - at the palm-held plain, at the young stone still soft with the sea in it, at the new room the earth inhaled open and the sixty-odd people who fitted themselves inside it so exactly - would understand immediately what it was looking at.

Not a problem to be solved.
Not a people to be saved.
Not a project.

A completion.

It would come close the way the evening air comes close: without weight. It would not part the canopy. It would not stand on the rim and cast a shadow across the oval of sky. It would lie down around the sinkhole the way water lies in limestone - everywhere and nowhere in particular - and listen.

And what it would hear would tell it what to do, which is almost nothing as men count doing.

It would hear the *peep* ... *peep* ... *peep* of the frogs, sparse as the houses. The tick of condensation finding a leaf. The soft shift of a gecko. The woman sweeping not to clean but to read. The man moving wall-drip with a gourd, drop by drop, refusing to spill. The hairline tick of a new crack opening in a white block. The small fires at dusk, each tended, none boasting.

It would hear a place that has learned the art that every wise power has to learn: how to be abundant without being crowded. How to leave emptiness on purpose. How to build as a vine builds - hold, don't pierce.

So it would be very careful not to ruin that with a miracle.

Here is what it would do. If you could call it doing.

**1. It would help them stay hidden.**

Not with a wall, not with a light. It would do it the way the forest already does, only a little more so.

It would lean on the attention of the world above. The survey plane that passes over would find its camera fogged just there by the same heat-haze that always softened the horizon. The logger looking for straight mahogany would feel, without knowing why, that this patch of low forest was not worth the drag. The satellite map would stitch itself with a perfect canopy where the sink's ragged oval should be, and no analyst would think to look twice. A hunter chasing peccary would step within ten feet of the lip, where the hanging roots curtain the fall, and his eye would slide right over it, and he would think *nothing there* and turn back.

Not blindness. Not a curse. Just a deepening of what the town already had: the strange good luck of being forgettable to those who only want what can be taken quickly. The power would make the forgetting a little thicker. The way moss thickens on the north side of every block. Quietly. Without anyone needing to believe in it.

From the rim, even more than before, you would walk past and think the forest floor was unbroken.

That is a terrible kind of blessing and the only kind a wise power trusts.

**2. It would steady the stone, but not stop it.**

This is young limestone. It hasn't learned to be stone. It is still full of unopened sea-pockets, still pitted and porous, still giving way all at once where water finds a new seam. The people know this. They build on stilts of unshaped sapwood. They lift a house half an inch with poles and slip another sliver underneath when the floor settles. They do not ask the earth to be finished.

A foolish power would pour concrete. Would say *I will fix this for you, make it solid, safe, permanent.* And in doing so would kill the exact intelligence that lets them live there.

A wise power would do what the fig does.

It would send its attention down into the weeping walls and coax the water a little. Not to dam it, but to slow it. To teach it to bead a fraction slower, to find the old seams instead of cutting new ones so quickly. In the night, in the deepest crack, it would leave a faint bloom of calcite - not a seal, a stitch. It would encourage the mycorrhizae to knit a little faster through the fallen blocks, so the bright white faces gray just a season sooner, and the edges lose their sharpness. It would let the sink go on becoming, because becoming is its nature, but it would ask it - gently, as you would ask a young animal not to grow so fast it hurts itself - to become a little more slowly.

So that a house might stay level for five years instead of one. So that the largest pool might hold its tadpoles for three days instead of two. So that a child born on a sleeping platform under the overhang might grow and have her own child on that same platform before it needs to be moved.

No one in the town would call this a miracle. The old man mending thatch would simply notice one morning that the vine he tied last season had taken hold a little stronger than expected. The girls splitting the hog plum would find the stone flake still sharp. They would say, *good year for stone.*

That is how you know it was benevolent.

**3. It would not give them anything they did not already have. It would make sure they get to keep it.**

It would not bring metal. It would not bring glass. It would not bring words for *north*.

If it had a voice, and if they ever thought to ask - which they might not, for a long time, because people who live within do not call upward often - it would answer like this:

> *You are well fitted. May you keep the size you have chosen.*

And its gift would be continuity.

It would keep the aquifer sweet beneath the pitted limestone, even in a dry year when the afternoon rains forget to come every few days. The wall-drip would still bead, even then, enough for the hollowed-block cistern. Just enough.

It would keep the bats returning to fold like dry leaves under the overhang, and the swifts stitching the oval of sky at dusk, and the blind white fish turning in the deepest pool, because the town has made room for them and a town that makes room should not be punished for it.

And most of all it would remember them.

That is the quietest work of a wise power and the most real: to hold a place in mind without needing to look at it. To be the one who knows the census without counting. Sixty, eighty with the infants and the very old. Brown as wet bark, barefoot, thick-soled, beaded with sweat that never quite dries. Compact because their ceiling is low. Speaking quietly because a voice can cross the whole pocket without shouting.

To be the memory that outlasts the sink itself. Because one day - not soon, but one day - the limestone will learn at last how to be stone, or it will give way all at once again and the pocket will close or double in size, and the town will have to shift a wall, or shift itself, or become something else. The power knows this. It does not promise them forever in that room. It promises them that whatever small, fitted, sparing way they find to live next will also be seen and left alone to be itself.

If you went down there a year after the power had come and gone - if it ever came at all, if it was ever not there - you would notice nothing.

The light would still fall in a column for two hours at midday and make dust hang. The causeway stones would still be bright, still unsmoothed by feet. The sow would still move under the houses with her piglets. A new papaya would have come up where an old house had left a gap on purpose, and no one would have cut it. At evening the bats would still go up like smoke and blot the stars for a few seconds. The frogs would still begin - *peep* ... *peep* ... *peep* - individual notes spaced far apart, sparse as the houses.

Only, if you stayed very still on the causeway, you might feel that the air in the sink - warm, dim, smelling of wet stone and smoke and ripe fruit - was holding you a little more evenly. That your skin stayed damp without discomfort. That the tick of the crack opening in the white block, which you had braced for, didn't come.

And a child, watching the blind fish turn, might look up at the weeping wall and, without knowing why, smile.

Not because she had been saved.

Because she had been noticed, and not taken.

From above, the forest would still close over the lip with hanging roots, the thatch still the exact color of leaf litter, the stone still looking like stone. An ordinary place. Not enchanted. Simply a new room that the low, warm, damp earth opened, and that vines and frogs and people, with nothing but hands and growing things, have made into a town that fits exactly inside it.

And around it, like condensation, like cool breath falling down the walls at dusk, something without a name would keep being there, not to rule, not to improve, but to keep the forgetting thick and the becoming slow, so that what is small may stay small as long as it wishes to.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do, if it loved them.

Which is the same as saying: it would do almost nothing you could photograph, and everything you could feel if you learned, as they have, how to live within.
