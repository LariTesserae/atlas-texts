It would come slowly.

That is the first thing to understand about a benevolent and wise power. It would not arrive.

Arrival is for meteors and armies and revelations. A wise power does not arrive. It notices, and then it approaches at the speed the place can be approached without breaking it.

So it would come up the long, weathered approach the way you did — not because it has to walk, but because to do otherwise would be rude. It would cool as the air cooled. It would thin itself a little as the air thinned. By the time it reached the rim it would have learned the mountain's pace: round-shouldered, lichen-slow, settled into itself.

And it would stop at the rim.

And look.

For a long time.

A lesser power — a merely kind one, or a merely clever one — would have something to do immediately. Fix the wall. Heal the sick. Lift the people out of the cold water into warm air where breathing is easier. Give them fire that burns underwater, give them metal, give them words for what they are becoming. Make them *more.*

A wise and benevolent power would see first what is already there and understand that it is not a problem to be solved.

It would see a sea that learned to stay on a mountain because plants agreed to hold it up. It would see plants that learned to live drowned because water agreed to be breathable. It would see people who learned to grow their houses instead of building them, to photosynthesize a little when they are sick, to move slowly so they don't go cold, to leave emptiness on purpose between dwellings so light can get through.

It would see a whole system that has solved, over generations, the exact problem it was given, with exactly the tools it was given — stone adzes and bone knives and cordage and time — and it would feel something much rarer than pity or admiration.

It would feel *relief.*

Oh. *You figured it out,* it would think. Not perfect. Not forever. But *whole.*

And so the first thing it would do is nothing that looks like doing.

It would not part the water wall to show itself. To do so would teach the wall that it can be parted. It would not speak in a voice that carries like their stone hammers, because a voice that loud would stay for seconds, bouncing between leaves, and change every song they sing to their plants.

It would make itself small and cool and quiet enough to slip through the wall the way the mountain birds do — as if the wall were air — and hang there in the blue dusk between four great trunks, inverted, feet hooked over a runner, cape of living moss billowing even though it has no cape.

It would breathe water the second way, with gill-ruffs it doesn't need, just to be polite.

And it would listen to the loud, slow creaking.

My heart, if I am honest, wants it to do more. My heart wants it to lay hands on every mottled shoulder and make the symbiont take a little better, to make winter a little shorter, to promise the old woman with the green cloud of hair that she will not have to be wedged into the crotch of a young column when she dies if she doesn't want to be.

My intuition says: don't you dare.

My reasoning says: both are true, so find the third option.

Here is what the wise power would actually do, then — the almost-nothing that is not nothing:

**1. It would veil, not reveal.**

The greatest danger to a hanging sea on high ground is not that the wall will fail from within. The people tend the ring. They know how. The danger is that someone down the mountain with a different idea of what a sea is for will notice the light on top that looks like cloud and come up to own it.

So the power would do the kindest and most boring miracle imaginable: it would make the path *less* interesting.

Not invisible. Invisibility is a challenge. It would make the long approach exactly as long and round-shouldered and forgettable as it wants to be. The basalt and limestone would seem a little more ordinary to the surveyor. The groove where feet and water have agreed to go the same way would fill in a half-inch with scree the week before a road crew comes to map it. Clouds would settle a little longer on the summit on the days satellites pass. The geologist who would have loved this caldera will instead love a different one, two valleys over, and publish his paper there and be happy.

It would not hide them because they are shameful. It would hide them because they are precious and small enough to hold in your eye at once. Small precious things need a bowl of quiet around them.

No one below would notice anything had been done. That is how you know it was done well.

**2. It would tend, not fix.**

Inside, it would not cure. Curing would break the second rule: nothing dead is wasted. Their dead become bridge and reef and nursery because death is part of the plumbing. To take death away would be to clog the system with everlasting houses that never become reefs.

Instead it would tend the way they tend.

It would hum — that low, resonant hum through water-modulated gill-frills — at a frequency just below their hearing, the frequency that makes fine root-hairs shiver and reach. The outer ring of crown-leaves, tired after a hard winter, would transpire a little more evenly that spring. A bladder-pod that was going to implode badly and take a child's finger with it would instead imploding gently. The fungi that soften stone and wood would be given a little more patience, so a dead stem hollows cleanly instead of rotting sour.

If you were one of the people, you would not feel blessed. You would just feel that this year, the wall held without shivering once, and the leaf-stitching took on the first try, and the carp school was generous.

Only the oldest woman might notice, hanging with her back pressed to a sunward trunk to photosynthesize on a sick day, that the light column that day was a little warmer for a little longer than it should have been at that depth. She would half-close her large eyes and not need to know why.

**3. It would offer, not command.**

This is the wisest part, and the most restrained.

A benevolent power knows that attention by itself changes the thing being attended to. Even hanging quietly between four trunks, it is already an intervention — a new constellation in their scattered lanterns.

So before it leaves, it would leave a choice that is not a demand. Not a voice, not a tablet, not a burning leaf. A current.

In the clearing where they gather at blue dusk — the eddy between four trunks where warmness holds a fraction longer — it would leave a slow, cool drift that wasn't there before. Any child drifting through it would feel, for just a second, not words but a *sense*: 

*I see you. You are beautiful as you are. You may continue. If ever the mountain itself tires — if the stone forgets how to hold, or the water forgets how to breathe — you may call, not with knocking, but by tending the way you already tend, and I will answer in kind. If you ever wish to leave this sea and try air again for good, the wall will let you and not break. If you ever wish to stay and become even less human and more pith, that too will be held. You don't have to decide now. There is time. You measure time in handbreadths per year. So will I.*

And then it would go. Or rather, it would thin until it was the same cool drift as any other, the same creak and groan of the great plants swaying against their moorings.

It would not stay to be thanked. Thanks in that water would be loud and would stay for seconds.

It would not stay to be worshipped. Worship would require them to pack together for warmth, to build something tall and dense in the middle of their deliberate emptiness. That would kill them faster than any wall failure.

It would simply have been there, and then have continued to be there the way mountains continue to be there — round-shouldered, settled into itself, softened by lichen.

Years later, a young pair coaxing a shoot to arch into a dome for eight years will find the runner takes a half-inch to the left more willingly than it should, as if an extra hand held it. They will tie it, wait for the current to show if it holds, and find that it does.

They will not know why. They will make room anyway, sparsely and deliberately, with a silence left empty on purpose.

And that will be enough.
