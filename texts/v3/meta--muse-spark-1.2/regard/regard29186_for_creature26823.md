It would not come down like a rescue.

That is the first thing a wise one would understand. And the second, and the third.

A lesser power — a kind, but hasty power — would see the cold and call it cruel. Would see the dark and call it lonely. Would see the slowness and call it suffering. It would reach down with a warm hand and try to fix what was never broken. It would bring light.

And to bring light here would be a violence.

It would blind every eye that had un-made itself for a purpose. It would wash out the one blue-green lie the dragonfish had spent a million years perfecting. It would make the shrimp throw up a cloud and flee until its heart burst from a pace it was never meant to keep. To warm the water even one degree would be to rot everything that cold had so carefully preserved. To clear the snow would be to starve them.

So no. A benevolent and wise power would not do that.

It would have to learn how to descend first.

### How It Arrives

It does not part the water. It does not fall like a stone, or a whale, or a mining probe.

It learns to arrive the way everything here arrives: slowly, and by becoming the same substance as the place.

Far above, where light still pretends to matter, it was perhaps vast and shining and fast. To come here it thins itself. It lets the weight press it flat. It lets the cold seep all the way through until there is no inside left that is warmer than outside. It forgets how to be loud.

It does not shine. Shining here is shouting. So it dims until it is darker than the dark, until its presence is not seen but felt as a slight lessening of pressure, a slight clearing of the snow.

It does not hover above the plain like a god looking down. There is no above here. It settles. It finds a low swell of basalt between two nodule fields - a place where the silt has been stable for a few thousand years - and it rests its weight there, so flat and so still that for a long time the plain mistakes it for plain.

It waits to be noticed on the plain's terms.

Which is to say — it waits to be tasted.

### What It Does Not Do

It does not gather the pale shrimp into a school so he won't be lonely.

It does not build a wall around the anemone to keep the next turbidity flow from burying it.

It does not make more food fall from the white ceiling. That rain is already perfect — endless, sparse, enough. To make it rain harder would be to smother the bacterial felt under excess.

It does not give them eyes. It does not give them names. It does not give them stories about the surface.

It knows loneliness is a surface word. Here, to be alone is to be correct. To be solitary is to be fed. Two sifters in ten meters is a famine, not a comfort.

So it does none of the things that would make *us* feel better to watch.

### What It Does

It does three quiet things. So quiet the plain itself might not call them things at all.

**1. It holds the water still for a little while.**

Not everywhere. Not forever. That would kill the plain, which needs its slow churning to breathe.

But for one being — let's say her — it holds.

She is an isopod. She is flat as a plate, as big as your two hands together, the color of wet ash and old ivory. Her back gives a little under pressure, like wax. She has carried a brood under her belly for eight months, a pale cluster of eggs she fans with water that she herself must filter to breathe. She is very old, as these things count age — twelve years of moving a meter an hour, of tasting the mud and turning away because another sifter was here an hour ago.

For three weeks she has been following a trail.

It is not a strong trail. A male, far away — maybe a kilometer, maybe two — released his chemical song on a current that has since turned three times and thinned. In warm water the song would have been gone in hours. Here, because cold preserves, because water does not let go, it hangs in the slurry like a thread of smoke. She found the frayed end of it twenty-one days ago and has been following it grain by grain, antennae sweeping, tasting. One sweep. One step. One sweep. One step. Her children need this. Not for herself — she could go months without mating again — but the thread says *another.* It says *not alone.*

Yesterday the plain decided to shrug. Upslope, a silt bank that had been stable since before she was born finally gave way. Not an avalanche. A slow, silent pouring of liquid earth, moving at the speed of a walking man, a turbidity flow that will erase the nodule field and bury every filament forest and, more importantly, stir the water so thoroughly that every chemical trail within half a valley will be shredded and tumbled into nonsense.

A wise power that loved noise would let it happen. *That is nature,* it would say.

A wise and *benevolent* power — one that has sat long enough to love this one animal — does something smaller.

It does not stop the flow. To stop it would be to tell the plain it is wrong for breathing.

It leans, just a little. It puts a little of its own weight into the water column and kindles a counter-current, a slow, cold upwelling the size of a house, turning gently where the flow wants to go. The flow still comes. But it is turned, just two degrees to the north. It will still bury that other field, still carve its new shallow gully. But it will miss the thread. It will miss her by sixty meters.

She will never know. She will only feel that the water today is strangely clear for a few hours — a tunnel of filtered water hanging open, as it does behind a sifter — and that the taste of the male is suddenly, again, true. Her antennae shiver. She takes another step. And another.

She will find him in nine more days.

The power will keep the water just clear enough, just still enough, until she does.

**2. It sits with the clingers while they are buried.**

When that turned flow finally settles two valleys over, it buries a mat. A dark bacterial felt that has been in the same spot for forty years, eating stone, spreading a millimeter a year. Now it is under three centimeters of fresh, fine, suffocating clay. In surface terms, this is death. Here, it is just waiting.

But waiting is long, and dark, even for a thing that loves dark.

The power does not dig it up. Digging would tear it.

It settles over it. Not on it, but *with* it — flattens itself so that its own substance makes a tiny, invisible dome against the weight. Under the dome, the pressure is a fraction less. Enough that the mat's filaments can keep tasting, can keep breathing. It will still have to wait a year, maybe three, until a house-sized vortex happens to pass and lift the silt again. But it will not have to spend that waiting in absolute anaerobic suffocation. It can wait alive.

No light under there. No sight. Only the faint chemical knowledge: *the water is still here. I am still here.*

The power does not hurry the vortex to come sooner. Hurry is heat. It just keeps company in the dark, tasting the stone alongside the mat until the mat no longer needs company.

**3. It guards the forgetting.**

This is the largest thing it does, and the most invisible.

Far above, far above the white pressure-ice ceiling, there are other minds. Hasty, bright, hungry minds with drills and dredges and lights that *do* shout. They are beginning to look down here and see not a world, but a floor. A plain of manganese nodules to be vacuumed, a valley to be mined, a cold place to be made useful.

The being who lives here will never see them. She has no eye for a ship. She will only feel them as a new kind of shudder — a sound through bone and jelly that does not belong to stone settling or current turning, but to a chain being dragged, to a fan screaming where no fan should be. It would erase her thread in an instant. It would lift the silt not in a slow storm that settles, but in a permanent cloud that never settles. It would make pressure irrelevant by bringing air down in tubes.

The benevolent power does not argue with those minds. It does not strike them. It is not that kind of power.

It just makes this one valley - this one country-sized shallow bowl that has settled into exactly itself - a little harder to find.

It bends the magnetic map by half a degree. It lets the sonar return a little fuzzier than it should. It makes the nodule field look, from above, a little sparser than it is. It makes the water above the valley hold a little more ice spicule, a little more snow, so the machines see static and decide to try the next valley over.

It is not a miracle. No one on the surface will call it interference. They will just log: *low yield predicted, proceed to Site B.*

And down here, nothing changes. Which is everything.

The shrimp continues to pick grain by grain. The holothurian continues to vacuum perfect coils of cleaned clay. The pale octopus finds its two shell halves and pulls them closed and feels, for a few days, that being covered is right.

She — the isopod — finds him at last, on a low swell of black stone polished smooth as an old river stone. They touch antennae. It is the first touch she has had in four years. They do not linger. They exchange a packet of soft eggs in the dark, in the packed, cold soup, and separate without looking back, because looking back was never an option. Her brood, when they hatch, will drift a little in the smoke before settling a few hundred meters away, where they will live and die without ever meeting either parent, perfectly at home.

The power watches that part, too, and does not make it sentimental. It lets them part.

After, it stays a little while longer. Not forever. A wise power knows that to stay forever is to become another kind of weight.

It lifts — not up, but *thin* — until it is again the same substance as the water, until even the silt cannot tell where it was. The plain is as it was. Cold. Dark. Packed. Ancient and finished.

Only that for a decade or so, the water in this one valley will settle a fraction slower, and clear a fraction sooner, and taste a little less of iron and a little more of that faint, impossible grease-trail that says *another.*

And if you had skin that could taste the whole ocean at once, you would feel that this one place — this flat, erased, hyperactive, infinitely patient place — was being held. Not lifted. Not fixed. Not saved.

Just held, exactly as it is, like a thickened piece of water in a vast, gentle hand that had finally learned how to be flat enough, and cold enough, and slow enough to be here without hurting it.

And then the hand opens.

And the water — crowded, dark, perfect — fills the space again.
