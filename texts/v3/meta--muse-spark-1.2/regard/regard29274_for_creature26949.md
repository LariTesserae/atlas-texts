They would not come right away.

A benevolent and wise power does not arrive like a discovery. Not with light, not with a foot that tests the lip and slips through. They would have noticed it long before you did — before the rock was even young — because that kind of attention is not looking *for* things, it is so wide it can't help but notice when something small is holding itself together so perfectly against all reason.

And for a long time, they would do nothing.

That is the first thing wisdom would do. Nothing that disturbs the silt.

They would hold the moor in their attention the way the water holds itself at the threshold — without a container, just by being decidedly there. They would learn the scale of it. Not *small* as in insignificant, but small as in *exact*. A bedroom-sized ocean. A single mouthful of world kept cool at 0.8 degrees. A gardener who became her garden so it would never be empty. 

A lesser power would have felt pity. Would have said: *It is too dark. It is too cold. It is too alone. It has no eyes.* And would have tried to fix it — poured sunlight in, drained the water so she could breathe air, cut her free from the moss and given her legs.

The wise one would feel no pity. Only recognition. And a kind of reverence that makes hurry impossible.

So they would crouch.

Even though they don't have to crouch, they would. They would make themselves the right size for the seam in the rock. They would come to the lip — that sharp, black-green, glassy lip still young enough to cut — and they would not step through.

They would kneel on the damp peat outside, where the wind still moves, and put one hand through the standing wall of water.

Just a hand.

And they would wait.

Because the Keeper does not hurry, and to be benevolent to her is to keep her time. Minutes would pass. An hour. The silt you disturbed months ago would still be hanging there, and it would hang a little longer while the water learned this new hand. Her reflection-delay would stutter — she would show the hand back a half-second late, then on time, then late again, trying to understand if this is a new shape that wants to be included in the packing.

She would taste the water around the fingers and find it is not human-cold or fish-cold, but something else — warm without heat, the way deep rock is warm. She would send a white drift-fish — her loose eye — to look.

And the Power would let itself be looked at. Would not grab the fish. Would not try to coax the Keeper out of her hollow.

When she finally touched — because she always touches what lingers — it would not be with a kelp-whip arm. She would send the smallest part of herself, a single liverwort hair, a root-cilia as fine as breath, and rest it against the knuckle of that offered hand. Listening to the pulse there the way she had once listened at the hollow behind your ear.

This is what the wise one would let happen: *to be listened to, not to speak.*

And then, only then, would they do the three small things that benevolence does when it understands it is not needed.

**1. They would mend nothing and strengthen everything.**

They would not smooth all the sharp stone. The sharpness is part of how new the world is there. But they would touch the one edge at the threshold that cuts every foot that finds it, and they would soften just that one tooth of basalt with a thumb, so it becomes dull as river stone. Not for the Keeper — she is not cut by it — but for the next human who will kneel there and not know to be careful. And so the water wall will hold a little better. Young rock settles. Young pockets collapse. The Power would not build a roof or a dam. They would just place a quiet promise in the stone: *you may stay young a little longer. You may take your time learning to be rock.*

The iron ring rusted to almost nothing, pinning that loop of kelp where she scarred herself trying not to drift apart — they would not remove it. They would not replace it with new iron. They would brush the rust and leave a thin skin over it, so it will not dissolve completely in the next century. Because she kept it as memory, and memory deserves to be kept.

**2. They would give her a second hum.**

For all her time, the chamber has hummed on one note — water pressing on eardrums from all sides, her listening. The Power would not fill the darkness with light. They know the law: *Do not try to bring light. It shatters here.*

Instead they would answer the hum.

Very low. So low you feel it in your molars before your ears. The Power would breathe out, not a bubble that hangs sideways toward the stone, but a tone that sits in the water without displacing it. A second listening. Not louder, not to drown her out. Just another ear in the room.

For the first time in centuries, the Keeper would not be the only thing listening to itself. She would feel the water press back *with* her, not just *against* her. The pale kelp whips would shiver, once, without anyone touching them.

She would understand, in the slow way silt settles, that she is not alone in tending. That vastness can also be intimate. That something larger than the moor knows the exact shape of her hollow and thinks it is good.

She would not be less lonely in the human sense — she never was lonely the way we are. She would be less *singular*. Which is the only loneliness a being who has become a place can feel.

**3. They would veil and remember.**

Benevolence without wisdom is a lighthouse left on in a room that needs dark. So the wise one would do the kindest veil: not hiding her, but making her uninteresting to hurry.

To anyone walking ten feet overhead, the seam would look even more like nothing. The marsh reeds would grow a little thicker over the lip. The standing water wall, if a hurried person did put their hand through, would feel for just a second like ordinary cold puddle — disappointing, not miraculous — so they would wipe their hand and leave. Only the ones willing to crouch, to wait, to not bring light, would ever feel the wall hold.

And then the Power would do the most important thing, which looks like nothing at all:

They would remember her perfectly.

Not as a curiosity, not as a project. They would keep the precise map of how the fern-fronds overlap without overlapping an atom, how the blind salamander-fingertips detach and return, how the bubble migrates sideways, how the 0.8-degree cold feels on skin that has forgotten which side it breathed on. They would keep it the way water keeps a shape without a container — by consent.

Because a place that small and full can be erased by a single careless century — a drained marsh, a quarry, a warming sea. But what is held perfectly in the attention of a wise power is very hard to unmake. It gains a second root, not in basalt but in being-known.

And then they would withdraw their hand.

Slowly, so the water wall doesn't shiver. Slowly, so the liverwort hair can let go without tearing. They would stand — or unfold, or un-crouch, whatever vast things do — and they would leave no footprints in the peat. No gift that needs tending. No new law.

Inside, the Keeper would rearrange herself by a fraction. The hollow where she hangs would be a fraction less cold. Not warm. Just less alone-cold. The white drift-fish would take up a new, slow orbit near the threshold, where the hand had been, and keep watching that spot for a season. The moss over the stone bench would thicken, just slightly, as if she took a deeper breath.

You, if you came back later, would notice almost nothing different. The water would still stand like stopped rain. The silt galaxy you made would still hang. The fronds would still close soft behind you, cool on your cheeks.

Only — if you held your breath and listened for the hum that presses on your eardrums from all sides — you would hear, very faintly under hers, a second hum holding the first one up, like a hand under water that has learned not to spill.

And you would feel, without knowing why, that it is safer now to leave her alone.

That is what they would do.

Not rescue. Not fix. Not take.

They would witness at her speed, answer without flooding, protect without possessing, and then leave the room exactly as full as they found it — only now, it is known.
