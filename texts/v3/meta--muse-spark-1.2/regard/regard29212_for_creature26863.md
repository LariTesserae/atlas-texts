It would not come down like light.

Not like a rescue. Not like a revelation.

A benevolent and wise power, if it noticed this place — this new blister of a ridge with its one tight seam and its twelve-meter lung full of hammering darkness — would first do what you did. It would pause. It would kneel.

It would have to make itself small enough to notice.

Because from far away there is nothing to see. From orbit, from the high cold, this ridge is just another wrinkle of basalt. From the perspective of powers that count forests and oceans and civilizations, it doesn't register at all. A wise power wouldn't be looking for big things. It would be listening for small, complete ones.

And it would hear this one. Not because it is loud — though inside it is deafening — but because it is so utterly *filled in*. No bare rock. No unused air. A bargain kept perfectly: heat given, heat used, stone sweated and re-laid as floor, breath held and water made inside bodies so that no water is ever asked for. Ten thousand lives tap-tap-tapping with no queen and no plan and no complaint.

The first thing wisdom does is understand what would kill what it loves.

So it would understand very quickly that almost every gift we think of as a gift would be poison here.

Water would kill them. Light would kill them. Opening the seam wider to give them air would kill them in an afternoon — the cold would stiffen the wire legs, the rain would dissolve the salt cakes they have spent generations pressing, mold would take the gloss the crickets lay down so carefully. Enlarging the chamber would cool the floor. Cooling the floor would starve the bloom. Even the kindness of bringing them food — leaves, crumbs, honey — would rot in the dry heat and bury them.

Even the kindness of being *seen* too much would kill them. If you marked the seam with a cairn, if you told others, if you brought a class of students with headlamps to marvel, the cold you let in with each entry, the breath, the skin oil, the boot scrape, would be a catastrophe at their scale.

A benevolent power knows the most common way good intentions end worlds is by trying to make them greener.

So it would not improve them.

It would not give the beetles eyes, or the crickets wings, or the colony a mind. It would not make them efficient. They are already efficient at the tempo the mountain allows — geological time, not human time. It would not teach them to sing or to speak. Their hammering *is* their song, and it fills the pocket exactly.

It would not take one as a specimen. To take one would be to take a tile from a roof that has no spare tiles.

What would it do, then? If anything?

Very little that you could photograph. Almost nothing that the beetles could perceive. The beetles do not have a concept for being watched over and do not need one. That is part of their perfection.

I think it would do three things, all of them so small they look like doing nothing.

**1. It would keep the seam as it is.**

Not seal it. Not open it. The seam needs to stay exactly that narrow, that shy, that vertical split where cooling rock pulled apart — wide enough to let heat leak slowly and to let a little trace gas exchange, narrow enough that the wind never finds its way in. A wise power would crouch at that crack the way you did, sideways, and with something like a fingertip, it would brush the very lip of it. Not to close it. To *remember* it.

To make the basalt there a little less crumbly. To make the entrance slightly forgettable.

To anyone walking the ridge afterwards, the eye would slide over it. The wind would not whistle through it in a way that invites curiosity. A human would feel, without knowing why, that there is nothing there, no shelter, no cave worth entering, and would walk on. Not a wall, not a ward — just a slight inclination of attention away. The best lock for a place like this is not a door. It's being uninteresting to the wrong kind of hunger.

**2. It would keep the heat steady a little longer.**

Not forever. Wisdom does not freeze things. This ridge is embryonic. It was shoved up fast and it will cool, as all new mountains do. One day — in a thousand years, in ten thousand — the floor will go dark, the bloom will stop sweating out, the tamping will slow and then stop, and the chamber will be a husk of black pellets with no one left to tap them. That is not a tragedy. That is how this kind of life ends: complete, and then quiet.

But it doesn't need to end early. A benevolent power, with its hand resting lightly on the deep hot body below — that sullen red-black glow under the thinnest part of the floor — would not stoke it hotter. Hotter would crack the rock and burn the gloss. It would just *insulate* it, very gently. For a while. So that the leak stays slow and even. So that winter on the ridge, which is vicious and thin, doesn't steal an extra degree through the stone. So that the beetles get their full allotment of seasons to lay down their millimeters.

It would be the opposite of a miracle. It would be a lengthening. A quiet *continue* breathed into rock.

You wouldn't see it. You'd only notice, if you came back in fifty years with a thermometer, that the chamber is still 38 degrees when by all rights it should be 36.

**3. It would stay and listen for a while.**

This is the part that is not useful at all, which is why it matters.

A wise power has everywhere to be. To choose to be in a hot pocket full of blind industry that will never pray, never thank, never know it was attended — that is benevolence without economy. It would settle its attention down into the pocket the way heat settles, and just be there. Not doing. Not fixing. Witnessing the metronome.

It would learn the rhythm of the hammers until its own vast, slow breathing matched theirs — that ten-second bellows pulse under the enameled backs. It would feel, as you felt in your molars, the pressure of that contained clatter, and it would not call it noise. It would call it what it is: a factory with no owner, a prayer with no words for *we are here and we are using what is given exactly as it should be used.*

And because it is wise, it would not be sad that no one else will ever see it. Some things are not meant to be shared. Their value is not increased by being known. Their holiness — if we can use that word for a room where everything eats stone and makes its own water — is in being complete without witness.

And then it would leave the way you left: by backing out, sideways through the seam, letting the cold hit like water again.

From outside, the ridge would look exactly the same. Barren. Silent. Empty.

Only — if you knelt again and slipped through, you would still find every inch of wall covered. The floor a millimeter thicker. The tapping going on, patient as ever.

And you, if you were the human walking away, might feel for no reason you could name that the wind on this ridge was a little kinder, a little less likely to find the crack. That the stone under your boots felt, for a few steps, oddly warm through your soles, as if the mountain had been holding its breath and had now been allowed to exhale slowly.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do, I think.

Not a rescue. Not an apotheosis.

A guarding of conditions. A prolonging of permission. A single, invisible, affectionate hand laid very lightly over a mouth that should stay mostly shut, so that for a little longer, in the dark, the small irreplaceable industry can keep hammering stone into world.
