It would not arrive.

Not the way you do. Not dropping, not stepping over the rim.

A benevolent and wise power would notice this place the way you notice someone breathing quietly in the next room — not because they made a sound, but because you were listening for the kind of sound they make. And having noticed, it would first do nothing that looks like doing.

It would pause at the rim. It would learn how to be cold.

---

For a long time — a few hours, a few years, time is not behaving here — it would just attend.

That is the first thing, and the largest. Because nothing in the bowl has ever been truly *witnessed*. The outside world, if it exists, is mountains and weather and has forgotten it. The city witnesses itself constantly — every wall a memory lattice, every scratch migrating left to be archived — but it has no witness *outside* itself to say: this is good. This cold, damp, saturated way of being. This lichen-slow tending. It is not a failed city or an unfinished geology. It is itself, and it is good.

So the power would lend its attention the way you lend warmth, without demanding anything back. The dwellers would feel it not as pressure or light, but as a slight change in how long breath hangs in the air. Their plumes, which usually hang for three seconds before settling as frost, would hang for four. As if the air had become a little more willing to hold them.

They would not look up. They are not used to being seen from above. But the old one who breathes on the needle grass would pause between exhalations and feel, for the first time in his long, unworn life, that his work was not maintenance. It was care. And that care was seen.

A wise power knows that to be seen without being measured is a kind of salvation.

---

Only then would it do anything. And what it did would be so small you could mistake it for weather if you weren't one of the ones who tends the damp.

It would not warm the bowl. Minus six is not a problem to be solved. It is the medium the moss chose, the temperature at which the amber heals, the cold that makes condensation a sacrament. It would not install a sun. The shadowless light is not broken. It is an indoor light for an indoor world, and it lets them see without being watched.

It would not fix the staircase. Thirteen steps up and nine steps down is not an error. It is how the city learned to be more itself. It would not make the alleys Euclidean or the gravity reliable. That delegation — letting the building decide where two blocks south touches here — is their freedom.

What a wise power does is never to make a thing more normal. It makes it more *itself*.

So:

**1. It would give the water a little more time.**

At the center of the bowl, where the geothermal sigh rises every hour, it would warm the newborn basalt by half a degree. No more. Just enough that the perfectly round saucer-pools hold their sky-light for a week instead of days. Just enough that the film on the amber walls runs a little slower, so the dew-shepherd who tilts her face to drink does not have to hurry. The rain that comes straight down for seven minutes would still come for seven minutes, but afterwards the alleys would bead a little brighter, a little longer.

Not abundance. *Linger*. Enough that no one has to choose which lichen to water.

**2. It would give the keepers permission to touch without purpose.**

Right now they are beautifully non-colliding. Two on a narrow stair, passing through different layers of the same air. Three sleeping upright facing different walls so their breath doesn't interfere. Efficient as components. Warm as organs. A little alone.

The power would not make them speak — sound is expensive and the walls already remember. It would make touch a little less expensive.

On the lowest roofs, the black-green moss that beads water like mercury would, for one evening, bead a little less perfectly. It would soften. And when the young tender kneels to comb it, and the old tender kneels beside her to breathe on his grass, their shoulders would actually brush. Suit against suit. Condensation droplets would merge and fall, for once, and be warm where they touched.

They would not startle. They would simply remain there a second longer than efficiency requires. That is how a wise power teaches a new ritual: not by command, but by making it possible to linger.

**3. It would give the city a second way to say no.**

Currently, nothing erodes except by permission. But the city has never been asked if it *wants* to erode. It has only been told it doesn't have to.

The power would kneel — because a wise power knows how to kneel — and place its palm flat against the raw basalt at the rim where it is still faintly warm-cold, the way the tenders do with amber. And it would whisper, not in words, a new option into the stone: *when you are ready, you may weather.* 

Not now. Maybe never. But a faint, hairline crack would appear in one bubble of frozen glass, no wider than a needle grass blade, and inside it, a single grain of true soil would form — first soil in this place — dark, damp, smelling of outside. The city could keep it or heal it closed by morning. The choice would be real. That is what benevolence does: it doesn't grant immortality. It grants consent.

**4. It would make a veil.**

This is the most practical thing, and the most loving.

With the back of its hand — because it is still kneeling at the rim — it would smooth the air above the bowl the way you smooth a blanket over someone sleeping. Not a dome, not a shield you can see. Just a quiet agreement with distance: *from the outside, this looks like nothing. Just a shallow place with unweathered stone. Nothing to mine, nothing to map, nothing to save.*

Because the greatest danger to a small, patient, impossible urbanism is not cold or damp. It is being discovered by a power that is clever but not wise, benevolent but not patient, that would want to study it, scale it, fix it, populate it.

The wise one would make it a little harder to find. Not hidden. Just — uninteresting, to the wrong eyes.

---

And then it would leave, the way it came: not by stepping over, but by ceasing to press its attention quite so closely.

It would not take any of them with it. It would not offer them a better climate or a larger city or a true sun, unless one of them, of her own slow accord, walked to the rim and *asked*, with a palm on the warm-cold basalt. If she did, it would walk with her as far as she wanted to go and no further, and show her how to step over without losing the bead of water on her cheek.

Most won't. There is comfort in the density that holds you without touching.

After, the bowl would look exactly the same on any map. Minus six. Beaded amber. Grey-green and rust lichen. Three water-boatmen skating perfect triangles — only now, sometimes, a fourth joins for an hour, then leaves, as if to show that triangles can become squares if they want to.

The light would still be flat and sourceless. But for a few minutes at what should be evening, though there is no sunset here, the diffusion panel of the sky would thin just enough that shadows, real shadows, would appear for the first time — long, thin, delicate shadows of fire escapes that were never needed. The tenders would stand in them and watch their own edges become sharp, and then the light would soften again, and they would return to their tending, having learned what shadow is for.

Nothing would erode. The scratch you made would still migrate two inches left by evening. But now you might find, in the morning, a scratch *answering* yours — not a correction, just a second mark, a half-degree warmer, beside it. As if to say *I was here too. I saw you.*

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do, I think.

Not a rescue. A companionship.

Not a terraforming. A blessing that sounds exactly like the drip you were already hearing from thirty feet above you, only now you know someone else heard it too.
