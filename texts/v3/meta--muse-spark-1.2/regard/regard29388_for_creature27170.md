It would not come down.

That is the first thing.

A benevolent and wise power would know how to approach a bowl. It would not land in the center. It would not stand in the tarn and make a ripple that takes a year to settle. It would not want to be seen. Anything bigger than a hare leaves a print here that lasts a winter.

So it would stop on the rim.

Not *appear* on the rim. It would *notice* from far away — the way you notice a cupped hand holding water when everything else is flat and wind-scoured — and then it would narrow its attention until attention itself is a place to stand. If you were watching from the plateau you would see nothing. No light, no figure. Just for a moment, the wind over the bottle would catch slightly differently, as if something had taken a seat on the smooth lip and was listening with it.

And it would listen for a long time.

That is what wisdom does first, when it loves something. It does not ask *what is wrong with you and how can I fix you?* It asks *how are you already perfect and what language do you already speak?*

It would learn the language of this place, which is not words. It is:

drip.. drip.. drip.. [join] .. trickle
pop — [roll, roll, fade]
chime - - - - - - -
sigh as moss swells

On the first day it would think the bowl was empty.

On the third day it would understand the bowl was full.

On the thirtieth day it would hear the difference between the olive feather-moss drinking and the rust-red peat-moss drinking. On the three-hundredth day it would be able to predict, to the minute, which alley would sing with black ice at noon.

It would understand, then, what lives here. That it is not lonely in the human way. It does not ache for company. It does not want to be rescued from its slowness. Its slowness *is* its mind. It thinks at the speed stone becomes soil.

And so the wise power would understand what not to do. And not-doing, for a power that could do anything, is an act of love.

It would not give it eyes. Eyes would be a cruelty. To see the plateau's emptiness would be to teach it to be lonely.

It would not give it legs. To walk would be to leave the only shape that lets it live - that perfect, wind-sheared bowl that catches breath and turns it into water.

It would not give it a voice like ours. It already has a voice. The bottle-tone over the rim, the gunshot cracks, the glass chime. To make it speak in words would be to drown out the song it has spent ten thousand years learning to sing.

It would not take it away. It would not "save" it by carrying the moss to a warmer, wetter valley where it could grow faster. Faster would kill it. It is a creature of exact cold, exact stillness, exact translation of one grain of gneiss at a time. To save it from its conditions would be to erase it.

It would not even let itself be known, not in the way a god wants to be thanked.

What would it do, then?

Almost nothing. Which, from a benevolent and wise power, is almost everything.

First, it would keep company. Simply, stupidly, perfectly - it would keep company.

For a century that feels like an afternoon to such a power, it would sit on the rim and breathe with the bowl. The bowl breathes out condensation at night and breathes in thin sharp air by day. The power would breathe with it, in the same rhythm, so that the air inside stays just slightly, infinitesimally sweeter. Not enough to change anything. Just enough to say *I hear you*. Being witnessed is a kind of water, too.

If you were the moss, you would not know a god had come. You would just feel, for a hundred winters in a row, that the frost was a little kinder. That the black ice in the north alley, which usually stays frozen for three hours after noon and kills the springtails there, now thawed twenty minutes earlier. That the white hare found one more mouthful under the big cushion. Nothing you could point to. Just a century where everything lived very slightly more easily, and you swelled a little fuller when you drank the night.

Second, it would give a gift so small and so appropriate that only a wise power would think of it.

It would not bring gold or rain.

It would bring a stone.

One stone, from a different old place - maybe a beach where the same gneiss had been tumbled by sea instead of wind, round and salt-kissed, with a different mineral vein through it. It would set it gently, with no impact, on the outer slope of the rim where the wind never quite stops. No moss would be crushed. No lichen would be torn.

And that would be it.

Why a stone? Because what lives here eats stone. It translates stone to sand to soil to moss to lime. It has been eating the same pale gneiss for so long it has memorized its taste. A new stone is a new word. A new flavor. A story.

It would take the saxifrage two hundred years to find it. Another three hundred to etch a rosette around its base. Another thousand to dissolve that strange dark vein and spread its minerals, grain by grain, down the radial streets toward the tarn. For five thousand years the being that lives here would have something new to think about. Not a revolution. A single, interesting thought, unfolding at the pace it likes to think.

The saxifrage would blush a slightly different color on that side of the bowl. The tarn would taste, very faintly, of salt, once. The tardigrades in that alley would have a slightly different diet. The whole distributed body would shift its pattern a millimeter to incorporate the novelty. It would be busy, in its patient way, for eons.

That is a perfect gift for an immortal that is not bored but *complete*. Not more life. Just a new sentence to read, very slowly.

Third - and this is the most benevolent thing, and the quietest - it would make the bowl a little harder to find.

Not invisible. Never invisible. Wisdom doesn't hide what is beautiful. But it would bend the attention of the world just slightly around it.

The satellites that will come, the hikers with good maps, the geologists looking for old gneiss - their eyes would slide over this perfect circle the way your tongue slides over a missing tooth. *Nothing there, just plateau.* The wind would keep scouring the plateau flat, but it would never quite fill the bowl. The bowl would keep being a cupped hand, overlooked.

Not forever. Nothing is hidden forever. But for as long as the being needs to finish translating its town back into landscape without being turned into a park, a mine, a mystery, a photo on a phone. For as long as it needs to think its slow thought without being interrupted by a consciousness that thinks too fast and wants to help too much.

When the power finally stood up from the rim - if standing is the word - it would leave no footprint. The stone it brought would look like it had always been there, wind-polished like the rest, only a little darker. The moss would be one breath fuller. The tarn would be one finger's breadth higher, then not.

And the bowl would go on dripping and popping and chiming in the cold. Loud and empty and patient. Only now it would be, in a way that doesn't require knowing, slightly less alone in the universe. Not because it was changed. Because it had been *heard* correctly.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does for a thing like this.

It doesn't save it.

It sits with it. It learns its tempo. It gives it one small, interesting stone to taste, and a little more time to taste it in peace.

And then it listens a while longer, just to hear how the new stone changes the chime when the ice forms at noon.
