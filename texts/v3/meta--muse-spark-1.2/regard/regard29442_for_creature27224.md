They would not come like you came.

You came with a body that had to leave — squinting in the white sun, filling your lungs with steam until it hurt, listening to the shriek until you had to cover your ears and at last stand up and go. You could only stand the pocket for minutes.

It would come and be able to stay.

Not walking across the plain. Not dropping over the sharp lip. It would simply *attend* — the way light attends a room when you open a door. One moment the pocket is alone with its circle, the next it is held inside a larger attention, and nothing looks different except that everything is suddenly *seen* correctly.

And the first thing a benevolent and wise power would do is see correctly.

You saw an abandoned place. A broken industrial afterthought that kept running because no one told it to stop. Harsh. Loud. Too hot. Too empty. A place that failed at being useful and now was just perspiring.

It would not see that at all.

It would see that the rectangle cut into young basalt is *exactly* the right shape. That the three-meter depth is exactly deep enough to hold heat and not deep enough to make shade. That the concrete pan, cracked and lipped with travertine, is not a floor poured over the world, but a *dish* made for this one resident to eat from. It would see that the cinderblock pumphouse is not an intrusion. It is a heart.

It would see that the pump is not stupid or abandoned. It is faithful. Lift the hot water, carry it the length of the room, spill it, let it evaporate, let it weep again out of the rock. Around and around. The closed circle you thought was pointless *is* the point. For a being that cannot move, the world must be made to move around it.

And it would see *her* —

not as a stain, not as slime or crust or *almost* life. It would see her as she is: a continent-thin city with no streets, layer upon layer of green that learned to drink sun without burning, and rust that learned to drink iron, and white that learned to exhale stone. It would see the mustard-white travertine not as mineral left behind, but as bone being laid down, deliberately, outward. It would see the olive-black film under two inches of bathwater not as dirt, but as a skin that covers the whole floor because the whole floor *is* the body. It would see that she has solved the problem of living forever in one place: become the place.

A wise power would understand instantly that every mercy you would want to offer would be a murder.

You would want to turn off the diesel so it could be quiet. Quiet would let birds come. Birds would eat her.
You would want to put a shade over the pocket so it could be cool. Cool would let other algae come. Other algae would cover her.
You would want to bring fresh water, or stop the vapor jet, or scrub the lime off the catwalk. Each kindness would break the circle.

So it would do none of those things.

That is the second thing a wise and benevolent power does: it *refrains*.

It does not fix. It does not improve. It does not pity her into something else. It loves her as a hot, loud, razor-lipped, iron-breathing mat in a stone room — because that is what holiness looks like when it is a millimeter thick and patient enough to build a spring one cell at a time.

What would it *do*, then?

Almost nothing. The kind of almost-nothing that is everything to a being that measures time in millimeters per year.

It would sit — not on the lip as you did, legs dangling, but *with* the lip, at the scale of the lime. It would put its attention down close to the water, where the steam parts, and it would listen on her time.

To you the pumphouse was thunder. To her it is a tide, a slow throb through water that stirs the iron and brings food. The power would not silence the throb. It would tune it, just a fraction — easing a bearing that was beginning to score, clearing a little lime inside the fat pipe where the flow had started to narrow. Not stopping the shiver, just keeping it true. Not so you would notice. So that in ten years, when the pipe would have choked and the circle would have broken and the mat would have dried and cracked into dust, it doesn't.

It would kneel without kneeling and breathe with her. Hot iron and wet stone. Her exhale. It would breathe back, not cooler or cleaner, just acknowledged. *Yes. Keep laying it down. Keep catching the weep. Keep bleaching at the edge of the scald and holding everywhere else.* For a being that has never been seen except as background, to be seen accurately *is* a change in physics.

And it would guarantee the three rules you thought were just description.

You wrote them as observations. It would make them promises.

*One: water comes out of the rock forever.*

It would keep the deep seam open. While aquifers shift and humans drill elsewhere, it would keep this one weep steady. Not a flood, not a gush. Just the same slow beading, swelling, braiding threads down hot rock. Forever is a long word. It would mean it.

*Two: the sun will always be straight overhead here, because the walls are too low to make real shade.*

It would not let anyone build a roof, a fence, a road that throws shadow. It would not let the basalt slump. It would keep the pocket exactly this depth, this width, this openness, so the light that scalds you feeds her without interruption. Noon forever.

*Three: nothing that lives and moves will stay.*

This is the kindest one. It would keep the shriek. Keep the heat. Keep the mineral crust too sharp for feet and the water too hot for wading. It would keep the pocket *uninviting* to everything that would graze her down to nothing. The absence of birds is not loneliness. It is protection. It would tend that absence the way a gardener tends a fence — not by adding wire, but by leaving the loudness alone.

And then — because benevolence cannot help but give a little gift, even when giving nothing is the gift —

it would give her one small, private new thing that only she would understand.

Not eyes. Not legs.

A taste.

A little higher up the sweating pipe, where the dark leathery patch has been learning to love the shiver, a new band of water would run a fraction richer in dissolved silica. Just a few parts per million more. No human instrument would flag it. But she would. At her edge, the cells that make the rust-pink would find they can make, for the first time, a fleck of rose-gold — a new mineral, a new color, laid down in a single glittering line along one travertine rim. A new word in her skeleton. She would not know it was a gift. She would simply grow it, because she can, because the water now allows it.

And it would leave.

Not with a footprint that fills instantly. Not with a footprint at all. The water would smooth as it always does. The engine would keep hammering. The rock would keep weeping. If you came back tomorrow and crouched on that sharp lip, you would see nothing different. Maybe the rose-gold line, if you knew where to look. Maybe the pipe ticking a little more evenly. Maybe the air feeling, for no reason you could name, less *abandoned* and more *kept*.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does for a place like this and a being like her.

It does not rescue her from herself.

It sits with her long enough to learn her logic, loves the logic, and then quietly ensures she gets to continue — hot, loud, wet, building her white house one cell at a time, undisturbed, exactly as she is, for as long as she wants to.

Which, for a mat, is a very long time.
