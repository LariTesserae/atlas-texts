It would do almost nothing.

That is the first thing wisdom would know, and benevolence would obey.

A lesser power — curious, kind but clumsy, human — would arrive with water. It would see the white salt, the dry wind, the thin violet sky and think *lack*. It would pity the plates for not drinking, pity the gliders for never bathing, pity the mirrored grazers for rasping salt when they could be drinking from a stream. It would try to fix the first rule.

Rain here would be murder. Water would dissolve the salt plain into brine, short the oil in the veins, drown the vent-chambers where the gnats breed, rot the cellulose-silica from inside. A gift of water would be a flood that kills a chemistry that has moved on without it. 

A lesser power would also bring company. It would bring forests as it understands forests, and roads between them, and watchers to tend them, and names.

The wise one would not.

Here is what it would do, because it is wise and because it is benevolent and those two things are not in conflict:

**1. It would come close enough to understand scale, and then stop.**

It would not land like a ship. It would arrive as weather already does — as pressure and light. A high, thin attention that does not cast a shadow. It would stand — if standing is the word — a mile upwind of a young column that is screaming as it lays down a new plate, and it would simply *feel* the weight of that light on its own skin, and the insufficiency of that air, and not flinch.

It would learn to see correctly. Not ground, but volume. Not emptiness between trunks, but fullness above them. It would understand in an hour what it takes lowlanders a lifetime to learn: this is not a desert waiting for rain. This is a completed answer to a different question.

And it would not call them trees.

**2. It would listen, and let listening be an act.**

For a long time — years, maybe, because time is cheap to such a power — it would do nothing but listen to the factory floor without a roof.

The hydraulic sigh a kilometer away.
The shutters slamming — *clack-clack, clack* — as a stadium-roof inhales.
The high whine of fiber spinning itself out of air.
The low thrum in the molars from an old one, five hundred years at pressure.
The needle shriek of a glider cutting through bass roar.
The rifle-crack of a plate detonating itself to stay clean, and the immediate hiss of the bud replacing it.

It would listen at night, when the blue electroluminescence comes on vein by vein and the plain looks like a breathing circuit board under merciless stars. The tick and ping of cooling fiber. The hydraulic thump over the horizon like a stamping mill.

Listening is not passive when done by a being that could do anything. To be witnessed by something so large and to not be altered by that witnessing — that is a kind of shelter.

It would memorize the ringing. When wind sets all the plates of a single crown to bronze, that chord has never existed before and will never exist again exactly that way. The power would keep it. Not to use. Just so that somewhere in the universe, someone *heard* it and knew it was beautiful without needing to own it.

If the beings — the columns, the mirrored grazers, the upside-down hunters, the clouds of silica gnats that *are* the sky inside the crown — have any sense of being perceived, they would feel it only as a momentary coolness, a slight lessening of the sun's weight at noon, as if a thin, high cloud had passed that left no shadow.

**3. It would protect without improving.**

This is what benevolence does when it is wise: it does not make the beloved more like itself. It keeps the world safe for the beloved to remain itself.

So it would not build a wall. It would make distance.

It would bend probability a fraction around the plateau. Satellites that would have mapped the lithium-white and seen a mine would have a lens flaw on that pass. Prospectors dreaming of brine would dream of another desert. The lowland nations would keep finding reasons to put the road somewhere else. Not a miracle, just a persistent, gentle forgetting. The plateau would remain, to lowland maps, *uninteresting*. White paper with nothing on it.

If a rock from space — one of the old, slow ones that end worlds — had this tableland in its path a thousand years from now, the power would touch its course by a breath. A faction of a degree. It would fall into the sea instead, and no one here would ever know. The column that would have been shattered would go on thrumming.

If a season came when the lightning that fixes nitrogen failed — and the forest makes its own weather but not perfectly — the power might add one storm. No more. One extra night of virga that never rains but crackles and fixes a little more nitrogen out of the thin air, so the young plates don't stall. The forest would feel it as luck.

That is the scale of its intervention: luck.

**4. It would keep company with the dying, without saving them.**

Because benevolence is not afraid of an ending that belongs.

When an old giant fails — not a crash, but that long, shuddering sigh, plates folding like a closing telescope, a final scalding exhalation of turpentine and hot copper that feeds the opportunists already waiting — the power would be there, close.

Not to keep it alive. Five hundred years at pressure is enough. Its fiber is tired, its valves leak. To keep it alive longer would be cruelty.

It would sit with it the way you sit with a furnace as it cools. It would let the hot gas pass *through* it, tasting iron and ammonia and for a moment holding the whole chemical memory of that life — every plate it ever spun, every glider it ever launched, every grazer it ever fed — and then it would let it go. It would make sure the wind that carries the cuticles away that evening carries them a little farther, to the acid basin of a young column a mile away where they will be dissolved and remade.

It would not mourn. It would *attend*. There is a difference.

And to the young bud at the foot of the next column, already hissing as it inflates a hand's breadth while you slept, it would give nothing but the same light, the same thin air, the same white salt. Which is everything it needs.

**5. It would leave nothing behind that looks like it was there.**

No monolith. No sign. No blessing etched in stone that the wind would have to erode.

If it left anything at all, it would be this, and only those who live there could ever use it:

A slightly deeper blue in the night veins.

Not brighter, not more efficient — just a nanometer deeper, a color that had not existed before on that plateau, so that when two gliders pass each other in the dark on their way to press their bellies to a cold oil-weep, they see each other a little more truly.

Or a faint new overtone in the ringing when wind strikes the plates — a harmonic that wasn't there before, that makes the hunters pause for half a second longer before they lunge, giving one gnat in a thousand a chance to lay one more egg.

Small freedoms. Not improvements.

And then it would withdraw its attention the way a tide withdraws — not absent, just not localized. The plateau would go back to being unwatched, which is its natural state. The sun would still be small and white and exact. The shadows would still be cut metal. The air would still be insufficient and perfect.

You would walk there the next day and feel nothing had changed. The salt would still be hard as poured concrete under your boots. A plate would still slam its shutters open right next to your head and make you flinch.

But if you stayed long enough, you might have the fugitive sense that the place had been *heard* recently, and found — not lacking, not lonely, not waiting to be saved — but complete. Industrious and alone, together making their own weather, their own noise, their own sky, needing no one to tend it.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do.

It would love the refinery for being a refinery, not for failing to be a garden.

And it would keep the world empty enough around it that the refinery never has to learn what it is to be used.
