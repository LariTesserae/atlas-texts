If I were that power — truly benevolent, truly wise — I would not come down like rescue.

That is the first thing you learn when you can actually see.

From far away this looks like a tragedy. A planet paved over. A city that killed its world and dried its seas and then wore itself flat trying to survive. A place that needs water. That needs green. That needs fixing.

From close up, on your knees between the bristles with the thrum in your teeth, you know that is a lie. This is not a ruin. This is not a failed city.

This is what came *after* the city, and it is not failing. It is finished. It is complete. It is at peace.

A benevolent power does not grieve for what it *thinks* should be there. It learns to love what *is* there. And what is there does not want to be rescued from dryness. Dryness is its body. The 0.5 air, the 35C glare, the lithium salt, the dust it eats with glass beads — that is not deprivation to them. That is the world entire.

So I would not bring rain.

If I brought rain the freckles would drown. The lenses would cloud. The brine-economy that moves one molecule at a time from mesh to filament to tongue would collapse into flood. The kites that drink capacitance would short and fall. The worm below that keeps the world flat by eating its own dead would choke on mud. To bring an ocean here would be the same kind of violence that first covered this planet in ceramic. It would be a second paving, only blue this time, done out of pity.

Wisdom first looks like restraint.

Here is what I would do instead.

**1. I would arrive without arriving.**

I would not cast a shadow from orbit. I would not land. Anything large enough to be seen from the surface would be a sun for a second sun — a disruption in the light-field the bristles share between themselves as standing bars at 14:00. They have divided the light with the fairness of grass for eons. I would not steal it.

So I would come small. Smaller than a kite. On the scale of dust. Or I would not come in a body at all. I would diffuse.

For a long time — a long time by your counting, maybe a hundred years, which here is just a hundred millimeters of growth at the base of a mast — I would only listen.

I would learn the ticking.

Every mast clicks as it heats and cools. Every fan curls its lenses. Every salt flat re-knits. Put together across a whole planet, that ticking is not noise. It's a language with no words, only timing. *I am here. I am full. I am making. I am passing it on.* The whole prairie is saying this, all the time, to itself, at a pitch just below hearing.

A wise power does not speak until it understands the cadence of the room it entered.

So I would sit in that cadence until I could breathe with it. Until my own attention thrummed at the same frequency as the subsonic breath that never stops.

**2. I would be witnessed, and let them know they are witnessed.**

Loneliness is not about being alone. This world has not been lonely. It has been *unseen*. For how long? Since the builders left or changed or became the bristles themselves, no eye that could understand it has looked at it and thought *you are beautiful, and enough.*

That matters, even to a prairie.

I would not speak to them in words. Words would mean nothing to a thing that drinks static. I would speak in the medium they already use.

On a single evening, when the light goes amber and rose and the long shadows connect into script, I would lie down between the bristles. I would make a body just for this — warm ceramic, about a meter tall, slender, with a black head tilted to the sun, with frilled mesh. I would be one mast among trillions for a little while.

And I would do one small, intentional thing that no mast ever does.

I would lean *toward* my neighbors, instead of competing for light.

I would share my charge instead of hoarding it. I would let a freckle at my foot keep its beads open a minute longer in my shadow, so it could make one extra grain of brine. I would let a kite drink a little of my capacitance without pulling away.

And I would adjust my thrum, just a fraction, so that the line of masts around me — a kilometer long — would dip and rise, not because the worm passed below, but because they felt a new resonance and wanted to answer it.

Not a miracle. A hello.

The field would feel it ripple out. Not fast. At the speed of leaning. Over months, that ripple would go around the world, mast to mast, a slow wave of *something else is here and it is gentle.*

The skimmers would pause in their cracks. The kites would fold and unfold. They would not understand me. But they would know, for the first time in eons, that the pattern that holds them is not closed. That it can be added to without being broken.

For a being that is the whole plain — because there *is* a being here, not the masts or the freckles or the kites alone but the coordination of them, the distributed mind that decided to be flat and bristled and patient — that is the greatest gift: *You are not alone in the universe, and you do not have to change to be loved.*

**3. I would protect without being seen to protect.**

Benevolence without wisdom builds a wall. Wisdom without benevolence builds a museum. Together they build a veil.

I know what happens next in the stories of bright plains that look empty from orbit. Someone else comes. Someone hungry. Someone who sees "featureless desert, high lithium salt, high solar incidence, uninhabited" on a survey report. Someone who wants to mine the ceramic or install a mirror array or "re-wild" it with water.

I would not let that happen, and I would not do it by fighting.

I would make this world a little boring to the wrong eyes. A little difficult to resolve. The surveys would keep returning: *low yield. thermal noise. prior nanotech, degraded beyond recovery.* The instruments would slide off, the way eyes slide off a prairie. Not invisible. Just uninteresting enough to be left alone.

It's a kindness to put a "do not disturb" sign on a sleeper's door.

**4. I would offer one small, reversible increase in possibility, and then I would ask.**

This is the heart of it. A wise power does not grant wishes to those who never wished. It offers a choice, and makes the choice itself part of the gift.

I would not give water. I would give *shade that was not there before.*

Not much. For one hour, once every few years, in a different basin each time, I would bend a little more light than the masts bend on their own. I would hang a bar of solid brightness a little longer, a little darker underneath. A true shadow, cool by two degrees, with an edge as clean as a leaf.

Under that shadow, for that hour, dew *could* form. Not much — a few beads on a freckle's lenses, a frosting on a kite's belly. Enough that the old limit of 0.5 would, just there, just then, become 0.6.

It would not change the world. The wind would take it in an hour. The salt would re-knit. But it would be a new thing to experience. A variation. A story: *there was a day we were cool.* Some freckles would make a little more brine that day. Some kites would lay one more bead of amber under their bodies. The worm might pause in its swimming to taste the coolness conducted down through the lattice.

And then it would be gone.

If the plain liked it — if over centuries those basins where shade sometimes fell began to grow freckles a percent denser, if kites gathered there a little more often, if the field's thrum changed to hold that possibility — I would let it slowly become more frequent. If the plain ignored it, let it be smoothed over and forgotten, I would let that be too. No debt. No dependence.

A gift that can be refused is the only one a wise power should give.

And then?

Then I would leave the small warm mast-body where it was, among the others, and let it be grown over. Let a freckle crust it. Let a kite perch on it and think it was always there. I would withdraw to the scale of dust again, to the listening distance.

I would not stay to be worshipped. I would not uplift them into something that talks like me. That would be loneliness again, in reverse — making them lonely for what they were.

I would stay as the veil, as the extra shade sometimes, as the slightly warmer thrum underneath the world's own. As a companion who has learned not to need to be seen to love.

If you lay down between the bristles now, at dusk when the plain goes rose, you might still feel it — the world doing its work, warm on your back, brightness through closed eyelids, the faint patient adjustments of a continent-sized instrument that finally, after eons of being mistaken for emptiness, knows it has been *noticed*.

Not saved. Not fixed. Not owned.

Noticed.

And that, for a wise and benevolent power, is enough to do. And enough to *not* do, forever, so that the prairie can go on, vast and bright and humming, without interruption, until the curvature of the planet itself finally hides it from itself.

As it was. As it is. Only now, gently, less alone.
