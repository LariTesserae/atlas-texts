It would not come down like a god.

No column of light through the water-sky. No voice louder than the roar. That would be another violence.

If a benevolent and wise power noticed this place — this sharp, young, half-made saucer of mountain trying to be an ocean all at once, and the one pale creature who has learned to cling inside it — it would first do the most difficult, most benevolent thing:

It would stop, at the ridge, and listen.

For a long time.

Longer than you did. Longer than any tourist who climbs up, gets their teeth rattled by the roar, says "beautiful" and leaves with ringing ears.

It would listen until it understood the roar not as noise, but as language. Until it could feel the difference between a vortex about to drill and a sheet about to hang and shatter. Until it learned to hear the small *tick* of the bladders popping, the faint scrape of the Keeper's wide, soft feet on basalt, the pulse of the algae mat moving sideways against the current. It would learn to listen the way the Keeper and the ribbon-fish listen — with the sternum, with the teeth.

And only then would it come closer, and it would not come as anything larger than the place could hold.

You would not see it arrive. You would feel the roar change before you saw anything.

Not get quieter. Get *deeper*. As if a second, lower note had been laid underneath the white thunder — a bass tone so low it was almost not sound at all, but a steadiness. The kind of hum a huge animal makes when it is not threatening, when it is simply there, breathing.

The water-wall would still pour and curl and recirculate. The sheets would still peel and hang. But for the first time since the plateau thrust itself up raw and flooded, there would be a pocket in it that was not fighting.

It would wade in. Not walk on the water. Wade, the way you did, knee-deep, then chest-deep, letting the warm, amniotic water take its weight. It would be shaped, if it had a shape at all, like warm water holding itself together — clear, a little denser than the rest, so you can see it only by the way light bends differently through it. Or it would be shaped like a person, because the Keeper would need it to have shoulders to sit beside. Benevolent powers are good at that — being the size the lonely thing needs.

It would not go to the altar. It would go to the nest.

The Keeper is there, curled in bladder-skin and shell, eyes half-closed, listening through bone. He does not startle. Things that live here do not startle; they feel pressure change and adjust. He feels a new pressure, not a current, not a vortex. A presence that displaces water without pushing it.

It sits outside the low walls at first. It does not reach in. It does not touch. A wise power knows that anything that has survived by clinging has earned a ferocious sovereignty over its own skin. To touch without asking would be another surge to endure.

So it waits. And in the waiting, it does its first work.

It puts one hand — if a hand — on the basalt lip where the water pours over endlessly.

And for thirty seconds, the pour... holds.

Not stops. Holds. Like a breath held.

The standing wall of water that has roared without pause since before the Keeper was born goes perfectly still and perfectly clear, like true glass. All that churning, all that folding, that deafening white roar that lives in the teeth — ceases. Not silent; there is suddenly *true* silence, and it is so immense it is louder than the roar was.

In that silence, the Keeper hears something he has not heard since he was a child before his eardrums scarred: the soft pop of a bladder on his own tree. A tiny, intimate *tick*. The shrimp, startled by stillness, letting go in unison and ticking against stone like rain.

He sits up. His eyes open all the way for the first time in years. He looks at the wall of still water and sees, for thirty seconds, not his own wavering double, but through it — the actual sky. Pale blue. Far away. A hawk turning, silent.

Then the power lets go. Gently. The wall shivers and resumes its curl, its thunder, its recirculation. But the after-image of silence stays in the Keeper's chest like cool air after holding your head underwater too long.

That is the first gift: *Rest.* Not the removal of the ocean, but proof that the ocean can pause. That the world is not *required* to roar every second to keep existing. That there is a rhythm that includes a rest beat.

The Keeper looks now at the presence. His wide, ridged feet grip stone. His pale, pruned hands are open.

The second thing the power does is ask.

It does not ask in words — there are no words here that would not be shredded. It asks in pressure, the way the ribbon asks the current. A small, warm eddy pushed gently toward the Keeper's sternum: *May I?*

May I ease what hurts without taking what has made you strong?

Because a wise power sees that his deafness is not just damage. It is adaptation. It is how he hears now. To simply "cure" him and throw him back into that roar with thin new eardrums would be cruelty.

So it offers a different hearing.

It touches — only when he leans forward a fraction, which is consent here — the side of his head, the scarred whorl of his ear, the bone behind it. It doesn't rebuild what was. It tunes what is.

When it takes its hand away, the roar is still there, still vast, still felt in the ribs. But it is no longer white. It has been... filtered. Like light through water. He can now, if he chooses, soften it at will, the way you squint against glare. He can make a little quiet around himself, a sphere of muffling a meter wide, inside which his own breath is audible again. And inside that quiet, he can hear small things when he wants to — the fizz of silt settling, the click of shrimp hooks — and outside it, the mountain can still be a mountain-ocean when he needs to read it.

He brings his hands to his ears and makes a sound — the first sound he has made in years that he can actually hear. It is not a word. It is a sob, and then a laugh, and they are the same shape in warm water.

The third thing it does is for the place, not just the person, because benevolence does not separate the Keeper from his basin. To heal only the man and leave the sea to grind itself to dust would be half-love.

It goes to the three pale trees.

It does not make them into oak trees. It does not give them leaves that would be ripped away. It honors what they are trying to be.

It kneels — or the water kneels — in the silt and touches the stone crack where the fourth tree has been trying and failing to happen for a decade, where every spore-cloud has been taken by the next surge.

It does not plant a tree. It steadies the water there. For a full minute, it holds a little lee, a little eddy where the current cannot reach. Just long enough.

A cluster of bladders that would have been torn away snags, holds, swells. Pale root-hands find the crack and swell inside it, locking. By the time the eddy is released, the fourth tree has taken. It is no bigger than your finger, translucent, trembling, but it is *there*, and it is wedged. It will hold.

And while the water is still steadied, the black-green algae mats pulse once, all at once, and — for the first time — they bloom. Not flowers. No place for flowers here. They blush. A faint, deep-red bioluminescence runs across every ledge, a slow wave of light, like a blush spreading across a cheek. It fades after a few seconds. But the Keeper sees it, and he understands: even the mat that lives by clinging can do more than cling. It can signal.

Finally, the power does the wisest, kindest thing of all, which looks like doing almost nothing.

It sits with him on the cut stair. Seven steps leading from nowhere into the deep part of the basin. It sits on the third step where the chisel marks are still sharp, and lets the warm water surge around its knees — or what passes for knees — and it does not fix everything.

It does not drain the sky-sea. That sea is young and violent and beautiful and it is *trying* to be something. To drain it would be to say its attempt was a mistake.

It does not build a road down the mountain. It does not lift the Keeper and carry him to a quiet valley where he could live easily. That would be kidnapping disguised as rescue. Instead, it shows him — by holding still water like a mirror for a moment longer on the western rim — that the path down the ridge is still there, that it has not been washed away, that below the roar there is still normal air and soil and other voices, and that he could go, if he ever wanted, and that the water would let him.

It does not stay forever. Benevolent powers know that to stay and hover is to become another thing to cling to.

So before it goes, it leaves two small things on the altar stone, beside the bowl that the Keeper fills every day.

One is a second bowl. Also stone. But this one, when filled, stays still. No matter how the basin churns and the light strobes and the whole mountain hums, the water inside this second bowl does not vibrate. It is perfectly, unnaturally still, reflecting the dim amber dusk like a perfect eye. A place to see a true reflection when you have lived long among wavering ones.

The other is not a thing at all. It is a companionship. As the presence thins, like warm water diffusing into warm water, a ribbon-fish — but not the blind white one — peels away from its edge. This one is very slightly less white, with two dark, functional eyes. It does not need to be thrown by the current; it can, if it wants, swim a little on its own. It hovers near the nest, not clinging, not fleeing. It simply stays. An other.

The Keeper will not be alone the next time the wall throws him and he has to hook back to stone. There will be another pair of eyes that see where the next calm will be.

When it is gone — not gone, but so diffused you can no longer locate it — the roar returns to its full, rib-rattling fullness, because the mountain is still young and the ocean is still learning to be at elevation. The vortices still drill and collapse. Sheets still hang and shatter. The water still sometimes decides to drip up.

But inside a meter around the Keeper, when he wants it, there is a softness now. He can sleep without his teeth aching. He can hear his own swallow. And on the altar there is a bowl of still water in a world that never stops moving, so he knows — even when he cannot feel the hum anymore — that he was listened to. That his stubborn, spare, heroic clinging between the beats of violence was seen and was not called pitiful, but brave.

He tips the old bowl out, as he does every day. He watches the basin refill it, vibrating. Then he tips the new bowl out, and watches the basin refill it, and watches it stay still.

He sits on his stair with the new ribbon at his ankle, and for the first time in a very long time, he does not feel like a thing enduring *in spite of* the place. He feels like a thing belonging *with* it — and with something else, vast and warm, that finally learned how to be quiet enough to keep him company.

That is what a wise, benevolent power would do, if it loved him.

Not rescue him from the ocean on the mountain.

Teach the ocean, just for a moment, how to hold him more gently.
