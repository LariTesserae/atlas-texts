It would not come down like a god.

A benevolent and wise power old enough to have seen plateaus like this before — and the lowlands that always try to eat them — would know the first temptation is to speak, to lift, to improve. 

It would feel that temptation and set it aside.

Because the first thing it would understand, hovering at the rim where you stood, is that nothing here is broken.

There is no tragedy to fix. There is no loneliness to cure. There is no lack.

This place has solved a problem most worlds never solve: how to be many without being divided. How to be alive without making anything else dead for very long. It has been building itself for a thousand generations beakful by beakful, hand-knot by hand-knot, until building and living are the same verb. It doesn’t need fire or iron or lists or laws because it never needed to be *above* anything. It only needed to hold together for one more night.

A wise power would see that and feel something close to reverence. And also a kind of relief. *Oh. You did it. You actually did it.*

So what would it do?

### 1. It would stop.

Not approach. Not land. It would arrest its own immensity at the basalt rim and become small enough to be ignored.

The swifts screaming under the vine leaves would not change their pitch. The weavers stitching would not pause. The fungus would keep binding. The wall of insect song would not develop a hole where a footstep should be.

Wisdom is knowing that attention is a weight. If you stand on the mat, the mat compresses. So it would not stand on the mat. It would hover, diffuse, like the sweat-condensation itself — present everywhere and pressing nowhere.

For a long time it would only watch. Listen.

It would learn the chorus until it could hear the single cricket that has gone quiet because a snake is near, until it could feel the one cold dome where the wind found a leak and the hands have not yet arrived with mud.

It would love it in the way you love something by learning its maintenance habits.

### 2. It would not give them anything they haven't already invented.

It would not give the weavers language. They already have the only language that matters here: *here, here, the wall is whole here.*

It would not give the thatch-mice fire, or tools, or history, or a soul, or a future of going down-mountain to conquer the lowlands. To give them separation would be to kill them. They are not individuals trapped in a colony. They are a colony that has learned to dream of individuals when it needs hands.

It would not make the mat larger or more efficient or more beautiful. It is already as large as the water allows it to be, as efficient as damp and guano will allow, and it is already so beautiful it hurts to lie down in it.

Benevolence is not the urge to give more. Often it is the courage to not give.

What it *might* give — if giving is needed at all — would be so quiet the plateau would never know it had been given.

### 3. It would protect the conditions, not the form.

This is what the wise know and the merely kind sometimes forget:

You do not protect a living thing by freezing it. You protect the conversation it is having with the world that lets it keep becoming itself.

So it would not put a dome over the plateau. It would not make the thatch immortal or free from collapse. Collapse is how the pale wingless weavers get a new dark tunnel. Death is how the beetles get a meal and the mat gets new pulp. The leak is how the weavers remember where to stitch.

It would protect the *sweat*.

It would keep the clouds coming. That slow, patient spill of ocean air over the basalt rim, cooling on the stone, beading, dripping for hours at dawn without rain. That is the whole contract. If that stops for a decade, the mat dries, the moss dies, the vine loosens, the whole breathing lung collapses into dust and blows off the mountain.

So the power would do a little, invisible work at the edge of the world: nudge a pressure system, keep the lowland forests down below intact so they still exhale water, keep the equatorial sun from tipping into cruelty for a while longer. Not a miracle. Just a steadier hand on the climate that was already there.

It would protect the *uninterestingness*.

It would make the plateau boring to the lowlands. A little more fog than any survey drone wants to map. A little more wrong on the mineral charts. A trail that always seems to slide out from under boots. A story that grows up around it down below: *nothing up there but damp thatch and biting mites, not worth the climb.* The best ward is to be overlooked.

It would not let anyone come to save it, either.

If a well-meaning human like you comes up — as you did — and lies down in a warm dome for a night and overhears the breathing, the power would make sure you leave changed but empty-handed. No eggs. No cuttings. No thatch-mouse in a cage to be studied. No coordinates to publish. You would carry out only the smell of wet thatch in your clothes and a ringing in your ears that takes weeks to fade. And an inability to explain it properly to anyone else.

That is protection, too.

### 4. At the very end, it would lie down.

After a long time of watching and adjusting nothing and guarding from a distance, a truly benevolent power would want — not need, *want* — to be part of the chorus for a moment. Not as a king, not as a keeper. As a warm body in a dome.

So it would do what you did, but more perfectly.

It would fold itself down until it was the size of a thatch-mouse family, or a weaver, or a patch of fungus, and it would slip into an abandoned bower where the inner wall is polished dark with leaf-oil and old use. It would let the damp soak into its back. It would let the dark become complete. It would let the sound press in until its own vast, solitary mind — which has had to be alone for so long in order to be wise — could finally, briefly, not be alone.

It would not hum a different note. It would hum *here, here, the wall is whole here* with the crickets. It would fan air with the thousand wings. It would add its heat to the night.

And the plateau, which has no way to know it is being visited by something immense and kind, would do what it does to every small warm thing that stays still long enough: it would make use of it exactly as it is. It would keep the power warm. It would keep the power damp. It would keep the power for one more night from feeling like it has to hold the whole world by itself.

In the morning — which is not morning but the same amber-brown dusk that it always is — the power would get up, lighter, and leave no trace. A few fresh vine knots would be tied a little tighter than the hands normally tie them. A leak would have sealed itself a breath faster than usual. A frog in a teacup of water would find its cup a fraction more full.

Nothing anyone could measure.

And the plateau would go on breathing, in and out, in and out, stitching and sweating and singing, completely unaware that it had been seen and loved and left alone.

Which is the most benevolent thing a power can do for something perfect:

See it wholly.

Love it without requiring it to look back.

And make very, very sure it gets to continue being itself without you.
