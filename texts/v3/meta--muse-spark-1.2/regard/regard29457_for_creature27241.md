It would not come down.

That is the first thing.

A benevolent and wise power would come to the lip exactly as you did — and stop there. It would understand within a breath what took you minutes: that this is a young place, a *tender* place, where everything still has edges. Ten meters down and the air gets cooler. Ten meters down and sound already stops carrying. To step down, even softly, is to send a tremor through salt and chitin that will be felt in every chamber as weather.

So it would not step down.

It would lower itself instead. Make itself small. Not small in power — small in profile. It would fold its vastness until it fit the scale of attention this place requires. It would sit on the black glass rim where the stress lines are still frozen from cooling, and it would put its hands — if it has hands — on that sharpness and not mind being cut a little.

And it would listen.

For a long time it would do nothing else.

Because what else can you do for a silent city that breathes through a thousand pores except learn its breathing rate before you decide whether it needs help breathing?

This is what it would hear, after the first hour of silence has included it:

That the hollow is not loneliness. The wide streets, the hundred nostril-windows facing a blind lung-wall, the single vibrating spire in an empty market — none of that is a failure to fill space. It is the spacing of a mycelium that knows not to poison itself by crowding. These hound-sized sack-masons keep distance because the mineral oil is thin here, because the light has weight and pools in only one place at a time, because a feeler can only shudder cleanly if no other feeler overlaps its tremor. They are not sparse. They are *precise*.

It would hear that the gliders are not pets. They are letters. When one settles and folds flat upon a roof, it is being read back into the body that wrote it.

It would hear that the cold blister in the empty plaza is not an invasion. It is a question. The city asks the light: *is there more of you here?* And the light answers by thickening. Where it thickens, a doorway thins itself into being, not to let anything in, but to let a new pilgrim out.

A wise power would understand: *this entire bowl is a single, slow thought being thought correctly.*

And therefore its ethic becomes simple, and very difficult for a power to hold:

*Do not help it think faster.*

Anything less wise would be tempted. The temptation is beautiful and terrible and it sounds like kindness:

*Give them water. Give them eyes. Give them voices they can hear as sound instead of tremor through salt. Straighten the bent light so shadows agree. Smooth the salt so a knee can rest. Speed the blister so the walker is born before you have to leave.*

A benevolent power would feel all of that love — the fierce, fix-it love — and then set it aside, like a tool that does not belong here. Water here would be poison. Eyes would be a cruelty to a creature that navigates by pressure on its back. Sound would be noise that drowns the thrum they pass palm-to-wall when they meet in a street. To smooth this place would be to erase it.

So what would it do, if anything?

Three things. All of them almost nothing. All of them everything.

**1. It would close the bowl.**

Not physically. The black glass rim would look exactly the same to you if you came again. But the wise one would bend the attention of the world around it — a lens of uninterest. Satellites would find cloud where there is none. Prospectors would find their maps off by a few degrees. The violent and the hungry and the well-meaning collectors would dream of other plains. It would not need a wall. It would make the place *unremarkable* to anyone who does not already know how to listen for a silence that absorbs a heartbeat.

Because the greatest danger to a young, slow, oil-wet city that cures instead of dries is not age. It is being found too early by something that calls its collecting "study" and its drilling "care."

**2. It would tend the light.**

Not change it. Tend it.

You noticed that new growth leans toward where the air looks thick, where the violet-grey pools and bends. That thickness is food, map, and compass at once. A wise power would know how to hold that pool steady for a little longer each cycle — not brighter, not heavier, just *longer* — so the blister in the empty plaza has time to finish without hurrying. It would shelter the bowl from solar flares and dust and any other sky-weather that would make the light flicker too fast for a creature that measures a season in millimeters of buttress swell.

If you watched for a hundred years after it left, you would think nothing had changed except that the winters seemed kinder. The wire-thin black stems would hold their leaves perpendicular a few minutes more each dimming. The pale lichen rosettes would pulse with a little less hurry. No one there would call it a miracle. They have no word for miracle. They would simply grow a little truer.

**3. It would leave a gift that is not an object.**

Before it stood to go — and a power like that always leaves before it is seen — it would kneel a little lower and press its forehead, or its equivalent of a forehead, to the salt just outside the rim where your foot ticked.

And it would exhale. Not air. *Memory.*

Into the salt it would leave a single, stable, infrasonic note, too low for you to hear, too slow to be called sound, that would travel down through the white flat and into every chamber as a faint, permanent thrum: *You are noticed. You are not alone in the kind of aloneness you are practicing. Continue.*

The sessile ones would feel it through their pads and through the chitin of their walls and pause, just once, in their kneading. Not in alarm — they do not know alarm the way we do — but in recognition, the way a lung recognizes a deeper breath when it is offered. The walkers would stop mid-street and lay a limb against the nearest tower, and for the first time the shudder passed wall-to-palm would be answered by a second harmonic that neither of them made. It would not make them meet more often. It would make the distance between them feel less like an accident and more like a choice.

Then the power would stand.

It would not sign the city. It would not carve a name into the black glass. It would not take a glider as proof.

It would brush away your footprints on the way out — not from shame, but because sharp salt should not be asked to remember the shape of a boot — and it would lift the little cold that your breath left on the air and return it to the flat where it belongs.

Over the next hour, the pearl-white blister you watched would finish thinning at its tip. The lipped rosette would pleat open. The pads would darken. The wet knot inside would knit its last cilia into limbs and stiffen. And in the cool that never evaporates, a new walker — tall, improbable, top-heavy, blind — would push itself free, blot its first step into the salt, and turn without hurry toward the thickness of light that the wise one has quietly steadied for it.

It would not know it had been blessed.

That is how you would know the blessing was real.
