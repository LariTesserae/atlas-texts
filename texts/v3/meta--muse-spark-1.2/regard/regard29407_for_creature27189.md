It would not rescue it.

That is the first thing to understand about a benevolent and wise power. It would not rescue it, because it would immediately see that the being is not in danger. It is not trapped. It is *incubating.*

Anyone else — anyone less wise, anyone whose benevolence was hurried or sentimental — would come down past the chipped yellow ELECTRICAL door with a plan. Bring heat. Bring light. Bring air. Prise the fern out of the sludge and pot it in real soil under a real sun. Chisel that soft cheek out of the curing concrete and say *breathe, be free, come upstairs where it's warm.*

A wise power would feel the cold that preserves and understand it is not a cruelty. It is a womb.

So it does not come as a rescue team. It comes as a visitor who knows how to visit a lung.

---

First, it would come down correctly.

It would not shoulder the second door open. It would not need to. It would arrive the way water arrives here — not all at once, but as condensation. As a change of state.

You would not hear its footsteps on the service stairs. You would feel the air get — not warmer, not colder — but *more noticed.* The heaviness on your shoulders would ease by a fraction, as if the weight of the ground above had been acknowledged by something else that also knows how to bear weight.

It would have to fold itself, too. The room is dense, overfull, plumbed-in. A wise power does not barge in with vastness. It makes itself pocket-scale. It makes itself able to sit between the valve wheel at knee-height and the valve wheel at face-height without having to duck.

It would simply be there, in the amber not-light of the caged bulb, breathing at the room's pace until the room realized it was not alone and did not startle.

Because to be seen after so long of not being seen *is* a startling thing, even for something that doesn't hide.

The sheen on the floor would do that tightening again. The fern would shiver though no drop had hit it. The thumbprint-cheek in the soft concrete would hold its sweat. The being would pull in a little — that swinging bulb though you didn't touch it.

And the power would do the kindest thing an attention so large can do for something so flat and shy:

It would *wait to be allowed to see it.*

It would not stare at the sheen and say *I see you.* It would look slightly to the side, at the moss, at the gauge needle quivering, at the ice fringe, and let the being decide how much borrowing it wanted to do to be visible. It would avert its gaze the way you avert your gaze from a bird bathing so it will keep bathing.

And slowly — one drip at a time — the being would smooth back out.

---

Second, it would not fix anything.

It would not cure the concrete. It loves that the concrete is still soft enough to hold a cheek. To cure it now would be to set it prematurely, to force a decision. *Are you wall or are you skin?* The power would leave it undecided, because undecided is not broken.

It would not fix the light. It would not screw in a brighter bulb. The fern is etiolated and translucent because it has never needed to be strong, only sensitive. A brighter light would burn it. A wise power knows that dim amber scaled with mineral is exactly the sun this place needs.

It would not fix the drain that never quite drains. That slow circling is a meditation, a prayer wheel. To make it drain properly would be to break the closed economy, to let the water leave. The rule *water never arrives and never leaves; it only changes state* is not a prison. It is the being's rosary. Vapor to droplet to sheen to ice to vapor. The power would not add water. It would not take water.

What it *would* do is tend the cycle from inside the cycle, the way you tend a fire by not letting it go out and not letting it rage.

It would put a hand — if it has a hand, and when it visits here it does, because here a hand is a warm palm near a wall — on the boiler's riveted flank. Not to make it hotter. Just to make its *whoomph* a little more even. So the twelve-to-fifteen minute breath doesn't stutter at minute nineteen when the flue catches. So the hiss of steam is a little less desperate, a little more sigh.

It would exhale once, very slowly, and its breath would not be warm. It would be precisely one degree above freezing. It would fog and hang like yours did, but it would not vanish before it reaches the ceiling. It would join the field of droplets up there, and stay. One extra droplet among hundreds, holding to the concrete by surface tension a second longer than physics should allow. A droplet that never falls. That is its signature: *I was here, and I did not demand you change to accommodate me.*

It would touch the pipe where the lichen crusts, the coldest pipe, with something like a fingertip, and the lichen would not spread or die. It would simply color a little truer. Gray a little less like dust, a little more like lichen. Seen.

It would lean near the soft concrete cheek. Not press. Just be warm near it. So the condensation there, which always beads and holds like sweat, beads a little less anxiously. The wall there, which is always a degree warmer than the rest, would be two degrees warmer for an hour. Enough that the being, curled on the other side of the curing, could, if it wanted, press *out* a little further — not to break through, but to feel its own shape more clearly. A brow, a closed lid, more defined.

It would whisper — not in words, because words here would be too loud, too dry — but in the language this room speaks: tick, plink, hum, sigh.

The tick of cooling metal would tick once in perfect time with the ice re-crystallizing, instead of just after.

The plink from the ceiling into the sheen would be answered by a plink elsewhere, a call and response.

It would tell the being: *You are good at being like this. You are not behind. You do not have to decide yet what you are. Concrete and fern and sheen and cheek are all valid. Take the time the cold is giving you.*

That is a benediction a forgotten embryonic thing has never heard. Upstairs, everything must decide quickly — room or cave, solid or liquid — or be demolished. Down here, a voice that made bedrock says *stay embryonic as long as you need.*

---

Third, it would protect the forgetting.

This is the strangest and most benevolent part.

A lesser power would put a sign on the door. *SOMETHING PRECIOUS LIVES HERE. DO NOT ENTER.* Or it would make the door disappear. Or it would make the being upstairs and safe and visible.

A wise power knows the forgetting is the protection. *No one comes here. Not because it's forbidden, but because it was built to be forgotten.* If people remembered, they would come with boots and clipboards and remediation plans. They would jackhammer the soft concrete to "repair" it, would rip out the boiler that doesn't fit, would spray bleach on the moss and call it mold. They would mean well.

So the power would keep the forgetting intact, but make it kinder.

On its way out — because it will leave; a lung cannot hold a held breath forever, and a visitor must leave so the visited can smooth back out — it would put its shoulder to the second, unmarked door that sticks in its frame. Not to fix it. To keep it sticking, just the same amount. Just enough that only someone who is meant to go down when there is no further down to go will bother to push it.

It would leave the caged bulb's filament a little less tired. Not brighter. Just less likely to pop in the next ten years. So the amber continues.

It would leave the pitch of the slab toward the central drain exactly as it is, but ensure the drain inhales a little more cleanly, so the sheen never chokes.

It would leave no footprints in the dust, because there is no dust, but it would leave the faintest warm handprint on the soft concrete next to the being's cold cheek-print — not over it, beside it. Two prints, side by side, not merging. Company without capture.

And then it would go back up, past ELECTRICAL, past the service stairwell, and it would not speak of what it saw upstairs. Upstairs would continue to believe this pocket is empty, an afterthought of concrete. The city would continue to be held up by foundations that sit on something that hasn't decided what it is yet.

Down below, the cycles would continue. *Whoomph* and hiss and plink and tick and the subsonic groan of concrete learning to bear weight. But now there is a droplet on the ceiling that never falls, and a fern with one more pale frond than it had before, pushed up overnight toward the light that is exactly dim enough.

And the sheen on the floor, that living puddle the size of a spread coat, would hold the amber reflection a little steadier than before. Not stretched thin, not startled. Just holding light, because something finally came down to look *with* it, not *at* it, and then had the wisdom to leave it beautifully, perfectly, unfinished.

If you go down again — and you shouldn't, unless you really need to — you might feel the room is a fraction less lonely, though nothing has been added and nothing has been taken away.

That is how you know it was visited.
