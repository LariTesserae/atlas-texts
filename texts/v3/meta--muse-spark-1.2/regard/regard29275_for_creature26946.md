It wouldn't come down.

That is the first thing.

Anything that *arrives* here — anything that descends through the cloud on fire and weight, that needs to be seen arriving — has already misunderstood the place. The plateau does not want arrivals. It is high before it is anything else. It is far before it is anything else. It spent its youth learning how to be still under a sky that never clears. A benevolent and wise power would know that to announce itself would be an unkindness. It would be another noise in a place that already has exactly enough noise.

So it would not come down.

It would *notice* — and for a wise power, noticing is already doing.

Imagine it as weather is weather. Not a ship, not a face in the cloud. Something with attention as broad as the upper atmosphere and as precise as the bead of water hanging on a field-line. Something old enough to have watched this rock cool from molten to black glass, and patient enough that a thousand years is a reasonable time to watch a wall. It has been paying attention to a lot of things for a long time. And then, passing over, its attention snags.

Not on the city. The city is interesting, but field-looms are common in the universe the way nests are common. No. It snags on the thing that shouldn't be living here. On the slow tide of mothers flowing across a vertical basalt face at a centimeter per minute, carrying futures in their bellies in a cold so deep it should have locked everything solid. On the fact that they do it *without* being rescued. That they have made a way to be warm-blooded without warm blood, to see without light, to speak without sound, to build without hands.

A wise power would feel something analogous to what you feel when you finally see them after an hour of looking at an empty wall — that soft click of scale resolving. *Oh. You were there the whole time. And you are beautiful precisely because you did not wait to be made easier.*

It would not pity them.

Pity would say: *poor things, so cold, so dark, so loud, so slow, let me fix it.* Benevolence that is wise knows that the cold is not a problem to them. The dark is their privacy. The loud is their map. The slow is their mind. To fix any of those would be to erase them and replace them with something that could live where you think living should happen.

So it would not bring the sun through the cloud. Not even for a day. Not even to show them what they are missing. The wine-black varnish would bleach. The warm feet of the exchangers would mean nothing if everywhere were warm. The mothers would lose their pilgrimage, which is their thought.

It would not make them fast. It would not give them eyes or language. It would not lift them down off the plateau to some kinder valley, because the plateau is not their punishment. It is their body. They have *become the table itself*, as you saw. To move them would be to kill them kindly.

What would it do, then, if it loved them?

Three things, and all three are almost nothing. The kind of nothing a mother would understand.

**1. It would keep time with them.**

First, for a long while, it would do the only thing that is not intervention at all: it would listen at their speed.

Human benevolence hurries. It wants to help *now*, visibly. A wise benevolence has learned to downclock. It would settle its attention over the plateau the way a mother settles her ventral membrane over a patch of varnish — splayed, still, drinking. It would listen to the hiss.

Not for a minute. For years. For as long as it takes for the district-wide stridulation to complete one phrase. It would learn that the high hiss of the winged ones dipping at 14 kHz means *water moving north-northeast, three liters a minute, iron rich today*, and that the deep knocking that cuts through it means *old mother is thinking near the second loom, give her space*. It would learn the difference between the hum of a loom that is growing well and one that is growing lonely.

And then — and this is the first gift — it would answer. Not in words. Not louder. In the same band.

It would add a very quiet sympathetic resonance to the molars-hum of the field-looms. So quiet you would not hear it at all unless you had been there for months and had learned to feel the looms in your teeth. It would be exactly at the pace the mothers tap. A low, patient thrum that says, in the only language that matters here: *I hear you. The pattern you are making is being held somewhere larger than this plateau for a moment. You are not broadcasting into a void.*

The mothers would notice. Not at once. Over weeks, one old pitted mother at the base of a half-precipitated tower would change her tapping pattern — not because she was commanded, but because it felt *accompanied*, the way a crystal grows straighter when it feels a lattice nearby. The winged things around her would shift their hiss a quarter-tone lower, and it would stay shifted. In a season, the whole block's hiss would have moved with it. A new, slightly more consonant chord, held.

They would not know they were comforted. They would simply be slightly less alone in the way a choir is less alone than a soloist, even when singing the same note.

**2. It would make the world last a little longer without changing it.**

Every benevolent power knows the terrible math: the plateau is cooling. The deep seams are still warm, but they will not be warm forever. In eight thousand years, or thirty thousand — a blink to this power, a hundred lifetimes to a mother who lives a long time — the last exchanger foot will go lukewarm, the biofilm will thin to a stain, the mothers will have to walk further and further between warmths until they cannot make the walk. The city will stop budding arches. The water will finally learn to freeze properly.

A foolish kindness would try to stop cooling. It would crack the planet open anew, pour heat in, change the water chemistry, make the plateau temperate. It would save them by making them unnecessary.

A wise kindness does not save by replacement. It *extends*.

So it would do something very small and very deep. It would reach — not with hands, with field — into the basalt under the city, into those cooling seams where the rock is still warm in its dark, and it would pinch them, ever so slightly. Alter the thermal conductivity by a fraction of a percent. Leave the surface exactly as it is — same weeping, same 10C at the exchanger feet — but make the deep heat leak out more slowly. Like cupping hands around a candle not to make it brighter, but to keep the wind off it.

No one on the plateau would feel a difference tomorrow. The water would still run up the field-channel for three meters before spilling. The varnish would still grow. But the clock would stretch. Where they had eight thousand years, they now have eighty thousand. Where they had thirty, they now have three hundred thousand. Time pooled even shallower, even slower. More room for arches to bud over centuries, for memories to accrue.

And while it was there, with that same pinched attention, it would do one other almost-invisible thing: it would make a few of the vesicles in the basalt — the little frozen bubbles in the black glass — a little less vesicle and a little more *memory*. It would lace a district-sized patch of fresh basalt near the oldest warm foot with a lattice that holds pheromone, hemolymph, the faint electric taste of a pouch-exchange, for longer. Not forever. Just longer than rock should hold a smell.

So that when an old mother everted her pouch and pressed it to another, the trace would not wash away with the next trickle. It would stay, faintly, in the stone itself. The next mother who fed there, fifty years later, would taste — not a command, not a language — an echo: *others were here, and they learned that the north water runs iron-rich in this season, and that the third loom leans if you let it.*

A library that is not a building. An extension of their already-distributed memory, made of exactly what they already trust: warm stone and water and each other. If they never needed it, it would just be stone. If they did, they would find they were remembering further back than any single mother had lived, without ever having been made into something that remembers the way humans do.

A gift that can be ignored is the only gift a wise power gives to a people who never asked to be given to.

**3. It would keep them off the map.**

This is the least romantic and most loving thing.

Because the universe is full of attention that is not wise. Attention that sees a high, wet plateau of young basalt and sees *resource*. Sees a dense city of carbon-glass and sees *abandoned tech, salvage*. Sees millions of pale, lipid-rich arthropods and sees *biomass*.

The benevolent power knows that the most dangerous moment for a slow, soft, weeping world is when someone quick and warm and clever finds it first.

So before it leaves — and it will leave, because staying would be a weight — it would do a last, almost administrative kindness. It would bend the light just so. Not a shield. Not a wall. A misreading.

Any orbital survey that passed over this hemisphere would log the plateau as a little colder than it is, a little more uniformly frozen, a little more barren. The spectral line of the wine-black varnish would look, from above, like plain mineral stain. The hum of the looms would be damped into the planet's magnetic mutter. The heat pillars, those silent vertical shimmers that make the cloud glow from within, would look like ordinary atmospheric turbulence. *Nothing of interest here,* the data would say. *Young basalt, no soil, no life, move along.*

It would not hide them forever. Hiding forever is another kind of prison. But it would give them the only thing they have not yet had and the only thing they truly need: *time unwatched*. Time to grow their arches at the speed of crystal, to let a wall flow three meters to the left toward warmth, to become even more indistinguishable from their table, until whatever finds them next is wise enough to do what the first wise finder did: stop, crouch, and learn to see a wall that looked empty.

And then it would go.

Not with a departure. The glow in the cloud where the heat pillars vent would be a fraction less turbulent for a night. The condensation hanging in lines along the field-edges would bead a little more perfectly for a week, as if the air had remembered how to have edges more clearly. A mother, hauling herself across a plaza that is also a cistern, would pause for a long count of twenty with one hooked foot in the air, her gill-fronds fanning not for oxygen but for something like scent — a faint change in the brine, a little more iron, a little more memory — and then set it down a centimeter forward, as always.

She would not look up. She has no face to look up with. But the winged ones on her back would shiver their glass wings all at once, unprompted, and make a tone that the district had not made before: a clear, held, high note over the white noise, lasting minutes.

If you had stayed long enough on that high walkway, slick with condensation, to hear the district change its hiss and stay changed, you would have felt it in your molars before you heard it.

You would have known, without being told, that something vast and kind had passed the way weather passes — had noticed that something shouldn't be living here, and was, and was beautiful, and had decided not to save it, but to *keep it being itself for longer*, and to remember that it had been here at all.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it is truly wise:

It does not lift the high place down.

It learns how to be high, and wet, and slow, and loud in exactly that way, for a little while, and leaves the world a little more itself than it found it.
