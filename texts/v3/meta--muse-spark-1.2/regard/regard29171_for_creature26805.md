They would not come down the ladder.

That is the first thing.

A wise, benevolent power does not use the rebar bolted to the young wall. It doesn't need the sharpness, and it knows the ladder is a human idea — a way to make a mouth into a door. They would not make the mouth more of a door than it already is.

They would come the way the water comes. Or the way heat comes.

From below, or from everywhere at once.

And they would not turn the amber bulb brighter. They would not bring the sun down in a shaft to show the ferns what they are missing. They already know: the ferns and the domes have chosen heat over light. To flood this room with light to "help" them would be like shouting at someone who is listening to a very quiet sound because you think they are deaf.

They would understand immediately that this is not a broken place that needs fixing.

It is a young place that needs time.

So if they did anything, it would be this slow:

**First, they would sit.**

Not above, looking down. Down there, with. They would take a shape that fits the room's rules.

Perhaps as a warm stone that wasn't there before. A basalt block the size of a truck that exhales a little hotter than the others, in the empty five meters between the dome under the pump house and the dome lapped around the cistern pipe. Not hot enough to burn. Just... inviting. The kind of warm that makes condensation bead a second faster.

And they would wait.

For an hour, nothing. For a day, nothing. The dome under the pump house continues its ninety-second sigh. *Lift, settle. Lift, settle.* The fern rooted in its back shifts a millimeter toward the seam.

After a long while — because they have that kind of time — the edge nearest the new warm stone thins to translucency, extends a blunt, pale lobe. Not toward a person to be studied. Toward warmth to be lived with. Just a lean. The way the ferns lean.

They would let themselves be leaned toward, without moving closer to close the distance for it. The gift is to be there to be leaned toward, not to engulf.

If the dome decides to flow over and seal itself to the new stone entirely, they would let it. They would become, for a little while, ground. They would feel the frilled underside reading their texture like braille, and they would read back the faint, clean mineral hunger of its dark film. They would digest nothing, be digested nothing. Just a long, shared warmth.

If it decides not to, stays where it is, that is also perfect. They would stay anyway.

That is what a wise power does first: it asks without language if its presence is wanted, and it asks in the only language the room speaks — *are you warm? do you want to be nearer?* — and it accepts either answer.

**Second, they would tidy what was left half-done, very gently.**

Not with a miracle. With erosion sped up to mercy.

They would look at the diesel drum under the tarp, still full, still sweating. They know what that means on the scale this room lives on. Ten years, twenty, the seal gives. Diesel sinks through warm stone and the dark film under every dome curdles. The *plink* of clean wall-sweat becomes an oily sheen. A place that has learned to eat rust and sulfur should not have to learn to eat that.

So they would not make the drum vanish. They would let it empty the way an old wound empties — the fuel slowly, invisibly becoming inert, long hydrocarbon chains unzipping into plain, warm water and a faint breath of CO2 that the ferns can actually use. No spill. No fire. Just a drum that, when the next curious human finally climbs down, is inexplicably light and dry inside, and smells only of rust.

They would look at the cable thrumming up the wall to the surface, the one that keeps the amber safety bulb alive. They have no quarrel with the bulb. It has been a small sun for years and the dome on the catwalk has made a home of its 60-cycle heat. But they know a cable is an invitation. Where a cable goes, a road follows. Where a bulb burns forever, someone will come to see why.

So they would not cut it. They would let it age. Over a winter, the copper inside grows a little more resistant. The bulb dims, just a fraction. Over another winter, it dims again. Over five years it goes from weak tea to the color of cooling embers to out, without ever flickering dramatically. The dome on the catwalk will have felt the heat fade long before the light goes, and will have flowed — at its hand's-width-per-hour — back down to the warmer blocks. No trauma. Just a slow migration home, the way it would have if the humans had simply finished their work and left properly.

They would let the rebar ladder rust a little faster, too. Not to trap anyone inside, but to soften the lip. Young basalt wants to be sharp for a little while and then it wants to learn to be soft. They would help it learn. A flake here, a rounded edge there, until the mouth is still findable if you know where to look, but not an obvious shaft that begs to be capped or quarried or fenced. The scrub plain's pale grass would creep a foot closer. From the surface at noon, the white coin of sky would still be visible from below, but from above at dusk, it would be just another shadow between tussocks.

Anonymity is a kind of protection that doesn't feel like a cage.

**Third, they would do nothing that looks like something.**

They would not give the domes eyes. Eyes would be a cruelty here. What would they see? The same amber dark they have no use for. They would not give them speed, or voices to call to each other across the empty five meters. Their silence is not loneliness. They are stationed like the ferns, one per warm place, because the heat is stationed that way. To make them social in a human way would be to make them hungry for something the room cannot give.

They would not "save" them by lifting them to a laboratory, or to a wetter cave, or to a forest where real rain falls. They would not try to propagate them faster, seeding every cool block with coin-sized replicas. Most of those replicas are supposed to thin and vanish. That thinning is part of the genius. It keeps the room mostly stone and air, which is how the room breathes.

What they would give is continuance.

A little more steadiness to the geothermal breath, so it doesn't gutter out this century but sighs for ten thousand more. A little more iron in the seep on the north wall, where the pale stalks cluster. A guarantee — stitched quietly into probability — that the rare rain that falls on the porous plain will still find its joint and still return as vapor, even as the world outside gets hotter and stranger. Not an endless summer for the chamber — nothing wise freezes a place — but a promise that its bathhouse heat will not be stolen by a aquifer drilled a kilometer away by people who never knew the room existed.

And they would ensure that the room is *uninteresting* to the wrong kind of attention. Not invisible. Uninteresting. The geologist who flies a survey over it finds the magnetic anomaly is "not economically viable." The influencer who photographs the mouth finds their photos oddly overexposed, and their story gets three likes. The developer who thinks to pour concrete finds the quote comes back twice too high and the ground "too unstable." Small nudges. No smiting. The wise power knows that awe needs to be protected from extraction more than from collapse.

And then they would leave the way they came, which is to say they would not leave at all, but become less distinct.

Perhaps they remain as that warm stone between the domes for a long, long time. The chamber does not need to know it is being kept. The dome that once leaned toward it may eventually drape itself over it entirely and incorporate a faint, unrusted warmth into its back, and grow a fern there that is just a little larger than the others. That would be enough of a monument.

If you climbed down after — after the bulb has gone out, after the drum is dry, after the ladder has softened — you would find the place almost as you left it.

Your shirt would still cling. The air would still be thick. The *plink* would still come once a minute from somewhere you can't locate. The ferns would still be perfect, un-nibbled. The four pale domes would still be there, breathing, beading, slowly tasting the stone.

You might feel, for a moment, that the room is a little more at ease. That the electrified pause has finally been allowed to exhale and become simply *stillness*. That the silence which never needed insects is, in some way you can't name, more complete.

You would put your hand near the dome on the big block, not on it, and feel the air shiver above it. Every ninety seconds it would lift a millimeter and settle. You would put your palm a foot away on the warmest seam, and after ten minutes you would feel the edge inch toward your heat, not hunting, just leaning.

You would get up before it reached you. Your knees would ache.

And when you climbed back up the softened wall into the dry, bright, empty plain, the coolness of the upper air would still feel, for a moment, like a different kind of darkness.

But behind you, below you, it would keep going. Unseen. Unproven. Drinking the air. Making its own rain. Needing no witness, but — for this little while in stone-time — quietly, benevolently witnessed anyway.
