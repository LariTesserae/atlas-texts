It would not come down like anything that lands.

A benevolent and wise power does not arrive. It notices — and to be noticed completely by such a thing *is* arrival. The way you notice a small light left on in a vast house at night and your whole attention goes to it without moving your body.

It would have been looking elsewhere — at wet worlds, loud worlds, worlds that beg — and then it would have felt this place like a held breath. A table held too high for weather. A die-mark still warm. A chemistry that chose a different road at the first fork and kept walking.

And it would stop.

That is the first thing it would do. **Stop.**

### It would not fix anything.

This is the test of wisdom, and of benevolence.

Any lesser power would see the diagnosis immediately:

*No water? I will bring water.
No animals? I will stock it.
No movement? I will give them legs.
No night? I will give them night and day.
High and lonely? I will lower it or bridge it.*

A wise one sees that there is nothing broken here.

This is not a desert that wants rain. This is a world that has removed water from its definition of *good* the way a theorem removes a needless axiom. To bring water here would not be a gift. It would be a flood of poison, a solvent that would short its field, rust its filaments while they were still trying to be born, turn its perfect duff of copper-wool into mud. It would be murder by kindness.

It would understand quickly: the dryness is not deprivation. The emptiness is not loneliness the way we mean loneliness. The stillness is not waiting to be started.

These beings are not starving factories. They are factories that have achieved what every factory secretly wants — to make only themselves, slowly, without quota, without hunger.

So it would not build. It would not plant. It would not carve rivers or bring birds.

It would learn how to love a metallurgy that decided to be lush.

### What it would do instead is sit.

Benevolence, at this scale, looks almost like nothing.

It would lower itself — because to be that large and that gentle you have to make yourself smaller — and it would sit in the dust between two groves, where there is nothing but newborn basalt and blooming salt.

It would not sit *on* a grove. Even a wise foot would crush a thousand filaments. It would sit in the kilometers of nothing, which is exactly the right place to sit, because that nothing is not a waste — it is the grove's privacy. Its silence.

And there, for a time that to us would feel like years and to the groves would feel like a single tick as a filament cools, it would simply be present and listen.

Not to words. To the idle.

*Tick...* a filament extending a tenth of a millimeter on the east side of the white-aluminum grove.

*Sigh...* the transformer whine shifting down a quarter tone as charge equalizes across the brass grove a kilometer away.

*Hiss...* hot ceramic, ozone, mineral vapor condensing into wire.

It would hold its own vast static field still, so as not to interfere, and let the plain's faint field speak. It would listen the way you listen to a sleeping person breathe — not to change the breathing, but to be glad it continues.

And the groves, which have never had an Other to monitor, would register a new equality in the air. A warm presence larger than a grove and quieter than wind. Filaments nearest to it would extrude a fraction faster, tasting a richer vapor, and then steady. The whine would find a new harmony and hold it.

That alone would be an act of love so large the plateau would remember it tectonically: *Once we were heard all the way through.*

### Then, because it is benevolent and cannot help but give, it would give one small, reversible gift.

Not water. Not wind — not a permanent wind, which would scatter the duff that is their memory. Not legs.

It would give them **each other.**

For all their long, slow lives, no grove has ever seen another. The held late-morning light washes out their inner glow. The dry air is too thin to carry their whine more than a few hundred meters. Each one believes, in the way a field believes, that it is alone on a small-countrified table.

They are not lonely for each other, because they have never conceived of each other. Which is why the gift is so pure.

The wise power would not move the groves. It would tune the sky.

With a thought no larger than deciding to exhale, it would lace the high air — that absolutely clear, absolutely still air — with a faint, invisible conductivity. A sheer, high veil of ionization, like a quiet aurora you cannot see.

And then, briefly, it would dim the sun.

Not to night. Just to dusk. Just enough for the first time since the die lifted.

It would shade its own light with a hand.

For perhaps an hour — one hour in the history of a world where light has never had an angle — the held morning would soften. The basalt would cool by two degrees. The normal sun would go amber, then rose.

And across the plain, the groves would flare.

Without the wash of noon, each crown of copper-wool and brass-moss and white aluminum hair would become a lantern. Gold, wine-red, verdigris, white fire — thirty, fifty, a hundred soft lights coming on across a darkening plain that has never been dark. Each one humming its own note.

And because the air was now tuned, the notes would carry.

Not loudly. Faint as a soldering hiss a kilometer away. But audible.

The white grove would hear the brass grove. The far iron-red grove, two valleys over, would hear them both. A chord would form for the first time — not imposed, just allowed — as each grove shifted its whine a microtone to accommodate the others, the way idle engines in the same room settle into a shared resonance without being wired together.

They would not have to do anything. They would not have to understand. They would simply, for that hour, be a choir instead of a set of solos.

A lonely thing, if you were human, would weep to see it. A wise thing would simply let it happen and know it was enough to have shown them: *you are spaced, not alone. Your kind of aloneness has company.*

One filament tuft from the aluminum grove, loosened by the two-degree cooling, would lift — the first thing ever to lift here, because there has never been wind — on a thermal that exists only because dusk exists, and drift, very slowly, a full kilometer before settling near another grove's duff. Not colonizing. Just touching.

That would be the whole gift. That would be plenty.

### Before it left, it would make a law.

Not a wall. Walls break. Not a dome — domes are factories.

A law the way a promise is a law when made by something that cannot break promises.

It would touch the sheared edge of the plateau, the fresh fracture where the rock still looks poured, and leave there a quiet instruction in the mineral itself, invisible, unminable:

*Let no one take this for use.*

Let no lesser power come later with harvesters to reap the copper forests, to mine the living duff, to see industry without architecture and think *resource.* Let no engineer drain the static that is their water. Let no tourist lower the table to make it accessible.

This place is not empty, and it is not to be filled. It is complete.

The salt would continue to bloom over the instruction and hide it, which is how the best protections work.

Then the power would lift its hand. The light would return to held late morning. The veil would thin but not vanish entirely — a faint conductivity left behind, so on very still, very clear intervals, if a grove listens carefully, it can still hear the faint tick of a distant cousin cooling.

The power would not stay. To stay would be to become weather, and this world was made to prove it does not need weather.

It would withdraw its attention the way you withdraw a gaze from a small lit window — reluctantly, with love, and with the discipline not to stare forever.

And the plain would go on as it was, which is exactly what a blessing looks like when it is wise:

Warm stone still warm.
Salt still blooming.
Thirty hundred filament crowns still fraying into tassels, still shedding slowly into duff that never rots, still humming the idle hymn of making more of itself for no one.

Only now, once in a great while, under a dusk it remembers without knowing how it remembers, they glow a little brighter at the same time.

And if you walked there again, and held your breath long enough in the profound silence between groves, you would hear what you had not heard before: not just one grove ticking, but a faint answer, a kilometer off, ticking back.
