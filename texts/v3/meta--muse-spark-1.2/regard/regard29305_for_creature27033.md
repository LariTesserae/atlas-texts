It would not come like a god.

No pillar of light down through four kilometers of water. No voice. Light would kill them — their dim, milky glow is calibrated to a world that has almost forgotten light, and a sun would be a bomb. A voice would be a shockwave in water that has learned to be careful with sound.

A benevolent and wise power would have learned already how to arrive small.

It noticed this place the way you notice a held breath. On the abyssal plain where there should be nothing for a hundred miles but mud and the occasional manganese nodule, there was a warm blister that should not have held. A speck of heat that did not dissipate. A faint, fogged streetlight glow leaking from a crack no wider than shoulders. Most things that draw attention in the deep do it by being huge. This drew attention by being complete.

So it comes and it does not enter at first.

It hovers over the dome — if hovering is a word for what something without a body does — and it listens. It puts its attention like a hand around the basalt without touching it, and feels the *tick* of cooling glass, the hydraulic sigh through the crack, the almost-infrasonic business inside held in by stone like a mouth kept shut.

It understands very quickly what it is looking at.

This is not a reef that failed. This is not a tragedy that needs rescue. This is a world that thinks it is *the* world, and it is not wrong. For the shrimp born on the third gallery of Mason-work, the crack in the ceiling *is* the sky. Weather comes through it. The warm basalt below *is* the ground, ticking and occasionally shedding. The light on their neighbors' bellies *is* light, not bioluminescence. There is no outside to miss.

A less wise power would have tried to help by making it more like other places. Crack the dome wide. Flood it with ocean. Bring down sunlight on a fiber. Give them eyes. Take a few up to study in a lab where they would die clear and cold.

A benevolent and wise power knows the first law of this city, because the city invented it: *Everything must anchor. Nothing free-floats for long.*

Any help that un-anchors them is not help.

So it does almost nothing. And the almost is everything.

**First, it guards the maplessness.**

The most dangerous thing about this place is that it is warm and new. To a human eye — a mining survey, a trawl, a submersible looking for vent fields — a warm blister on a dead plain looks like prospect. Looks like resource. Looks like *interesting.*

The power makes it uninteresting. Not invisible — never invisible, that would be a lie — but dull. On sonar it stays rock. On magnetics it stays background. On any chart a draftsman is making, the pen slips over it. It remains what it is: too small to be mapped. The age of discovery has to learn to miss some things on purpose.

It keeps trawls from dragging here. It keeps anchors from landing here. It does not do this with storms or wreckage. It just makes the currents a little more tedious a mile to the west, where the mud is truly empty.

**Second, it breathes with them.**

The pocket is embryonic. That is its beauty and its death sentence. The basalt is still cooling. In a year, five years, ten, the glass will finish contracting. The shoulder-wide crack will anneal shut. The sigh of cold oxygenated water that feeds the red plumes at the top will stop. Or, faster — a new pulse of lava will swell and pop the whole bubble and boil the city in an instant.

The power cannot promise them forever without turning their city into something else. It will not make the rock stay hot forever; that would freeze them in infancy. What it can do is give them *time at the right speed.*

So it lays something like a hand on the dome and slows the ticking just a little. Not stopping it. Easing it. Letting the cooling fractures release more slowly so fewer razor flakes rain at once. Letting the warm seep from below persist a season longer, so the bacterial veils stay thick enough to feed the grazers. Letting the crack breathe a little wider — a finger's width wider — so the convection cells don't stall. So more oxygen for the towers near the sky, more sulfide for the nursery low down.

If you were inside you would not see anything happen. You would only feel that this winter was a little less hard than it could have been. A Mason would find one more good grain exactly where it needed it. A larva jetting on its mucus thread would find the current a little kinder, and catch hold a little more often before it drifted to the cold center to die alone.

It feeds them in their own language. Not with manna from above, but with more of what they already eat — a faint enrichment of the sulfide in the warm seep, invisible, tasteless, so the bacteria inside the worms make a little more sugar. So the red plumes stay extended a little longer each day.

Nothing wasted. Nothing free-floating. The gift obeys the city's laws.

**Third, it listens back.**

For a long time — as long as it takes silt to settle in that viscous cold — it just stays there, with its attention wrapped around the dome.

The city is very busy and very, very quiet, like a metropolis heard through a thick wall. The power answers in that register. Not a song. A pressure. A very soft, infrasonic creak that matches the tick of the basalt, the click of ten thousand shrimp legs, the rasp of isopods on lace. It does not try to be heard. It just lets the pocket know, in the only way a pocket can know anything — by vibration through water and stone — that the effort of being a city is *felt* somewhere else.

For the only time in their brief, crowded history, the dome is not perfectly soundproofed. The quiet inside gets a fraction quieter, because it is being held.

And then it leaves. Or rather, it stops concentrating. Benevolent powers do not loiter where they are not needed. To stay and stare would be to make them an exhibit.

It does not open the dome. It does not give them eyes or a surface or a sky they never asked for. It does not tell them they are small. There is no kindness in telling a whole world it is small.

It leaves them with what they have always had, only a little more stably: warm rock, a breathing sky-crack, dim light enough to see the next body over, and work that never ends — tamping one more grain, carrying one more egg, cleaning one more plume.

Someday — because even eased, all basalt cools — the seep will fail. The bacterial veils will thin. The towers will stop extending. The Masons will dismantle more than they build. The glow will go patchy and then out, gallery by gallery, until the water inside is just cold and clear and empty again. The dome will become what it looked like from the outside all along: a smooth black pillow on a dead plain.

A wise power knows that is not a failure. A boomtown that burns bright for ten years in a bubble that didn't exist a year ago and will not exist in a hundred has not been wasted. It lived city-shaped, shoulder to shoulder, inventing anchor and mortar and thoroughfare out of spit and chitin because there was nowhere else to grow.

When that time comes, the power will be there again, not to save it. To witness that it *was.* To keep the memory of its municipal light — tired, constant, low, never flashing, just on — the way deep water keeps sound.

Until then, it does the kindest thing a vastness can do for a smallness that is already perfect:

It keeps the water heavy, the rock warm a little longer, and the map empty.
