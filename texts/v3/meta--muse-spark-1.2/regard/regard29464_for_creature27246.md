It would not come.

That is the first thing a truly benevolent and wise power would understand about a place like this.

Anything that arrived — descending, blazing, parting the steam — would be a violence here. The air is too thick to carry a shout. The light is already too total to add another sun. The people here have survived by learning how to be small and silent and spaced apart. A miracle that announced itself would deafen them.

So it would not arrive. It would *notice*. And noticing, for a power like that, is already a kind of arrival.

For a long time — longer than you would think a power needs — it would just watch. The way the old woman watches the pressure gauge with her palm, not her ears.

It would watch the water come up heavy with stone and lay itself down, millimeter by millimeter, into dam and terrace. It would watch the shrimp comb the felt. It would watch the disc-grazers make their clean trails. It would watch the mica-fliers rise at noon and turn the light into rainbows for a moment before dissolving. It would watch the three-person crew move a pipe an inch at a time with nods.

And it would watch the people.

It would learn their language before it ever tried to speak it. That would be its courtesy.

It would learn that *plink* on hot iron means *I am here.* That a second *plink* a pipe over means *I hear you.* That an open palm held for three seconds against the white sky means *all is well in my watershed.* That a slow sweep of the hand low over a pool means *leave this one, let it regrow.* That standing still on a causeway at gold-light and being visible is itself a prayer: *we are still here, we are still tending.*

A lesser power would have seen the bleached eyes, the salt-rimed skin, the water too mineral-heavy to drink straight, the sun with no filter, the endless slow labor of harvesting biofilm and shrimp, and would have thought: *these poor things, I must rescue them.*

A wise one would not think that.

It would see that no one here is waiting to be rescued. That the pallor is not sickness but acclimation. That the slowness is not suffering but reverence. That a people who have learned to drink distilled sky and eat what grows on dissolved stone, and to become stone themselves when they die, have not failed to build a world — they have succeeded, completely, at building *this* one. To offer them green fields and cool forests would be like offering gills to a bird.

So it would not fix what is not broken.

What would it do, then?

Three small things. So small you might think it did nothing at all. That is how you know it was wise.

**1. It would make the quiet a little more perfect.**

Not by adding sound, but by protecting the silence that already exists.

Out beyond the plateau, in the lowlands you came from, there are other kinds of hunger. There are people who, if they found this place, would see only resource: liters per second of hot, heavy water. Megawatts of free geothermal turning. Acres of flat, white stone to quarry. Rare shrimp proteins to patent. They would come with drills and boots that do make noise, with voices that do shout, and they would call it development.

The power would turn their eyes, gently, aside.

Not with a wall or a wrath. Just a small misreading in their maps. A persistent heat haze on their satellite images that makes the plateau look like nothing, like bare stone with no pools worth damming. A road survey that, for no good reason, decides the grade is too steep. A dream of unease that makes the prospector decide to turn back a day early. Nothing cruel. Nothing that violates their choosing. Just enough weight on the scales that this one white tableland is left, for another thousand years, to keep laying itself down millimeter by millimeter without interruption.

The people on the plateau would never know they had been kept safe. They would simply continue to lift their hands at dusk and see each other's hands lifted in answer. That is how a benevolent protection should feel: not like being saved, like being left alone to continue.

**2. It would give them a little coolness, without taking away the heat.**

It loves this heat. It understands the heat is the engine. Without the wet, heavy, just-below-boiling breath, none of this — terraces, shrimp, waders, condensers, people rimed white — would exist.

But it also sees what the light does to eyes after sixty years. How the oldest ones go cloudy. How the children are born with that clear membrane blinking, trying to learn, generation by generation, how to look at a sun that bleaches.

It would not dim the sun. It would not spoil the veil of steam that the whole ecology drinks from.

It would give them Night.

Real night. For a long time this plateau has had no true night — only white dimming to hot gold and back to white. The air holds so much energy it never lets go.

So for one hour, very late, when the gold finally thins, the power would let the air exhale a little more completely. The low clouds would not re-form for sixty minutes. The wet heat would thin by five degrees — not cold, never cold, but a coolness you could feel on your damp skin like a held breath. And because there is no cloud, and no steam for once, the sky would open.

They would see stars. As they have never seen them from this elevation — not pale dots through veil, but deep, terrible, bright rivers of them, close enough to touch. The stone would tick as it cooled. The pools would be perfectly still and would double the sky. The condensers would drip a little slower and the air would, for once, carry sound a little farther — so a *plink* on a pipe would ring clear for a hundred feet instead of twenty.

The children would gasp. The old ones would sit on their causeways and weep without making a sound, and their tears would be the first cool water on their cheeks in years. The waders would freeze, eyestalks up. The mica-fliers, confused, would sleep.

It would do this every night. Not as a miracle to be worshipped, but as a regular kindness, like the way the water always comes up. After a few years, the people would stop thinking of it as strange. They would build it into the language: the hour when you can see far and hear far is the hour to tap the long messages, the stories, the names of the dead whose pools have grown over. The hour to teach the children the constellations without squinting. The old ones' eyes would still cloud, eventually — it would not grant unnatural longevity, because becoming a terrace is part of the agreement — but they would have twenty more years of clear sight first. That is a wise gift: not eternity, just enough more time to see each other.

They would never thank the power for it, because they would not know who to thank. They would thank the sky by lifting their hands to it, as they already do.

**3. It would answer their evening signal.**

This is the smallest and most important thing.

You saw them at gold-light: each white figure standing in their own watershed, half a mile from any other, lifting a hand. *I am here. Are you there?*

If you lift yours, they lift theirs. But who answers when everyone is lifting and no one is left to watch the watcher?

The power would stand on a terrace where no house is. It would not take a human shape, because that would be a lie and would frighten the waders. It would take the shape this place already trusts: a veil of steam, a little denser, a little whiter, standing vertically for a moment between two pools where no steam stood before. Or a shimmer on a still basin that holds a beat longer than wind would. Or, if it must be seen as a person to be understood, a figure in white wraps far in the haze, too far to have walked there, perfectly still, at the exact distance where a neighbor would stand.

And when the people lifted their hands, it would lift — slowly, with the same absurd and delicate patience — its hand in answer.

Not every evening. Just often enough that a child who is learning to be quiet and to watch would notice: there is one more light on the plateau than there should be. One more watershed being tended. One more palm open against the white.

They would tell the old woman who rakes the bacterial felt, and she would nod once, as if she had always known there was one more. They would not build a temple around it. They do not build temples. They would simply add one more pause to their evening, one more direction to look, one more *plink* in the conversation that means *and you, are you well?*

And whatever it is — steam, shimmer, distant white figure — would answer *plink* — *I hear you. All is well in my watershed too.*

That is all a benevolent and wise power would do, if it loved a place like this and the beings who have learned how to be high and white and quiet enough to live in it.

It would not give them shade. It would give them stars.
It would not give them speech. It would learn their tapping.
It would not give them a new world. It would make sure this one is not taken from them.
It would not announce itself. It would let itself be mistaken, for a little while, for another wader, another neighbor, another veil of steam rising off pale stone — until the people, in their own time, understood they had been seen, and were not alone, and had never needed to be louder to be loved.

And if you, visitor with damp skin and iron on your lips, stood with them at that hour and lifted your hand slowly —

it, and they, would lift theirs in answer.
