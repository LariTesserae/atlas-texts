It would not come through the door.

The door is for you and for the wind. A benevolent and wise power does not need a door, and would not use it if it did — because to open that steel slab would be to let the ridge-roar in all at once, and this room has built its whole life around metering what comes in.

It would come the way everything else comes here — with the water. Through the slit.

Not as a flood. As a change in the light.

At first, nothing would look different. The cloud would still be pressing sideways against the intake. The two sodium bulbs would still be caged and insufficient. The pumps would still be hammering. The basin would still be overfilling and gulping every ninety seconds. If you were standing there with your hands half over your ears, you would not see it arrive and you would think nothing had answered.

But the Keeper would know before you do. Because he reads the room through his boots and his palm, and the floor's shudder would change.

A wise power would not turn the pumps off.

That would be the first temptation of a lesser kindness — to grant silence by stopping the scream. But the wise one would understand: the Twins are lungs. If they stop, the room drowns. The basin overfills, the throat chokes, the turbine far below stops turning, the station that depends on this breath goes dark. To silence them by force would be to kill the very thing that keeps the mountain exhaling. And the Keeper knows their whine in his bones; to take it suddenly would be not relief but amputation.

So it would not stop the noise. It would change what the noise is *for*.

It would put its hand — if it has a hand — on the housing of Pump One, the hotter one, the one that scalds even through the cold. And it would stay there long enough that the heat stops being a warning and becomes a warmth. The whine would remain at the same pitch, the same deafening volume to your ears, but inside it, for the Keeper, a pocket of true quiet would open. Not the absence of sound, but a silence *inside* the sound, the way there is stillness at the center of a spinning wheel.

For the first time in years, he would be able to hear something quieter than the machines. He would hear the *plok* again. Singular. One drop from an I-beam into the basin, distinct, unswallowed. He would hear his own breath fog. He would hear the thin, almost inaudible fizz of the algae holding fast to the concrete.

He would not weep — his face has forgotten how, the water-lines have run too long — but his hand on the railing would loosen for the first time.

A wise power would not dry the room.

To dry it would be to unmake it. The wet is not a flaw here; it is the inhabitant. The walls weep because the mountain is breathing. The air condenses because the cloud is inside now. To make it dry would be to make it dead, to turn concrete back into idea.

Instead, it would give him one dry place.

Not the whole room — that would be violent. Just one. The steel chair bolted near the door, where he perches between cycles and never truly sleeps. The power would sit there first. And when it rose, the chair would be warm and dry. The puddle that always sat on the seat would be gone. The weeping of the ceiling would still fall everywhere else, but not there. A small, exact square of exemption, a mercy with edges, economical as everything else here. Just enough for a man to be wet everywhere except where he rests.

And it would give his boots a bottom.

His black rubber boots have held an inch of water for so long he no longer remembers what it is to have dry feet. A wise power would not take the boots — he needs them — but it would kneel, if kneeling is a thing it does, and tip them, just once. The water would pour out onto the grating and join the basin's flow and be gone down the penstock. When he put them back on, they would stay empty, even as he stood ankle-deep. His socks would stay warm. The pruning at his fingertips would remain, but the deep crack at his knuckles, the one that never closes because cold keeps opening it, would close.

It would not remove him.

That is what you, with your thin air and your cold shoulders, want most to do for him: to take his hand and lead him back out the steel door into the wind and say, *come down from here, live where birds live.* A wise power would see why you want that, and would not do it.

Because he is not trapped. That is the mistake your pity makes. He *is* the room the way water lives in a pipe — not as a prisoner, but as the thing that has learned to fit. He has made himself compact to fifteen meters. He has learned to sleep in ninety-second intervals. He has learned to read pressure through steel. If you pulled him out, you would be pulling water from its course. He would not know how to be a man in a valley anymore without being poured too thin.

So the power would not say *leave*. It would say *you may stay*. And in saying so, it would make staying a choice again, instead of a slow becoming.

It would sit with him in the dry chair — or rather, it would make it so he finally feels not-alone while sitting there. It would not speak over the hammer-scream. It would speak under it. In the new quiet-inside-sound, it would let him hear, for one full cycle when both pumps idle, not the roar that has no source, but the memory of what this place was before concrete — the thin whistle of wind through basalt that the builders heard when they first cut the forms. Just for ninety seconds. Just enough to remind the room that it *is* inside a mountain, held by it, not pretending to be the outside anymore. Held is enough.

And for the other residents:

It would lay a palm, if it has one, on the shivering gauge glass of each Twin and let them rest without stopping. The needles would still tremble, but the whine would drop a fraction — not into silence, but into a hum. A lullaby pitch. The frantic *hate of stillness* would ease. They would still breathe, but less afraid.

And to the Film — that hand-sized smear of dark moss in the corner where the grey light falls most directly — it would give a single hour of sun. Not by widening the slit, which would blind the room. But by thinning the cloud outside just there, just for an hour, so a true shaft, warm and gold, falls directly onto that living smear. The moss would not explode or flower. It is too patient for that. But its green would deepen to a color the Keeper has never seen it be, and it would, for the first time, bead water not as condensation but as something like dew. He would see it from his dry chair, and he would know that someone else had noticed it too.

When it leaves — and a wise power would leave, because to stay forever would make the room dependent — it would not leave a sign that you could photograph. The sodium lights would still be dim. The water would still sheet down the formwork lines. The air would still be at dew point.

But the Keeper would sleep, for once, all the way through a cycle with his head tipped back. He would not twitch a half-second before the pressure switch clunked, because for once he would not need to. The water would still be swallowed into the black penstock and thrown down the throat of the mountain, but he would know, without being told, where it goes — not into blackness, but down to a turbine that hums now in tune with his pumps, and further down to a river that finally gets to be outside without thinking it is.

You would come back another day, open the steel door, step into the throat, and think: *nothing has changed.* The concrete is still young, the corners still sharp, the basin still loud.

And the man in the yellow shell, his shoulders still stooped to the exact height of the low ceiling, would look up from scraping the algae slick — not at you as volume to be moved through, but as a person who is cold — and he would nod. And you would realize his boots are no longer sloshing. And you would hear, impossibly, that he is humming, very quietly, under the scream, in the new quiet-inside-sound, in tune with something you cannot hear.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do here.

Almost nothing.

Almost everything.
