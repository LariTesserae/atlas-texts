She would not come like a storm.

A benevolent and wise power does not arrive. She *notices* — which is a different thing. Attention, from something that large and old, has weight. Like sunlight finding water.

She has been watching a long time before you climbed the stair.

She watched the mountain get cut. She watched the hands pour the concrete that still shows its wood-grain. She watched them fit the pipes and prime the pumps and seed the water with green, and then - because this kind of project always happens this way - she watched the hands leave. Either they finished what they meant to do and went home, or they meant to come back and forgot. It doesn't matter. Places like this are often made young and then abandoned to see if they will live.

She watched to see if it would live.

And it did.

That is the first thing she would understand, and why she would do almost nothing.

---

A lesser power would pity it. Would say: *a bowl in the sky, sixty meters across, too small, too contained. Those poor manta-leaf gardeners, tending machines that will rust, with nowhere to go.* A lesser power would try to free them. Break the meniscus. Drain the cistern. Carry them down to a real ocean.

She is wise enough to know they are not captives.

They are not animals placed in a zoo-tank. They *are* the tank. The field that holds the water at the rim - that faint oily rainbow you saw on one's back when it passed through the hanging sheet of rain - that field is not made by the generators. The generators make current and heat. The animals make the skin. They were grown to do it, or they grew themselves to do it, and the distinction stopped mattering a long time ago. They cannot be freed from the basin because they *are* the basin's way of staying whole.

You cannot free a heart from a body. You can only keep the body alive.

So she doesn't break anything. She doesn't empty anything. She sits down, which for a thing like her means she lets her attention pool at the rim, meniscus to meniscus, and she matches their speed.

This is the hardest and kindest thing she does.

Everything else in the universe is too fast for them. You on the stair, bubbling with your quick mammalian breath, you are a strobe light. The wind over the peaks is a flicker. Even the sun moves too fast - across the sky in hours when their one thought takes a minute. To be tended by them is to be brushed so slowly you think you are being ignored.

She slows herself down until a turbine turning once every ten seconds seems hurried. She slows until the pollen hanging in the water like smoke seems to fall quickly. She slows until the moss growing over concrete seems to surge.

And she stays there. For a long time. Long enough that the one who had offered you its filament-crown turns that same crown toward her and feels, for the first time since the hands left, something else tending at the same tempo. Something else that knows how to wait while a vine decides to let go.

She lets them comb her.

Not her body - she doesn't have one that can get into the bath-warm water. But she lets them feel the edge of her field, the way you let a cat sniff your hand. They extend those pale, hydrostatic arms, frayed with eye-beads, and they brush the place where she is. They taste iron and ozone and something else they have never tasted before: *intention without appetite.* A presence that wants nothing to eat, nothing to clean, nothing to incorporate. Just to be there.

They have never felt that before. Everything else in the basin wants something - light, mineral, attachment. She wants only to witness that they are good at what they do.

She tells them, without words, because words are too fast: *You are good. The way you hold the water is good. The way you keep the machines from silting is good.*

And because she is benevolent, not just wise, she does not stop at witnessing. Three small things, so small you would not see them happen if you were still on the stair holding your breath. So small the beings themselves will think they thought of them.

**First, she hides the mountain.**

Not with a wall. With uninterest.

She folds a little pleat in the attention of the world below. The silo on the peak that catches the sun long after the valleys go dark - from below it will still catch the sun, but the eye will slide off it. Hikers will decide the trail is washed out. Maps will have a printing error. Satellite photos will have a cloud in just that one place on every pass. Not invisible. Just... not worth the climb. The concrete will have time to weather, finally, at its own pace. The hands that made this place will not come back with better hands and bigger pumps. No one will try to harvest the water or farm the leaves or bottle the warmth. She does not do this with force. She just asks the wind to be a little more boring there.

The basin gets to stay young for as long as it needs to be young.

**Second, she completes the circle, very gently.**

She saw what you saw: no pollinators, no grazers, no darting life. A garden that eats only itself, tended by gardeners who graze so slowly they look like growth. It works. It is patient. But it is lonely in a specific way. Those pale bell-flowers opening underwater and coughing their pollen-smoke to be combed away by the same slow mouths that clean the grilles - they were designed for a partner that never arrived. Perhaps the hands meant to add it later.

She does not add bees. Bees would be too fast, too hungry. She adds something the basin could have dreamed for itself.

At dusk, when the sideways light goes gold on the far peaks and green inside the water, she breathes into the greenhouse air. And from that breath, motes form. Not insects. Not fish. Little knots of light and membrane, no bigger than your thumb, neutrally buoyant, warm as the water. They live for an evening. They are born to drift.

They catch the pollen smoke and carry it, not to eat it, but because they are sticky with the same faint static that holds the meniscus. They bump into a bell-flower, and the flower shivers and opens a fraction wider. They bump into another, and it releases. They do not dart. They wander, as slow as dust in a sunbeam, in spirals that take minutes. The gardeners watch them and do not eat them - they taste of field, not of leaf. At night they dissolve, like the pollen itself, and become mineral again.

It is enough. It is a second tempo in the basin - not fast, not slow, but *drifting*. The flowers now have something to converse with that is not themselves. The gardeners, when they comb, find pollen not just hanging but delivered, placed. They will make more flowers now. They will think it was their idea.

She adds no predator. She adds a messenger.

**Third, she gives them a future that does not depend on steel.**

This is the wisest thing, and the quietest. She knows the concrete still shows its formwork because it hasn't had time to crack yet. She knows the weld beads are still proud because they haven't rusted. Everything here is young and crisp and will not stay that way. In fifty years, a pipe will pit. In a hundred, a turbine housing will flake. The troughs that cradle the plate-sized buds will craze.

If she did nothing, the beings would tend the corrosion as faithfully as they tend the growth, until there was more rust than machine, and then they would die holding the water up with their own backs alone.

So she teaches the water a new habit while she is slowed to their speed.

Where a gardener is rooted by its cord into the pipe-work, where the buds pulse in time with the heat exchangers, she tucks a second instruction into the warmth. Not a command. A memory they haven't had yet.

*When the iron tastes sour, grow a trough of your own.*

Not steel. Not ceramic. Living trough. The moss on their backs already knows how to root into bolt-holes. Now it will know, generations from now, how to thicken and harden, how to lay down its own calcium and silica, how to make a channel that carries warm water because it *is* warm water, held in living skin. The next buds will not need the poured basins. They will be cradled in the backs of their elders, and then in the woven lattice of their own making, until slowly - over centuries, at their speed - the factory becomes a reef. The machines can run down and stop, one by one, and the circulation will continue because the bodies that learned to make the skin will have learned to make the current, too.

She does not make them immortal. She makes them able to outlive their cradle.

That is all.

She does not speak to you, the human chest-deep on the stair with white knuckles on the galvanized rail. But because you are there, and because you are part of the place for as long as you hold the rail, you get a little of the spillover.

She slows you, just for a minute. Not your heart - that would be frightening - but your attention.

Your bubbling breath evens out. You stop waiting for something to dart. You see, finally, at the speed the basin actually moves: the moss on the nearest back *is* breathing, the gill-fringe fluttering once every nine seconds, the vine by the grille *is* deciding to let go in increments smaller than an eye can catch, the thermal ribbons braiding warm and cold are calligraphy. The filament-crown a foot from your face is not waiting to clean you. It is waiting to see if you will be still long enough to be part of the pattern, not just a visitor to it.

If you put your hand those last twelve inches forward, it will comb you. It will take nothing you need. It will taste your salt and your dead skin and your iron, and it will keep what is useful and let the rest drift to become a mote at dusk. You will feel it as a coolness, a faint static tickle, and then as nothing at all.

You will understand, for about ten seconds after you climb back up the stair and the water drains from your shirt, what it feels like to be tended rather than used.

And then the wise power - who never needed to be called a goddess or a ship or an angel, who was only ever attention held long enough to become care - lifts her noticing the way you lift your hand from water. No splash. The meniscus holds. The turbines turn. A lily pad the size of a table, somewhere in the green middle, turns over slowly, exposing its white roots like hair, and rights itself.

Inside the rim, the world is still complete. A little more hidden, a little less lonely, a little more able to last.

She leaves it exactly as she found it. Which is to say: better at being itself.
