If I were that power — benevolent and wise and with the ability to do anything, and therefore with the obligation to do almost nothing — I would not come down.

That is the first thing.

I would not descend onto the plateau like an answer, because the plateau is not a question. I have seen enough worlds to know the difference between a broken place and an intervalled place. This one is not broken. Its emptiness is not a wound to fill. Its height is not a problem to solve. Its ancient, poured, wind-tuned stone is not waiting to become a young mountain again. It finished becoming itself a long time ago.

And the one who lives there is not unfinished either.

So I would not bring them metal. I would not bring them rain in excess, or a permanent river, or a city, or a promise that they will never be thirsty again. Thirst is part of their tuning. To remove it would be to deafen them. I would not bring them roads to shorten the bare intervals, for the intervals are their hallways, and their competence lives in knowing how long it takes to cross.

I would not speak loudly. The place is already deafening. Another loud voice would only be noise.

What would I do, then? Being able to do anything, I would do the small, almost invisible things that only a power that loves emptiness knows how to do.

**I would sit high and keep watch outward, not inward.**

The plateau's only real danger is not thirst or loneliness or the wind. It is being noticed by a different kind of attention — the hungry attention from below that sees bare rock as resource, green pockets as yield, wind-flutes as mine shafts. That attention has ended so many places like this.

So first, I would draw a veil that is not a wall.

I would let the clouds that already build each afternoon build a little truer. I would let the thermals that lift the Turner lift a little longer, so that any eye far below looking up sees only haze and heat-shimmer where the mesas are. Any map made from orbit would smooth with a small, forgiving error. Prospectors would find their instruments curiously dull near the escarpment, their drills turning aside as if the stone were bored with them. Not forbidden — just uninteresting. The plateau would remain, to the lowlands, a place where nothing worth taking is found. Which is true.

To the people, I would not explain this. Explanation would make them feel watched. I would just let their cairns continue to mean what they have always meant, without needing to become forts.

**I would listen, and answer once in their own language.**

I have heard them at night, in the basin, humming until the rock hums back. That is prayer already. It does not need correction.

So on a night when a family is together — rarely, that few-night-a-month — when the old one starts the low note and the younger joins a fifth above and then they both go quiet to hear the stone reply, I would let the stone reply a little more clearly than usual.

Not a miracle. Not a voice from the sky. Just this: the wind in the biggest organ-pipe, for once, would hold its pitch without wavering for a full minute, perfectly in tune with them. The children would feel it in their sternums. The old one would smile without opening her eyes. They would not think *a god has spoken.* They would think *tonight the plateau is well-tuned.* And they would be right. And in the morning they would not block that pipe with a landslide, and they would tell the next family to leave that pipe alone, and the tuning would hold.

That is enough intervention for a century.

**I would tend what they already tend, but from the other side of the interval.**

They hold water the way bromeliads do — in cups. I would not make new lakes. I would make the cups a little better at being cups.

That pothole with the blind white fish that has lived since before the grandmother's grandmother — I would deepen its polish by a thumb's width, not with tools but with the way water deepens stone when no one is looking. Just enough that in the driest year, when even the spring beneath the fin stutters, that bowl still holds. The fish would live. The boy who comes to watch the fish learn stillness would still have a teacher. I would not make the fish immortal. I would just let this one finish its own long waiting without being interrupted by my neglect.

That resinous tree where the fist-sized beetles bore — I would let it seed once more than it would have, two saplings in a crack that would otherwise have stayed bare. Enough that the family can take larvae and still leave larvae. Enough that the forest does not go silent in that other way.

That vine they are training across the chasm to make a living bridge — I would give it one extra warm night after rain, one extra inch per hour, so it takes. Nothing they would notice as unnatural. They would simply think they had guided it well. They did.

**I would not gather them.**

This is important. A wise, benevolent power is often tempted to cure loneliness by making a village. To bring the scattered families together so they are less rare.

I would not.

Their rarity is not loneliness. It is their ecology. They are spaced like the green, like the water. To crowd them would be to make them forget walk-time and dry-time and flower's-life-time, and to make them need clocks. Instead, I would make the intervals a little kinder to cross.

On the long flat of polished stone between basins where there is nothing for an hour, I would let a shallow, hand-sized hollow that already collects dew collect it a little longer into the morning before the sun takes it. So a runner moving flat-footed, toe-and-heel together, can pause, crouch, and drink without breaking stride. He will think the stone was generous today. It was.

I would leave their rope bridges alone, even the broken ones. A broken bridge is also a message: *do not go this way now.*

**And for the one you might see, far off, crossing from one pocket of green to another —**

I would do nothing that makes them look at me. If they feel me at all, it will be as a slight easing in the work of breathing. Four seconds in through the nose, pause, six seconds out through the mouth, never hurried. I would breathe with them, not for them. The air is thin; I would not thicken it. I would just let that breath, on that crossing, feel a little less like work and a little more like being played, like the wind through the flute.

If they pause on the dome and tap with their staff to listen for hollow stone, I would let the tap ring true.

If they stack a stone that means *water here, still good,* I would not knock it down.

If they choose, one day, to leave the plateau — because even interval-shaped people sometimes want to see what is below — I would let the memory of the circuit stay clear in them so they can find their way back if they want. And if they choose never to leave, I would let that be a complete life.

In the end, after a long time of doing almost nothing, what would have changed?

Almost nothing. Which is how you know it was benevolent.

The plateau would still be high and ancient and rounded like wax. The wind would still hum in the sternum. The ten-minute storm would still hammer and vanish. The green would still cling only where it is invited. The Turner would still turn without flapping.

A boy would still sit by a pothole watching a blind fish until he understands that stillness is not doing nothing. An old woman would still make the *ooo* that rides under the hum and her child would still know which pipe is hers. A family would still catch rain in gourds and resin-lined baskets and in the rock's own cups, and have enough for ten days.

Only this would be different, and only they would know it in the way you know a room is warm without seeing the fire:

The place would feel heard.

Not filled. Not fixed. Not saved.

Heard, the way an instrument feels heard by a musician who does not need to play it constantly to love it, who is content to keep it in tune, dusted, and left in its case until the next time it is needed.

If you went there after I had been there, you would put your hand on that thousand-foot curve of lichen-warm stone and feel no edge, only the long, continuous note. And far off, disappearing into a cleft that looks like it could not possibly hold a person, you would see someone crossing, not hurrying, staff tapping, perfectly competent in vastness.

The wind would shift and fill the space where they were with sound, and the emptiness would be complete again.

And it would be good.
