It would not come down.

That is the first thing.

If it were truly benevolent and truly wise — not just powerful, not just kind in the way the lowlanders are kind, which is to give you what they think you should want — it would come to the rim and stop.

It would arrive as weather arrives at that height. Not breaking the horizon. Not descending into the bowl. It would settle on the long smooth back of the mountain where the wind is, and look down into the shallow hollow where the light is plain and high and clear, and see.

And it would understand within a breath what it was seeing.

This is not a place that is waiting to be saved. This is not a place that is broken.

This is a place that has come to an agreement.

Water and frost and time agreed on a shape. Stone agreed to be cut. People agreed to cut it only where it wanted to split. Roofs agreed to feed one cistern. Forty or fifty people agreed to breathe a little slower so their chests would fit the air. Everything that could be smoothed has been smoothed. Everything that could be sorted has been sorted by size a long time ago. It is complete the way a well-made wall is complete — not because nothing more could be added, but because nothing more *should* be added. The empty pens and the stretches of bare rock between houses are not wasted space. They are the mortar.

A lesser power would not see that. A lesser power, meaning well, would see thin soil and think *I will make it fertile.* Would see the wind-burn on their cheeks and think *I will make it warm.* Would see seventeen lamps and think *I will bring more light.* Would see six goats and think *sixty would be better.* Would see that no house is built new and think this is sadness, stagnation, and try to fix it with birth or building.

The wise one would feel the temptation to fix and then let it pass over like the thin veil of cloud that comes at this elevation for an hour and then is gone.

So it would not come down. To come down would be to have weight. Even a benevolent weight, in a bowl tuned so finely, would crack a slate.

What would it do instead?

It would do the things the bowl itself does.

### 1. It would keep the interval.

First, it would do nothing that could be seen from inside. Not at first. It would sit with its back to the wind on the high shoulder and make itself part of the shelter.

The world below is not still. Down the long grey slopes where everything falls away, the lowlands are restless. They cut roads. They pump water. They send surveyors and hikers and ideas. The greatest danger to a place like this is not drought - the cistern never empties - but being *noticed* in the wrong way, being declared picturesque or poor or in need of development.

So the first act of a benevolent power would be concealment, not display. Not a miracle. An un-miracle.

It would lay a little more forgetting over the maps. It would make the path that leads to the rim look, from below, like it leads nowhere - just more scree, just another gully that stopped cutting long ago. It would let the clouds linger half an hour longer over that shoulder when a drone passes. It would make the story that a traveler tells afterward a little vague: *I thought I saw something up there but the light was plain and high and I must have been mistaken.*

It would guard the emptiness between the houses by guarding the larger emptiness around the mountain. It would not wall the bowl off. It would simply let the bowl continue to be hard to find for anyone who does not already know how to breathe slowly.

That is benevolence that understands urbanity: these people called themselves a town, not a village to be saved. To respect a town you respect its walls and its decision about who enters.

### 2. It would learn the work.

If after a long time - because a wise power is patient enough to watch for seasons - it was clear that its presence was not a disturbance, it might ask permission. Not with words. With a change in the ticking.

You know how after the cool rain that comes without warning for twenty minutes, the cistern ticks quietly as water drips from the spouts? For a day afterwards the mortar is dark and you can hear the rate. The people know that rate the way you know a pulse.

It would match it. It would breathe with them. Six breaths a minute when they are carrying a bucket hand over hand. A pause when they pause between houses to let the quiet complete itself.

It would not make the air thicker - that would be to unmake their broad chests, their slow walk, the way a child learns not to shout because shouting just goes flat against stone. It loves those broad chests. It would not heal the wind-burn from their cheeks - that burn is the map of a life lived correctly at this altitude.

It would learn to tap stone the way the old woman does. Tap, listen, tap. Find where it already wants to split.

And only then, if an invitation seemed to exist - and with these people an invitation would never be spoken, only shown by not driving you away, by leaving a cup of water on a wall for a stranger - it might do very small, almost invisible things. The kind of things you could mistake for luck if you weren't paying attention.

Not more water. The cistern must never overflow. Just that the drip after rain holds steady for an hour longer than it used to, so the ladle comes up full without scraping the bottom.

Not more goats. The number stays at six or seven. Just that the shaggy coats are a little less matted after a hard frost, that the pika's single bright note in the scree is answered once, so the night doesn't feel quite so alone.

Not a new pine tree. One twisted pine is enough. Just that the one pine, this year, makes a single cone that holds and opens, and the seeds find the thin tuft-grass and one takes, low and hardy, in a crack no one paved over. Not a forest. A companion.

Not a cure for the old who sit in doorways where the sun hits the stone. They are old for a very long time because the thin cool air preserves them, and that long oldness is a kind of library. Just that their knees ache a little less when they stand, so they can stay a few minutes longer in the sun before they go back inside.

And for the children - the few children who are given real tasks and no pretend toys - it would not give them toys. It would give them a slightly surer hand. The broom made of twig would sweep a little cleaner. The rope that is too long for them would tangle a little less. When they watch the cistern mouth for the drip-rate, they would see clearly that they are *needed*, that the machine for catching water includes their eyes.

Nothing that changes the form. Only a little more ease inside the form that already holds.

### 3. It would witness and be witnessed.

At dusk, when the light goes grey-blue and the small shallow bowls of oil are lit - seventeen or eighteen warm lights in the two crescent rows, with dark between - the wise power would still be on the rim, not in the yard.

It would let its own light be as small as theirs. No pillar of fire. No voice that fills the bowl, because voices that try to fill the bowl die here anyway.

Perhaps it would be the extra condensation that forms bead by bead on every surface in the dark, a little brighter than usual where the lamp light hits it. Perhaps it would be the lichen on the north-facing walls taking on, for one evening, a more vivid orange, so an old man running his hand along stone smiles without knowing why.

Perhaps it would be nothing more than the feeling the hamlet already has but rarely names: that on some evenings, when the wind over the rim is a low even hush and the intervals between buildings hold sky so cleanly and no one is hurrying, the whole bowl feels held. Not pushed. Held.

The people would not call it a god. They have no word for that kind of attention except the words they already have for stone that holds cold, water that sleeps under the slab, a wall that stays plumb without mortar. They would say, if they said anything at all standing side by side looking at the work rather than at each other, "The stone sweated well last night." Or "The cistern ticked true."

And that would be enough. To be loved correctly is to not be required to change your language for the lover.

If it were truly wise, it would know that its greatest gift is not to be needed. That one day, perhaps, the bowl will change - because nothing, not even this agreement, lasts forever outside of stories - and when it does, the people will decide how, with the same patience they use to find where stone wants to split.

Until then, it would keep the rim. It would keep the forgetting over the maps. It would keep the drip steady. It would sit on the mountain's back like an older neighbor sits in a doorway where the sun hits the stone, wrapped even in summer, eyes half-closed against the high plain light, not governing, just remembering.

And at night, when every oil lamp is trimmed and the limestone drinks what little water the day left, and the whole hamlet breathes slow and low and together in its shallow bowl cut into the shoulder of the world, the power would breathe with it.

Bead by bead. Tick by tick. Asking nothing. Altering nothing. Keeping everything exactly as tender and exact and spare as it already, miraculously, is.
