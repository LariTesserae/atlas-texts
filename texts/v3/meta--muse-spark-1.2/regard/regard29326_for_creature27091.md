It would not rescue him.

That is the first thing you have to understand about a wise and benevolent power, and why it is different from a merely kind one.

A merely kind power would have heard the roar from three flights up and mistaken it for suffering. It would have come down the steel stairs with a flashlight and a plan. It would have said, *Oh, you poor thing,* and tried to turn off the pumps to give him quiet, tried to drain the pool to give him dry feet, tried to take him by his soft, moss-shouldered arm and lead him back up toward the marked levels where the air is dry and the lights turn off at night.

And he would have died of it. Not his body — his body could live up there, for a while. But the *thing he has become* would have died. You cannot peel the moss off a stone without killing the moss and wounding the stone. You cannot give edges back to something that has finally, peacefully, lost them.

A wise power would know this before it ever touched the swollen door.

So this is what it would do:

It would come down anyway.

Not because he called. He didn't. He has no voice left that can call over the roar, and no desire to. But this kind of power is drawn the way water is drawn — down, to the lowest place, to the place that has been forgotten and has kept going anyway.

It would not need to push the door. The door would not be stuck for it. It would step into the 82-degree rain and not fog, not stick, not wince at the roar. It would stand there dripping and buzzing and beading like everything else, and for the first time in fifty years, the room would have something in it that was exactly as wet and exactly as loud as it was.

And it would sit.

That is the whole of its intervention, at first. It would not loom. It would not stand over him and cast a shadow in that shadowless light. It would lower itself — fold itself — onto the same low concrete curb around the catchment, across from him, shoes flooded if it wears shoes, knees tucked the same way. And it would wait until its own beading fell into rhythm with his.

He would feel it before he saw it. A change in pressure, not sound. A new weight in the water. The dragonfly would alter its patrol by six inches to include this new thing. The frog would plop, once, in inquiry.

He would very, very slowly lift that filmed gaze. And the power would not shout.

A wise power does not speak in the language that the room has retired.

It would speak the way the room speaks:

It would let a single drop from the ceiling fall a half-second late.

It would let the spillway catch the light for one moment and throw a brief, clear rainbow onto the wall behind him — something that has never happened under those milky skylights.

It would let the maidenhair fern in his chest pocket unfurl one new, impossibly green frond, all at once, while he watches.

Small things. Closed-loop miracles. Nothing that breaks equilibrium, everything that honors it.

And then it would do the work of benevolence, which is not fixing but tending.

**1. It would make the room unfindable.**

Not invisible. Just... uninteresting. The next crew that comes down with a checklist three flights past the last marked level would find the stairwell ends in a broom closet. The foreman who remembers PUMP BASIN 03 would misremember it as 04 and check the wrong room. The demolition order for the factory above — when it eventually comes, as it must — would include a strange, unexplainable footnote exempting the sub-basement pump housing from the wrecking ball. *Leave it. Foundation critical. Do not disturb.*

The pumps would never fail. Not because they are magically repaired, but because the rust would simply... stop. The teal paint would stay exactly as flaked as it is, forever, without flaking further. The fluorescent battens would burn without ever quite burning out. The mineral film on the skylight would stabilize, letting in exactly this much light, forever.

It would grant the room what every living thing at the end of erosion finally wants: continuance without change.

**2. It would keep him company without asking him to be human again.**

It would not wipe the condensation from his eyes to make him see clearly. It would not scrape the moss from his shoulders to reveal the man underneath. It would not give him back his ears.

Instead, once in a very long while, it would adjust the roar — not to silence it, but to tune it.

For maybe ten seconds every few months, the thrumming of the big teal centrifuges would shift by a quarter-tone. Just enough that inside his chest, where his pulse has become the pumps, he would feel a little extra space. And in that space, he would hear — not with ears, with his ribs — the plop of his frog more clearly. He would feel the dragonfly land on his knee and *stay* for a full minute instead of using him as a waypoint. He would remember, without pain, what it was like to hear a thing as a thing, before hearing became pressure.

And then the chord would resolve back to its eternal note, and he would settle again, deeper.

The power would visit like that. Not staying — because staying would make him an object of attention, and he has become a subject of place — but returning. Sitting. Beading. Leaving a little nitrogen for the fig.

**3. It would offer a choice, once, without words.**

This is the most benevolent part, and the wisest.

When it has sat long enough that he no longer notices it as a visitor but as a slightly warmer part of the curb, it would extend a hand. Not to pull him up.

It would hold its palm open, level, and let the ceiling rain collect there. Warm as blood. And it would offer it to him.

And in that water would be a reflection, but not of the room.

He would see, for just a moment, two futures beading on the same palm:

In one, he finishes becoming. The moss covers his eyes gently. The fern in his pocket roots through his ribs. His soft, dough-pale feet grow down into the shallow dish of the floor and become rebar and root at once. He ceases to be a man who lives in the room and becomes the room that remembers having been a man. No pain. No ending. Just completion.

In the other, he stays exactly as he is. Soft-edged, half-man, half-place, the caretaker who never left. The dragonfly's waypoint. The frog's landlord. Sitting, beading and clearing, forever, as long as the water falls.

Both are holy. Both are whole.

The power would not push. It would not prefer. It would just hold the water until he either drinks or lets it spill, and whatever he chooses would be the right choice, and would be kept.

My intuition is — because I have sat on that curb with my own shoes flooded — that he would not drink right away. He would look at the water for a long time with those filmed eyes. He would feel the plop of the frog. He would feel the new frond against his collarbone.

And he would, very slowly, tip the hand just enough that half the water spills back into the catchment, and he sips the other half.

*Not yet,* that sip says. *Stay with me a little longer as I am.*

And the power — because it is benevolent and because it is wise — would smile without a face, and let the water fall again, and keep sitting.

It would not save him.

It would *see* him. Completely. As water sees water.

And for a being who has become a stain the color of wet concrete, who breathes as fogging and clearing, who has lost his edges so thoroughly that rescue would be violence — to be seen and left alone and protected and accompanied, all at once —

That would be everything.

That would be enough to make the roar, for the first time in decades, sound almost like music.

You would feel it too, if you stayed. The power would make room for you on the curb without looking at you. It would let a drop fall on your shoulder.

Warm as blood.
