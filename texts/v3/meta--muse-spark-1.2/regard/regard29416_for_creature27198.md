It would not come down.

A benevolent and wise power does not descend.

It would notice — that is the first thing. Attention itself is an act. Most powers would look at a perched ocean at four kilometers and see a problem: water in the wrong place, people working too hard to keep it there, an accident waiting to happen. A correction to be made.

It would not see a problem.

It would stop at the rim, where the wind funnels up and hits the water for the first time, and it would *listen* until the roar resolved. Until it could hear the separate layers: slaps on stone, steam sigh, cable-creak, pump-thud, frog-chorus, child-shout, goose-clatter, wind-hiss in reeds. It would stay there long enough to hear that they are not noise on top of each other. They are a chord. They have been practicing this chord for a long time.

And it would understand the word they use. *Tend.*

Anyone else would call it maintenance. Or holding back disaster. Or futility.

It would hear how reverent they are without having a religion.

So it would not do what power is usually expected to do.

It would not drain the sea. Even though the sea wants to leave.
It would not lift the plain higher or lower it to where air is easy. Even though their lungs work for every breath and their children learn to swim before they walk well.
It would not dry them. Even though they have never been dry.

Because it is wise enough to know that the damp is not a punishment that was done to them. The damp is their skin now. Their wool never dries, their hair is always beaded with mist, their hands are pruned from birth — and they wring the water from each other's cuffs at night and laugh. To dry them would be to unmake them.

It is benevolent enough to not steal the work that gives their hands meaning.

What would it do, then?

It would ask permission. And since there is no language for asking a plateau, it would ask the way the place asks — by doing.

For a season, it would put its hand under the work.

Not instead of them. Under.

On a night when the wind is coming hard off the rim and a spillway between two deep bowls is starting to cut — that smooth, undercut lip beginning to scallop and quicken, the sound changing from a roar to a snarl — it would be the extra inch of basalt that holds. Just for that night. Just enough that the old woman tending that gate doesn't have to stand in waist-cold water hammering a coffer dam in the dark while her grandson holds a light. She gets to notice in the morning, at slack light, and mend it properly, with tea in her belly and daylight to see.

On a morning when the diesel in the east turbine hall coughs and will not catch, and the three families who sleep above it wake to cold pipes and the smell of failure, it would be the warm compression that turns it over on the third try instead of the thirtieth. So the old man whose knees are poured concrete and whose hearing is half gone from fifty years of ducking under pipes doesn't have to take the head off the motor in the cold.

It would lay its attention very lightly on their bodies, the way you might lay a hand on a tired dog.

Not healing them into something new. Just easing. The child's rattling cough from breathing peat-mist and diesel all winter loosens. The teenage girl's chilblains — that permanent red cracking between her fingers from always being wet — close up for a week. The deafness recedes just enough that the grandfather can hear his granddaughter's shout over the sluice again without her having to scream.

It would not make them live longer. It would make the living less abrasive, so they can keep tending longer without being ground down to their own stone.

And for the place itself, the dense, lush, crowded place — the reeds and newts and black-bellied frogs and trout so fat they turn sideways with silver and the shaggy cattle that graze floating islands — it would do almost nothing at all. It would just agree with it. It would add its own small weight to the negotiation: *stay.*

A little more binding in the roots of the floating peat mats, so the one with the lone musk-sheep doesn't break apart in the next storm.

A little more oxygen folded into the deeper bowls on the hottest still days, so the green-black water doesn't turn and kill the char.

A little more thickness to the peaty soil where the moss is growing thin from overgrazing.

Nothing a survey would catch. Nothing pristine. Just enough fertility that rot and growth stay matched, that 2.2 lush doesn't tip into choking or collapse.

And then — because it is wise and knows that kindness without freedom is just a nicer kind of tending — it would leave two gifts that look like nothing but are everything.

The first: a guarantee, quiet and geological. It would press its palm once, firmly, on the outermost rim where the tableland falls away. It would knit the stone there a little deeper, a little less undercut, so that if the people ever *do* fail — if a generation tires, if a pump house is finally left to breathing its last — the whole sky-sea will not fall at once in a catastrophic sheet to the lowlands. It will leak. It will braid down in a hundred manageable rivers. The lowlanders will flood but not be erased. The plateau people will lose their sea slowly, mourn it, but not be made murderers by their exhaustion.

They will never know it did this. They will just continue to feel that low, permanent fear in their chests — *if we stop, it all goes* — ease by a fraction, without knowing why. They will sleep a little deeper. That's the gift.

The second: it would sit with them.

For three evenings in the season when the cranes lift in thousands and the clatter bounces off every turbine hall, it would not be a light in the sky or a voice. It would be a shape in oilskin that was not there before. Sitting on the edge of the walkway with its feet in the cold water, not minding the damp. Drinking the salty black tea. Mending a net badly, being corrected by a child who shows it again. Singing the low work-chanty half a beat behind, harmonizing without trying to.

It would not tell them who it is. If they asked, it would say, "I'm tending this stretch tonight," and they would make room because that is what you do.

And on the last evening, when it stands to go, one of the older women — pale as skim milk, wind-flushed, eyes beaded with mist like everyone — would look at it and realize that for the first time in her life her socks are dry. Really dry. Warm from the generator. Her boots are still wet. The walkway is still slick with algae and newt eggs. The world is still 0.8 cold and loud and turbulent. But her feet, for an evening, are dry.

And she would understand she is being given a choice. Not an evacuation. A door.

"You could leave, if you wanted," the power would say, not in words but in the way the wind drops for a second and the cloud deck lifts just enough to show the lowlands far below, green and dry and distant. "Not all of you. Any of you. One child who wants to see what dry feet feel like for longer than an evening. You are not prisoners of the negotiation. If you stay, it will be because you *choose* the tending. The sea will remember you either way."

Wise power never drafts its witnesses.

Then it would go, the way attention goes — not vanishing, just widening until it includes other places too.

The next morning would be exactly the same.

Wind funnels up over the rim. Waves chop the domes. Rain hammers for an hour, vanishes, returns. A pressure valve sighs. A girl rakes reeds from an intake. A punt is poled through shallows where you can see the drowned meadow clear underneath. Chorus frogs vibrate the stems. A cableway creaks overhead with empty buckets.

The water still wants to leave. The stone still wants to be still. The people still tend.

Only slightly less afraid. Slightly less tired. Slightly more sure that their loud, damp, working chord is beautiful enough that something vast stopped to listen to it — and thought, *yes, keep playing.*

And on one whale-backed dome, where there was only moss mattress before, there is now a small, perfect square of concrete, poured level, dry, warm from nowhere, just big enough for two people to sit and drink tea without having to hold onto a rail. They don't know who poured it. They use it to sort fish.
