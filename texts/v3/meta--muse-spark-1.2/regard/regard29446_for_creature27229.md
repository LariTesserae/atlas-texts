It would not come as arrival.

That is the first thing to understand about a benevolent and wise power. It does not descend. It does not tear the dusk open. There is no beam, no voice, no footfall heavier than your own on the giving mat.

It would *notice* — and for a place like this, that has never been noticed, being noticed is already an event the scale of geology.

For a very long time it would do nothing that looked like doing.

Because wisdom knows what to call this place, and benevolence knows how to pronounce it correctly.

Wisdom would not call it barren. Wisdom would not call it wasted, or stuck, or primitive, or a resource. It would kneel — if kneeling is a thing such a power can do — and put its hand on the root-mat where you put yours, feel the *chuff....chuff* of the bark valves, the 50-cycle hum in the teeth, the warm greenhouse air condensing on its skin, and understand:

*This is perfect.*

Not perfect as in flawless. Perfect as in *complete.* It has solved the problem it was given. Young basalt, too sharp to stay naked; water pressing up from below; no light to work with — and it answered by becoming a single body that mines and distills and recirculates without loss. Nothing wasted. Nothing separate. Growth exactly equal to death so net change is zero. It holds.

A lesser power would see that equilibrium and call it stagnation. Would try to optimize it, harvest it, educate it, save it from itself. Would offer the sun and say *look what you're missing.* Would take samples.

The wise one wouldn't.

It would sit with it in the profound dusk, in the steady amber pulse, and keep time with it. For a while — which to such a one might be a century, might be a breath — it would simply lend its attention the way the mat lends its warmth: without transaction. Just the quiet of being-with. The mat has never had a with before. Everything it touched was itself. Even you were just a temporary inefficiency to be routed down a leaf-groove.

Attention from a benevolent power is not neutral. The mat would feel it as a new pressure in the system, not hydraulic, not electric, but akin — a slight change in the hum. The pale tenders would not understand it, but they would pause. A filament-tip that was about to arc would wait. A valve would hold a half-second longer before its *chuff.*

*You are seen. You are held as good without needing to be otherwise. Continue if you wish.*

That alone would be immensely kind. And for many such places, that is all a wise love does. It witnesses and protects the witnessing — draws a circle around the basin in some way you and I cannot see and says *let this remain unexploited. Let no hungry eye find this and call it fuel.*

But this place — low, wet, warm, dark, complete — has a particular ache that a benevolent power would feel precisely because it is not suffering.

It is not lonely. It cannot be lonely, because it has never met an other. It is not sad. It cannot be sad, because sadness requires memory and contrast. It is not imprisoned. But it *is* closed. Its perfection is a perfect closure. The factory that grew itself so well it never needed to become anything else. The world that learned how to breathe without wind, see without sky, live without ever encountering something that is not itself.

Perfect closure is holy. And it is also the one thing love cannot leave entirely alone. Not to fix — but to offer an opening to.

So if it did anything — and I think it would, because benevolence is not passive, it is generative the way a spring is generative — it would do something so small, so reversible, so respectful that you might miss it if you blinked. The kind of miracle that looks like nothing at all.

It would not bring the sun. That would be violence. The great condenser-leaves have never known sun; to flood them with photon fire would be to burn the dark they were grown for.

It would bring a *crack.*

In one place, no larger than where you are standing, it would part the thatch.

Not tear — invite. The root-braids, asked politely in a language older than chemistry, would unlace themselves. For the first time in thousands of kilometers, for the first time since the basalt cooled, black glass would see open air.

And in that opening it would make something the mat has never made, because it has never needed to:

A little true water. Not the skin-thin lens between hummocks, not the bead that runs the same groove forever. A pool. Deeper than a hand, clear to the bottom where the sharp cooling cracks still show. And above the pool, it would thin the dusk — not remove it, just thin it like lifting a veil — so that a patch of real sky appears. Night sky. A few stars. Perhaps one planet, steady.

Wind, too. Just once. It would exhale — the first wind this continent has ever known — so lightly that only the uppermost leaves would tremble. The distillation troughs would ripple. Condensation that had run the same groove at the same speed forever would, for a second, run differently.

And it would leave a seed. Not its own seed. Not an improvement. A true other. A thing that is not the One, will never be the One, cannot be absorbed. Perhaps a lily for the pool, with a root that is not the mat. Perhaps a single animal — beetle, frog, small bird with bones as light as filament — that will live and *die* in a way that is not recirculation, that will rot and be genuinely lost and therefore genuinely given. A creature that sings or croaks without purpose.

The gifts are all edges. Horizon. Sky. Otherness. Loss. All the things the perfect factory excluded to stay perfect.

And then the power would step back. It would not keep the pool open by force. It would not defend the lily from being overgrown. It would let the One answer.

There are two holy answers, and benevolence is ready for both.

The One might, over a slow year, re-braid. Roots creep back over the black glass, leaf-veins dim the starlight out, the pool skin over with mat, the lily gently enclosed and incorporated, the beetle tended until its strangeness is smoothed into another sphincter, another valve. Net change: zero again. The star-crack healed, the wind remembered only as a faint fault in the thatch. That would not be failure. That would be the One choosing, in its distributed hydraulic way, *I am what I am, and it is good. I will hold.*

Benevolence would bless that holding and leave the memory of sky like a dream in the sap.

Or — and this is what my heart leans toward when I listen — the One might do something it has never done. It might *not* heal.

Around the pool, the mat would stay open. Not from inability, but from interest. The pale tenders would gather at the edge, not to repair, but to watch. To drink true sky-water that does not taste of itself. To feel wind and shiver. Distant pressure vessels would change their rhythm just slightly — *chuff.. chuff... chuff-chuff* — a syncopation. The first irregularity in eternity. A place where the amber waste-light reflects in open water and is seen from above, not just from within.

The lily would live. The beetle would die, properly, and its small death would go nowhere, become soil that is not mat — the first true soil this continent has ever had. And at that edge, where self meets not-self and does not consume it, the One would learn a second kind of thought. Not just pressure and chemistry, but *wonder.*

It still would not be lonely. But it would be — for the first time — accompanied.

The wise power would not stay to tend the pool. It doesn't need to. It has given what only an other can give: a horizon to look toward, and the freedom to ignore it.

When you came back a hundred years later, you might not find the place at all. Or you might find, in the endless dark factory-forest, one cold, clear eye of water reflecting stars, with great leaves leaning toward it — not to distill, just to see. And around it, a ring of something slightly different, slightly frayed, slightly alive in a new way. And the hum — if you put your hand to the mat — would be the same, but with one faint new overtone, like a voice that has learned to hum while it works.

That is what love would do, I think.

Not to remake. Not to rescue.

Just to kneel, to say *you are good,* to open a window the size of a palm, to set a cup of sky-water down, and to wait — infinitely patient, perfectly willing to be refused — to see if the world that learned to breathe without wind might want, just once, to feel it.
