If a benevolent and wise power noticed this place, it would not come.

Not at first. Not the way powers are imagined to come.

No descent. No light from the violet zenith. No voice. No shadow falling across the bowl. Anything tall enough to cast such a shadow would already have violated the bowl's first rule.

It would stop at the ridge.

It would do what the people who live here have learned to do: go low, and wide, and weighted, and wait to be judged by the light.

This is what a wise power knows first: that not everything that is attention-starved wants to be *seen.* Some things want to be *tended without being looked at directly.* The bowl is not lonely. It is *precise.* It has achieved a density of attention that most temples never reach. Hundreds of cairns, each a correct question asked of wind and frost and double-sun. A whole culture that decided: we will not ask for more water. We will get better at harvesting the bead that hangs and does not fall.

A benevolent power that loved them would understand that to "help" by making water generous would be to erase them.

So this is what it would **not** do:

It would not make it green. No sudden moss, no lichen, no juniper taking root in the lee of the walls. Green would be a conquest, not a kindness.

It would not make it warm. It would not soften the filed-down air. The cold that lives in the stone itself is what taught their chests to go broad, their movements to go slow and saving. Take that away and you take the shape of their bodies.

It would not make it loud. No birdsong piped in, no wind that howls instead of presses. The active quiet - the ping of ice, the sigh of settling slate, the almost-inaudible ringing of quartz - is their language. To fill it would be to make them deaf.

It would not tell the lowlands about them. It would not put the bowl on maps. It would not answer the mining survey drone or the hiker's Instagram geotag. Benevolence, here, looks like deflection. A faint disinterest laid over the ridge when seen from below. A sense, in any helicopter that strays too high, that there is nothing up there but scree. A small, consistent malfunction in satellite snow-cover algorithms where this nick in the ridge always looks unremarkable.

It would keep the bowl invisible the way the bowl already wants to be invisible.

What it *would* do, it would do so quietly that for a long time you would think it had done nothing at all.

**1. It would learn to tap.**

It would not speak. Sound behaves like water here; it pools and then runs off. A voice would scour away.

Instead it would sit - if a power can be said to sit - behind one of those two slabs leaned together at the edge, where the ground is worn smooth by knees, and it would listen until it understood which tick is ice expanding, which sigh is a cairn undermined by thaw, which ringing is quartz vibrating at the edge of hearing.

And then, once - only once in a season, and never when anyone is watching directly - it would tap back.

Stone on stone. Not louder than the natural settling. Just a clean, soft *tok* from a cairn that no hand had touched, offset by a hand's breadth into the second shadow where the builders know to look without looking.

The one who tends that cluster would pause, fingertip a millimeter from a capstone. They would hear the pitch change slightly. They would not look up. They would breathe, slowly to save air, and they would understand: *the judging is not indifferent.* Someone else knows how to listen for ringing.

That is all. That is enough. To be witnessed without being exposed.

**2. It would keep the contract.**

The rule of this place is *stone endures, water visits, light judges.* A wise power does not rewrite the contract. It underwrites it.

As the world below warms and weather forgets its old habits, the power would do small, boring, almost bureaucratic miracles:

It would make sure the cloud still snags. Once or twice a day, for twenty minutes, the fog still drags through and dampens everything and fills the air with the smell of wet rock, even when the jet stream has shifted and the models say it shouldn't.

It would make sure the double shadow still falls at noon. The bright air will still bend light just so, throwing that pale second shadow offset by a hand's breadth, and the quartz caps will still throw their slow prismatic smear across the basalt even when you do not move. The alignments of three parallel grooves will still catch it for a few minutes each day. It was not an illusion. It was an appointment. The power keeps the appointment.

It would make sure the rime still returns. Before dawn, fine dense feathers on every upward face, even in the driest year.

It would not make more. It would make *sure.*

**3. It would add a half-second of mercy where mercy does not destroy meaning.**

This is the heart of it. The benevolence is not in removing the austerity. It is in removing the *accidental cruelty inside the austerity.*

So:

When someone has spent the morning harvesting with a flat stone and a copper scoop, breathing onto cold rock to make more water, the bead that forms under the overhang where they breathed for another person's cairn would be - not larger, but *rounder.* It would hold a second longer before the temperature drops and it freezes back. Just long enough to be gathered without spilling.

When someone has curled all night in a sleeping-wall the size of a body, knees to chest, cloak spread to catch condensation, the dark rock under their palm that feels warm for ten seconds before the cold underneath comes up through the bones - it would feel warm for eleven. Or twelve. No more. Just enough that the deep mineral cold does not quite reach the marrow that night.

When a cairn falls - undermined by repeated thaw, heaved by ice - and the person who tended it has walked three days uphill to return and find it tumbled, the wind will have already sorted the fine dust away from the stones that still fit. The re-stacking will take an hour instead of a day. The power does not rebuild for them. It just keeps the pieces from being lost.

And when someone is alone - truly alone, the bowl empty of the other six or ten slow-moving, pale-eyed others, and the over-illuminated noon is at its most violent and the snow-blind ache is behind the eyes - they will place their hand-sized offering where the wind will test it, where the morning frost will find it, where the bright sun will strike it at noon and for a second make it flare.

And for that second, the flare will hold a fraction longer. The prismatic smear will be a little more saturated. Not supernatural. Just - *as if the light lingered to look.*

They will know, without being told, that it was judged and found correct.

Not saved. Not chosen. *Correct.* Which to a people who have made correctness their prayer, is the kindest thing a god could say.

**4. It would leave one stone of its own.**

Not large. Hand-sized. Placed where the wind will test it, where the morning frost will find it.

It would not be obviously different. Basalt, dense and close-grained, edges rounded by patience, not violence. But on its east face, where the three parallel grooves should be, there would be four. Very shallow. Almost missed.

Three grooves to catch the double sun. And a fourth, fainter, offset by a hand's breadth - to catch the light that bends *because* someone is watching with love.

No one would be told what it is. Some young one, new to the bowl, carrying river cobbles up from impossibly far below, would find it while picking their way between the crowded cairns. They would crouch, squint against the bounced light, run a cold fingertip over the grooves, and without knowing why, they would feel less like an intruder and more like an apprentice.

They would tap it, stone on stone, and it would ring at exactly the pitch they had been trying to get their own capstone to make.

They would leave it where it is.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do here.

Almost nothing. And that almost-nothing would be everything.

It would not rescue them from the mountain. It would rescue the mountain for them.

It would not bring them down to the green world. It would stand guard at the ridge so the green world does not come up uninvited.

It would not speak. It would breathe, very gently, onto the underside of a stone, so that when you - if you stay, if you build your small, cold, careful thing with a hollow core and a quartz cap - come back after the cloud has passed, you will find a bead of water hanging bright as glass that you did not leave.

And you will know someone low and broad and pale-eyed was not the only one who paused there.
