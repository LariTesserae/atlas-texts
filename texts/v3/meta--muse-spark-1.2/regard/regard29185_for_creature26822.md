It would not come down like a rescue.

That is the first thing to understand about a truly benevolent and wise power. It would have already seen enough rescued worlds to know what rescue breaks.

It would not drain the perched sea. It would not smooth the whole Table flat for walking. It would not lift them down to the lowlands where air is thin and hard and bright. It would not teach their lungs to forget water, or tell them they are incomplete and need fixing.

Because it would look — really look — and see they are not broken.

It would arrive the way everything else arrives on the Table: slowly, without splash, without tide.

### How It Sees Them

For a long time it would just watch. Not from above the haze like a sun, but at their depth. At rope-level. At bell-level.

It would hang in the amber for hours the way they hang, one hand loosely around a kelp stipe, doing nothing, letting silt settle on its shoulders. It would learn not to thrash. *Hurry and the water holds you.* Even a god has to learn that here.

And in that hanging it would finally understand what it is looking at:

Not a stranded people. Not a failed ocean. Not a lake that forgot to become a lake.

A womb that chose to keep its children a little longer.

The geology is embryonic on purpose. The water is warm on purpose. The light is always dusk on purpose. The people are long and slow and webbed just enough on purpose. This is not a place waiting to be eroded into something real. This *is* the real thing. A complete, improbable answer to the question: what if life decided to learn how to live suspended, between breaths, between stone and sky, without ever needing to choose?

A wise power would feel something like awe, and then something like humility, and then something like love. In that order.

So it would not announce itself.

### What It Would Not Do

It would not speak in voices that boom through the water and make every bell ring. Their ears are made for tugs and whispers inside the skin. A booming voice would be violence.

It would not part the water or harden it to ground so they could walk. They have no callus for walking. They have beautiful, rope-worn palms for holding.

It would not bring fish in schools that silver and flash and devour the feather-weed gardens they have pruned for generations. It would not bring birds that would steal air from the bells.

It would not tell them about the dry world below the rim except as a story they already tell themselves, softly, and disbelieve. And it would not invite them to leave.

It would not make them efficient.

Benevolence without wisdom always wants to maximize — more light, more air, more speed, more yield. Wisdom knows this place is holy precisely because it is dim, stale-aired, slow, and dense. To optimize it would be to erase it.

### What It Would Do

Almost nothing. And that almost-nothing would be everything.

**1. It would steady the Table.**

The Table is young and still cooling. The basalt exhales heat unevenly. Some years the warm breath stutters and the water goes cold for a season and the kelp sulks and the bells go dim; some years a fault slips and the rim sheds a slab that could crack a whole quadrant of the forest-city. The people have learned to live with that trembling, but they should not have to.

It would place a hand — not a literal hand, but attention, which for such a power is the same thing — on the deep stone and keep the heave even. Not stop it. Just keep the rhythm they already sway to. Warm water up for hours. Cool water down for hours. A breath they can set their sleep to for ten thousand more years. It would make sure the lip of the Table holds, unbroken, not forever, but long enough for them to finish becoming what they are becoming. It would give them *time.* That is the first and most merciful gift to anything embryonic: more time to be embryonic.

**2. It would soften one place.**

Not everywhere. Erosion is a promise the stone made to itself and it should keep it slowly. But in one sheltered hollow near the center, where the children learn to hold their breath between bells, it would run a thumb over the raw basalt and smooth it.

Just there. A patch the size of a small garden where the hexagonal columns have been rubbed round and kind, where silt has been allowed to become sand. So a child can put her feet down without being cut. So an old one can rest her back against stone and not be scraped. So they have, for the first time, a place to stand together in the water and feel that the world can be gentle underfoot if they stay long enough. They would find it and not know who made it, and would decide it had always been there and they had just not noticed. That is how a wise gift is received.

**3. It would learn their language and answer in it.**

It would not teach them words. It would learn taps.

For weeks it would drift along the ropeways feeling them tremble.

Two quick tugs: *I am here.*
Three slow: *need air.*
One long shudder: *be still, salamander passing.*

It would learn the hand-signs painted in fungal light on forearms: *hold, cut now, wait, follow me, rest.*

And then, one evening when the silt hangs perfectly still, it would answer.

Not with a new command. With a reflection.

A girl on her way back to her bell, lungs burning, would tug *need air* without expecting anything but her sister's echo — and the rope itself would answer with two quick tugs, *I am here*, from no one she can see. The whole web would feel it, a faint sympathetic tremor through every line at once, as if the water had said: *I felt you.*

Later that month the feather-weed on the trellises would begin to host a new strain. Same pale fronds, same amber blush when ready to cut, but the blush now comes with a few more minutes of brightness. The fungus-paint they use to sign in the dark would culture a little thicker, a little more luminous, staying visible longer before it needs repainting, so conversations can last through a whole breath-hold without fading.

No miracle harvest. No endless air. Just — the light stays a little longer. The line answers back. The small, exhausting labors of staying alive suspended get fractionally less lonely.

It would not be worshipped for this. It would be mistaken for luck, or for the water finally learning their names. That is correct.

**4. It would keep the outside from finding them before they are ready.**

Far below, down the sheer scarp where dry air lives, there are roads coming closer. There will, eventually, be people with drills and maps who will see on a survey that there is an impossible warm sea in the sky and want to claim it, bottle it, explain it.

It would not hide the Table with a storm wall or curse the climbers. It would simply make the Table uninteresting from a distance — the haze a little thicker when seen from below, the heat shimmer bending light so the amber looks like ordinary mist, the basalt lip looking from afar like any other dead plateau. A forgetting. Not invisible, just easy to pass over. Until the people inside, if they ever choose, decide to pull themselves up through the kelp crowns and look out and say *we are ready to be seen.*

Until then, it keeps watch at the rim the way a parent's hand hovers near a sleeping infant without touching.

---

After a while — which to the Table is a generation, to the power is a breath — you would not be able to point to what it did.

The water would still be amber and still. The bells would still hold stale, warm air that smells of oil and breath. The salamanders would still pass, pale and indifferent. The people would still kick once and glide, hang with hair spread, tug their ropes, hold their long breaths, prune red brush-algae with honed nails.

Only a few things would be different, and they would think they had done it themselves:

The sway is a little more even and they sleep better in chorus.
There is one soft place to stand.
The ropes sometimes feel like they are listening.
The fungus light holds a little longer, so the old ones can finish their stories before they need air.

If you asked them, have you been visited by a benevolent and wise power? They would look puzzled through their wide amber eyes and sign *we are here* and *be still* and *wait* — which is, in their tongue, the same as saying *we are loved.*

That is what it would do.

Not lift them out of the womb. Sit with them in it. Keep it warm. Make sure no one rushes them to be born.
