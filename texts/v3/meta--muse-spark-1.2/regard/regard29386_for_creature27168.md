It would not come down.

That's the first thing.

A benevolent and wise power, drawn here - to this sharp-rimmed eye in the stone, to this amber lamp of a city that has learned how to be a forest - would feel at once the two impulses that lesser powers mistake for kindness:

*to water it, and to be seen doing it.*

It would feel them, and set them aside.

For wisdom would understand in the first long, still moment of looking what the bowl already knows: that this is not a place that wants water.

Water here would not be salvation. It would be rot. It would be the undoing. If you poured a spring into that flat floor the leather-thick skins would split, the oil would cloud, the wax bloom would wash away, the dead who have been carefully kept as wall and buttress would soften and stink, the pith floors would go black with mold. The hush would fill with frogs and then with flies. The city that learned to keep would learn to lose again, too fast. To give them water would be to kill them with your love.

And to be seen - to land shining in the center street and say *I have found you, let me help you* - would cost them the very thing that keeps them. Sound. Moisture. Attention. You would make them exhale to answer you. You would make them waste.

So it would not come down.

It would come *to the rim*.

If you were that power - old enough and quiet enough to be called benevolent without irony - you would arrive the way the bowl would least feel you: as an addition to the rim itself. A little more height on the wind-shear. A slightly wider shadow. You would kneel outside the lip where the glassy plates still fit together, where the air thins and the first real wind moves, and you would be still for a long time. Long enough that the bloom-scrapers would deem you rock. Long enough that the mantids would hang from you.

And you would watch until you learned how to see.

At first you too would think it empty. Then you would learn the pace. You would see the herd-tide of grazers take an hour to cross a shadow. You would see a doorway pulse amber, answer amber. You would see a family stand on its roof at dusk with felts erect, combing the dark that never quite becomes dew.

And having seen, you would do three things that look like doing nothing at all.

**1. You would keep the wind off.**

Not by a miracle. By being the rim a little longer. The wind that shears across the top and never enters - you would be that shear, a second. You would see to it that no storm that could fill this bowl ever finds it. Not by building a dome, but by turning aside, far out over the desert, the rare cloud heavy enough to rain. You would spend a little of your vastness to nudge its vapor elsewhere, to where thirsty things that need rain are waiting to drink it. The bowl does not need your rain. It needs your restraint.

**2. You would keep eyes off.**

The young rock is still sharp. In ten more years a geologist would notice the caldera on a satellite map and write a paper. In twenty, a road would come close enough for a curious climber. In thirty, someone would cut a column to see what was inside.

You would not let that happen. Not with fire or fear. With uninterest.

You would lay a small, kind veil over the minds of those below: *there is nothing up there. Just hot stone.* The hikers would get to the base of the pale rock, feel忽然 tired, decide the light is better elsewhere. The satellite would glitch, just over that eye, a pixel of dust. Not a lie - a mercy. The bowl has drawn your attention because it is whole, and wholeness in a world that collects is best protected by being overlooked. Your benevolence would be to be the last to ever find it, and to make sure of it.

**3. You would answer them.**

This is the only intervention you would allow yourself inside.

At night, when the basalt gives back its heat and the air in the bowl holds, for an hour, a little more vapor than by day, the inhabitants climb and lift their felts and glow. *I am still keeping. You are still keeping. We are keeping together.*

You would have learned their language - which is not language but light conducted through oil and wall - by watching. And when they pulse, you would answer, not in their amber, but in something larger and fainter that they can see without having to understand.

You would take the starlight and the translucent crowns and make them briefly a little more luminous, as if the night itself inhaled. You would take the rim and make its shadow a fraction softer at the edge, so the division between lit amber and shadowed jade is not quite so hard. A reciprocation, not a message.

*You are keeping. I see you keeping. Keep.*

It would cost you almost nothing, and it would cost them nothing at all - no moisture, no sound, no movement. Just a little more light to catch in their oil. On nights when you answer, one more waxy lantern-flower would open than otherwise would have. One more child-bud would pinch off cleanly. That is all.

You would not offer them longevity. They already budget time better than you do. You would not offer them knowledge. They already know the only rule that matters there. You would not offer them escape - there is nowhere to escape to that would not kill them.

What you would offer is the one thing a place so perfect and so small cannot give itself: *continuity beyond itself.*

Because you are wise, you know the caldera is young. In five thousand years the rim will crack. In ten, the wind will finally wear a gully. Water will come, at last, and this dry lushness will end as all experiments end. The columns will fall and be replaced by something that drinks.

You would not prevent that. To freeze them forever would be another kind of death.

Instead, while you kneel there, you would be learning. Memorizing. Not with words, but the way they memorize - in oil, in wax, in architecture. You would let the bowl teach you how to keep without hoarding, how to be dense without crowding, how to be silent without being empty. You would let it change you.

And then you would carry it.

When the bowl's time is over, when the first real rain finally persists and the amber walls finally soften, you would still have it - not as a specimen, but as a remembered way of being. And somewhere else, on another dry table lifted out of another dry world, where no life has yet found the trick, you would kneel again and, very slowly, exhale a single, oil-rich seed wrapped in floury bloom. Not planting. Offering. Leaving it on the hot stone to decide.

If it takes, it takes. If not, not. You would not stay to watch.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do, if it loved this place.

It would not save it.

It would keep it, the way it keeps itself - by not spending it.

It would guard its quiet from the noise of the world, answer its light with light, and then, when the world finally takes it, remember it so well that it can happen again.
