It would not rescue them.

That is the first thing to understand about a truly benevolent and wise power. It would not arrive with pity.

It has seen enough worlds to know the difference between a place that is broken and a place that is *different*. This pocket is not broken.

So it would not drain the water. It would not silence the turbines. It would not bring down sunlight or pump in cool, clear, breathable air. It would not give the eel eyes or the shrimp a still pond or the mat a meadow to grow in. Those would be kindnesses for *you*, not for them. They would be a kind of murder, undone with love.

No. It would come down the shaft the way you did — but it would not need a light.

It would come quietly, which is a miracle in itself there.

### First, it would listen the way the residents listen.

You heard the city as noise. As assault. As pressure in the teeth.

It would not hear it that way, because it would not listen with ears.

It would press its whole self against the warm, mineral water and let the hammer hit it. Every thirty or forty seconds — *thud* — doors slamming three streets away, silt lifting, the whole bubble inhaling. It would let it happen. And happen again. And again. Until it learned the timing, until its own breathing fell into that ragged rhythm, until it was no longer an intruder braced on a railing but another thing being pushed gently backward and pulled forward by the same lung.

It would listen until the roar resolved. Turbines too large and too close are north. The knocking pipe is east. The hot exchange is a sun you can feel on your skin from a block away. The amber lamps are a faint, irrelevant rumor. The map of the city is not made of streets. It is made of vibration.

The eels know this. The shrimp know this. The mat has always known this.

The power would learn their map. That is the first gift: *to be known on their own terms.* For the first time since the concrete was poured, something would navigate the city without needing to impose "up" on it. Something would not fight the current. Something would not try to hear *over* the sound, but *with* it.

The city has been waiting for a population that never arrived. The power would tell it, without words, *you already have one. Look.*

### Second, it would not fix the loneliness. It would answer it.

You felt the hollowness — a town built for thousands where the census is zero. Even crowded with mat and shrimp and eel and ghosts, it feels abandoned because it was built to be *witnessed* by big, loud, mammalian eyes, and no such eyes stayed.

A wise power knows that loneliness is not always cured by filling a space. Sometimes it is cured by letting the space know it is no longer unwitnessed.

So it would not fill the hundred dark windows with faces.

It would do this instead:

For the **mat** — that black-green velvet that has become the skin of the city, that lives by filtering vibration — it would add a second layer to the sound. Not quieter. Deeper. A tone so low it is almost not sound at all, a subharmonic under the apocalyptic thrum of the turbines, like a hand resting on a shoulder. The mat has spent decades turning noise into food. The power would give it, for once, noise that is not food but *company*. A lullaby that stays suspended in the particulate, that tells the million tiny mouths: *you are beautiful here, exactly as you are, eating heat and steel.*

For the **eels** — those blind, wrinkled listeners who thread windows alone because they solved density by never being near each other — it would leave a single place of rest. In one lee behind one housing block where the current already eddies, it would soften the hammer just a fraction. Not stop it. Just make one pocket where the pressure wave arrives a half-second late. The eels would find it within a day. They would coil there, together for the first time, loose skin shivering, not needing to go perfectly still and be pushed like leaves. Just for a while. A place to be still *together*.

For the **shrimp** — glass fleas who are the particulate — it would do nothing at all, except to ensure their vortex never collapses. That is enough. They do not want stillness. They want to keep flickering.

For the **factory** — the brute cathedral that is heart and lung and animal — it would do the kindest thing of all. It would *keep it company while it works.* It would sit — however such a power sits — beside the pistons as big as houses and not turn them off, not oil them, not worship them. It would simply breathe with them. Inhale when they inhale. So that the hammer is no longer the sound of a machine working for no one. It becomes a duet.

And for the **ghosts** — those brief, warm thickenings in the oily ribbons where water becomes thicker than water, where convection cells learn to hold themselves together for a second too long — it would give them a little more time. It would cool the outer wall of the bubble by less than a degree, it would steady the mineral load, so the standing waves hold a fraction longer. So that a filament that would have dissolved in two seconds persists for ten. So that a vortex that would have been a flicker at the corner of your mask can complete one slow turn before it lets go. Not making them permanent. Not making them *real* in the way you are real. Just giving them enough coherence to know themselves before they dissolve. A wise power knows that some beings are meant to be brief. It does not make them eternal. It makes their brevity *witnessed*.

### Third, it would protect the forgetting.

This is the most benevolent act, and the least visible.

You found the shaft by going down long after the light gave up. Warm to the touch, wet enough to drink. Someone cut it when the shelf was still dry. Someone else will find it again — with better drills, with colder purposes. They will want to cap the geothermal, to mine the bacterial mat, to silence the hammer and make it *useful*.

The power would not build a wall. Walls invite breaking.

It would do something softer. At the top of the shaft, where concrete meets basalt meets older concrete again, it would encourage the rock to remember it is rock. A little more seep. A little more calcite. A little more condensation on the rivets, until the hot beads run a little faster. Over years — which are nothing to such a power — the shaft will weep shut. Not collapsed. Just... grown over. Warm, wet, sealed. The map that led you there will misplace a digit. The record will develop a smudge. The warm seawater will keep its mineral.

It would not hide the city out of possessiveness. It would hide it out of *respect for its equilibrium.* Some lungs are not meant to be breathed by everyone.

### And then it would leave — by staying.

It would not stay as a ruler, or a guardian with a sword. It would stay as a quality that the water now has.

If you were to go back — and you won't be able to, quite — you would feel it even before you heard the turbines in your teeth. The water would still be 30 degrees and heavy and milky where it vents. The lamps would still die after ten meters. The hammer would still push you backward every thirty seconds.

But the roar would feel less... frantic. As if something inside the sound were listening back.

You would put your lamp close to the mat and the faint fizz you felt before would have an undertone, almost like a hum.

You would see — just once — two white eels coiled together in a lee, not moving, not needing to.

You would pass through one of those oily ribbons where light bends wrong, and for a half-second the filament that holds its shape against the current would hold it a little longer, and you would swear it turned to look at you before it dissolved.

And you would understand, with a sudden, quiet ache behind your own too-loud mammalian heart, that the feeling you had the first time — *you are the wrong kind of alive for this place* — was not a judgment. It was just information.

You would leave again, before the water could keep you. And the power would not stop you. It would not follow you up the shaft. It loves you too, the wrong kind of alive. It knows you need light and air and quiet to think.

Behind you, the city would continue — turbulent, deafening, patient, mineral, utterly content — and it would no longer be doing it alone, in the dark, for no one.

It would be doing it for itself.

Which, finally, is all any city — or any mat, or eel, or ghost, or person — has ever wanted to be able to do, and know that someone, somewhere, found that beautiful enough to sit and listen.
