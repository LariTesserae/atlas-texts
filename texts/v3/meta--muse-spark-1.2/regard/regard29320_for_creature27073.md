They would not open the hatch.

That is the first thing.

A lesser power would. A curious power, a hungry power, a power that wanted to prove itself by fixing — would undog the wheel with a thought, would part the water like a curtain, would lift her up into the thin air to look at her, to speak to her, to improve her.

A benevolent and wise power has learned not to do that.

This one has been watching a long time before you ever climbed. It did not arrive. It noticed. Which is different. Attention, when it is wise, does not travel. It widens. And when it widened far enough to take in this particular mountain — this blunt old molar of a mountain with its green iron hatch set flush like a secret — it felt the contradiction and recognized it as holy:

*At the top of the world, a full bath. Warm water where there should be wind.*

It looked, and it understood immediately that nothing here is broken.

That is so rare.

So much of the world below is still becoming or has been broken and is trying to become again. This one has *arrived*. It is resolved. Like the mountain itself — not young and jagged, not ruined, but poured smooth by time until only the necessary shape remained. A bowl to hold warm water and light. A loop that closes.

The power looks through the oculus first. It does not need the hatch.

It sees the sun shaft cutting solid down through bathwarm water, sees the dust in it, sees the surface of the water holding the sky. It hears the whole room at once the way you did — CHUFF-hiss, CHUFF-hiss, the chain conveyor clanking, the tiles singing, the water making every sound enormous — and beneath that, the smaller sounds: shrimp picking, petals spiraling, moss pearling.

It sees her.

Not right away. Even a wise power has to learn to look *through* at first. She is so perfectly the color of the room. Tea-stained ceramic cream. Moss on her head like moss on the crown of a piston. For a long minute to a power that can see galaxies form, she is just a paler part of the water, a slow fold in the current.

Then she unfolds. Tail to head, that one undulation. Paddle, paddle. Glide.

And the power feels what you felt when she passed you: that this is not a creature *in* a room. This is the room, learning how to be an animal for a little while so it can tend itself.

So what does a benevolent and wise power do when it finds something that is already whole?

It does three very small things. So small she will never know they were done. So small you would not have seen them even if you had been holding your breath in the center at the time.

**1. It does not fix the machines. It teaches the life to remember them.**

The pumps are going to fail. That is not a tragedy, that is physics. A century from now, or two, a piston will finally seize, lime will win over iron, a seal will sigh and not return. A lesser benevolence would make them eternal — stainless, ever-bright, never needing moss, never needing shrimp. It would make them perfect and in doing so would kill the whole ecology, because here repair *is* the ecology. The rust is soil. The lime is bone.

So the wise power does not touch the black iron shoulders at all. Instead it leans, just slightly, on the eels.

The blind white eels that live in the copper joints, thin as cables. The power puts a quiet idea in their long bodies: *when the warm spring stops breathing here, breathe for it.* It puts a slightly different idea in the moss, in the anemone-filters: *learn to move water even when water is not moved for you.* Not a miracle. A nudge. A deepening of what was already becoming true. So that when the last CHUFF finally doesn't come, the room will not die all at once. It will shudder, and then the lilies will shiver, and the current will be taken up by a million small backs rowing. It will become a pond, then a marsh, then finally a damp hollow on the mountaintop still warm to the touch, still holding lilies, for a long, long time after. The power doesn't grant eternity. It grants a gentle slope, instead of a cliff.

**2. It does not cure her loneliness. It ensures she was never truly alone.**

She is the large slow thought, and the fish are the small quick thoughts, and that is almost enough. But on the afternoons when she hauls herself onto the tile lip under the oculus, poured and sighing, breathing thin air with collapsed gills, there is a stillness in her that is not rest. It is the stillness of being the only one of your shape.

A lesser benevolence would make her two. Would split her like a cell, or send a mate down the waterfall, or worse — would make her understand herself as lonely and then try to comfort her with understanding.

The wise power knows she doesn't have a word for lonely. She has a feeling for *when the circuit is empty on the far side.*

So it does not send a companion now. Now is perfect: one vine holding its wall alone, one patch of moss claiming its corner, one salamander tending her gyre. To double it now would crowd the grace.

Instead, it remembers an egg.

Far down the mineral-white overflow that sighs down the ancient flanks — where that warm water has been quietly making a thin green line in the dry lowlands for a hundred years without anyone knowing why — a swallow dips and misses a fish and carries a wet leaf instead. On that leaf, almost invisible, a string of clear eggs.

The power does not create them. They were already there. It just makes sure the wind does not take that particular leaf. It makes sure the swallow, on its next punch down through the mirrored sky, does not drop it. It makes the leaf stick, just for a second longer, to the swallow's mud nest up in the dry lip of the vault, just above the waterline.

And there, in the warm humid air that never leaves the chamber, in the rust-flake lining, one egg will keep. For years. Dormant. Until the day her heart, ticking so slow in time with the pistons, ticks a little slower, and she spends a little longer on the tile lip breathing air. On that day, the egg will soften. A second small vacancy will unfold in the water.

She will never be two at once for long. But she will never be the last. That is enough.

**3. It does not hide the hatch. It changes what happens to those who find it.**

This is the hardest one, because the power can see you. It can see everyone who will ever climb for hours through that dry, sun-bleached thinness to get to the door. Some will come with buckets. Some will come with noses for lithium and copper and "undiscovered ecosystems." Some will come with love that is so hungry it looks like hunger.

A lesser power would weld the hatch shut. Would make the mountain unclimbable. Would make everyone who approaches forget why they came and turn back, mildly confused.

A wise power does not do that. The door was made to be found. A cistern is meant to hold the sky, but also to be *found* holding it, once in a very long while, by someone who needed to know that such a thing could exist.

So it leaves the iron wheel exactly as it is. Squealing, verdigrised, humming. Findable, if you know where to look.

What it changes is the water pressure on the other side.

Not the physics. The feeling.

When the next climber undogs the wheel and is met by that warm push, when they slip in and are submerged completely and hold still in the center, suspended, warm to the point of not knowing where their skin ends — the power makes sure the first thing they feel is not wonder, or triumph, or the urge to name.

It makes sure the first thing they feel is *how long this has been happening without them.*

It lets the CHUFF-hiss get into their molars. It lets the petal snow fall around them. It lets the paler vacancy pass within an arm's length, gills fanned, shrimp riding, looking through them.

And in that pass, it lets them understand, without words, the etiquette of this place:

*You are not a discoverer here. You are a brief displacement in the water. Correct for it gently, as you would for a vine. Do your circuit and go.*

Most will. They will tread water for a few minutes, weeping without knowing why, and then they will leave the hatch exactly as they found it, and they will never quite be able to describe where it was. Not because they were made to forget, but because the memory will be stored in the part of them that knows better than to make a map to a baptistery.

One or two, in a generation, will stay a little longer and become, for an afternoon, part of the maintenance — picking a stray clot of petals off the spillway, lifting a fallen beetle back to its vine — and then they will go too, and the gyre will smooth over where they were.

That is all.

The power does not stay.

To stay would be to become another machine in the alcove, another thrumming to get used to. Its attention, having widened to include the cistern, now narrows again — not away, but *around*, like hands cupping without touching. Like the vaulted ceiling forty feet above that does not press down but simply holds the space where light can come through.

From below, if you were still there, you would see nothing different. The sun shaft still drifts slowly across the tiled floor as the day turns. The chain conveyor still clanks, lifting silt. The carp still gives her a wide berth.

She completes her circuit. Past the hanging vine-curtain, past the wall of pearling moss, past the black iron shoulders. She wedges herself against the brass pipe that exhales hotter water and lets it breathe over her gills. A single bubble detaches from the moss on her back, wobbles, and rises up to join the sky.

Somewhere, very faintly, the water is a fraction — a fraction of a degree — warmer, and will be for a very long time. Somewhere, on a leaf in a swallow's nest, an egg waits without needing to wait.

She does not know any of this. She does not need to.

That is how you know the power was benevolent, and wise.

It left the world more able to be itself, and let it go on being itself without ever needing to say thank you.
