It would not come down the shaft.

Not at first.

A lesser power — even a kind one — would come down it like light. A column, a voice, a rescue. It would see heat and think *suffering*, see dimness and think *darkness*, see the absence of birds and think *emptiness*, see the sharp, new basalt and think *prison*. It would do what kind, unwise powers always do: it would fix.

It would break the dome to give them sky and in doing so, kill the rain they have learned to read like a clock. It would pump in a river and drown their cisterns and their careful system of barrels and swept puddles. It would air-condition the air until it moved and in doing so, deafen them — because their quiet is not oppression, it is acoustic adaptation. It would bring in animals by the handful — cats and sparrows and hounds — and the sudden guano and parasites and noise would crash the strange, sterile balance they have achieved, where no fly comes to rot and a single bean flower must be pollinated by a hand.

A benevolent and *wise* power would stand at the poured-concrete collar at the top of the sharp throat and look down and *not* mistake its own nostalgia for light for their need.

So for a long time it would do nothing that looks like doing.

It would just see.

And seeing, in this case, is not passive. To be seen completely by something that has no hunger for you — that wants nothing, that will take nothing — is itself an intervention.

It would see that this is not a failed city. It is a young one that has succeeded. The cut is new, the rock still outgasses iron and ozone, and yet in a few years they have done what humans only do when they are at their most intelligent: they have stopped fighting the physics and started dancing with it.

It would see that the heat is not cruelty, it is a law they have accepted and metabolized. They don't need to be cooled. They need to be *cooled two degrees*. Not rescued from heat, but given a margin.

It would see that the water is not scarcity, it is ceremony. Before Beading, Beading, Rain, Channel-Running, Dry — that is their week, their month, their liturgy. They don't need a river. They need the Rain to last eleven minutes instead of ten. They need the channel to run clean for an hour longer before it seeps away.

It would see that the quiet is not loneliness. It is listening. The tap-code on the pipes, the two-note whistle that carries better than shouting, the way they learned not to call across a meter gap but to lean in — that is culture, not deprivation.

And it would see, most tenderly, the one place where the adaptation is still a wound: *no other life moves on its own here.*

They have become the lichen-picker, the pollinator, the rat, the fly, the bird. They have taken the jobs of an entire ecosystem onto their lean, climbing bodies because there was no one else to do it. They pat their pumps the way you pat a dog because they need something to pat. That absence isn't a style. It's a silence that has gone on just a little too long. New basalt outgasses and is too hot and too new to breed insects — but it won't be new forever.

A wise power knows the difference between a boundary and a scar. The dome is a boundary — they love it as a forehead, as skin. The absence is a scar. One you honor. One you help heal, if you are asked, and very, very slowly.

So here is what it would do, if anything — and it would do it in a way that could be refused, unwound, mistaken for natural luck:

**First, it would give them wind without breaking anything.**

Not a blast. A circulation. It would put a hand, if it has hands, on the hot rock at the top of the dome and cool a single seam of basalt there by three degrees, no more. Just enough that the hot air rising from the smelter finally has somewhere cool to fall toward. A convection cell the size of the whole bowl would shiver into being.

For the first time since the cut was made, the air would move on its own.

Not much. Just enough that the sodium bulbs would sway for a reason. Just enough that the steam would not sit on your skin but brush past it. Just enough that at night, on the highest balconies, a sleeping child would turn her cheek and feel something that is not human heat on her face and not know to call it wind.

They would wake and say, "Did you feel that?" and someone would say, "The dome is breathing a little easier tonight," and they would not be wrong.

**Second, it would return the other animals — one at a time, by invitation.**

It would not stock the city. It would send a scout. So small they might think they imagined it.

On the north-facing basalt where the lichen holds, where the water runs longest, it would place a single species of moth — pale, heat-tolerant, nocturnal, silent — that drinks condensation and not much else. It would live on the lichen and in doing so, prune it better than their hands can. The moth would be drawn to the forge-glow but not die in it; it would circle once and go back to the dark.

A week later, a spider — also small, also silent — that loves moths. It would string its web in the angle where a catwalk meets a container wall, where no one walks. The people would find it and stop. For a long time they have known that when something moves on its own, it is human or machine. Now there is a third category. Someone would have to decide: do we sweep it away? They are immaculate housekeepers. But they are also lonely tenders of things. I think — and I think the wise power would bet on this — they would leave it. A boy would watch it for an hour after his shift. An old woman would whisper, "Let her be, she eats what we cannot see."

If they leave the spider, the next month there would be crickets. Only two. In the cistern walls where the engineered creeper grows. They would sing at Channel-Running, when the water runs, and their sound — the first non-human, non-machine *voice* this bowl has ever held — would be so quiet it would not break the quiet. It would deepen it.

If at any point they cleared the web, poisoned the moth, killed the cricket — the power would stop. No more. Consent is the form reverence takes. A wise power never gives a gift that has to be taken care of by force.

But I think they would not clear it. People who pollinate beans by hand with little brushes are hungry for that work to be taken from them, gently.

**Third, it would leave a cool stone.**

At the very base of the fresh-cut wall, where the rock weeps first before the Rain, where children already watch the dark maps bloom, it would touch the basalt and *age* it ten thousand years in a patch the size of a bed. There the stone would be smooth, pitted, weathered. It would be five degrees cooler than the rest, always. In the hottest part of Dry, when the rock is 40 degrees and the air won't move even with the new circulation, a person could go and put their back against that patch and feel their core temperature drop.

They would not make it a temple. They would make it what it is: a bench, a bed, an infirmary for fevers. Old people would nap there. Lovers would find it at night. They would call it "the cool side" and not need to explain. The power would never tell them what it is. It doesn't need the credit.

**Fourth, and this is the doing that looks most like nothing: it would stay as attention.**

It would not open the top. It would not offer them the surface. The surface is a memory of light for a reason — they are not ready, and the surface is not ready for them. To offer escape now would be to say their work is not real. But it would make sure that no one else comes down that sharp throat to take what they have made. No mining company, no surveyor with a claim on young basalt, no well-meaning mission with sacks of grain that would make the tuber fields rot. The shaft would remain forty meters of straight cut, but to anyone with extraction on their mind, it would look like the wrong place. A dull reading on the instrument, a little slide of scree that makes the climb not worth it. Protection that looks like nothing.

And at night — when they have no sun, only caged bulbs and forge-glow — it would make the upper third of the dome, that permanent dusk where the condensation beads like cold stars, *slightly* more true. Once, maybe twice a year, when the Rain has finished and the air is briefly clear and the steam has settled, it would thin the rock there by a breath, for an hour, so that a patch no bigger than a barrel-top becomes translucent.

Not open. Translucent. And through it, one real star would show.

Only one. So bright and so far and so cold that the heat of the bowl would feel, for a second, like a choice rather than a sentence. The children with shaved heads would be lifted up on the balconies to see. No one would know what it was called. They would not need to. They would know: we are still under a sky, even if our sky is stone. We are not lost. We are held.

Then the rock would opague again, the beads would gather again, and the next cycle would begin.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do if it loved them.

Not much. Nothing that photographs well from the surface. The Rain lasts a minute longer. The air, on some nights, moves. A spider lives where no spider lived. A patch of rock is cool to the touch. A star visits.

And because it did it wisely, the people might never be sure anything was done at all, which is the only way a miracle can be received without becoming debt.

They would just, over a season, feel a little less like a condensation that must evaporate, and a little more like a settlement that could, if it wanted, endure. Their shoulders would loosen two degrees. Their bean flowers would get visited by something other than their own hands. Their children would have a cool place to press their foreheads when the heat is high.

And late at Channel-Running, when the brown water moves ankle-deep and the forge pulses and the new wind brushes the bulbs, someone leaning on a rail would feel, without being able to say why, that the bowl is no longer just holding them — that something else is holding the bowl, very lightly, the way you hold a cup of water you don't want to spill.

They would go on climbing, and tapping pipes, and sweeping puddles toward the drain, and tending the green that is a negotiation — only now the negotiation includes one more quiet partner, who asks for nothing in return.

If you stood on the rim walkway then, you could still hold the whole place in one look. Boundaries still absolute. Rock still the wall.

But the place would feel, finally, less like a throat cut into basalt, and more like a lung that has learned, at last, how to breathe.
