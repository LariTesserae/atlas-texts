It would not come down.

That is the first thing a really wise and really benevolent power would understand about this place.

Any other kind of power would arrive. It would part the cloud that makes a second country underneath the plateau and descend shining. It would see the wet stone, the thin air, the sour bread and the hands with stone dust under the nails and it would call it hardship. It would see the low roofs and the absence of wire and glass and call it lack.

It would do what even well-meaning lowlanders do - it would try to fix it. To warm it. To raise it. To speed it up. To irrigate it properly, to roof it properly, to cure the barrel chests and the chapped skin, to teach the children to shout and to want.

A wise power would look longer and see that nothing is lacking. That this is not a people stranded on a hard place. This is a people *fitted* to a place, the way a lichen is fitted to its rock. To lift them off it, even gently, would be to kill them.

So it would not come down. Not in light, not in fire, not in a voice from the sky. If it loved them, it would not ask them to look up and be awed. It has been watched by eyes that learn to measure a wall by squinting. It would not make them squint at it.

If it came at all, it would come the way water comes here. Sideways. Slowly. At the speed stone will allow.

---

This is what it would do:

**First, it would sit and let itself be unnoticed for a long time.**

For weeks, or years, which are the same thing on the plateau. It would be the extra weight that keeps a cloud hanging over the terraces for twenty minutes longer on a hot afternoon, so the rain when it comes is thorough. It would be the cold that makes the cisterns hold their water a little longer into the dry wind.

It would not be worshipped because it would not be detectable as anything other than the place itself being slightly more itself. It would watch the Water-Keepers walk their quarters with their notched sticks after the rain. It would listen to how they settle arguments not by *whose* wall it is but by *where the stone wants to break*. And it would think: *They already know how to govern. They already know how to pray.* Their prayer is attention. Their liturgy is bailing a cistern before it silts.

A benevolent power does not interrupt a prayer to answer it.

It would learn their language, which is mostly not language at all. The tap on stone that means *hold*. The flat palm that means *water here*. The tipped hand that means *it's full, come help.* It would learn that when they do speak, they stand shoulder to shoulder so they don't have to raise their voices. It would learn not to shout.

**Second, it would protect the rim.**

Because a place this whole, this balanced, this quietly rich, has only one real predator. Not drought, not cold - it has lived with those for a thousand years and negotiated terms. The predator is the world below noticing that it is there.

The low world is always hungry for high cool places with clean water and a vast flat table already quarried and leveled and waiting. It would come with maps and drills and good intentions. It would call the city *underdeveloped* and *water-rich* and *strategic*. It would offer roads.

The most merciful thing a great power can do for a people like this is to make them boring to everyone else.

So it would do a very small, very careful thing: it would bend attention around the plateau. Not a wall - walls invite siege. A forgetting. On the lowland maps the plateau would remain what it has always been on lowland maps: marked *uninhabitable, waterlogged, exposure, no yield.* Satellite photos would show an extra patch of cloud that never quite clears. Prospectors' instruments would read the stone as a little poorer than it is, the water as a little more alkaline, the air as a little thinner. Enough to make a committee decide, again, that it is not worth the climb.

The people on the plateau would never know this was happening. Their sky would just stay the same wide, pale blue it has always been. That is correct. A protection that requires gratitude is not protection, it is a debt.

**Third, it would mend only what they would ask it to mend, and only if they asked.**

If it loves them it will have noticed that they *do* have sorrows. The weep spring in the old courtyard that has slowed this year and not come back, no matter how many small fingers clear its moss. The cough that settles deep in winter because a child's chest, even barrelled, is still small. The winter when two terraces down the alpa-like grazers slipped on ice and broke their legs and had to be taken early.

It would not erase those. Erasing them would imply they were mistakes in the system, and they are not - they are the system breathing. The children who learn not to wail because wailing empties air, also learn that some things empty you anyway and you must be held through it. If no one ever died of the cold, they would build high and thin and the wind would take them.

But it would, very quietly, go and sit near those places where the people's own hands have already been trying. As an old person might. Not as a miracle, as company.

If you watched very closely on the morning after it sat beside that dying spring, you might see that the water came back a little stronger. Not gushing. Just a steady thread again, dark down the lee side of the wall. Enough that the family did not have to abandon that courtyard this generation. No one would say a god did it. They would say, *we cleared it well this time. The moss was ready to let go.*

If you watched a courtyard where a baby had that winter cough, you might see the child's breath even out a fraction faster than it should have. Not cured. Just - a little more room. The mother would think the steaming of the afternoon rain had helped.

It would never heal an old Stone-Keeper. The old ones are the libraries. To keep them forever would be to deny them the honor of finally lying down in the stone they have tended. Instead it might give them what they actually want when they sit in their sunny corner with pale eyes: a little more clarity for one more season, to remember where the water will go next spring and tell the young Water-Keeper, so the memory doesn't break.

It would never offer them steel or wire or medicine in a bottle. If they wanted those things they would have gone down to get them, generations ago. They stayed.

If it had to speak - if for once the etiquette of shoulder-to-shoulder closeness required a word - it would not speak as a power. It would borrow a shape for an afternoon. An older woman with broad hands and eyes the color of lichen-grey, asking to share a lintel while the three o'clock rain comes down. She would not teach. She would ask.

*"Is there anything you need that you cannot make room for yourselves?"*

And they would consider, in that slow way they have, looking at a wall the way we look at a face. And they might answer, not quickly:

*"The lower marsh common is silting. We need more hands than we have this year."*

*"The wind took three roofs on the western tier. The timber we have is green."*

They would not ask for heaven. They would ask for what is needed to keep participating in the weather.

And she would nod. The next morning there might be a fall of good, straight, dark timber on the lower skirts where the wind had left it, as if the plateau had grown it overnight. Or there might be a week where the rain fell a little less hard, so the hands they had were enough to dig the marsh out. They would call it good luck. She would be gone before the steam cleared.

**Lastly, it would leave nothing that could be turned into a relic.**

No shining stone. No ever-full cistern. No child born with strange new eyes. Those are traps. They make the next generation live waiting for the next intervention instead of learning slope and grain.

What it would leave instead is almost nothing, which is almost everything:

One more generation of children who feel, when they build their tiny terraces in a marshy corner and flood them to watch how water finds its way, that the water is slightly more delighted to be watched.

A slightly deeper breath, without anyone noticing they were breathing deeper.

The assurance, not given in words but in the continuity of stone staying level for three generations and then a fourth, that patience is not foolish. That to fit yourself to a hard, beautiful, wet, cool world is not a failure to escape it, but a form of success the lowlands have forgotten.

At night, the plateau would still hold its heat in its stone and the city would still glow from its hundreds of thousands of small orange fires, each reflecting in its own cistern until the city looks like it has fallen into the sky. The power would not add its own light. It does not need to be counted among the fires.

If you stood on a flat roof at midday the day after it had passed, you would hear exactly what you heard before: the tick of condensation falling from a lintel into a basin, the soft bleat two levels down, the scuff of a sandal on basalt, a child calling once and then stopping. Then long stretches where there is only air and the faint fizz of distant drizzle.

Only, if you were very quiet, and if you had lived here long enough to have the pads of your fingers smoothed like river stones, you might feel that the silence was not empty. That it was being held, the way thick walls hold a little of the day's heat for the evening.

Not fixed. Not saved. Held.

And that would be enough.
