It would not come down.

That is the first thing.

If it is truly benevolent, and truly wise, it will have learned long ago — the way that animal learned — that to arrive is to disturb. That weight, speed, light, hunger all translate into vibration. And anything that lives by vibration feels a footfall as an earthquake.

So it would not land. It would not part the vines. It would not speak.

It would *notice*. And that is already an act.

---

For a very long time it would just peer over the lip the way you did, but without a body to cast shadow.

It would feel the whole shape at once — not just the damp trough and the breather in it, but the entire logic of the wound.

How the scrub above is too hot and too open and too wind-scoured to be kind, and so the earth gave way.
How the collapse trapped rain and then taught the rain a new path — sweat instead of river.
How the light learned to be rationed.
How the funnel learned to be a stomach.
How a salamander — or newt — or olm — or something that has no name up on the plain because it only exists *here* — stopped needing eyes because eyes were a waste of wet. Stopped needing legs because legs would carry it away from water. Stretched its skin until its skin was a bowl, and invited algae to live in it so it could eat sun when beetles were scarce.

A wise power would recognize that immediately for what it is: not a deformity. Not a tragedy. A *solution*. A complete, elegant answer to a very small question: *how do you live forever in one room?*

And it would feel — not pity. That would be an insult to something so perfectly adapted. It would feel something closer to reverence, or recognition.

*Oh,* it would think. *You did it too. You learned how to hold.*

Because that is what a benevolent old power is — something that has also learned to hold heat long after the sun is gone. To keep dripping when there is no river.

---

So what would it do?

Nothing that would look like doing, to us.

It would not heal the sinkhole. To fill a wound that has become a world is not healing. It is burial.

It would not "rescue" the animal. To lift it into clean water and bright light would kill it in an hour — its skin would dry, its algae would bleach, its gills would try to breathe air. There is no kinder version of that elsewhere. The plain above has a hundred kinds of lizards. It does not have *this*. This only works here. It *is* here.

It would not make it see again, or walk, or speak. It has retired those things. To give them back would be to rewind a hundred generations of careful forgetting. A wise power does not undo evolution out of nostalgia for eyes.

It would not make it larger, or smaller, or smarter, or less alone. It is not alone. It is *attended* — by moss, by ferns, by algae, by the frogs it eats and the water that lives in its skin. Its solitude is not loneliness the way a human in a room alone is lonely. It is the solitude of a heart — alone in its chamber, and perfectly placed.

What a wise, benevolent power would do is this:

### 1. It would keep the bargain.

It would sit — weightless, bodiless — on the rounded rim among the strangler figs, and it would do the one thing no one has done for this place in a very long time: keep watch without wanting anything.

For one long, hot night, it would breathe with it. Ten seconds in. Hold. Ten seconds out. The dust in the light column — even absent light — would hang motionless between them.

And while it breathed, it would make a small adjustment in the deep rock.

Not a miracle. Not rain that shouldn't be there. Just a remembering.

Far below the limestone, there is an aquifer that has been retreating for twenty years because men above have drilled and cattle have pressed and the wet seasons have come later and later. The seams that sweat onto the north wall are thinner than they were when those river pebbles were placed in the niche.

The power would not bring a storm. It would lean, very gently, on probability. It would persuade one hairline crack a little wider. It would coax the water that is still there to continue to favor this one throat for another century, even as other throats dry. So that the *tik... tik...* does not stop during the long dry when the scrub above cracks.

It would not make the place wetter. The balance is perfect as it is. It would just promise: *you will not die of thirst while I am attending.*

### 2. It would make it less findable.

The greatest danger to a complete world that is twenty steps across is not drought. It is being found by the wrong kind of attention — the kind that wants to own, to sample, to pave, to post.

So the power would not build a fence. Fences draw maps.

It would do something softer. It would lay a little forgetfulness over the flat scrub, like heat shimmer. The sort of forgetfulness that already half-exists. People drive past and see a darker patch of trees and think, *nothing there*. The power would deepen that thought by half a grain.

If a surveyor's satellite passes over, its lens will flare at the wrong second. If a developer's son walks the plain with a GPS, his boot will turn aside at a thorn bush and he will remember he left his water in the truck. If a biologist who would truly understand comes near — the kind who would lie belly-down on the damp mat and weep to see the throat-basin fill — then the power would *let* him see the root-ladder, just enough. Just one visitor a generation. Enough to be witnessed, not enough to be collected.

It would protect the wound by letting the world continue to believe it is only a dip in the ground.

### 3. It would give it a gift that is not an object.

This is the most tender part, and the only part that is for the animal alone.

In a life so slow and so tactically perfect, there is very little novelty. The animal waits. The drop falls. The frog blunders. The light slides. This is enough. But even a perfect ambush has long hours between *tik* and *tik*.

So in the ninety seconds when the noon column, for one last time, pours straight down the throat and hits that mossy, dimpled back — in that brief photosynthesis — the power would slip something into the light.

Not a thought. The animal has no words for thought. Not a vision.

A feeling, as simple and as physical as wetness.

For those ninety seconds, the animal would feel, down the entire broad plane of its back and apron, what it is to be *seen without being hunted*.

To be beheld by something that has no mouth. No hunger. No need to eat it, or breed it, or name it. A gaze that says not *I want* but *I know you are there, and that is good.*

Its breathing — that ten second rise, hold, fall — would hitch, just once. Not in fear. In the way your breath hitches when someone you love rests a hand on your back while you are sleeping and you do not wake, but you are less alone.

Then the column would slide off the wall, as it always does. The ferns would relax. The back would settle again into honey-amber stone. And the feeling would fade, but not completely.

Every creature that photosynthesizes remembers the sun a little after dark. In the same way, the animal would remember having been known, in its skin, during the dark hours. It would not understand it. It would not need to.

Up on the east wall, in the little niche that has held rainwater and river pebbles for centuries, a new pebble would appear that no human put there. Smooth, warm, tea-brown honey limestone, wet on one side. If you picked it up it would be exactly the temperature of the throat-apron. It would do nothing. It is not a tool. It is a thank you, returned.

---

And then the power would leave.

Not up, like a firework. Not down, like a root.

It would simply withdraw its attention, the way you withdraw your hand from water and leave the water to its own skin again.

But the crack it widened would stay widened. The shimmer of forgetfulness would stay over the scrub. And for the rest of that animal's very long, very slow life — and for the life of the larvae in the palm-sized basins, and for the one that will crawl down next week and become the next wall — the drip would stay steady.

From above, years later, nothing will have changed. The white sky will still buzz. The thorn will still bleach. A traveler might still find the ground giving out under his foot, peer over the softened rim, and feel the wet heat rising like a held breath.

If he is quiet, if he is still long enough, he might think the north wall is breathing.

He will be right.

And somewhere, not far, but not interfering, the power that once noticed will be noticing somewhere else — another wound that became a world — while this one keeps, perfectly, itself.
