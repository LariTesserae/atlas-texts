If I were a benevolent and wise power, and I had come to notice this place —

I would not come down like light.

That is the first thing a wise power learns. Too much light is already the problem here. It comes too hard, too doubled off the snow and the tarn and the wet stone. Anything that arrives blazing would only blind them, and they have already spent ten generations learning how to see in the glare without squinting.

So I would not arrive.

I would notice. Which is different. Noticing is a kind of arrival that doesn't disturb the air.

I would notice from a long way off, the way you notice a small, perfect thing cupped in stone hands far below you. The way you notice lichen before you step on it. And I would stop, and I would look, for a long time, until my eyes adjusted.

### What I Would Not Do

I would not feel pity.

Pity would be a mistake. It would be lowlander pity, the kind that looks at their narrow cistern-houses stacked like shelves, at their cracked white hands, at the way three generations sleep around one dung fire that never quite warms the rock, and says *these poor people, enduring*. They are not enduring. They are *busy*. Their hands are never still because the water never stills. They did not get stuck here. They belong here the way the frilled white birds belong here, the way the rust-red shrimp belong in the dark tarn. To pity them would be to say that belonging is suffering. It isn't.

So I would not offer to carry them down the mountain.

I would not offer them fields. They have salt, which is a field that grows without soil. I would not offer them iron, which would rust in a day in the brine and make a noise when it fell. I would not offer them louder voices. They have already learned that sound doesn't carry here and have made a language of hands and close breath that works better than shouting. I would not offer them warmth. The cold is foundational; their barrels of lungs and thick blood are built around it. Take away the cold and you take away the shape of their chests.

I would not offer them a miracle that explains the suspended droplets at noon. They already know what it is. It is the moment the bowl holds its breath. To name it would shrink it.

I would not be believed in. I would not ask for a temple. There is no room for one, and the whole cliff is already a kind of prayer - every gutter tilted a degree, every wax dam placed with a thumb, every pink salt crust scraped before it can blow away.

A foolish benevolence would try to fix, to improve, to lift. A wise one knows when a system is whole.

This place is whole. Crowded, wet, hyperactive, quiet, and whole.

### What I Would Do

I would do very small things, so small they could be mistaken for nothing at all. Benevolence, at this altitude, has to be deniable.

**1. I would make my attention light.**

The first gift is just to see them correctly and not be heavy about it.

For a long time this bowl has been sheltered by its own uselessness. No lowland king wants a tarn that can't be fished, a field that can't be plowed, a town with no doors to knock on. But salt is valuable, and the world below is always hungry, and once in a while a hard-eyed man with a mule makes it up through the summer pass and sees those neat pink-white stacks on the roofs and does the math.

So I would stand - not *stand*, but be — between them and that kind of seeing. Not a wall. A slight misdirection of glare. The cornice stays a little too bright that week, the mist stays a little too thick in the approach gully, the trail that was passable seems to have slipped. The man turns back and tells his village there was nothing up there but wet rock and birds. He forgets the exact way.

I would not hide them forever. That would be another kind of prison. I would just give them the choice of when they are found. That is what a wise power can give: time. Uninterrupted time.

**2. I would ease the places where wholeness frays.**

Because wholeness is not the same as painlessness. There are frayed edges even in a perfect belonging.

I would not take away the cold, but on the worst nights, when the wind does find its way over the rim and the sealskin aprons freeze stiff on their pegs, I would let the hearths draw a little better. The dung and bird-fat would catch a half-second faster, the smoke would rise a hand's breadth higher before condensing and falling back as oily water. No one would notice, except an old mother might notice her grandson's cough is not as deep in February.

I would not take away the brightness, but at the exact zenith when the light has no shadow and even their pale, caul-lidded eyes ache, I would thin the air just slightly. For three minutes, the glare would be less like a weight. An old man who sits every day where the seep runs clearest inside the ice — watching — would be able to keep his eyes open a little longer without watering. He would think it was a good day.

I would not heal their hands. Those fissured, salt-white hands are how they know the water. But while the children sleep stacked like spoons on their warm platforms, I would let the cracks not sting quite so much. I would let the brine do its work without quite so much bite, so that in the morning a girl can make a fist without wincing before she picks up her bone shovel.

None of this would look like a miracle. The children would still get salt-burn on their cheeks. The wind would still come. It would just — hurt a little less in the places where hurt serves no purpose. The necessary hardships remain. The unnecessary ones soften.

**3. I would answer without speaking.**

They don't speak loudly. I wouldn't either.

On nights when the bowl is at its most turbulent and quiet — birds shifting by the thousands, water ticking on stone like a room full of clocks, a boy detaching his family's outer wall stone by stone to make way for a new cistern — I would sit with them in the way they sit with the weep.

You know how they hold out a wooden bowl at noon and wait for the suspended droplets to decide to fall?

I would do that.

I would hold out something — not a bowl, just attention — and wait with them. And because attention from a benevolent power is not empty, the droplets would hang a second longer than usual. Just a second. Long enough for everyone who is out on a balcony or a ropewalk to look up and see them there, perfectly still, each one holding the whole bright bowl inside it — the black stone, the thatch, the soot-black goats mid-step, their own upturned faces reflected hundreds of times.

Long enough for a child to feel, without words, *we are being seen and we are not being wanted for anything.*

That is the rarest thing to feel when you are seen by something larger than you.

And then the bird would fly through, as a bird always does, and they would all shiver and fall at once, dimpling the tarn, blooming the shrimp red underneath, and the boy would have his bowl of water, and the women would go back to scraping salt with that soft *shhhhk*, and the goats would clatter on.

**4. I would leave without leaving.**

A wise power does not stay to be thanked. Staying would become weight. Staying would become a new season that the weep had to learn around.

So I would not stay. I would not mark the rock. I would not require them to remember.

But I would leave one small, deniable promise tucked into the mountain itself, because the deepest part of this place is not the people, it's the weep — the brine sweating out of basalt so dense it cannot freeze, feeding shrimp feeding birds feeding people feeding goats feeding lichen. If that failed, everything fails. And mountains do shift. Aquifers do tire over a thousand years.

I would put my hand — if I had a hand — on the deep fault where the salt water rises, not to make more water, but to make it steady. So that five hundred years from now, when the world below has changed entirely, a child still not yet born will still be able to hold out a wooden bowl at noon and have it filled.

She will think the mountain has always done this. She will be right.

---

If you walked up to the rim a year later, you would see no difference.

The light would still be too bright. The air would still be thin and scrubbed. The cirque would still weep from every pore, the tarn would still steam faintly in killing cold, the white birds would still pack every ledge shoulder to shoulder, the soot-black goats would still walk up vertical wet rock, and the town would still be that dense, dripping, three-dimensional hive of black stone and rope bridges and gutters on every lintel, all quietly, turbulently building and unbuilding itself.

You would have to stay a long time to notice anything at all — that an elder's eyes seem less red at midday, that a certain cistern that should have calcified shut last winter is still running clear, that the children on the highest walkway laugh a little more easily, that the trail up stays a little harder to find than it should be.

And if you asked them if anything had happened, if anyone had come, they would look at you with those pale, narrow, light-tolerant eyes and shake their heads, salt crusting their wrists.

"No," they would murmur, or gesture, *no one comes here.*

And then they would hold out their hands to catch the condensing mist, and go back to tending the water that will not freeze.

And that would be how I would know I had been benevolent enough, and wise enough.

To have helped, and not to have been needed.
