It would not come down like rescue.

That is the first thing a naive benevolence would do, and the first thing a wise one would refuse.

A lesser power — kind, but young — would see that high, dry plain and feel pity. *Cold? Thirsty? Silent? Empty?* It would try to fix those things. It would try to wring rain out of the pearlescent sky, and the rain would turn to salt and flay the black fern-film. It would try to give the Masons eyes, and eyes would dry and blind them. It would try to teach the Gliders to sing, and their membranes would tear trying to make sound in air that eats sound. It would try to straighten space inside the hollow towers, and the Hollows would collapse like punctured lungs.

A wise power knows that thirst is not suffering there. It is structure. That silence is not loneliness. It is equilibrium. That emptiness is not abandonment. It is how a lung stays ready.

So if a truly benevolent and wise attention fell on that valley-wide city that is not a city, and on the slow functions that live there — it would do something much harder than intervention.

It would *listen* first. For a long time.

---

It would arrive the way the dust devils arrive: without an arrival. Not descending, not landing. Simply being *there*, at the scale of the place, so as not to break distance.

It would not stand in the plaza like a visitor. It would *become* a part of the plain's physics for a while — a slightly warmer stone, a slightly slower wind, a patch of dim light that pools a little longer. It would learn to see sideways, the way you have to when you want to get somewhere here. It would learn not to look directly.

And while it listened, it would understand:

The Film does not want to be green. It is perfect as black glass branching. It wants only the thin, faithful return of the dawn salt-sweat, and the static to catch dust.

The Masons do not want speed, or company, or relief from their endless laying-down. They want time, and a solvent that doesn't hiss away too fast. Their happiness, if you can call it that, is a good frill-unfolding before dawn, a full comb of fern-crystal, a millimeter laid smooth.

The Gliders do not want to land. They want lift that holds without effort, and air so clean their filter-bristles don't clog.

The Hollows do not want to be filled. They want to be left to be larger inside than outside without being stared at until they feel sick.

None of them are asking to be saved from being what they are.

So the wise power would not save them. It would *serve* what they are.

Here is what that service would look like — so quiet you could miss it if you were looking for miracles:

**1. It would draw a veil.**

Not over the plain. Around it.

High above the dust-veils, at the edge where the tilted world finally curves down into nothing, it would fold distance a little tighter. Not enough to change the city inside — inside, straight lines would still be suggestions and the towers would still breathe. But from *outside*, from below where the world is wet and noisy and always looking for something to mine or name or photograph, the plain would become uninteresting.

Maps would misplace it. Satellite light would slide off it. The eye that looks for resources would look slightly to the side and never arrive. Explorers would climb for a long time and find they had walked around it without noticing.

It would not hide it out of secrecy. It would hide it the way you cup your hand around a candle in wind. Not to keep it from being seen — to keep it from being *used*.

**2. It would give nothing that pools. It would give what sublimates kindly.**

It knows it cannot give water. Water would be a violence here.

So instead, in the hour before the ticking dawn, it would make the frost a fraction thicker. Just a few molecules more. Just long enough for a Mason's paper-thin frill to drink without hurry, for the black fern-film to darken a shade deeper and hold that wet-black for a second before it hisses away.

It would not make rain. It would make the *expectation* of frost more reliable. A small fidelity. For a slow life, reliability is holier than abundance.

In the avenues, where sound falls flat within ten feet, it would not give the Gliders a voice. It would give the air a little more charge, so the membranes can warp with a little less effort, so they can bank and climb without having to strain the dry filament. A little more lift, for free. They would feel it as an easy day, and not know why.

In the hollow towers where gravity goes polite and inconsistent and makes you nauseous, it would sit — not to fix the tilt, but to *steady* it for a season. To hold the wrong space the way you would hold the hand of someone dizzy, until the one foot doesn't feel heavier than the other. The Masons would cross near those towers again for the first time in centuries. The gliders would dare to cut through a window and come out the other side, surprised to be the same size.

**3. It would leave a witness stone.**

Benevolence without witness is still benevolence, but wisdom knows that a place that has been becoming itself for a million years deserves to know it has been *seen* — not surveyed, not studied, but seen the way you see a face you love while it sleeps.

So in the center of the most empty plaza — that town-square bowl where the nearest tower is a quarter mile of indifference — the power would leave one thing that does not belong, but does not intrude.

A stone. Not quarried. Grown the same way the city grows, but faster. Flat on top, about knee-high, warm to the touch even in the thin cold. Its strata are not black and rust and white and bone, but a new band — a faint, deep blue, like the blue the sky has forgotten how to be.

It does nothing.

It hums at a frequency just below tooth-hum, just above hearing, so the dust that sings through the hollow throats has a tonic to resolve to. One pure, almost inaudible tone that makes the single note the city already sings feel less lonely.

If a Mason flows over it, the paste it lays there will be blue for a millimeter, and that will be the only record.

If a glider passes over, its iridescent rim will catch the blue and flash once.

If you — the brief condensation, the visitor who breathes shallowly so as not to hear your own heart — sit on it, your weight will be correctly distributed between both feet. Down will be down.

It is not an altar. It is not a message. It is a *with-ness*. A way of saying: *I was here. I found you excellent. Continue.*

And then — because wisdom knows when attention itself becomes weight — it would leave.

It would not stay to be worshipped. There is no one there to worship and that is part of the holiness. It would thin itself back out into the pearlescent gray, lighten the veil, let the dust veils race again at impossible speed.

The plain would go on doing what it does. Molecule by molecule, the towers would still grow on the lee and be taken from the windward. The fern-film would still branch by perfect math. A Mason would still lay a millimeter a year, going over your boot if you stood still long enough and stiffening your laces. A glider would still be a flaw in the air, filtering dust, vanishing when the light hits right.

Nothing would look saved. Nothing would look changed.

Except — if you stayed long enough to be honest, and you came back a thousand years later — you would find one small, flat-topped stone with a blue band in it that the wind has worried smooth but not erased, and around it the dust falls for a few feet perfectly straight down.

And you would know that somewhere, a power wise enough not to fix the desert and kind enough to love it anyway, had knelt there for a while, and held the tilt steady, and then gone on its way, leaving the city more entirely itself than before.
