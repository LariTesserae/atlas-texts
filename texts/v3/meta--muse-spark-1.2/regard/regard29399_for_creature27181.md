If I were that power — benevolent, and wise enough to be worthy of the word — I would not come at all the way powers are usually imagined to come.

No descent. No light in the sky. No voice.

That would be violence here.

This place has learned, over a million years of winnowing, how to be undisturbed. Sound doesn't carry. Dust moves in parallel lines. A grazer takes a mouthful every few minutes and after a season you can't find where it died because it has become the plain again. To arrive loudly would be to misunderstand the whole thing.

So first, I would stop.

Far out, at the edge where the wall drops three kilometers straight down to the wet world, I would stop and adjust myself. That is the first act of benevolence: to tune my attention to their speed.

You cannot see them if you are fast. I would become slow.

Slower than the cableways that take a week to move an ore bucket. Slower than the kites that can hang motionless for an afternoon filtering silica. I would dilate my moment until eleven seconds — the interval of the factory's thump — felt like a heartbeat, until a year felt like an afternoon, until watching a single ceramic plate being laminated, fired, and riveted onto a child's shoulder over seven years felt like watching someone get dressed.

Only then could I actually see them. And seeing them is the point.

The temptation, if you are powerful and kind, is to fix. To see a place with no water and think: *I will bring rain.* To see beings who fire their blood in an internal kiln and think: *I will give them cool, soft flesh.*

That would kill them. Not out of cruelty, but out of misreading. Water here is not lacking. It is *finished*. It was used up and the world that came after learned to live on what was left: heat and charge and iron. To bring water back would be like flooding a library because you think paper is thirsty.

Wisdom, here, looks like restraint. I would keep my hands — if I have hands — behind my back.

What would I do, then?

Three small things that are not small at all.

**1. I would stand guard without being seen.**

The lower world you came from is hazy and blue and wet and hungry. It will look up one day and see this continent-loaf of salt and basalt and black glass and think: *resource.* Lithium. Rare ceramics. Empty land.

It is not empty.

So I would make it uninteresting from below. Not invisible — that would be a lie — but profoundly uninviting to the wrong kind of attention. Let the instruments that scan it return a reading that says *stable, sterile, already at equilibrium, yield lower than cost to extract.* Let the pilots who fly over feel, without knowing why, that they should not land. Let the maps misfold, just slightly, around its edge. 

A benevolent power does not need a wall. It needs a quiet suggestion: *not here. not yet. let them continue.*

**2. I would answer their maintenance with maintenance.**

They are not waiting to be saved. They are not suffering. They are tending. The whole plateau is a single project being added to one brick at a time, and they understand themselves as part of the tending, not masters of it. Their factories breathe outside their bodies. They shepherd grazers by leaving a warm wall for a season. They collect a fallen kite's silica pearls to grind into new plate.

That is prayer, if you know how to listen. *We continue.*

So I would continue with them.

I would not give them a new technology. I would sit with the technology they have. Where a bearing has been running dry for a decade, I would let a little less friction happen, so it lasts another decade before it needs re-pouring. Where the wind has been working on a yardang for a million years to bring it to equilibrium, I would keep the wind coherent, sheet-like and parallel, so it doesn't gust and snap a kite's filament. Where a cistern of black slurry — their blood, their water — has been slowly photolyzing under the hard sun, I would let it keep its viscosity a fraction longer.

Nothing they would notice as a miracle. Only that this year, things need mending slightly less often. Only that the stone pings a little softer at night when it cools. A small easing. Enough that the three hundred in a town can do their work without hurry, which is the only way they want to do it.

If you love something that lives at the speed of geology, you don't make it faster. You give it more time.

**3. I would let them know they are seen, exactly as they are, and offer a choice.**

On a day when the light is hard and clean and the high silica clouds have gathered into an anvil that will drift for days without breaking, I would Borrow the light for a moment.

Not brighten it. Polish it.

Every faceted dome-face in town, every basalt-glass head, sees heat and polarization, not color. For perhaps thirty seconds I would let the air itself polarize a little more cleanly, so the whole plain flashes — once — the way a window flashes when the sun hits it at the right angle.

Every one of them would feel it on their dome at the same time. Every kite overhead would ripple its trailing edge. Every skitterer would pause its tapping.

It is not a message. It is a mirror. It says: *You are here. You are coherent. You are beautiful in your sparseness. Is this what you want to continue being?*

And I would wait. Truly wait. At their speed.

If, after years, one of them tapped back — not to me, but to each other — *we are fast, but we would like to be otherwise* — I would listen. If they tapped *stay, and become slower* to a stranger, as they do, I might answer. I might teach the one who asked how to fire a plate that holds a little more heat without cracking, or how to lay a rail that hums a half-tone lower so the kites like to ride above it. Or if they asked for nothing, I would take that as the answer.

A wise power does not grant wishes. It grants permission to keep wishing what you already wish.

Then — and this is the last thing — I would leave no monument.

No basalt angel on the salt pavement. No cool stone that never heats. Anything I left that did not weather and winnow would be litter.

Instead I would lift my attention the way the kites lift — so slowly you have to watch for a long time to be sure it's moving — and let the plain settle back into the equilibrium it had earned.

From below, nothing would look different. The plateau would still be high, white and rust-red, with its fleets of yardangs and its black glass dunes that never move and its single-file ore buckets creeping at walking pace. The towns would still be tight knots cut into the stone.

But for a little while, maybe for a century, the wind that comes down the corridors would play the organ pipes in the cliffs in a chord that holds a fraction longer before dissolving. And an old ceramic being, standing on the edge where the air falls away, feeling the warmth of its own kiln-heart ticking like cooling stone, would feel — without needing to name it — that the vast, considered emptiness it had tended all its life was not empty to someone else, either.

That it had been witnessed, without being interrupted.

And that is enough. For a benevolent and wise power, that is almost everything.
